# Re-engineering the executive: The 4th generation of EIS

Andrew Stein $^{*}$ ,1

Department of Business Computing, Faculty of Business, Victoria University of Technology, P.O. 14498 MMC, Melbourne, 3000, Australia

## Abstract

The executive information system (EIS) has long promised to provide executives with computing power and information access. The reality is that the EIS of today is moving down the organisational structure and the executive is still waiting for the benefits of information technology. This paper describes and extends an executive implementation methodology as a tool in re-engineering executive work practices in two Australian firms. It first describes the nexus between computing, executive management style and information systems. Then it discusses action research, as carried out on two small Australian companies. In using this methodology particular attention was paid to addressing any inherent weaknesses. The outcomes provided one executive with information tools that allowed for an extension of his creative work processes and the other with rapid internal monitoring of crucial production data. In the former case, all aspects of the methodology were successful, however the latter was flawed due to external commercial forces.

Keywords: Executive computing; Executive information systems; Implementation methodologies; Case studies; Australia

## 1. A conceptual framework

"The questions that we face today are finally about leadership,... will they be able to create organisational innovations that can exploit the unique capacities of the new technology and thus mobilise their organisation's productive potential to meet the heightened rigours of global competition? Will there be leaders who understand the crucial role that human beings from each organisational stratum can play in adding value to the production of goods and services?"

(Shoshanna Zuboff [14]).

## 2. Executive roles

One model for describing executive decision making is Anthony's hierarchy of organisation [1]. This model depicts operational, tactical, and strategic environs. These sub-divisions of decision making are not neatly encapsulated in either provenance or time allowance and are redundant in modern large organisations. Each decision may overlap into the other's domain and each may require similar information. Another point of interest is that executives of small organisations will often be involved in making decisions at the operational and or tactical levels. Alvin Toffler sees the third wave executive as being on-line and using the computer as a personal productivity tool [12]. They are electronically aided executives who will run nearly paperless offices through the use of electronic meetings, mail, and filing systems. He also sees executives operating in a team environment and further using the computer to assist their orientation. The supporting technology is expanding rapidly. Organisations are using platforms such as open systems, client-server, networking, and inter-networking to make information. Externally, communications technology is: shrinking organisational boundaries; reducing product operating spheres; and opening up the organisation to global information.

Boone categorises the roles an executive may play within the organisation [2]. The executive may be the coach, communicator, change agent and commander of the organisation. There is a notable exclusion of the traditional control role of the executive. The nexus between the executive and the executive work roles, decision making processes, information characteristics, information delivery platforms and global change places a focus upon the executive. An information system executive [5] even stated that the transaction processing system that generated cash flow for a large Australian company was far more important than any executive use of computing. This debunking of importance of the computer in the executive suite portrays the view that the executive and the computer are immiscible, or are they? Are the implementation methodologies used for EIS suitable or do we need to look at other methodologies?

## 3. Executive computing: Three generations

Rockart [10] sees three generations of EIS based upon the user group that the system serves. These are the individual, organisation and the corporate EIS. (See below) So if EIS (executive to enterprise) technology is migrating down and across into the organisation, then issues such as design and support assume even more importance. More importantly do EISs remain as executive based tools as they migrate down the organisation? Do they provide differing sets of information to middle management and have vendors jettisoned the executive? Are vendors now concentrating upon the middle management domain? Dresner [6] debunks the notion of a single EIS platform delivering information using one tool to multiple users. He sees multiple users using multiple tools depending upon the role the user has in the organisation.

TIME User group

1982- Systems designed for individual executives

1987– Systems designed for teams of executives

1989– Systems support multiple levels of management

Sidebottom [11] comments that an EIS is 20% technology and 80% management. Cottrell [4] sees the management component as management of organisational resistance, data, technology and systems spread. Sidebottom reaffirms Rockart and Daniel, saying that critical success factors must drive the data. Kishore [9] sees organisational goals and critical success factors being inherent to the EIS idea. Volonino et al. [13] supports the CSF approach when they set out several guidelines for the development of EIS. Karbowski [8] states that research is the first step in EIS development; here it infers the process of finding a solution to a business problem. He also makes the point that the tool chosen for the implementation can be abstracted from the solution. Erickson [7] identifies three issues central to implementation of an EIS: (1) Conformity to the organisation's long term strategies for computing platforms, (2) completeness in meeting all the requirements of disparate users, and (3) the economics of using EIS in dynamic organisations.

## 4. Information success factors

“Computers give executives the opportunity to empower or oppress. If executives are truly interested in expanding their own minds and the minds of their people, they will use computers in ways that are consistent with that philosophy.”

(Mary Boone [3]).

The information success factor (ISF) method is a five step procedure beginning with definition of up to 10 business goals of the organisations. These are then prioritised. The top ranked goal is then used to ascertain three to five business strategies. The top ranked strategy is then used to ascertain leadership strategies. Leadership strategies are the personal tasks that the executive will perform in order to achieve the business goals. These are also prioritised, aggregated, and related to three or four information success factors. The interviewer introduces technology by describing one or more tools that may help in achieving the information success factors. For each tool the executive is informed of implementation issues, like cost and training and the executive then decides upon further exploration. An important part of this process is the management interview coupled with realistic goal setting and prioritising. Selection of the tool or technology is the last step in the process. This method should yield benefit if the goal and strategy setting are realistic. Executive co-operation is sought at every stage of development and this methodology de-emphasises the technology of the solution. Leadership strategies to carry out critical business strategies are emphasised and this individualistic approach yields a fourth generation of executive information systems (See Below).

TIME User group

1991– Individual executives guide, decide and desire REAL systems

The fourth generation is not a return to the first generation, systems designed for individual executive use. The executive guides, decides, and desires systems that meet the executive's information needs and work requirements.

## 5. Case study 1: Freight Limited

In 1982, Freight Limited was formed to act as an export and import consultant and freight broker to exploit the niche markets opening up in metal recycling. The company's mission now includes adding value to export commodities before they are dispatched. Presently metal recycling is the main revenue provider but increasing emphasis is being placed upon the export of primary produce, including rice, cotton and diary products. In 1992 Freight Limited had eleven Australian based suppliers and thirteen Japanese and European clients. Of the revenue gained, 50% comes from one supplier and 20% from three others. The business provides freight services to these four suppliers in the metal recycling area. The main supplier operates on yearly contracts. The major clients who receive the recycled metal are Japanese trading houses who supply to Japanese car component manufacturers. In 1991, the former Soviet Union flooded the world markets to obtain hard currency thereby pushing the price of aluminium down to an historical low. In January 1991, the price was \$US1950 per tonne but by November 1993 it was \$US1050 per tonne. This has had a deleterious effect upon the recycling of aluminium as manufacturers can negotiate lower prices at the world ingot price. This has had the effect of forcing Freight Limited to expand revenue sites and diversify its client base.

## 5.1. The interview

The interview followed the completion of a preliminary survey questionnaire. It helped the executive understand the terminology and process involved. This document also allowed preparation of ancillary questions to allow ranking of the CSF's.

## 5.2. Step 1. Business goals

The goals of the organisation were:

Business goals

\- Provide niche exporting consultancy to the metal recycling industry.

\- Add value to the exported commodity.

\- Provide a stable, modern, and profitable business organisation.

• Diversify into other commodity markets.

The executive had difficulty in rank ordering them. The STABILITY issue was a constant goal of the organisation. The ADDING VALUE and DIVERSIFICATION goals were considered to need imminent expansion. This uncertainty is inherent to Boone's methodology. Computerisation of the most crucial item would be paramount to the acceptance of this methodology for later applications. This conflict between executive and consultant and their differing aims must be managed to provide solutions that are amenable to both. A balance must be achieved between the executive's business knowledge and the consultant's system knowledge. In Freight Limited the goals were ranked, with the executive choosing the NICHE METALS area as the primary business goal. The rationale was that existing core business had to be protected before seeking diversification. As the consultant, I felt that ADDING VALUE and DIVERSIFICATION goals were going to require access to external information sources. As this was the first application of Boone's method, a solution that involved maintaining existing contracts would be easier to manage. Time scales can also be important as the next iteration of this method could involve either the ADDING VALUE or the DIVERSIFICATION goals.

## 5.3. Step 2. Business strategy

There are several areas that are critical to the ongoing success of the company. Stable staffing, supplier control, client control, cash flow monitoring and container utilisation are just some of these.

Business strategies

\- Retaining the three major contacts with the major supplier.

\- Maintaining stable and skilled staffing profile for client management.

\- Ensure accurate monitoring of projected cash flow.

Boone's methodology requires the executive to prioritise these. In this instance, he selected retaining the major contract. When asked “why?”, the executive stated that “maintaining existing contracts in harsh economic times is prudent.” It is important to always refer to goals as leading to strategies, there was difficulty as the word strategy can have powerful connotations. I found that asking the questions without using the words goal or strategy was useful; e.g., “Can we increase our revenue and market share for can scrap?”, “How”, “What single thing was crucial last time the contact was negotiated.” I have a good knowledge of their business processes which helped, but a consultant using this method would need to spend time learning the business processes.

## 5.4. Step 3. Leadership strategies

Next, the executive must determine the leadership strategies he/she is going to implement to achieve the top business strategy. These were:

Leadership strategies

\- Foster personal business relations with the supplier.

\- Adhere strictly to contract requirements.

\- Provide prompt and accurate month end reporting.

\- Monitor the supplier's industry position.

\- Be pro-active in contract negotiations.

Developing leadership strategies from the business strategy was relatively easy. The executive had learnt and was able to expand the strategies relating to retaining the major contract. Again the questions referred back to previous negotiations and I tried to determine any peculiar factors that were likely to apply to the next round of negotiations, such as a further drop in aluminium prices due, China's purchase of a larger stake in the Portland (Victoria) smelter and a slow down in the Japanese economy. The executive was able to supply the first three strategies and he thought that the contractual adherence and monthly reporting strategies were very important. He could easily associate these strategies with the use of a computer. When the possibility of monitoring the supplier's industry position was raised, he was surprised that this could be a possible information solution. He had been doing this monitoring continuously through informal channels within the supplier's middle management structure. When external databases and news clipping services were mentioned he said: “..any bit of information is useful as it may verify my other sources..”.

## 5.5. Step 4. Information success factors

Once the leadership strategies are in place, the information success factors are defined by the consultant. These are the doable tasks that must be achieved for success.

Information success factors

(a) Subjective assessment of the nature of relationship with supplier contacts.

(b) Collate textual information about suppliers.

(c) Tap external information networks.

(d) Monitor supplier complaints and be pro-active in complaint resolution.

(e) Monitor adherence to first day next month rule.

(f) Be available to the supplier.

(g) Be pro-active in contract negotiations, explore direct links with supplier and explore adding value to commodity.

## 5.6. Step 5. Identify potential

ISFs are grouped according to Boone's methodology, A, B and C involve staying well informed about both internal and external activities. Tools include tapping external databases and a personal database organiser to record and collate any internal or external information concerning suppliers. A computer with an outlier or personal database may be useful. Factors D and E concern monitoring of internal information but could be incorporated into the personal database organiser, the aim being to build a complete and integrated picture of the supplier. Factor F infers high quality communications, facsimile, mobile phone, tele-conferencing and perhaps voice mail. The use of word processing to personalise messages may also be indicated. Factor G increases decision making creativity. The use of a spreadsheet to build financial models would be paramount, as would the use of presentation tools to improve the effectiveness of the contract meetings. Table 1 gives a summary of the Factors and Tools. The personal database organiser was selected and the chosen tool was a windows based word processor with outlier together with a database package. Using a word processor allows several advantages over a traditional database. Training is reduced, creativity is enhanced by linking data to an advanced writing tool, and there is a greater ability to present data with presentation graphics. The tool matches the way the executive handles textual information.

## 6. Case study 2: Processing Limited

Processing Limited was formed in 1991 as a value adding manufacturing entity to Freight Limited. This includes adding value to several scrap metal commodities before they are dispatched to Japan. The company provides a strategic link in forging an alliance between Freight Limited and the Australian scrap producers. In 1992 Processing Limited was undergoing difficult trading conditions, with startup costs ballooning and income pegged due to decreased aluminium can usage. A vital piece of processing equipment repeatedly failed, resulting in production slow-downs and increased overtime and maintenance costs. Losses encountered in securing contracts became significant and rental costs were disproportionate. The symbiotic relationship between Freight Limited and Processing Limited cushioned the unhappy trading situation, but the final trading figures for 1991/92 were poor.

## 6.1. Step 1. Business goals

The executive stated the goals as follows: Business goals

\- Develop an independent profit making centre through metal recycling

\- Provide a strategic link between scrap suppliers and Freight Limited.

\- Provide a stable, modern and profitable business organisation.

• Explore alternative ways to add value to exporting metal products.

Here the trading stress that Processing Ltd had met in 1991/92 and 1992/93 was the reason the executive chose the “independent profit making centre” as the major goal. The executive said: “..I know Aluminium is an up and down business but all I am doing is bouncing off the floor..”.

In small firms like Processing Limited, it is important for the consultant to help the executive select the primary goal. Whilst this appears to be circumventing the executives business acumen I feel that there is a trade off between the possible and the impossible. Of the four goals, STABILITY was a motherhood goal, whilst the ALTERNATIVE goal was in place for expansion once the current difficult trading problems were passed. The STRATEGIC goal was linked to the fact that Processing Limited should lock suppliers into Freight Limited's export consultancy. The two goals that were ranked together were the DEVELOPMENT of a profit centre and the LINKING goal. An interesting dichotomy is that the profit making goal is essentially an operational goal while the linking goal is strategic. Why was an operational goal chosen over a strategic goal? In this case the executive and I felt that company viability outweighed the development of the locking-in goal. So the executive of Processing Limited is not only a strategist but equally importantly the leader in the operational domain. So executive computing solutions need not be strategic in scope.

## 6.2. Step 2. Business strategy

Several areas are critical to the creation of profit within a scrap metal processing company: stable staffing, supplier control, cash flow monitoring and plant maintenance. Two business strategies were identified as paramount in importance.

Business strategies

\- Develop plant maintenance schedule to minimise repair costs and maximise machine up time.

\- Ensure daily, accurate cash flow monitoring and make good predictions.

The executive commented: “..I can get a raw cash flow, but I cannot foresee outflows more than a few days in advance..I need to have a regular flow of scrap through the plant..”. The executive prioritised these business strategies with the maintenance strategy first. He selected it because it was a continuous drain upon the financial resources and workforce morale.

## 6.3. Step 3. Leadership strategies

The executive was then able to determine the strategies that could be adopted. As there had been a year long history of breakdowns and piecemeal efforts at recording machine usage the problems had been known but strategies had not been considered in a detached way. By providing augmented leadership and by being a creative thinking process, the harassed executive could see a structured method for problem solving. Each outcome could be independent and yield benefits.

Leadership strategies

\- Develop optimum material throughput rates.

\- Develop machine usage schedules.

\- Provide maintenance and workflow schedules.

\- Provide a problem monitoring log.

## 6.4. Step 4. Information success factors

The information success factors mainly related to recording internal data.

Information success factors

(a) Record throughput for minimum machine downtime.

(b) Project workflows to meet voyage shipping deadlines.

(c) Record machine hours, breaks, and operators.

(d) Develop a maintenance schedule with; shipping schedules, minimal cost overhead (staff), material workflows.

(e) Track the conditions that exist when breakdowns occur.

## 6.5. Step 5. Identify potential

Factors A, C and E involve staying informed on internal activities. Tools used include a database and/or a spreadsheet to record the rates, hours, and conditions. Factors B and D relate to scheduling two or more variables to achieve optimal conditions. A spreadsheet with a modelling component could be useful. Consideration could also be given to specialised scheduling software. The executive was presented with a set of tools that could enhance his work, with estimates of the installation time, cost, training and usage required. A spreadsheet was chosen. It had two sections; a recording section for factors A, E and D and a scheduling section to model machine usage, material throughput, and machine maintenance. ## 7. Outcomes

The two outcomes yielded information tools that enhanced executive creativity in Freight Limited and risk management in Processing Limited. In Freight Limited the executive had his leadership augmented through the application of ISF. In Processing Limited the avowed benefits of the solution were not realised yet additional benefits were achieved.

## 7.1. Export services: Freight Limited

The Freight Limited executive emerged as an all writing, all analysing executive. Change in the business world is often shaped by financial consideration. The change in work practices were shaped by the goals of the organisation. The most crucial question is how else can the executive use technology?

There are two crucial stages in applying this method. Firstly, use a combination of information gathering techniques to determine the way the executive works. In Freight Limited I was able to draw upon four years of consultancy experience in order to understand the way the business operates and further I had the confidence of the executive. The executive was able to transform his work patterns: “..I wait until it is quiet and I tap away in the study..away from the phone, the family, and the office..when I am finished I give the work to my assistant and I have more control..”. Secondly, keep the technology last.

## 7.2. Manufacturing: Processing Limited

Processing Limited was suffering from the common business malady of start up. High initial costs coupled with short term contract periods had placed extreme pressure on the executive. Whilst strategic decisions are required the executive is down in the operational mire of survival. The leadership that this small business executive required had to do with schedules, feedback, maintenance, and other operational problems. The scheduling tool provides positive mechanisms whereby the executive can control the costs in the firm and at the same time free up time for the strategic planning. The executive was more concerned with using communication as a coaching measure, but could not be in the head office while directing operations on the factory floor.

My selection of information success factors was flawed as the executive desired communications rather than the internal data reporting tools. This failure can be attributed to: my inexperience in manufacturing operations; misreading the executive's desire to “be present” on the factory floor; and the realisation that the methodology requires great care when firms are in financial stress.

## 8. Concluding remarks

Information success factors as a methodology makes the crucial link between the executives leadership strategy and technology. In large organisations this link is made by IS staff, but in small organisations this knowledge often does not exist. In both case studies the executive operated alone, further applications of ISF in team situations following more traditional CSF team methods could be the basis for future work.

The executive and the computer were linked, though seeming incompatible, and immiscible. But why must they be linked? Leadership has assumed greater importance in the 1990s as world competitive boundaries are shrinking. We live in a global village and our neighbours are everybody. Change used to be periodic, freeze –unfreeze –freeze. Now change is a pantechnicon. In response to change, many techniques are used to assist organisations. Some are: business process re-engineering, quality management, change management, and fifth generation management. They are designed to look again at old processes and see if there are better ways of doing them. The executive is at the apex of all this change, both initiating and responding to it. Whilst organisations are adopting re-engineering to survive in the 1990s we must ask, can the executive be re-engineered? This paper presents a possible methodology.
