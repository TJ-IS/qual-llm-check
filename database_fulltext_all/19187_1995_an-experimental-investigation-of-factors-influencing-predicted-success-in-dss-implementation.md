# An experimental investigation of factors influencing predicted success in DSS implementation

Shailendra C. Palvia ${}^{a,*}$ , Norman L. Chervany ${}^{b}$

$^{a}$ School of Management, Long Island University, Long Island, NY, USA $^{b}$ Curtis L. Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, USA

## Abstract

This paper describes the results of an experiment that examined the effects of four factors hypothesized to influence the predicted success at various stages of the implementation of a proposed Decision Support System (DSS). The setting for the experiment was the development of an interactive financial budget planning and expense tracking system. The experiment was framed using two basic models: the three-stage change process comprising unfreezing, moving, and refreezing; and second, a model for the management of technological change. The four factors studied were: (a) the time allowed for completion of the systems development and implementation effort; (b) the presence or absence of a champion; (c) the ease with which the managerial change required by the proposed system could be reversed; and (d) the level of success in the preceding stage or stages of the change process. The subjects were mid-level managers and staff attending evening MBA classes at the University of Minnesota.

Keywords: Decision support system; Implementation; Scenarios; Unfreezing; Moving; Refreezing; Project champion; Change reversibility; Predicted success; Resistance to change

## 1. Introduction

Changes in information systems (IS) technology have produced a significant revolution in the opportunities for improved managerial performance. PC-based processing technology connected via networks, along with the development of “user-oriented” fourth generation languages, has resulted in an explosion of decision support systems (DSS) and end-user computing activity. In today’s competitive environment, companies cannot afford to miss opportunities for higher quality decision-making through the use of elegant DSS. However, technical potential and elegance is not enough. What is needed is a way to increase the probability that white-collar productivity opportunities like DSSs will be successfully exploited. The identification of effective strategies for implementation of DSS is crucial for the realization of the potential gains that IS technology has provided. This paper reports the results of an experiment that examined the effects of four experimental factors on the predicted implementation success of an interactive financial reporting DSS.

## 2. A model for the management of technological change

The design and implementation of a DSS is an example of planned technological change. To the individuals and organization involved, the success or failure of a proposed DSS depends upon how well the change process is managed.

An overarching issue is: what distinguishes technological change in an organization from change in general? Webster (1967) describes technology as “...the application of scientific knowledge, methods, or research to the industrial arts.” Science, in turn, is defined as “...a branch of study concerned with... the establishment of verifiable general laws.” A DSS involves three major areas of scientific knowledge – one is physical (computer hardware) and two are cognitive (computer software and mathematical modelling).

Drucker (1988) captures the essence of the effects of technology/science on management processes. He defines an information-based organization as an organization where the expertise (technology/science) required to produce the goods and services resides “at the bottom of the organization” in its workers and professionals, as opposed to its general managers.

Drucker's dichotomy exemplifies the over-arching issue in the management of planned technological change. The two groups of experts (the professionals and the managers) do not share a set of common skills, abilities, and language. The general managers do not understand how the technical experts perform their tasks and vice versa. But the groups have a symbiotic relationship. They need each other to be successful. Both sides of the managerial equation must trust each other (Beath, 1989; Churchman and Schainblatt, 1965; Henderson, 1990; Smith, 1989). The two roles describe the situation faced in the development and implementation of a DSS. The manager does not have a full understanding of DSS sciences while the IS analyst does not have an in-depth understanding of management problems and responsibilities.

## 2.1. The components of the model

The model of planned technological change that provides the conceptual foundation for this research has three basic components. These are: (a) the time during which organizational change proceeds; (b) the analysis by the manager and the analyst of the potential effects and risks of the proposed change; and (c) the options that are available to manage the change process. The complete model, as well as a summary of the relevant theory and research, is described by Chervany and Karahanna (1990). Fig. 1 depicts the model.

## 2.2. The change process

The description of the technological change process begins with a system of current work patterns. The performance of the system at the individual and organizational levels is a result of the fit among the tasks to be performed, the individuals responsible for their completion, and the technology used to support the individuals (Chakravarthy, 1982 and Chakravarthy, 1987; Galbraith and Nathanson, 1979; and Venkatraman, 1989). Starting from the assumption that individuals and organizations seek stability in their work patterns, successful change involves activities that disturb the current pattern and then replace them with new ones. Lewin (1952) and Schein (1964) describe the process as moving through three stages. The first stage, “unfreezing,” encompasses the activities in which the affected parties come to see the opportunities and benefits of the proposed system as well as the limitations and problems with the current system. The second stage, “moving,” involves the development of the systems, procedures, and policies that will make the proposed system operational. The final stage, “refreezing,” includes all those activities at the individual, group, and organizational levels that reinforce the use of the new system. After refreezing, the performance of the new system is a function of the “fit” among the new tasks, individuals, and technology.

The model rests on a critical premise. In the absence of a clear perception of the benefits, the manager will resist change. A manager wants to know, “What is in it for the organization and for me?”.

## 2.3. Diagnostic activities

The diagnostic activities take place from two related vantage points: that of the manager and of the analyst. While these diagnoses should be conducted jointly (or shared), all too often they are made independently. The manager's analysis should ensure that the system will improve the organization's performance. For most IS projects, this is a difficult task. Because of this difficulty, a manager's assessment often gravitates to: "How will it affect the way I do my job?" How does a manager judge the merits of a proposed DSS? Joel Barker (1990), a futurist, has studied the process by which innovations occur: in particular, the characteristics of innovations that increase the probability of success. He has identified ten dimensions organized into three categories:

Cost and Benefits: (1) the DSS will improve performance of the manager; (2) it will require no more resources than the system it is replacing; and (3) it will present no significant down-side risk if it should fail (e.g., there must be an easily accessible back-up system).

Ease of Trial and Use: (4) there is a way to try the DSS on a limited scale with the same basic logic; (5) its output is understandable in a familiar language; (6) it is easy to use; and (7) it will be possible to return to current managerial processes if a trial of new DSS proves to be unsatisfactory.

History of the System: (9) the proposed DSS must appear reliable (i.e., it has worked elsewhere; and (10) that the analyst and IS group are credible.

The analyst's efforts are directed at two sets of issues. First, the analyst needs to determine the manager's perceptions about the proposed DSS. Failure to do this can lead to technically sound systems that are not used. Second, the analyst must assess factors in the implementation environment that will increase or decrease the chance that the proposed DSS will succeed. Alter and Ginzberg (1978) identified a set of seven “developmental risk factors.” Their work identifies an ideal development environment as one that has:

Minimum Complexity: (1) a small number of managers and (2) a small number of analysts. In this way, the likelihood of fundamental differences of opinion on the purpose or approach to the project as well as the chance for communication errors are reduced.

Strong Cognitive Environment: (3) the development needs managers and analysts that have experience with the type of DSS that is being developed; (4) all of them plus all managers affected by the system must understand and agree on the purpose of the system; and (5) all must understand and accept the impact of the system on their work.

Strong Project Environment: (6) It must have strong management support; management here refers to those managers in the organization to whom the actual DSS users report to, but who themselves are not directly affected by the system; and (7) it must be economically, operationally, and technically feasible.

## 2.4. Implementation management activities

The goal of an implementation plan is straightforward. It should build on “favourable developmental risk factors” and should ameliorate the effects of the “unfavourable developmental risk factors.” For planning purposes, implementation options can be organized into four broad categories: (a) the specific features included in the proposed DSS; (b) the structure of the design team; (c) the type of development process employed; and (d) the nature of the organizational context.

## 2.5. How the process works?

The initiation of a project occurs when an assessment of the fit or the performance of the current system (Model activities 1A and 1B in Fig. 1) produces a “felt need” (Havelock and Havelock, 1973) for improvement. As shown by the dotted line, this may not be the same for the manager and the analyst.

Within the model there is iteration between the diagnosis and implementation management activities. For the manager, the evaluation of the felt need (Model Activity 2A) and evaluation of the implementation management activities (Model Activity 4A) determine the perceived fit of the proposed system. In turn, the manager's perceived fit based on an assessment of the availability of organizational resources, influences his/her input into the implementation management activities (Model Activity

4A). The analyst experiences a similar, but more complex iterative problem. The analyst's judgement about the manager's perceived fit (Model Activity 3) also influences the diagnosis. In turn, the analyst's diagnosis influences the implementation management activities (Model Activity 4B). The model emphasizes a very important point: manager and analyst must cooperate actively. Failure to communicate can easily lead to problems and likely failure.

The selected implementation management activities (Model Activities 5A, 5B, and 5C) work on the current system (Model Activity 1C). Feedback from the stages of change (Model Activities 5A, 5B, and 5C) influences the implementation plan. The change process is completed (Model Activity 6) as refreezing produces a fit, and ultimately performance, for the new system.

## 3. The research approach

The field of DSS has seen explosive growth in the last decade. Many research activities have been undertaken to understand this field. In the past, emphasis has been generally on a combination of “applying” and “surveying.” However, this approach is costly and risk-prone. Several useful insights in this area of DSS development and implementation can be gained by a less costly and less risky approach of “experimentation.” This is the approach we chose for this research.

## 3.1. Research questions and the environment

In selecting the specific research questions, we were driven by two considerations. First, the question should involve the current state of knowledge in the field. Second, the usefulness of the implementation management model as described in Fig. 1 as an organizing device must be recognized. The specific research questions studied were to investigate the effects of:

(1) the time allowed to complete each stage in the change process? (Model Component: Implementation Options - Development Process Employed).

(2) the level of support of a project champion? (Model Component: Implement Options -Design Team Structure).

The base scenario. A recurring problem for all types of organizations is budgeting for various responsibility (cost and profit) centres. The inherent uncertainty in budgeting decisions is compounded by inadequate information. Furthermore, cost allocation methods are usually computationally complicated and circuitous. This makes the pricing decisions for output services and products difficult; it makes the examination of multiple alternatives difficult. An online interactive financial planning, budgeting, and expense tracking system named Budget-Aid is being developed to replace the current semi-automated batch system. These questions do not deal with a specific DSS: they are generic. Because of this, we decided to use an experimental environment that did not require an actual DSS to be used by the research subjects. The advantage of this approach is that the execution and the results of the experiment would not be confounded by the characteristics and syntax of a specific computer system. The challenge was to design an experiment without a computer but with enough realism to make results meaningful.

The specific DSS setting used was the building of a company-wide financial planning, budgeting, and expense tracking system. The research environment was a set of implementation scenarios. Each subject was presented with a base scenario (Table 1) describing the general nature of the required DSS. Then each subject was given a set of treatment scenarios that provided the different experimental stimuli. Such scenarios have been widely used in both management practice (McHale and McHale, 1976; Janis and Mann, 1977; Klein and Linneman, 1981; Willis, 1987) and research (Manley, 1971; Tydeman and Mitchell, 1977; Cosier, 1983; Monyihan, 1987). In both contexts, they present a “picture” of a possible state of affairs to which the respondents –manager or research subject –can respond. The basic requirement is that the situation described in the scenario can be intelligently envisioned by the respondent. Because our research subjects were experienced business people, the scenarios are ones to which the subjects could easily relate to.

## 3.2. Research hypotheses, treatments, and task

The research study examined several key factors. Four research hypotheses were examined for each stage of the change model.

(1) The likelihood of success of a stage increases as the time allowed for completion of the stage increases (up to a reasonable limit).

(2) The probability of success of a stage increases as the level of project champion support increases.

(3) The likelihood of success of a stage increases as the level of change reversibility increases.

(4) The probability of success of a stage increases as the level of success of the previous stage increases.

Table 2 contains descriptions of the two treatment levels – one relatively unfavourable to change and the other favourable to change. Care was taken to develop the descriptions so that no single level would be seen to guarantee either success or failure. Each research subject was presented with a series of scenarios for a single stage (unfreezing, moving, or refreezing) in the change process. Table 3 contains two examples of such scenarios. At the end of each scenario, each subject was asked the following question:

“What do you think is the percent chance of success of unfreezing (moving/refreezing) for the proposed Budget-Aid project? (Place an “X” on the scale below).”

In addition to collecting the standard demographic information, the pre-experimental session with the subjects explained clearly the three stages of the change process and also the four experimental factors.

Increasingly, attention has been directed at measurement issues in MIS/DSS research (Jarvenpaa et al., 1984; Straub, 1989). Because of the single-question nature of the measurement instrument, no formal psychometric properties of the scale could be studied. The comments of the subjects in the post-experimental debriefing, however, revealed that the implementation scenarios and the research task were understood and thoughtfully evaluated. For example, subjects who experienced different sides of the “time allowed for unfreezing” factor commented as follows:

“Management is backing unfreezing with a longer grace period of twelve months.” (Favourable treatment)

“Unfreezing is the most critical part of the change process –it should not be short-changed.” (Unfavourable treatment)

One additional comment about the research task is necessary. The question asks the subjects to predict the chances of success of the current stage of change for the proposed project. Their prediction of success is what is critical. Managers make their diagnosis on the perceived fit of the proposed project. If they do not think this is good, they are not likely to commit their time and energies to the project.

## 3.3. Research subjects

The research subjects were students in the evening MBA program of the Curtis L. Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. Their voluntary participation was solicited based upon a letter of introduction from the Director of the MBA program. The subjects had substantial experience relevant to the treatment stimuli. For the sample of 119 subjects:

(1) 42% were older than 30; 40% between 26 and 30; and only 18% between 21 and 25.

(2) 18% had upper or middle manager positions: 42% had first-line operating management positions; and remaining 40% held staff positions.

(3) 36% described themselves as very comfortable with computers; 45% as comfortable with computers; and only 19% as uncomfortable with computers.

## 3.4. Experimental design

The subjects were randomly assigned to one of three basic experimental settings –unfreezing, moving, and refreezing. The pre-experimental data were collected in the evening MBA classes; the evaluations of the scenarios were completed outside class and mailed back to the researchers. Within each experimental setting, each subject was randomly assigned six experimental scenarios. Subjects were allowed to compare their scenarios as they developed their evaluations.

The above design decisions were based on pretest results. In the pre-test response to the research questionnaire containing all sixteen possible (four factors each at two levels) scenarios, it took a respondent, on average, one hour and fifteen minutes to complete. Subjects' concentration was waning towards the end of these pre-test sessions. This problem, coupled with the need to make the time commitment acceptable to potential subjects, led to the decision to ask each subject in the actual experiment to evaluate only six scenarios. Completion of this final (out of class) research task took approximately 30 minutes for each subject.

In addition to the time constraints, the decision to assign each subject to a single experiment (unfreezing, moving, or refreezing) was made to reduce the sampling error in the primary focus area –the study of the experimental factor effects within each “stage of the change” model. Further, by holding the “stage of change” constant, researchers were able to avoid a potential problem in the results: a subject faced with scenarios across the three stages of change might subconsciously assign higher predicted levels of success to the later stages. The design allows stage-to-stage comparisons.

For each experiment the response rate was greater than 50% –44 responses for unfreezing, 38 for moving, and 37 for refreezing. The demographic profiles of the subjects were similar for each experiment.

The experimental design for each experiment is a Balanced Incomplete Block Repeated Measures design. The total number of treatments for the unfreezing experiment was 264 (44 × 6); for the moving experiment, 228; and for the refreezing experiment, 222. The appropriateness of this design is found in the following quotation from Cochran and Cox (1957):

Individuals are asked to make a comparative rating of different objects that are presented to them. ...In most of these experiments, the individual finds it increasingly difficult to reach a satisfactory rating as the number of objects presented to him become larger... One solution to this difficulty is to arrange the objects in a balanced incomplete block design.

Analysis of variance was used to analyze the results for each experiment. Table 4–6 show the results of the experiments.

The data confirm three of the four research hypotheses. Favourable conditions with respect to time allowed and project champion support resulted in large statistically significant increases in the predicted chance of success $-+13.6\%$ and $+19.6\%$ respectively –in the unfreezing process. Favourable conditions with respect to the status of previous stage of change produced a smaller $(+3.9\%)$ , but statistically significant increase in the average predicted chance of success.

## 4.3. Refreezing

The results here are similar to those in the other two stages of change. The data supported the hypotheses with respect to time allowed, project champion support, and status of prior stage of change. Favourable conditions produced large, statistically significant increases in the average predicted chance of success -13.1%, 15.7%, and 16.6% respectively. Similar to the pattern identified in the “moving” experiment, the effect of change reversibility was statistically significant –but the direction of effect was opposite of that predicted from theory. With the increase in change reversibility, the predicted chance of success decreased by 14.2% on average. In contrast to the first two experiments, however, there was a statistically significant (at 0.01 level) two-way interaction between the level of project champion support and change reversibility. For both levels of change reversibility, the presence of favourable project champion support resulted in a higher average predicted chance of success (Table 5). When there was a low level of project champion support, however, the average predicted chance of success dropped most dramatically –from 68.7% for low change reversibility to 48.5% for high change reversibility. In essence, the inability to reverse the proposed change easily seemed to compensate for the lack of visible project champion.

## 4.4. Comparison among the three stages

There are several interesting patterns across the three stages of change.

(1) The importance of favourable conditions are not limited to a single stage of change. Rather they produce improvement at each stage.

(2) For time allowed, the magnitude of the effect (+13.6%, +14.0%, and +13.1%) is essentially the same for all stages.

(3) The effect of project champion support is largest and similar for unfreezing and moving (+19.6% and +18.9% respectively). The effect drops slightly to +15.7% for the refreezing stage.

(4) The effect of the status of the previous stage is largest and similar for moving and refreezing (+16.5% and +16.6% respectively). The effect for the unfreezing stage, while significant, was much smaller at +3.9%.

One last pattern is highlighted in Table 6. There appears to be a process momentum associated with the change process itself. Given a favourable condition in the previous stage of change, the average predicted chance of success increases from 56.7% in unfreezing to 67.0% in moving and ends at 73.4% for refreezing. This pattern is in sharp contrast to the pattern associated with unfavourable results in the preceding stage –average predicted chance of success is 52.8% in unfreezing, 50.0% in moving, and 56.8% in refreezing. Remembering that different subjects were used in each stage in the change model, this result is real. It is not due to the comparative evaluations of the experimental treatments subconsciously forcing the subjects to produce this pattern. Furthermore, it must be emphasized that the subjects were not at all affected by any biases or beliefs of the researchers, since these MBA students were not taught by the researchers. Secondly, the subjects were mature enough, as demonstrated by their profile, not to be swayed by any implicit biases in the experiments.

## 5. Implications

With respect to the specific experimental findings, there are several implications for DSS practitioners. First, the evidence is very clear that success in the early stages of change process –particularly in “unfreezing” –will play large dividends later. Second, sufficient time should be budgeted in all stages of the change process. The pressure for “quick results” should be carefully weighed against the potential long-run gain that arises from allowing the people to come to grips with the system at a more measured pace. Finally, the role of the project champion cannot be understated. In all stages this experimental factor produced the largest gains in the predictions that the system will succeed.

The findings present several implications. First, the results demonstrate that experimental research adds to understanding the process. It will not, however, replace field work, but in conjunction with field work, experimental studies can add refinement to our knowledge. Second, the size and significance of the effects of “success of the previous stage,” “the time allowed,” and “project champion support” suggest a need for further study of these effects: a micro-level analysis of their internal workings. Finally, the unexpected results in “change reversibility” are disconcerting. We need to be cautious in including this feature in our implementation theory. However, we may find that it is still important.

Our model highlights the roles of the two key actors (manager and analyst) in the process. It emphasizes the interactions and potential discrepancies between the “truth” of a proposed system and the “perceptions” concerning a proposed system. In this interplay between “truth and perceptions” the implementation plan plays an important role. It is one source of a manager’s perceptions, and thus a source of his/her support of or resistance to a proposed system. The manager does not just look at the technical features of the proposed system. Rather, all of the aspects of the implementation plan –the system features, the design team, the development process, and the organizational context –influence the manager’s behaviour. Because of this, the manager and the analyst must share the responsibility for developing and guiding the implementation plan.
