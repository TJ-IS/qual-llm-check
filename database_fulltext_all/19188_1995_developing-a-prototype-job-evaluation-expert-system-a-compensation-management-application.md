# Developing a prototype job evaluation expert system: A compensation management application

Mo Adam Mahmood ${}^{a,*}$ , Mary A. Gowan ${}^{b}$ , Shwu-Ping Wang ${}^{c}$

$^{a}$ Information and Decision Sciences, University of Texas, El Paso, El Paso, TX 79968, USA $^{b}$ Marketing and Management, University of Texas, El Paso, El Paso, TX 79968, USA $^{c}$ New Mexico State University, Las Cruces, NM, USA

## Abstract

Compensation is a complex issue with broad ramifications for both employer and employee. The pivotal point in the establishment of a compensation system is job evaluation. An expert system, a rapidly emerging decision making technology, is applied to the job evaluation area. Various approaches to job evaluation are discussed. Recently published computer-assisted methods for job evaluation are reviewed. The complete process of designing and developing a Job Evaluation Expert System (JEES) and its limitations are discussed. Benefits offered by JEES are identified and future research opportunities are highlighted.

Keywords: Compensation management; Job evaluation; Expert systems

## 1. Introduction

Effective management of an organization's human assets is increasingly recognized as critical to organizational success. One important and complex issue in managing human assets is the design and administration of the organization's compensation system. In establishing compensation programs, organizations must be concerned with achieving three goals: (1) structuring the compensation system to aid the firm in gaining a strategic advantage over competitors [9,27], (2) ensuring that employees perceive the compensation system to be fair [28,31], and (3) designing a valid system that ensures governmental legislation and regulations are met [21].

Proper compensation levels help by attracting qualified employees, retaining high performance employees, encouraging employees to develop additional skills, and motivating employees to increase performance. For instance, several studies have reported the importance of compensation to information technology professionals. Inadequate compensation was found to be a possible reason for high turnover among information technology (IT) professionals in Singapore [32]. A significant and positive correlation was found between compensation and career satisfaction among IT employees in South Africa [16]. Additionally, in Hong Kong, IT professionals rated compensation as the second highest motivating factor [4].

For employees, the process used for determining compensation provides feedback on their value to the organization [6]. Compensation also largely determines standard of living, social status, and sense of worth and achievement. For employers, compensation is a major cost, as high as 40% of the operating costs for manufacturing firms and 70% of the operating costs for service firms [17].

The pivotal point in the establishment of a compensation system is job evaluation. This process is concerned with determining the relative worth of the jobs within the organization. The purpose of this research is to present a prototype model for the job evaluation phase of structuring compensation. This prototype differs from other modelling approaches principally because it uses expert system technology that has not been explored in the job evaluation area.

## 2. Background

Job evaluation is used by organizations to develop and to maintain their pay structures through a process of comparing similarities and differences of jobs on the basis of job content and value to the organization. The information used to conduct the job evaluation typically results from job analysis. Both external and internal market forces affect this evaluation process. Management must make decisions as to which jobs to evaluate and how often to conduct the evaluations. Additionally, organizations must decide which of several job evaluation approaches they will use.

## 2.1. Methods of job evaluation

The four most commonly used methods of job evaluation are: ranking, classification, factor comparison, and point method. The point method of job evaluation is the one most commonly used by organizations [26], as such, it will be the focus of our discussion and the method used in the prototype.

## 2.2. Point method of job evaluation

This type of evaluation divides jobs into compensable factors that are believed to be critical to, and valued by, the organization. Jobs are broken down into their component parts, each component is reviewed, and jobs are compared on the basis of these components. This quantitative system has generally been accepted by federal agencies and the courts as a valid means of job evaluation [25].

The steps involved are:

(1) select and clearly define the compensable factors –the fundamental elements of the job, the factors that make some jobs more valuable than others to the organization, and the factors that are common to all jobs in the organization;

(2) divide the factors into degrees that express the extent to which the factor exists within a particular job, and then define the degrees;

(3) assign points to the degree as a means of giving weight to each factor within the point system (more important factors will have higher weights);

(4) rate jobs on each factor, determining the degree that best defines the level required for the job (usually done by a job evaluation committee);

(5) assign total points for each job and divide the jobs into different functional areas, if appropriate, to develop separate pay systems for each area; and

(6) identify benchmark jobs within the organization to determine if the point totals appear to be in line.

Later, these benchmark jobs can be used for comparison with jobs in other organizations to ensure that external equity exists. Advantages of the system include the detail and specificity with which jobs are evaluated and the fact that jobs are evaluated on a factor-by-factor basis.

Job evaluation is traditionally accomplished by an experienced evaluator using a “mental algorithm” to which he or she subjects the information obtained through job analysis [22]. The evaluator uses facts and past practices to give a rating to each factor represented in the job. The result is a job score. Employees generally accept the results of such a method because its sophisticated methodology and mathematical nature appear objective. They can understand how jobs are evaluated and how the total points are derived. Also, under this system, reevaluation of jobs that change can be done relatively easily.

One of the biggest disadvantages of this method is the time and expense required to develop the system. Further, a great deal of interaction is required between the person(s) performing the job analysis and the person or group conducting the job evaluation. In large corporations, where many jobs are being evaluated, thousands of decisions have to be made relative to degrees and factors. Additionally, since job evaluation is primarily a subjective judgment process, charges of bias may be made against the individuals conducting the job evaluation when employees do not perceive the process to be fair [29].

This concern for evaluator bias has broad implications for both internal organizational equity and legal defense if a company is charged with bias in compensation practices. Several studies have investigated this issue of information processing and examined its reliability and validity. For instance, one study found that the job title set an expectation of the worth of the job being evaluated $[30]$ . In another study, the placement of information in job descriptions was more important than the material itself $[29]$ . Problems of this nature can lead to the organization paying a premium for jobs which contribute relatively less to the organization but that are perceived to contribute a great deal. When this occurs, employees view the organization as unfair and the pay policies become less defensible in court.

Point factor systems were developed to address the need for greater reliability and validity in making job comparisons. The system, however, requires a series of judgments that need to be tested for reliability. Using computer-generated methods of job evaluation would improve reliability of results $[25]$ .

## 2.3. Computer-assisted job evaluation

Job evaluation has come a long way. Today, computer-aided job evaluation is the state of the art. The advantages of computer-aided job evaluation (CAJE) include simplification of job analysis, ability to keep job descriptions up-to-date, increased objectivity of the evaluation, less time spent in committee meetings, improved credibility of the salary management system, and greater ease in system maintenance [24]. An additional advantage of the use of a computerized system is the increased reliability that can be obtained.

CAJE features computerized checking of questionnaire answers and automated output of both job evaluations and relevant compensation reports. CAJE is not, however, an expert system. It makes no decisions; it records, processes, and calculates data relevant to job evaluation. CAJE simplifies and streamlines the routine work in job evaluation. However, while computerized job evaluation systems allow us to capture and use information about jobs in new ways, they are only as good as their designers.

Both a paucity of expert systems in the personnel management area and a substantial demand for expert systems for evaluating employees have been noted $[18]$ . There are three main reasons why expert system technology should be used for a computerized job evaluation system $[8]$ :

(1) decisions of this kind require expert input and compensation expertise is scarce [15];

(2) a high degree of uncertainty is present in job evaluation since job tasks, compensation policies, laws and regulations are constantly changing; and

(3) conventional programming techniques can not adequately handle job evaluation because of programming difficulty and development costs.

## 3. The job evaluation expert system

The job evaluation expert system (JEES) is a prototype system designed to compare similarities and differences in information systems (IS) jobs using the point method of job evaluation. JEES prompts the user to provide information on different factors that pertain to an IS job and on different levels within each factor.

## 3.1. Overview of JEES

JEES evaluates IS jobs using nine job factors, levels within each factor, and point values appropriate for each level [14]. The jobs include:

(1) assistant vice president of information systems,

(2) information systems manager,

(3) system analyst (senior, III, II, and I),

(4) computer programmer (senior, A, B),

(5) data processing manager,

(6) computer room supervisor,

(7) computer operator, and

(8) data entry operator (A, B).

The nine job-related factors are:

(1) Knowledge and skills required by the job -including how the knowledge and skills are used in performing the work.

(2) Supervisory controls -extent and composition of direct or indirect controls, the employee's responsibility, and the review of completed work. Subfactors include how the work is assigned, how the employee's responsibility is determined for carrying it out, and how the work is reviewed.

(3) Guidelines – their nature and the judgment needed to apply them. Subfactors include their impact on performing the work and judgment needed to apply them or develop new ones.

(4) Complexity -type, number, variety, and intricacy of tasks, steps, processes, or methods used. Sub-factors include details of the assignment, the difficulty in identifying what needs to be done, and the difficulty and originality involved in performing work.

(5) Scope and effect -relationship between the type of work and effect of error on work products or services, including the purpose and impact of the work product or service on the organization.

(6) Personal contacts – what is required to make the initial contact, the difficulty of communicating, and the setting in which the contact takes place.

(7) Purpose of contacts – to describe the reason, ranging from factual exchanges of information to situations involving negotiation of controversial objectives.

(8) Physical demands - covers the requirements and demands placed on the employee by the work assignment.

(9) Work environment – considers the risks and discomforts in the employee's physical surroundings, the type of work, and safety regulations.

## 4.2. User interface

The JEES user interface is designed using the VP-EXPERT expert system software. This provides a friendly environment through the use of a combination of question-answer and menu mode. In addition, the system provides an interactive explanation if the user does not understand the questions during consultation.

The screen layout consists of two horizontal and two vertical windows. The top horizontal window shows the question being asked. This window is followed by a menu of possible answers. The user moves the cursor to the appropriate answer and depresses the Enter key. When asked by the user, the bottom horizontal window provides further explanation of the advice or recommendation from JEES. The rule being used in arriving at an intermediate decision is shown in the left vertical window. The right window shows the intermediate decision being reached. Fig. 2 shows a sample screen display.

The user interface generates a final report showing different factors (e.g., knowledge required by the job, supervisory control) for evaluating a particular job, skill levels, and points assigned. The point values for each factor, total point values for the job, the job itself, job grades, point ranges, job surveys, and the percentage of the job point values to market surveys are also provided. If the job point totals fall within the same range as market point range and grade, the job is considered substantially equal to the market survey for pay purposes. The percentage value shows the job salary as a percentage of the survey pay range. Job salary is compared with the first quartile, medium, third quartile, or average salary of the survey data (based on the latest available information). A sample JEES session is shown in Appendix B.

## 4.3. Inference engine

JEES employs a backward-chaining inference approach. The system is, therefore, given specific goals. From this, the inference engine searches the knowledge base for a rule that has that goal as its conclusion. Once the engine finds a rule, it sequentially searches through the knowledge base to determine if it can satisfy the premise of that rule. If this is not possible, JEES looks for another rule with that goal as its conclusion. The process continues until either the premise is satisfied or the engine runs out of rules. For example, suppose that the chosen goal is to find the job requirement for Level 2 in Factor 1 (knowledge requirement) that has a point value of 200. Rule #110 in the JEES knowledge base has this goal in its conclusions:

RULE 110

IF program = No AND ski\_mgt = No

AND routine = Yes AND equip = A

THEN level1 = 1-2

value1 = 200;

The premise of this rule is that the job does not require programming and management skill since it is routine and a keyboard control data entry device is used. This premise is either satisfied or not satisfied, depending on the responses obtained from the user.

In arriving at an advice or a conclusion, the inference engine keeps track of the logical chain created by successive use of rules from the knowledge base. When asked by the user, this information allows the inference engine to display specific reasoning associated with the advice or a conclusion.

## 5. JEES development process

The following were the steps in developing the JEES prototype. These include problem identification, hardware and software selection, knowledge base design, and knowledge base validation.

## 5.1. Problem identification

The first critical decisions were selecting the domain, an appropriate application within that domain, and a task $[2,3]$ . This was followed by selection of a domain expert and a knowledge engineer.

## Selecting the domain

An expert system domain must be precise, well organized, and well understood $[7]$ . The selected application within the domain will require expert knowledge $[5]$ ; however, the task selected within the application should not be too easy (a rule of thumb is that if a task requires only a few minutes then it is too easy) $[20]$ . At the same time, the task should be of significant importance to the organization $[19]$ .

The job evaluation phase of compensation requires knowledge and expertise. The problem is aggravated by the fact that compensation experts are scarce, and job tasks, compensation policies, laws, and regulations are constantly changing. The task of comparing similarities and differences of jobs on the basis of job content and value to the organization is not easy.

## Selecting a domain expert and a knowledge engineer

A knowledge engineer should be able to work closely with the domain expert [10,11] and should have an educational background in artificial intelligence, computer/information science, linguistics, philosophy, or psychology [33].

The knowledge engineer of the JEES had background in information science with several years of experience. She was also familiar with the job content of information systems jobs and had spent time learning the point method of job evaluation.

The domain expertise was derived from a number of sources: a human expert with an extensive background in personnel administration (over 20 years of experience) who presided over the entire knowledge gathering process and workbooks [See [13,14]] for background material. The knowledge engineer also provided her experience in describing the job content.

## 5.2. Hardware and software selection

The JEES was developed on a personal computer (PC) with 640 kilobytes of memory and color monitor. The disk storage requirement is under 20 megabytes. Generally, a rule-based system consisting of two hundred rules can be easily developed on a PC [34].

There are usually two choices in building an expert system. One is to develop components such as the knowledge representation scheme, the inference engine, and the user interface from scratch using conventional or artificial intelligence (AI) programming languages. The other is to use a commercial expert system “shell”. The advantage in using a shell is that it allows knowledge researchers to focus on problem solving rather than programming. JEES was developed using VP-EXPERT. By using a shell, the expert system can be built quickly and easily. VP-EXPERT contains a friendly builder interface that allows interactive entry of rules in quasi-English with key words. It can read from or write to dBASE II, Lotus 1-2-3, VP-PLANNER, and PC files. Therefore, JEES has no problem in communicating with other systems.

## 5.3. Knowledge base design

The knowledge base is the core of any expert system. The knowledge engineer must perform a detailed analysis of the inference process and develop the prototype knowledge base. The tasks involved in developing the JEES knowledge base include knowledge acquisition, knowledge representation, knowledge system design, knowledge programming, and knowledge refinement.

## Knowledge acquisition

The objective of knowledge acquisition is to obtain rules, facts, and data from the expert or from other published sources. The process of knowledge acquisition is very time-consuming and inaccurate $[12]$ . The process involves encoding the expert knowledge from the expert articulation to represented rules, facts, and data. The JEES knowledge was derived from the expert, published sources, and personal experiences of the authors. Alexander and Evans $[1]$ suggest the use of multiple experts when expertise is not clearly defined.

The JEES expert is the manager of a data base system; he was an MIS manager for several years and has worked in the information systems area for over 20 years. Because the raw expert knowledge was different from that of rule base knowledge, the knowledge engineer had to represent it in the form of specific concepts and rules. These concepts and rules formed the lexicon and contents of the knowledge base.

## Knowledge representation

Representing and encoding the facts and relationships that constitute knowledge was the next step. There are many useful ways of representing knowledge. These include frames, semantic networks, causal networks, rules, logic expressions, and scripts. “If-Then” rules are, however, widely used because they are easy to use and enhance the explanation facility. VP-EXPERT employs “RULE”-based knowledge representation.

## Knowledge programming

To organize the unstructured base of knowledge, the knowledge engineer must first design an overall framework and systematic representation scheme. There are eleven components of knowledge in the knowledge base. The knowledge engineer assembles the individual components into an organized rule base for the inference engine to interpret and use. The process involves actually entering facts, rules, objects, and relationships (Figs. 3 and 4: for brevity, detailed diagram of Factor 1 only is shown).

The knowledge programming of JEES is developed by the VP-EXPERT rule-based tool. JEES contains definitions of knowledge levels, relationships between levels, and rules that represent these relationships. The following is an example:

Needs programming skill = (yes, no);

Needs administration skill = (yes, no);

Needs system analysis and design skill = (yes, no);

Requires years of experience = (0, 1, 3, 5, 7);

Moderate or major project leader = (moderate, major).

The relationships between levels are expressed as follows: If the job requires programming, systems analysis, systems design skills, 3 years of working experience, and the employee working as a moderate-size project leader, then the skill level is 6 under Factor 1 (knowledge required by the job), the total point value is 950, and the job is system analyst II.

## Knowledge refinement

The knowledge base needs to be revised to reveal errors and omissions and to update the knowledge base as the knowledge is changed. The JEES knowledge base was improved by adding explanations for answering questions, displaying a more user friendly system, and providing a ratio of the total point values to the survey total point values.

## 5.4. Knowledge base validation

Validation is a necessary step to ensure the accuracy of the knowledge base. There must be cross-checking of the results against the advice provided by the expert [23].

While converting knowledge into rules, the expert was provided with intermediate results. He reviewed these to make sure that they reflected correctly and accurately his knowledge or that of other sources. He was also shown the data gathering protocol and some final recommendations; he then reviewed them and made sure they concurred with his and or the other sources' recommendations. In addition, he recommended some content modifications. The expert concluded the system was useful, valid, and user friendly.

## 6. Limitations

As with any research, this work also has a number of limitations. First, the JEES knowledge base only handles the point method of job evaluation. This is, however, one of the most widely used evaluation methods. Although job content varies from one company to another, we believe that one advantage of the JEES knowledge base is that it can be modified with relative ease to conform to various company human resource environments. Additionally, it could be modified to accommodate other quantitative methods of job evaluation such as factor comparison.

Second, no real-world validity test of the prototype was performed. Our intent was to show the feasibility of applying the expert system technology to the employee compensation area in general, and the job evaluation area in particular. We believe we have been successful in doing so.

## 7. Potential benefits

Effective compensation management decisions rely upon accurate, comprehensive, and up-to-date job information. Job evaluation aids in this process and is a systematic means of assuring internally and externally equitable compensation. Further, a well-constructed job evaluation expert system can enhance employees' perceptions that the organization is paying them fairly since evaluation of their jobs is automated and, therefore, consistent.

Use of an expert system for job evaluation also provides a means for the organization to evaluate changes in jobs in a reliable and valid manner. Job evaluation expert systems eliminate the need for the many human subjective decisions required by manual job evaluation systems and, thus, expedite the process while increasing reliability and validity.

Additionally, organizations are increasingly restructuring jobs in response to changing organizational goals. As these goals change, the compensable factors the organization wants to pay for also change. Use of an expert system expedites the ability of the organization to make the resulting necessary changes in compensation.

## 8. Conclusions and recommendations for future research

The purpose of this research was to discuss the design of a prototype job evaluation expert system. A review of compensation management (specifically job evaluation) and the development of a knowledge base was discussed. The development process of the system included problem identification, hardware and software selection, knowledge base design, and knowledge base validation.

JEES does not replace the job evaluator – rather it simplifies and streamlines the routine work, leaving time for humans to attend to the more important value added tasks. JEES provides a fair job point because the expert system creates an unbiased job evaluation system. The design process helps ensure that the evaluation is valid. JEES achieved a system of job evaluation that is consistent, accurate, and equitable.

Human resource personnel should keep in mind the following issues:

(1) Users can employ the job evaluation results to decide a pay scale or to change the pay rate or job content. The computer does not make the decision; it may, however, help the company explain the decision to employees. Moreover, although JEES ensures consistency, it cannot assess accuracy. For example, data entry errors or incorrect knowledge acquisitions and representation will lead to invalid job evaluations.

(2) JEES can falter and even fail if job content is not up-to-date. The knowledge engineer and domain expert need to modify the knowledge base as jobs change.

(3) JEES does not replace traditional evaluation systems. Analysis is still needed to provide the standards for evaluating benchmark jobs. JEES simply streamlines and speeds up the job evaluation process.

## 8.1. Future research directions

One of the first issues for future research is the validation of job evaluation expert systems. One type of validation needed is criterion-related validity to determine the relationship between the expert system outcome and the “true value” of the job [25].

Another issue is comparable worth. To address comparable worth, organizations must be able to show that jobs are evaluated in terms of their actual worth to the organization as opposed to their traditional worth. Logically, use of a knowledge base that can address comparable worth and a user interface addressing job tasks of value to the organization should help eliminate this issue.

Further, as organizations restructure in response to the rapidly changing global economy, organizations must also address how best to evaluate the changing value of jobs within the organization. At the current time, organizations are examining the best way to reward individuals for gaining new knowledge and skills consistent with the compens-

able factors of their jobs. A job evaluation expert system that would allow a quick incorporation of knowledge and skill acquisition pay into the point method of job evaluation should, therefore, be of interest to both future researchers and managers involved in making pay decisions.
