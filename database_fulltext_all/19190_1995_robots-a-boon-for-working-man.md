# Robots: A boon for working man?

Jos Benders \*

Department of Business Administration, University of Nijmegen, P.O. Box 9108, 6500 HK Nijmegen, The Netherlands

## Abstract

A theoretical model on the impact of technical systems on job design is formulated. This model is used in an empirical study on arc welding robots. It is shown that different types of jobs exist around these robots. In terms of job design, any technical system must be seen in the light of the conditions under which it is used. In the case of arc welding robots, output characteristics are presented as an important factor setting these conditions. The quality of working life may be affected positively or negatively after the introduction of industrial robots.

Keywords: Robots; Human factors; Technological impact; Job content; Technical systems

## 1. Introduction

Research on the generic theme of “technology and work” shows a distinct tension between the widely rejected notion of “technological determinism” on the one hand, and the impact of “technology”, which is often assumed to be large, on the other hand [8]. This paper is an attempt to throw more light on the importance of “technology”, or preferably “technical systems” [11], on work content and job design. First a theoretical model is formulated. Next, this model is illustrated by means of a multiple-case study on the division of labor around arc welding robots (see [3] for an elaborate account). Finally, conclusions are drawn and discussed with a special focus on the practice of job design around robot work stations.

## 2. Theoretical basis

The steady introduction of new technical systems, called “mechanization” or “automation”, generally involves the replacement of human beings by machines. However, at the same time machines take over tasks from humans, machine-related tasks to be performed by humans are often introduced. Hence, the implementation of a new technical system leads to changes in the organizational task pool, which is defined as “the totality of tasks that have to be carried out by human beings in an organization”. Whereas these changes are often incremental at the organizational level, they may be substantial at the workplace level.

For job design, it is more important to consider the question how the tasks that make up the organizational task pool are combined into jobs. Any organizational task pool, whether affected by the implementation of a new technical system or not, is spread over a number of jobs via design rules. These design rules can be seen as the manifestation of a specific organizing logic, which is called here the “design orientation”. This design orientation is adopted and implemented consciously or unconsciously by organizational decision-makers, and can be either “fractionized” (often called “Tayloristic”) or “integrative”. This latter design orientation tries to combine tasks in such a way that meaningful jobs are created, preferably by combining conception and execution. The result is a low degree of division of labor. In contrast, according to the fractionized design orientation tasks have to be split over different jobs, and the resulting division of labor is thus high. Thus, the design orientation adopted affects the range of tasks that employees are able to perform, the number of hierarchical levels, and probably the attitudes and behavior of employees. It is important to stress here, that as depicted in Figure 1, technical systems only play a role in changing job contents through their effects on the organizational task pool. Herewith, technical systems have an indirect effect in job design.

An issue not yet discussed concerns the frequency with which tasks occur. As far as production-related tasks are concerned, these are closely related to so-called “output characteristics” [27]. In organization and industrial sociology widespread support is found for a coupling between output characteristics and organizational structure or the division of labor [5,28]. At a lower level of aggregation, namely the level of work stations, the output characteristics “product variability”, “product range”, “batch size”, and “product complexity” can be hypothesized to be related to jobs. The higher product variability, the larger the product range, the smaller the batch size, and the higher the product complexity, the broader the job content is expected to be.

Since approximately 1980, the “flexibility paradigm” [24] seems to have become dominant, which means that the output characteristics product range, product variability, and product complexity have increased and batch sizes have decreased. If this is the case, this may have a positive impact on job content [25]. However, although such a trend has been signalled by many authors (see e.g. [29]), its empirical basis is at best flimsy, due to a lack of the necessary statistical and longitudinal data to demonstrate its existence.

Nevertheless, as job content can be expected to be related to output characteristics it is interesting to study a technical system around which a large variety of output characteristics is found. In this respect industrial robots are a suitable study object. An industrial robot is:

An automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multi-purpose, manipulative machine with several degrees of freedom, which may be either fixed in place or mobile for use in industrial application (ISO/TR 8373) [15].

Robots may be used for a variety of operations. Worldwide, the most popular applications of industrial robots include arc welding, spot welding, parts and tool handling, and assembly. In many countries arc welding is the most popular application. Furthermore, they are technically fitted to produce flexibly a large range of products, so that suppliers can to some extent produce customized components $[23]$ . The point here is, that with varying output characteristics robot operators' jobs may be expected to show a large variation as well.

When studying computer-aided manufacturing equipment in general, the following categories of machine-related (or “direct”) tasks occur $[1,17]$ : 1. writing programs to control the machine;

2. testing and optimizing programs;

3. operating.

If these tasks are combined into one job, it is termed “integrated”. If testing/optimizing and operating are part of the operator’s job, the term “partially integrated” will be used. If the operator’s job contains neither testing/optimizing nor programming, but merely operating tasks such as (un-)loading and monitoring, the function is “split” (indirect tasks are not considered here, but are included in [3]). Thus, the following categories of operators’ jobs occur:

I. Integrated (I): Programming, optimizing and operating.

II. Partially-integrated (PI) : Optimizing and operating.

III. Split (S) : Operating.

## 3.1. Output characteristics

The robot's “flexibility” is here regarded as “the ability to be reprogrammed in order to handle variations in the product range”. About half of all the robots were bought to produce one specific product or a small number of products. Four organizations still use their robots in this rigid way; there is no product variability, and no use is made of the robot's flexibility. Two of them even had the robot supplier deliver the programs, so that there was no programming experience internally. At Petra, an important argument in buying robots rather than automatic welding equipment was, that robots would allow them to make other products if needed in the long run. Thus in this case, "flexibility" was still an important argument, though it is latent at present. Field and Aerie tried to convert from a small to a large product range. This process generated substantial difficulties. Aerie's robot was underutilized for a substantial time. By undergoing a long trial-and-error process, however, Aerie ultimately managed to make a broad range of products. This requires substantial knowledge about making fixtures. Additional investment in computer memory and welding tables were made. Whereas Aerie and Field ultimately succeeded in converting to a broad product range, Plough, Furniture and Component intended to produce a large number of products from the start. Thus, arc welding robots are used in situations with strongly varying output characteristics. This has important consequences for job design. Roughly, two situations can be distinguished: stable versus dynamic output characteristics, as shown in Table 2. Note that product complexity is not included, as the nature of its influence is different from that of the other output characteristics.

Table 2 shows, that many different combinations occur. In case of stable output characteristics, programming never occurs so that there are no integrated jobs. Optimization scarcely (or never) appears, and operation forms the bulk of the work. Especially in case of stable output characteristics, robot operators' jobs are monotonous, unskilled and routine. In case of dynamic output characteristics, these effects are mitigated. Batches are generally smaller, and the product range is larger. Yet, for the reasons outlined above, integrated jobs are not the rule, but just a possibility.

The effect of product complexity has not yet been discussed. Complex products, consisting of many components, need more welds and at different locations than simple products. The number of components affects the product cycle time positively. However, the product cycle time is also influenced by the material to be welded and the cycle time of individual joints. Three categories of product welding times can be distinguished:

I. Less than 2 minutes (Yellow, Iron, Falcon);
II. Between 2 and 8 minutes (Plough, Furniture, Field, Petra, Component);

III. More than 8 minutes (Country, Aerie).

On average, product complexity is fairly low. Few products need more than five minutes of welding. But complexity has an important consequence for the operator: machine-paced work with short cycle times occurs. The effects of machine-paced work can be mitigated by designing fixtures that hold several products at the same time. This can reduce the monotony by having a mix of products. However, the cycle time must be longer than the set-up time to prevent machine-paced work.

## 4. Conclusions and discussion

As the empirical results show, different job designs around arc welding robots were found in practice. Theoretically, this proves that a particular technical system has only limited impact on job design. In general, research on the impact of technical systems on job design ought to distinguish sharply between changes in the organizational task pool on the one hand, and the influence of design orientations in assigning tasks to jobs on the other hand (see Figure 1). As a generic model, this is probably applicable for a wide range of technical systems. However, in addition to the effect of the technical system per se on the organizational task pool and the design orientation, the conditions under which the technical system is being used influence the outcome in terms of the job design, and hence the skills needed to perform a robot-related job (see [4] for the implications for the “skilling debate”). In the case of arc welding robots, output characteristics set these “conditions” to an important extent, but labor market conditions proved relevant, too. In general, these “conditions” are likely to vary for different technical systems. Thus, for a particular technical system researchers need to look for those factors which are most likely to influence job design in the new situation.

Another important conclusion is, that in most cases, unskilled work forms the major part of robot-related tasks. This contrasts sharply with the robot-friendly image created by suppliers and other promoters of industrial robots, who recommend the use of robots in order to eliminate monotonous and repetitive work, and to improve bad working conditions, such as work in dirty and hazardous environments. Two quotations in the “Industrial Robot” discussing robot statistics illustrate this:

How can these statements be understood in the light of the research findings presented above? Undeniable, job content and/or working conditions may change favorably after the introduction of industrial robots. For instance, monotonous handling and assembly work can also be performed by robots [6], so that inconvenient work is eliminated. For instance, around 1990 several

Japanese car manufacturers tried to eliminate so-called san-kei (dirty, dangerous and difficult) jobs by introducing industrial robots $[9,26]$ . However, negative consequences (in terms of the quality of working life) have frequently been recorded. These concern the creation of monotonous, repetitive, machine-paced, unskilled, and isolated work $[22]$ . The monotony is sometimes worsened due to increased machine pacing and more intense levels of working. Musculoskeletal complaints may occur $[14]$ . If only a few operating tasks are automated, the bulk of the work around stand-alone robots is likely to be simple $[31]$ . Thus, on the one hand robotized work situations may indeed lead to the improvements mentioned, but on the other hand new unfavorable circumstances are created (also see $[16]$ for a general overview). In the words of Landau and Brandenburg $[18]$ :

```txt
"The use of industrial robots does not necessarily lead to improved working situations. Both positive and negative effects occur. There are possibilities for improvement, but their realization depends on managerial goals and paradigms."
```

Herewith Landau and Brandenburg point to the critical role managers play in the formation of jobs. Ultimately and notwithstanding all possible forms of employee influence, managers make decisions concerning job content, and the quality of working life in general. Thus, if authorities want to prevent an unfavorable quality of working life for robot operators, their policies ought to address managers. The question arises then: why do managers approve of working conditions that have a negative impact on the quality of working life?

One possible answer is, that they are not aware of this. Especially in a high-tech field such as robotics, technical preoccupations often prevail at the expense of non-technical matters. Fleck [7] gives a stunning example, stating that in the early 1980s some companies bought industrial robots without even knowing how to put them to use. In the case of insufficient awareness, awareness campaigns combined with putting user-friendly information material at the disposal of managers, or perhaps preferably, robot-suppliers, may be an effective policy. In Germany [19–21,30] and the Netherlands [22] the development of such information material has been sponsored by the government, and the Geneva based International Labour Office has published a brochure about safety around industrial robots in general [13].

Another answer is, of course, that many managers are simply not interested in the quality of working life. Driven by short-term economic reasons, they are only interested in the “bottom-line” and consider any literature on quality of working life as humanistically-motivated, yet antithetical to business interests. One reviewer commented on an earlier draft:

```txt
"The author naively believes that industrial managers will regularly follow human factors and ethical rather than economic reasons for their choice of assignments for their employees."
```

The author does not. He does believe, however, that social and economic interests are not necessarily opposed, as this statement implies. Surely, the only way to influence these managers would be by proving that a better quality of working life pays off. Yet, although such attempts have been made $[10]$ and although there is some evidence that human resources management has a positive impact on organizational performance $[2]$ , there are so many methodological difficulties and pitfalls in this research, that its results are unlikely to convince sceptics. If key organizational decision-makers are not convinced of the importance of a considerate policy concerning the quality of working life, little can be expected from future programs.

Ignorance about the effects of introducing robots on the quality of working life can be resolved, and some managers may become convinced that a good quality of working life has positive economic effects as well. Yet, that seems to be the best that any policy can achieve. Problematic robotized work situations are likely to exist as long as there are robots.
