# An empirical examination of the impact of computer information systems on users

Randy Ryker $^{a,*}$ , Ravinder Nath $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Information Systems, Nicholls State University, Thibodaux, LA 70310, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management Information Systems and Decision Sciences, Fogelman College of Business and Economics, The University of Memphis, Memphis, TN 38152, USA

## Abstract

This research investigates the effects of computer-based information systems on users and their jobs. Overall, based upon data collected from 101 users, the results show that information systems have a positive effect on four of the five core job dimensions (identity, significance, autonomy, and feedback). No significant impact is detected for the “skill variety” dimension. Results are also reported when users are broken down according to their position in the management hierarchy, and their user type (primary, secondary, end-users).

Keywords: Job characteristics model; Core job dimensions; Computer-based information systems

## 1. Introduction

The computer and telecommunications technologies are essential to competing and running a business in today's markets. The introduction of inexpensive personal computers and the availability of ready-to-use software has accelerated the diffusion of computer-based information systems even in small organizations.

Whether these systems have had a positive or negative effect on the lives of working men and women has been a subject of debate $[1,2,19]$ . Also the empirical evidence on this issue is mixed. At one end of the spectrum, it is obvious that computerized systems rid workers of routine and mundane tasks $[21,28]$ . This frees them to focus on more meaningful decision-oriented tasks and consequently, leads to higher job satisfaction. Other examples of positive effects have been reported [7,12,16,24]. Nath [23], in a study of the impacts of local area networks (LANs) on users and their work, found that these devices made the users' jobs easier, improved their skill level, and made their job more challenging. Ghani and Al-Meer [14] examined the effect of computers on the job satisfaction of end users. They measured the job satisfaction of end users at one point in time using a sub-scale of the Job Descriptive Index developed by Smith et al. [27]. Their results indicate that there is a significant positive effect of using a personal computer on the job satisfaction of end users.

At the other end of the spectrum are those who report that computers have a negative impact on workers $[11,13,19]$ . They contend that such systems make the work place dehumanized, work becomes routine and monotonous, and decision making tends to be centralized.

There is some indication that employees may be affected differently by information technology depending on their position within an organization. Dearden [9], for example, has argued that the job of top managers, i.e., the president and top line executives, has not been affected significantly by the computer.

More recently, Pinsonneault and Kraemer [26] examined the impact of information technology on middle managers. Their review of the literature revealed that, in some cases, information technology is associated with an increase in the numbers of middle managers [15,25], while in other cases it is associated with a decrease [5,10]. To resolve this “empirical paradox,” they proposed a new framework in which the effects of information technology on middle management staffing are viewed as contingent upon the degree of centralization of decision making in the organization as a whole and in computing matters in particular. They suggest that when there is extensive centralization of decision making, the main roles of middle managers are structured and routine, and information technology will tend to be used by higher-level managers to reduce the size of the middle management workforce. On the other hand, when there is extensive decentralization, middle managers control computing, and their main roles are mostly unstructured and decisional in nature. In such a situation information technology will tend to be used by middle managers to increase the importance and number of middle managers. While the motivation of middle managers to increase their importance and numbers was not specified by the authors, it is assumed the goal is to improve the job security of middle managers.

Another perspective on this debate is presented by Bostrom and Heinen [4]. Their basic premise is that computer-related technology is essentially neutral; whether its effects are positive or negative depends on the decisions that are made and on how it is used. They cite examples computer systems that had a negative effect on the job satisfaction of supervisor and clerk jobs; while the effects appeared to be positive for middle managers [22,29].

The objectives of this research are to investigate the effects of computer-based information systems on workers and their jobs empirically using the Job Characteristics Model of Hackman and Oldham [17].

Also, the impact of these systems on their five core job dimensions is examined.

## 2. Job characteristic model

One of the more established theories of job motivation and satisfaction was developed by Hackman and Oldham; it is termed the Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) and it is used to measure the perceptions' of workers with regard to five core job dimensions:

Skill Variety - the degree to which a job requires a variety of different activities that involve the use of a number of different skills and talents.

Identity - the degree to which the job requires completion of a whole and identifiable piece of work; i.e., doing a job from beginning to end with a visible outcome.

Significance - the degree to which the job has a substantial impact on the lives or work of other people, whether in the immediate organization or in the external environment.

Autonomy - the degree to which the job provides substantial freedom, independence, and discretion to the individual in scheduling the work and in determining the procedures to be used in carrying it out.

Feedback - the degree to which carrying out the work activities required by the job results in the individual obtaining direct and clear information about the effectiveness of the performance.

The job characteristics model holds that various personal and work outcomes are a function of the core job dimensions according to the following empirical formula:

Motivating Potential Score(MPS)

$$
= (\mathrm{V} + \mathrm{I} + \mathrm{S}) / 3) * \mathrm{A} * \mathrm{F}\tag{1}
$$

where V, I, S, A, and F, represent scores of dimensions Skill Variety, Identity, Significance, Autonomy, and Feedback, respectively.

In general, the higher the MPS is for an individual worker, the more beneficial the personal and work outcomes, including job satisfaction. One moderator variable that should be mentioned is the growth-need strength of the worker. Employees with high growth-need strength can be characterized as having a strong need for personal accomplishment, for learning and developing themselves beyond where they are now, for being stimulated and challenged, and so on. Jobs with high MPS result in more beneficial outcomes for employees with high growth-need strength, than for those with lower growth-need strength. However, workers with weak growth-need strength also show an increase (although at a lower rate) in beneficial outcomes as their MPS increase [18].

The JDS has been used in MIS research to study the jobs of programmers and systems analysts $[8]$ . However, no one has reported using the JDS to measure the core job dimensions of computer system users before and after the computer systems are implemented.

## 3. Methodology

To gauge users' perceptions before and after computer-based systems were available, a survey instrument was used. It consisted of two main sections. Both sections contained Job Diagnostic Survey items. The first section's items solicited user responses by asking them to recall their perceptions of their job before the computer system was available. This approach has been referred to by Campbell and Stanley [6] as a retrospective pretest. It was necessary in this section to modify each item to reflect the past tense.

The second section's items were for the users' perceptions after the system's use. The same items from the Job Diagnostic Survey were used, but in this section were not changed for verb tense.

Data regarding the respondents' organizational level, type of user, and years of computer experience were also collected. In addition, users were asked to respond to the following statement using a 5-point Likert-type scale (extremely agree to extremely disagree): “Computer systems have changed your job a great deal.” The purpose of this item is to check the concurrent validity of the instrument. A copy of the survey instrument appears in the Appendix.

A pretest was conducted with a small group of computer users. The results indicated that the instructions and items were worded clearly. However, some users indicated that because their current job had always involved using a computer, they could not respond to the section asking them to recall their job before computers.

The instrument was administered to a convenient sample of several night MBA and executive MBA classes at an urban university in the Southwest U.S.A. All students in these classes worked full-time. In spite of the fact that this group does not constitute a random sample from the theoretical population of computer users, the sample is not significantly biased because: (1) each respondent held a full-time position while attending classes during the weekend or night, (2) the respondents represented various types of organizations and functional areas, and (3) each respondent had experience working with computers and those with no computer-related work experience were not included in the sample. This resulted in 101 usable questionnaires.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Sample profile

Table 1 displays the characteristics of the sample. Most of the respondents held jobs at the middle management (38%), or supervisory/staff level (39%). The majority identified themselves as either secondary users (46%) or end users (36%), while a few were primary users (19%), defined as those who make decisions based on information from a computer system, but who do not personally interact with the computer. Secondary users interact with the computer to obtain information but do not make decisions based on it: they are essentially intermediaries. End users both interact with the computer and make decisions based on the information they obtain from the computer. The respondent's years of computer experience ranged from one to twenty-one years with a mean of 6.9 and a median of 6 years.

For each dimension, a paired t-test was run to determine if the mean after-score is significantly higher than the mean before-score. Table 3 summarizes the results of these t-tests. In four out of 5 cases, the null hypothesis was rejected. Therefore, the identity, significance, autonomy, and feedback dimensions of computer users' jobs were improved after computer systems became available to them. No significant difference was detected in the core dimension “skill variety”.

In order to assess the overall effect of computers, for each respondent, the before and after Motivating Potential Score (MPS) was calculated using the formula in Equation (1). A paired t-test was used to determine if there was a significant difference between the before-and-after MPS scores. The results (last entry in Table 3) indicate that the after-MPS scores were significantly higher than the before-MPS scores.

Once again, paired t-tests were run to examine the before and after scores for the five core dimensions and MPS by user type. As can be seen in Table 5, primary-users did not perceive a significant change in the MPS. Only the “significance” core dimension of their job was perceived to have changed after computer systems became available. Primary users do not interact with the computer systems, and tend to be senior managers.

The secondary users, intermediaries who interact with the computer systems to obtain information for decision makers, showed an improvement in MPS. This was the result of a significant improvement in the “identity” core dimension of their jobs.

The end users, those who interact with computer systems and use the output to make decisions, perceived a significant improvement in four of the five core dimensions and MPS. This finding is consistent with Ghani and Al-Meer [14] who found there was a significant positive effect of using a personal computer on the job satisfaction of end users.

The validity of the results of this study is supported by the fact that a well validated instrument, JDS, was utilized. In addition, an effort was made to measure the concurrent validity of the instrument as it is used in the present study. Concurrent validity is studied by comparing test or scale scores with one or more external variables known or believed to measure the attribute under study [20]. If there is concurrent validity, one would expect that respondents with the largest differences in their before and after MPS scores would extremely agree with the statement: "Computer systems have changed your job a great deal." Similarly, those with the smallest differences would tend to extremely disagree with it. To check the concurrent validity, the difference between the before and after MPS scores was calculated. The correlation between this difference and the response to the statement was significant (r = .22, p < .05).

## 5. Summary and discussion

This research empirically investigated the effects of computer-based information systems on workers and their jobs using the Job Characteristics Model. The sample was examined as a whole, by position of the job in the management hierarchy, and by user type. Two observations can be made. First, in all cases where there was a significant change in the core dimensions of the job, the changes were reported to be positive; i.e., the core dimensions improved after the computer systems became available. Secondly, the core job dimension labeled “skill variety” did not significantly change for the sample as a whole, nor for any of the sub-groups examined.

When the sample was broken into sub-groups, the results indicated that computer systems affect jobs differently depending on the position of the job in the management hierarchy, and on the type of user. In particular, senior managers and primary users reported no significant changes in their jobs; while the other levels of manager and types of users reported significant improvements in the core dimensions of their jobs.

Even though this research suggests that computers do not significantly change the job of top managers, this will change as executive information systems (EIS) enable more top managers to become end users [3]. An EIS will allow a senior manager to access historical data directly, and to make some analysis of trends and forecasts. To the extent that an EIS enables a senior manager to bypass intermediaries, it may improve the autonomy and feedback dimensions of the senior manager's job.

These findings carry implications for the management of organizations. Practitioners need to be aware that the systems they design and implement have an impact on workers and jobs that goes beyond the technical requirements of managing information. While the majority of respondents in this study reported that computers improved the core dimensions of their jobs, some indicated that there was either no impact, or a negative impact. Practitioners who develop systems for senior managers may want to consider how to design the systems so as to improve the core dimensions of that type of job. As the computer revolution accelerates, researchers and practitioners should continue to utilize valid and reliable tools to examine the impact of computer information systems on users and their jobs.
