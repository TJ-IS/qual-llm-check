# Determinates of EIS acceptance

Dale Young $^{a,*}$ , Hugh J. Watson $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Decision Sciences and Management Information Systems, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, USA $^{b}$ Department of Management, The University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

## Abstract

The large number of organizations developing executive information systems (EISs) highlights the importance of understanding why executives use these systems. This survey investigated how ease of use, the number of features, and support staff characteristics are related to EIS acceptance. Acceptance was measured by the percentage of the targeted users who incorporate the EIS into their daily routine. High usage was not associated with ease of use, a large number of features, or the staff being physically close to the users. However, rapid development time was positively correlated with acceptance. Higher numbers of available features were associated with larger support staffs and larger user groups. The number of users was positively correlated with both staff size and EIS age. Existing EISs place a stronger emphasis on reporting internal rather than external data.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Information system's features; Information systems support

## 1. Introduction

The present competitive environment forces firms to analyze both internal and external data in order to respond to change. Decision makers need information about internal activities for controlling operations; they need it from external sources for developing strategic plans.

To satisfy information needs, organizations ask the information systems (IS) department to provide the needed data. Understanding the business well enough to implement ISs that have a strategic impact is a relatively recent requirement.

IS departments, in partnership with users, are successfully creating systems that give strategic advantage $[5,6,26]$ . Many systems are used for cutting operating cost, reducing head count, establishing electronic links with customers and suppliers, and monitoring competitive thrusts.

Executive information systems (EISs) have been identified as having a high level of strategic impact in the 1990s. Consequently, EISs are a significant topic for research.

This study examined the determinates of EIS acceptance. Acceptance was measured as the percentage of the target audience of users who actually employ the system in their daily work. More specifically, the study focused on how EIS and support staff characteristics affect acceptance.

## 2. Definition of an EIS

An EIS is a computerized system that is developed for senior executives to help satisfy their information needs. EISs often spread over time to additional users throughout the firm. The EIS may be used at a corporate or divisional level and can focus on any specified business function [27].

## 3. A model of EIS acceptance

Technology-related and support-related factors are components of the research model (Fig. 1); user-related factors were not incorporated. The research model shows the expected relationship between EIS characteristics (technology-related factors) and support staff characteristics (support-related factors) and EIS acceptance, or usage.

## 3.1. EIS characteristics

The technology-related factors are ease of use and number of features available in the system. EISs are designed to be intuitive and so easy to use that minimal training is required $[2]$ . The list of characteristics and the features list are evidence of the importance of “feature richness” in an EIS.

Respondents rated the ease of use of their system. Easier to use systems are expected to have higher acceptance rates; conversely, complicated systems are likely to hinder usage.

The features variable assessed the number of capabilities (e.g., access to other applications, organizing tools) available in the EIS. For instance, executives can sometimes interact with the EIS using a touch-screen, a mouse, or a traditional keyboard; users navigate through the system by way of windows, icons, or menus. An EIS can facilitate personal productivity and communication through other capabilities such as electronic mail and conference room access to the system.

User acceptance may be affected by the number of features offered. Even if the EIS staff is well qualified and strategically positioned within the organization, a lack of features can hamper usage. Some common features include:

\* current status access to internal information;

\* data extraction from databases;

\* graphic, tabular, and textual data on one screen;

\* easy retrieval of data;

\* multiple user interfaces (e.g., mouse, touch screen);

\* consistent use of color and graphic formats;

\* electronic mail access from within the EIS; and

\* security for data, user sign-ons, and screen access. Other desirable features in some EISs are:

\* access to external databases (e.g., Dow Jones News Services);

\* graphical interfaces to other applications (e.g., e-mail);

\* integrated decision support (e.g., Lotus 1-2-3);

\* hard copy output;

\* combining data from internal and external sources;

\* linking screens for viewing in a user-determined order;

\* menu or keyword access to information in the EIS; and

\* on-line help screens which are context dependent.

Tools to facilitate personal organization and make daily activities more efficient are added to some EISs, and include: electronic rolodex, calendaring and filing, and voice input to annotate documents. EISs with more features were expected to attract more users.

## 3.2. EIS support staff characteristics

In addition to technology factors, the study looked at the EIS support staff and how they affect usage. Factors suggested in the literature include support staff location, and their reporting relationship and qualifications. Several studies conclude it is important for an IS support staff to be in close physical proximity to the users $[3,20,28]$ . The reporting relationship has been associated with the breadth of influence of the group, the level of top management support, and the staff's visibility [16]. Survey data indicate the importance of IS support staff qualifications, such as formal education and business and IS experience [13]. The ability of analysts to have time with the executives they support is also important in developing a system that meets the executives' information needs [32]. Unfortunately, the higher the rank of the executive, the less likely time will be available.

The EIS support staff characteristics studied were: \* the number of executives supported by each staff member;

\* the amount of face-to-face time staff members have with users and the frequency of these meetings;

\* the physical location of the staff relative to the users and an assessment of how the location affects the performance of EIS duties;

\* the level in the organization at which the staff members report; and

\* staff qualifications for EIS support, as measured by work experience, professional training, college education, and interpersonal skills.

Staff location can range from the extremes of being in another city to being in the same office suite. Staff members who perform technical jobs most often report to IS. Individuals who supply data to the EIS typically report to their respective functional areas. Staff members who function as analysts and interact with the users may report to either IS or to a functional area.

## 4. The study

This study focused on how selected system characteristics and the support staff affect EIS usage. Executive usage or acceptance was measured as the percentage of the target audience who use the system to carry out their job responsibilities.

Respondents reported the number of executives who use the EIS at least once a week. Other possible measures of EIS usage (e.g., the number of screens accessed, the amount of time users spend on the system) are difficult to compare, because of differences in response times, screen layouts, and the number of applications available.

## 4.1. Research questions

The research questions explored in this study were:

\* What is the relationship between EIS characteristics and executive acceptance?

\* What is the relationship between EIS support staff characteristics and executive acceptance?

## 4.2. Research methodology

A field survey was used to collect data for testing the relationships in the research model. The questionnaire elicited information about the features of the organization's EIS, the reporting structure and qualifications of the EIS support staff, executive acceptance (usage) of the EIS, and various demographic data.

An initial list of issues was developed from a review of the EIS literature. This list was reviewed by EIS professionals who suggested several changes. Following modifications, the questionnaire was pretested in ten organizations that were selected to provide a sampling of different geographic locations, industries, and EIS software. Following this pretest, several changes were made and the questionnaire was tested a final time.

The list for the mass mailing was compiled from firms stated in published sources to have an EIS, lists of attendees at conferences in which EIS was a key topic, and previous surveys. The sample of firms was not random but it is believed to be representative of firms with an EIS.

A total of 223 questionnaires were sent to organizations; three weeks later, non-respondents were sent a follow-up survey. In total, 128 responses were received, for a response rate of 57.4%. (One firm was contacted twice because it was known to have two EISs. Two respondents were consultants and were excluded from the analysis.) Of the responses, 81 reported having an EIS (63.3%), 12 indicated an EIS was under development (9.4%); 33 reported no EIS (25.8%).

The organizations that reported having an EIS under development generally did not complete the questionnaire. A report of “No EIS” in a few cases indicated that an EIS had failed, but most often meant a lack of any EIS development at the time of the survey (several were still in the planning stages). The 81 questionnaires from firms with an EIS were used for the data analysis; in some cases the sample size is less because the respondents did not answer all the questions.

## 5. Findings

EISs are primarily associated with large organizations, but the data indicate that EISs are spreading to firms with less than \$1 billion in total assets. The number of such organizations reporting EISs was 2.5 times higher than in an EIS survey completed two years earlier [35]. Table 3 shows the asset size of the responding organizations. The respondents represent a variety of geographic regions (Table 4) and industries (Table 5).

The individuals completing the survey were primarily IS personnel. Their job titles are: executives, such as the V.P. of IS (3.7%); IS Manager (30.9%); EIS support staff (17.3%); IS staff (9.9%); functional-area staff (6.2%), and corporate managers or other IS and corporate staff positions (14.8%). The respondents average 16.8 years of work, 11.2 years of IS and 2.7 of EIS experience.

## 5.1. Ease of use and EIS features

The respondents rated the executives' perception of the ease of use of the EIS on a five-point scale. (The scale anchors, and the percentage of responses for each item, were: difficult, 0.0%; acceptable, 6.4%; moderately easy, 15.4%; easy, 56.4%, and extremely easy, 21.8%.) Because of the potential for bias, the measurement of ease of use from users is preferred. But the EIS staff who field questions and interact with users each day are believed to be well qualified to assess the user friendliness of the system.

EISs offer a large assortment of features to their users; the most frequently mentioned ones are shown in Table 6. On average, the respondents marked 9.5 items from a checklist of features. Access to decision support tools from within the EIS is available on slightly less than 40% of the EISs. Organizing tools, such as calendaring, appeared slightly less frequently, being included in 37%.

The percentages of organizations reporting the availability of specific features indicate that current EISs focus heavily on providing internal data (90.1%) and allowing drill-down to more detailed data (92.6%). These results are comparable with a 1991 survey [35] that reported frequent access to current status information (88%). Existing EISs place stronger emphasis on reporting internal data than on providing external data [22]. While almost all of the systems provide access to current internal operating information, only 60.5% include access to external databases. (Again, findings from [35] are similar; 57% of their respondents report access to external databases, 56% access to external news services.)

Analyzing the access to external databases feature, as a function of EIS age, reveals that external database access is available on 57.7% of the EISs in use for one year or less, 67% of the one to two year old EISs, and 81.3% of the two to three year old EISs. \* built with a commercial EIS package (69%);

\* developed in less than six months (5.8 months average);

\* supported by three staff members (3.1 average); and

\* used by twenty-four executives (23.6 executives average).

## 5.3. Staff-user interaction

Dividing EIS staff size by the number of users produces a staff-to-user ratio (Table 11). A ratio of less than one indicates more staff members than users, which can occur early in the life of an EIS when the system is not yet available to all users. More personalized support can be provided by higher staff-to-user ratios. The number of users supported by each staff member, four or fewer in most cases, suggests that EIS support is labor-intensive.

Considering the newness of the typical EIS in the survey and the small number of users, it is not surprising that a high percentage of the respondents report more than one hour per week in direct executive contact time with the staff (Table 12). Included in the group reporting 15 minutes or less per week are staffs that have no face-to-face time with users.

## 5.4. Data analysis

Correlation analysis was performed. Due to the size of the correlation matrix and the possibility of obtaining significant relationships by chance, a Bonferroni correction [24] was made to adjust the significance levels [36]. A family significance level of 0.10 was chosen because this was an exploratory study. The family significance level was adjusted by the size of the correlation matrix, $0.10 / (\# \text{ rows} * \# \text{ columns})$ , prior to the analysis of the correlation coefficients. Separate adjustments were made because each correlation matrix, composed of variables in the research model, had a different number of rows and columns. The result of the adjustment was a more conservative analysis in terms of identifying significant correlations.

The first research question examined the relationship between the EIS characteristics –ease of use and number of features –and acceptance of the EIS. Creating a correlation matrix with the two EIS characteristics and the usage variable results in a significance level of 0.05 after adjustment of the family confidence interval (i.e., 0.10/2). As shown in Table 14, neither EIS characteristic was significantly associated with acceptance. The highest (i.e., most favorable) p-value, which was 0.061, occurred between ease of use and usage. The negative signs in Table 14 reflect the way questions were worded rather than findings that are contrary to expectations.

## 5.5. EIS demographics

Three demographic variables are of concern because of possible interaction effects: EIS age, EIS development time, and the type of software used to develop the EIS (a commercial package or custom developed).

The age of the EIS is the length of time since the initial version of the EIS was rolled out to users. It was included to account for potential time dependence in variables, such as the number of features offered on the EIS. As might be expected, EIS age and number of users is correlated (r = 0.30, p = 0.008); the older the EIS, the greater the number of users. However, age is not correlated with the number of features offered.

There is no relationship between the size of the support staff and the age of the EIS; organizations are apparently allowing the number of executives per staff member to increase over time. Although staff size and number of users are related (r = 0.32, p = 0.006), a given size staff may be responsible for a considerably different number of users when comparisons are made across firms.

The demographic variable “development time,” the number of months required to develop the initial version of the EIS, is associated with acceptance (r = 0.26, p = 0.028); a positive correlation indicates higher levels of acceptance and shorter development times. The relationship between development time and the initial number of users is typically explained in the EIS literature by the need to deliver a tangible product quickly while executive interest is high. A long development period may exceed the allowable decision time in those situations in which an EIS is created to address a specific problem faced by the organization.

The use of commercial or custom software for EIS development may affect development time or the number of features offered. The percentage of EISs developed with commercial software (69.1%) was nearly the same as the percentage of EISs that were less than the average age of all systems in the study (64.1%), an indication that newer EISs are built with commercial software. More commercial (69%) than custom (52%) EISs were less than the average age of all systems in the survey, but a chi-square test showed no statistical significance to the difference. The average age of all commercial systems was 19.8 months; the average age of the custom EISs was 35.8 months. Fifty of the 81 respondents have systems less than two years old; 38 of these EISs were built with commercial software, only 12 are custom (i.e., there is a 3 to 1 ratio between commercial and custom for the newer EISs). The older EISs in the survey tend to be custom-built systems because until the mid to late 1980s commercial products were not generally known or available.

## 6. Implications and conclusions

Many EISs are used by all (29.8% reported such usage) or the majority (76.6% reported usage by 75% or more) of the group of executives who were initially targeted to be users. The support staffs are generally allowed time in front of the executives, as only 17.1% of the survey respondents indicate face-to-face times with the executives of fifteen minutes or less per week.

The fact that nearly half of the surveyed EISs have been in use for eighteen months or less shows their newness. It is possible to surmise some of the important factors that are contributing to the number of newly created EISs:

\* pressures, often brought on by competition, to improve corporate performance while simultaneously controlling the growth in the staff who support key executives;

\* widespread knowledge transfer about EISs from publications (e.g., trade journals, magazines, books) and conferences; and

\* easier to use, less expensive, more powerful technologies (e.g., personal computers and servers, windowed interfaces) for the presentation of information to users.

The majority of the respondents are from two industry groups: manufacturing (37.0%) and finance (23.5%). The finance category includes insurance, banking, and real estate. Domestic manufacturers are under intense pressure to control costs and improve product quality. Just-in-time production and electronic data interchange are currently very important cost control applications in manufacturing [1]. EISs are a valuable tool for executives faced with these pressures. EISs provide timely status information to support a variety of applications that are designed to reduce product costs and improve the firm's competitive position.

Problems in industries represented by the finance category have been widely reported. Banks are seeking to develop and introduce new services; these frequently require the help of technology to survive in an extremely competitive environment $[12,19]$ . Stock brokerage firms have been hit with layoffs and forced into mergers $[30]$ . Survival in such situations demands that key officers be aware of both internal and external (i.e., environmental) developments. EISs are meeting such information needs in many organizations today.

The majority of the recently developed EISs were built using commercial EIS software. The number of new offerings, lower-cost products available for PCs and LANs, widespread downsizing and decentralization of IS technology, the trend toward “buy” over “build,” and the need to deliver these systems rapidly are all pressures that may be influencing the decision to buy commercial EISs.

The data support the generally held beliefs that EISs should be easy to use, incorporate standards for good user interfaces, and allow quick access to large quantities of data by combining graphic, tabular, and textual information on a single screen.

Existing EISs focus on reporting current status information. They place stronger emphasis on reporting internal information than on accessing external information; however, access to external databases often becomes a part of an EIS as it matures.

Neither of the characteristics, ease of use or number of features, were significantly correlated with acceptance of the EIS. Offering a large number of features on the EIS does not guarantee usage by a high percentage of the targeted group of users. EISs that are difficult to use may fail but ease of use alone does not insure acceptance.

The demographic variable most highly correlated with acceptance was development time. It is reasonable to expect that if an EIS is to be used in the time immediately following its initial development, it should meet a legitimate business need. Not addressing such a need is a reason for failures. Thus, the EIS must be rolled out quickly while the need and executive interest is high.

Acceptance by the targeted group of executives was not correlated with any EIS or support staff characteristic. Executive acceptance was not significantly correlated with the number of EIS features, support staff size, staff-to-user contact hours, face-to-face time, staff location relative to the users, the staff-to-user ratio, or the training and interpersonal skills of the staff.

The lack of significant correlations indicate that factors other than the staff and the EIS are affecting usage. Further research is needed to identify factors most critical to acceptance; candidate factors include:

\* EIS development time and the presence of a business need;

\* previous computer experience of the users;

\* how actively the executive sponsor encourages EIS usage; and

\* the importance of technology in meeting organizational goals.

The findings support the idea that EISs are most successful when they are developed rapidly and are easy to use. Using prototyping methods $[10]$ and rapid screen building tools are ways for an EIS development group to meet these dual objectives. Because EISs change frequently (by the addition and deletion of screens, by offering new features or options, and by upgrades to the technology), the staff members who provide the on-going technical support of the system must be proficient in the use of these tools.

Developers and support personnel cannot control the changing information needs of users, but they can influence the infrastructure that makes possible a quick response to the executives. EIS developers must be aware of these most pressing issues (i.e., rapid delivery of the EIS, keeping up with changing information needs) if these systems are to be most successful and contribute to achieving business goals.
