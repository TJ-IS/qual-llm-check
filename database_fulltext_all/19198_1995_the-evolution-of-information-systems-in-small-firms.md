# The evolution of information systems in small firms

Paul B. Cragg $^{a,*}$ , Nancy Zinatelli $^{b}$

$^{a}$ University of Canterbury, Private Bag 4800, Christchurch, New Zealand $^{b}$ Lincoln University, New Zealand and Sierra Systems Consultants Inc, Vancouver, Canada

## Abstract

This paper presents the findings of a longitudinal study over an eight year period of information system sophistication and evolution in eighteen small firms. The focus is on changes that have occurred with respect to hardware, end user computing, and computer utilization. The paper identifies three areas of concern for small firm computing: inadequate hardware and software, lack of internal technical expertise, and insufficient attention by management to IS. Implications for managers and directions for future research are discussed.

Keywords: Small firms; Longitudinal study; IS sophistication; IS evolution; Inhibiting factors

## 1. Introduction

Since microcomputers became readily available in the early 1980's, more and more small firms have computerised, with accounting systems being the most common application area [1,18,24]. Despite the immaturity of the technology, some firms were very quick to acquire computer systems. Recently, newer technologies have been adopted by small firms, for example, end-user computing (EUC) [33,35,36], and electronic data interchange [2]. Small firms have started to use more diverse technologies in support of functions other than accounting [37]. Thus, information systems (IS) in small firms are becoming more sophisticated.

Researchers have developed instruments to help measure this concept of IS sophistication [20,42,37]. However, there has been little research into IS sophistication over time for small firms. One related study has identified many internal and external factors that have influenced IS growth in small firms over time [9].

## 2. Literature review

A number of models have been proposed on the evolution of IS in organisations, though none have been based on experiences in small firms. Saarinen [40] reviewed seven models; many were based on the assumption that computing moves through a series of growth stages. Saarinen identified other underlying principles within the models. Economic ideas have been important in influencing the demand for technology. Diffusion theory views evolution as a process where an innovation, for example, the use of IS in manufacturing, spreads through a country, industry, or firm. Organizational learning has often been depicted as a series of S-shaped curves, that can be applied to the adoption and spread of information technology.

Others have studied specific outcomes of IS evolution, often through the concept of its sophistication, which has many dimensions, including hands-on use [43], diffusion [14], types of applications [19,32], and hardware and software capability [20,32]. Raymond and Paré [37] define IS sophistication as a construct that refers to the nature, complexity, and interdependence of IS usage and management in an organization. Thus, both IS usage and management play important roles in determining IS sophistication.

Early studies of small firm computing showed IS sophistication to be low, with the typical firm having only one microcomputer $[6,13]$ . However, some firms had more powerful mini-computer systems, with a number of dumb terminals. Many of the early adopters subsequently acquired replacement or additional hardware $[31]$ . Furthermore, EUC and electronic data interchange exist in some small firms $[35,2]$ . More sophisticated levels of IS, for example, management support systems and integrated applications, have been reported $[37]$ Despite the increase in IS sophistication in terms of usage, their results show that many small firms still have a low level of sophistication with respect to their management of IS.

Many factors have influenced the evolution and sophistication of IS in small firms [9]. Important motivators included enthusiasm from the owner, as well as perceived benefits to individuals and the organisation. Important inhibitors to growth include low IS knowledge and skills, lack of managerial time, lack of funds, and technical complexity. Internal factors had a greater influence than external factors on the progressive use of IS [4].

Other studies have identified many success factors; however they also have some inconsistent findings $[11,16,25,32,41,44]$ . A possible reason for this is that IS may have evolved over time. Similarly, success factors could be expected to change over time. Therefore, the study of success factors, which is an important area of IS research, is likely to benefit from a study of its evolution. A better understanding of IS evolution will lead to greater insights into factors that influence its success over time.

## 3. Method

To obtain a good understanding of changes occurring over time, a longitudinal case based approach was adopted. Surviving firms from earlier studies were used in this investigation, primarily because historical data was available and data collection would therefore be more objective than simply relying on the memories of a few key individuals.

Data collection occurred in 1983, 1987 and 1991; it relied heavily on face to face interviews with key personnel. Typically, interviews were conducted with the owner and at least one regular hands-on user. In some organisations, another manager was interviewed if he/she was responsible for computing. Interviews covered a broad range of topics that identified major organisational events as well as developments in IS, including company history, major products, company performance, and market factors.

Our study focuses on eighteen surviving firms from an earlier study of thirty three small manufacturing firms [8]. The data collected in 1991 did not include fifteen of the firms from the original study. Some of these were known to have ceased trading since the initial study. At least two firms had been acquired and subsequently became part of a much larger organisation. Some of the other firms may still be in operation, but they could not be contacted, possibly due to a name or location change.

Significant organizational changes took place in many firms over the eight year period. All eighteen were originally manufacturers, though by 1991 one had become a distributor rather than a manufacturer of automotive parts. A major change during the period was that exporting had become a significant activity for six of the firms. Furthermore, three had changed their company name. Although five had increased their number of employees during the period, another five were smaller; two significantly.

By 1991, a total of four firms were subsidiaries, and thus would not meet the independence criteria commonly used in the definition of a small firm. In addition, two other firms had more than fifty employees and thus exceeded the standard maximum size for the term “small manufacturer” as used in New Zealand [3]. The following results refer to all eighteen firms, including subsidiaries and those larger than fifty employees.

## 4. Results

Prior studies of IS sophistication have shown that the concept is multi-dimensional. This study focused on hardware, EUC, and computer utilization.

## 4.1. Hardware

A total of 39 computers had been acquired during the period. Although most firms had changed their hardware, four had not, i.e., they were still using the computer they acquired in the early 1980's. Computer acquisitions are shown in Table 3.

Of the fourteen firms that acquired new computers during the period, five acquired only one. In all five cases, this was to replace rather than supplement their original equipment. Most of the other nine firms had replaced and supplemented their original machine; microcomputers dominated these acquisitions. However, six of the seven firms that had multi-user capability in 1983 were still using multi-user computers in 1991. Only one firm had a LAN for their microcomputers. Any data sharing was typically via floppy disk.

## 4.2. End-user computing

EUC can be broadly defined as the direct use of computing resources by non-computer professionals to support their own personal business needs $[39]$ . The results of our study indicate that most users could be classified as non-programming users who mainly used menu-based application packages. Packaged accounting applications were popular, with 5 firms acquiring new, full accounting systems during the period. Payroll packages had been acquired by four firms and CAD (computer-aided design) packages were being used by three.

All of the firms relied on external sources for their application software. During the eight year period, nearly all firms had increased their applications portfolio, although use in two firms had not changed. Typically, computers were being used for another two or three applications by 1991, as shown in Table 4.

Although packaged software was popular, six firms had acquired new custom built software to meet their specific needs. Most firms felt there was no need for them to develop their own systems, as most of their requirements were being met by packaged software. An exception was one firm which was in the process of using a programming language to develop an application to assist with engineering design calculations. Thus, end-user developed applications were not very common [38].

A significant change had occurred in the use of spreadsheet software. In 1983 only one of the firms was using a package. By 1991, seven firms were using them, five of them on a weekly basis. They were typically used for reporting to management, rather than for “what if” analysis by management. Database software was not as popular as spreadsheet software. One firm had used a database package to assist with marketing; however, this ceased when the user left the firm. In another firm, one manager used a database package for ad-hoc enquiries. Thus personal decision support tools were not commonly used by managers [36].

Our study shows that most users were capable of using menu driven applications, but that EUC literacy was generally very low $[25]$ . When users were asked about training, many felt that it was unnecessary, as the software was reasonably self-explanatory. Typically, any training had been limited to hands-on supervision when new applications were introduced. Additional training had been conducted internally within four firms, usually on an as-needed basis. Only five firms had sent an employee on a course in order to learn how to use a new software package.

Changes since 1983 show that more than half the firms had more users and had increased hands-on use by 1991. As shown in Table 6, the number of hands-on users decreased in three firms but increased in eleven, during the period 1983 to 1991. Similarly, usage time decreased in one firm but increased in eleven.

The hands-on use was predominantly at the operational level for data input and reporting. Most firms had at least one employee who spent a majority of his/her time on data input. Monthly financial performance statements were common, with nearly half of the firms having computerised budgeting and/or cash flow forecasting. Design was supported by computer in three firms. However, the production side of the firm was often only supported through computerised stock control. Exceptions to this trend were production planning and control (4 firms), job tracking (1 firm), statistical process control (1 firm), and raw material optimisation (1 firm). Marketing activities were rarely supported in these forms.

## 5. Discussion

Before discussing important issues raised by the data, the limitations of the study should be emphasised. Only eighteen firms were studied, and all were early adopters in terms of small firm computing. All firms were located in one urban centre, within the relatively small and isolated country of New Zealand. All but one firm were manufacturers. Other samples may provide very different patterns of change, particularly if strong external forces exist which support IS innovations in small firms, including local and national IT policies. Small firms in New Zealand have had relatively little assistance when compared with support schemes that operate in Australia, Canada, parts of Europe, Singapore, and the USA $[5,12,17,29,30]$ .

Despite these limitations, this is one of the few longitudinal studies of small firm computing, and it provides interesting insights into IS sophistication and evolution.

## 5.1. Inadequate hardware and software

The study confirmed that accounting is still the major application area for computers in small firms, and that other critical functional areas, such as production, marketing, and planning have been relatively unaffected by computers. Typically, the most used computer is located in the general office, and used primarily by a clerk/secretary. All eighteen firms had computerised some aspects of their accounting system, typically sales ordering and debtors control. Most sent computerised invoices, and payroll was another popular application. These applications dominated the total time for which the firms used their computers. Other applications were relatively minor and occasional, for example spreadsheeting for reporting to management and hands-on use by management.

The results indicated a low level of IS sophistication regarding the firm's applications portfolio. This could be because packaged software for supporting other functional areas is not readily available. Thus, small firms will have to purchase custom-made software or develop their own applications through EUC if they are to computerize other critical functional areas.

Although most firms had acquired additional hardware and software, little or no change had occurred for a small number of firms between 1983 and 1991. The fact that these firms did not upgrade their computer capabilities is surprising, considering that they were early adopters and thus likely to be inclined to investigate new technologies. Many inhibiting factors existed, including limited financial resources and low IS knowledge and skills [9]. For example, one manager knew that he would be wasting his time asking his bank manager for an increased overdraft to acquire additional hardware. The recession of the 1980's meant that some of these firms did well to survive. However, no single factor was common across all firms. Future research could improve our understanding of the inhibiting factors, and also examine the costs and benefits associated with increased IS sophistication. Further investigations could help provide better advice to small firms.

## 5.2. Lack of internal technical expertise

One factor that seriously inhibits IS sophistication and evolution in small firms is their lack of internal expertise. Although it is a common problem faced by many small firms $[44]$ , Raymond $[35]$ suggests that small firms could strengthen their internal expertise by developing their own EUC skills.

In most of the firms, internal computer expertise was limited. The data in Table 5 showed that about 20% of total employees are hands-on users. For the typical firm this refers to one clerk plus one or more professionals/managers. Thus any computing expertise is limited to a very few. The typical method of developing IS knowledge and skills was through on-the-job experience. Very few personnel attended computer courses, although free seminars were available. Many users felt that training was unnecessary since the software packages were easy to use. As a result, computer literacy was low; just sufficient to drive some parts of their application packages.

Since most small firms cannot afford to hire their own internal IS specialists, they rely heavily on outside sources for technical expertise. Furthermore, external support, primarily involving consultants and vendors, has been found to have a positive influence on IS success in small firms $[11,16,22,34]$ . However, the firms had limited contact with external sources of guidance; e.g., the contact with the computer industry in over half the firms was limited to occasions when problems needed solving. For others, contact revolved around software upgrades. Typically, they did not know what other firms were doing with computers, and were not aware of the best uses of technology. Only one of the eighteen firms sought regular advice from a consultant.

There are several possible reasons why the firms did not seek external help. One is that the firms perceived that they could not afford the external support. This is possibly a legacy of their prior experiences with accountants, as a common reason for firms turning to computers in the first place was to considerably reduce their accounting fees. Another explanation is that the firms did not have enough internal expertise to make an informed choice about using external support. A lack of trust in outside sources and poor service could also have prevented the firms from relying on external support $[11]$ . Finally, any training or advice seeking requires a time commitment. Time is a limited resource in the typical small firm, evidenced by most hands-on training being conducted at home during leisure time.

Lack of internal expertise was a factor that inhibited IS evolution and sophistication. In order to overcome this problem, small firms must either develop their own internal EUC skills or seek help from external sources. There is a strong need for research focusing on the impact of external sources on IS evolution and sophistication in small firms. A better understanding of the specific training needs is also required.

## 5.3. Insufficient attention by management to IS

A third factor that has inhibited the sophistication and evolution of IS is the inadequate attention by management to IS. Because of their small size, most small firms do not need an IS department, or even a fulltime IS manager. Typically, a professional or manager within the firm, with some prior experience with computers, took or was given responsibility for computers. Often this prior exposure to computers came through previous employment or in tertiary education. These people usually became the IS manager by default. They have little or no formal computer education, as well as significant other managerial responsibilities to perform. They were therefore poorly placed to lead IS developments.

Typically, the firms exhibited a very casual attitude to their management of IS. Formal training did not take place. Other sound IS management practices were taken lightly, particularly planning. Without an individual taking proper responsibility, it seems likely that little or no time will be spent considering the potential of new technologies, including, for example, end user computing tools, and ways of improving customer service. Since top management support is a key factor contributing to the success of IS in small firms $[11,44]$ , top managers must take an active role in the management of IS resources. Small firms need a staff member with a broad knowledge of IS issues, and the time to ensure that appropriate planning and control takes place.

The results show that management can influence IS evolution and sophistication, since most of the inhibiting factors involved internal rather than external factors $[4]$ . In order to determine the IS management practices that are appropriate for small firms, further research is required. This work could build on the methods provided by Raymond and Paré $[37]$ , who found a low level of IS sophistication with respect to management of IS. Their sample reported little use of planning documents, including requests for proposals, and few control measures, for example, over security and effectiveness.

## 5.4. Research on IS evolution

The study did not set out to test existing models of IS evolution. However, the evidence suggests that none of the stages of growth models reviewed by Saarinen [40] applied in full. The models had some applicability, but incompatibilities existed for some of the detailed elements of the models. For example, with respect to Nolan's stages of growth model [27,28], some aspects of IS in the firms were quite mature, while other elements were not. Some firms had sophisticated on-line, integrated, relational, accounting systems, and very weak managerial practices.

The adoption and use of information technology can be viewed as an innovation. The data showed that IS evolution was an on-going process, with some firms continuously innovative, and others intermittently innovative with respect to information technology $[10]$ . There is a growing literature on IS as an innovation, and this could further help our understanding of small firm computing $[7,21,23,26]$ . For example, innovation theory could be used to examine patterns and influences on IS innovation, as well as links between IS innovations and both IS and firm success.

## 6. Conclusions

This longitudinal study of eighteen small New Zealand firms has discussed IS sophistication and evolution. The results show that, in many small firms, computing efforts have become more sophisticated and have evolved, particularly in terms of hardware, software, and EUC. The paper also identified three main problem areas for computing in small firms: inadequate hardware and software, lack of internal expertise, and insufficient attention by management to IS. To help address these problems, several directions for future research are proposed, including identifying the needs of small firms regarding training, external support and IS management practices.

Paul Cragg is a senior lecturer in the Faculty of Commerce, University of Canterbury, where he teaches MIS. His masters was in Operational Research, University of Lancaster, England, and his PhD was in MIS at the University of Loughborough, England. His major research interests focus on the use of information systems and technology by small firms. Current research includes the role for IS to facilitate cooperation within networks of small firms. He has published widely, including MIS Quarterly, J. of Strategic Information Systems, Information and Management, Omega, JORS, and Entrepreneurship, Theory and Practice.
