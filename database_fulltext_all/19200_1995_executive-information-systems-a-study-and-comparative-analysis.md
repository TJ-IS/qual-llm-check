# Executive information systems: A study and comparative analysis

Jeretta Horn Nord \*, G. Daryl Nord

Department of Management, College of Business Administration, Oklahoma State University, Stillwater, OK 74078, USA

## Abstract

Decision support software designed to meet specific needs of executives is referred to as an Executive Information System (EIS). This provides a means for information to be accessed, created, and analyzed-on-demand by high-level executives using personal computers, local area networks, minicomputers, or a centralized mainframe. Executive decisions are generally broad and based, to a large extent, on intuition. These characteristics are reflected in the executive decision-making environment, which is itself characterized by a lack of structure, a high degree of uncertainty, a future orientation, informal sources, and a low level of detail. Executive Information Systems can directly aid and support some business decisions. Strategic-planning capabilities, an external environment focus, ease of learning and use, and custom tailoring to meet the unique needs of individual executives are common properties of Executive Information Systems. This paper reports the findings of a study of executives in major U.S. corporations regarding the status, trends, benefits, and plans for future development of Executive Information Systems. A comparative analysis of user satisfaction and specific issues associated with the leading EIS Systems is also presented.

Keywords: Executive information systems; Executive support systems; Decision support systems

## 1. Introduction

Executive Information Systems (EIS) are currently providing some decision-support for senior executives in an increasing number of corporations. Although decision-support is only one of the components of a complete EIS, it may well be the component that is most closely linked to increased profits. As an example, a recent study found that improved decision making capabilities attributed to an EIS generated considerable calculable savings [4], and, indeed, not having an EIS can deprive a company of the competitive edge it may need to survive in today's fast-paced business environment [12].

An EIS or ESS (executive support system) has been defined in several ways. In its basic form, it is an information system specifically designed for and tailored to the chief executives of a company. It can spread out from the chief executives to their immediate subordinates – many companies have 50 or more users of their EIS – but the fundamental idea is that the design of the system is aimed at satisfying the needs of top executives [31].

In addition, EISs usually include personal productivity, communication, and coordination components, providing executives with a broad range of services that increase productivity. Experts [10,27] agree that an EIS has powerful analytical tools and the following characteristics:

(1) an easy to use and maintainable graphical user interface;

(2) integrated capabilities for data access, security, and control;

(3) on request “drill down” capability to lower levels of detail;

(4) depiction of organizational health indicators;

(5) functionality for decision support, ad hoc queries, and what-if analysis;

(6) data analysis features;

(7) advanced report generation;

(8) statistical analysis tools for summarizing and structuring data; and

(9) access to a variety of external data sources.

International Data Corporation, a market research firm, predicts that EIS software sales for mainframe, mini, and micros will reach \$230 million in 1995, with the U.S. market growing at a compound annual rate of nearly 40 percent [9]. Although the trend toward the implementation of EISs in major corporations is undeniable, research on their usage in industries is extremely limited. Our study was conducted on Chief Executive Officers in Fortune 500 companies to determine their use of EIS, including the status, trends, benefits, and plans for future business.

## 2. Background

What is an EIS? Definitions vary according to the weight given to the three elements in the name itself. Executives' roles differ according to company size and hierarchy as well as personal management styles. The acronym itself is a chameleon, according to Frenkel [13], sometimes meant to stand for Executive Information "Support." The term "executive support system" usually refers to a system with a broader set of capabilities. Whereas EIS connotes providing information, ESS implies additional support capabilities. Rockart and DeLong [32] further define the term ESS to mean the routine use of a computer-based system, most often through direct access to a terminal, or personal computer, for any business function. John N. Kelly [18] provides yet another definition:

An EIS “is a set of tools designed to help an organization carefully monitor its current status, its progress toward achieving its goals, and the relationship of its mental model of the world to the best available clues about what’s really happening.”

Pappas [29] supports this contention by stating that “An EIS gives non-technical senior executives the ability to navigate through strategic information culled from company databases and on-line information services.” For our purposes, an EIS is a computerized system that provides executives with access to internal and external information that is relevant to decision making and other critical success factors.

Fig. 1 depicts a general model of an EIS. Internal transaction data are collected by management. Data from external sources may be retrieved and/or stored in the firm's database. Data from both sources are then transformed into a format that facilitates the development of management reports and inquiries. Executives are primarily interested in summaries and various compilations of these data. The transformations therefore provide meaningful formats, including graphics and colour to aid executive users of the system.

Quick acceptance and fast payback are essential to the success of any EIS project. Snyder [35] lists seven keys to a successful EIS:

(1) Contain precisely the information that its users most want and need to see -nothing more, nothing less.

(2) Have the potential to access virtually any information source within the organization and any publicly available information that is relevant.

(3) Present information in a format that reflects the executive's preferences.

(4) Be championed by at least one strong executive who realizes the system's benefits.

(5) Be supported by a dedicated EIS support staff. This staff must include both people with traditional data processing skills and business information analysts who understand the executives' specific needs.

(6) Be up and running, at least in an initial version, in three to six months. A survey conducted by the University of Georgia revealed that the average length of time to get started was 4.9 months.

(7) Be simple enough to use; an executive should be able to use it with about 30 minutes or less of instruction.

The literature provides extensive descriptions of EIS, with their features and characteristics. However, little research has been conducted on this topic; e.g., Watson [47], who is currently involved in the University of Georgia's “Executive 2000” program of research and outreach to the business community, feels that the effect of information technology on executives must be further studied.

## 3. Methodology

An investigation was made of EISs in major U.S. corporations. The objectives were to:

(1) Determine the extent to which executives use EIS for their decision making.

(2) Identify software technology used by executives for strategic decision support.

(3) Identify status, trends, advantages, and future development of EIS.

(4) Compare user satisfaction and related issues about the effectiveness of decision making using EIS software.

## 3.1. The survey instrument

A questionnaire was developed to gather data to accomplish the described research objectives (see Appendix A). A pilot version of the survey was completed by ten executives in Fortune 500 companies. Suggestions were incorporated into a second version that was pilot tested by five different executives in Fortune 500 companies. Additional suggestions were made and incorporated in the final version. Such a piloting process was used to establish content validity and reduce bias in response due to misinterpretation of the instrument. The survey instrument was mailed to the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of all Fortune 500 companies to determine the following:

\- whether EISs were used within their firms and... if so

\- the specific EIS used;

\- the purpose of using it;

\- the level of management using it;

\- the perceived benefits from utilizing EIS software;

\- the EIS user satisfaction;

\- the perceived importance of selected characteristics of the EIS software.

Realizing that time is a valuable resource to an individual at this level of management, the questionnaire was designed to take less than five minutes to complete. The CEO was asked to fill out the questionnaire or forward it to another executive. Four weeks after the first mailing, another copy of the questionnaire was mailed to non-respondents. The initial mailing and follow-up resulted in a total of 152 usable responses (i.e., a response rate of 30.4%). The percentages reported are based on the number of responses received for each survey question.

## 4. Analysis and results

## 4.1. Validity

The sample was validated by determining the representation of the respondents to all Fortune 500 companies. Since the vast majority of respondents (88.5%) considered the primary purpose of their firm to be manufacturing, the corporate identification code on the questionnaire was used to determine the reppresentation by specific industry. The 1993 Fortune 500 consisted of 26 industries; therefore, industries with 25 companies (5%) or more were compared to the respondents' industry return rate. Forty-eight (9.6%) companies were ranked by industry as Chemicals, 47 (9.4%) as Food, 39 (7.6%) as Electronics, 36 (7.2%) as Industrial and Farm Equipment, 34 (6.8%) as Forest and Paper, 31 (6.2%) as Petroleum Refining, and 25 (5%) as Computers, Office Equipment; whereas 14 (9.2%) of the respondents were categorized as Chemicals, 16 (10.5%) as Food, 10 (6.6%) as Electronics, 11 (7.2%) as Industrial and Farm Equipment, 9 (5.9%) as Forest and Paper, 12 (7.8%) as Petroleum Remaining, and 8 (5.3%) as Computers, Office Equipment. Each of the remaining industries accounted for less than 5% of the population of Fortune 500 companies and respondents. Geographically, the majority of the respondents were located in either the Central (48.1%) or Eastern (38.5%) segment of the U.S. This corresponds with 44.8% of the Fortune 500 population in the Central region of the U.S. and 41.8% located in the East. These data provide substantial evidence that the demographic characteristics of the sample are quite similar to the Fortune 500 population, thus resolving any question of non-return bias.

Content validity and reliability testing were used for instrument validation. Content validity – the representativeness of the measures – was assessed by subjecting the first version of the survey instrument to pilot testing by ten executives, with the revised version piloted by five executives. The pilot testing suggested that the instructions and questions were clear.

Reliability – the stability of the measures – was assessed by Cronbach's alpha. This analysis was used to assess the interitem reliability of the survey's EIS related variables. While a reliability score of 0.6 is usually considered acceptable [38], all of the variables reliability scores exceeded 0.8. Thus, the high alphas indicated reliability.

## 4.2. Demographics

Over one-half (61.5%) of the respondents were Chief Executive Officers, with one-fourth of the questionnaires completed by individuals at the Vice-Presidential level. The remaining 13.5% of respondents were made up of Presidents and other upper-level managers. Approximately one-half of the respondents held a masters degree. The largest number of respondents indicated some area of business as their degree specialty, followed by the sciences. Finally, it is not surprising that 90.7% of the respondents claimed annual six-digit and above salaries, with some climbing as high as eight digits. This correlates with a recent study of CEO annual salaries indicating the average at \$1,214,090 before long-term compensation such as stock options is considered, which raised Chief Executive Officer's average total to \$1,952,806 [6].

## 4.3. EIS issues

EISs were relatively late to evolve. Approximately one-third of the respondents (30.8%) currently use an EIS for decision support, to aid in personal productivity, and/or for communication. One-fourth of the respondents not currently using an EIS indicated that they were implementing or had plans to implement one within the next two years. A prime tool for gaining competitive advantage, EIS installations are expected to spread even further across the Fortune 500.

Who are the leading players in the EIS arena? Previous studies report two EIS vendors as dominant: Comshare in Ann Arbor, Michigan and Pilot Software in Boston [28,39,16]. Results, in Fig. 2, show that over one-third (37.5%) of the respondents using an EIS selected Comshare's Commander EIS, followed by an equal number (31.3%) of in-house developed EISs and IBM's Office Vision. Pilot software was among the top three vendors. A combination of “other” EISs totalled 18.8% with no specific EIS dominating. Finally, Digital DEC Decisions has been implemented by 6.3% of the respondents. The researchers were not surprised that a number of respondents checked more than one EIS on this survey item. In many cases, this included a vendor developed system in addition to an in-house developed EIS.

A previous study by Watson, Rainer, and Koh reported that the majority (68%) of initiators in the development of an EIS are company executives. This research explored the level of management that is utilizing EISs within the corporation (see Table 1). The chief executive officer is using an EIS in one-half of the responding firms in which an EIS has been implemented. Although many experts agree that they are intended for very senior managers [27,37,31], it is interesting to note that other levels participate substantially.

Past research has shown that many pressures lead to the implementation of an EIS, with the most critical external pressure being an increasingly competitive environment. Competition mandates that executives within major corporations improve communication, increase their level of confidence in decision making, have access to a wealth of information, and increase profits.

Executives were requested to indicate whether they perceived these benefits to be a direct result of utilizing Executive Information Systems. As shown in Fig. 3, the majority (87.5%) believed that communication improved as a direct result of an EIS. More than one-half (62.5%) indicated that their confidence in decision making increased as well as their access to otherwise unavailable information. Approximately one-fifth of the responding executives attributed increased profits directly to the EIS. Although the costs of Executive Information Systems are high, many companies have reported a sizable increase in profits following the implementation of an EIS. For example, Robert Wallace of Phillips 66 indicates that an EIS increased their profits from oil trading by \$20 million per year (from \$30 million to \$50 million) [19]. Other studies have shown that an EIS can pay for itself within hours of installation [22].

Common characteristics of an EIS were listed, and respondents were asked to rate each characteristic based on their perception of its importance. A five was to be awarded for “very important,” four for “important,” three for “somewhat important,” two for “of little importance,” and one for “not important.” The responses were averaged for each characteristic. It is interesting to note that seven of the eight characteristics earned averages of 3 and above, indicating “somewhat important” to “very important” (see Fig. 4). Ease of use rated highest with a 4.44 average. This is not surprising since an EIS is typically customized for an individual executive and takes his or her skills and needs into account. Mohan, Holstein, and Adams [26] cited three reasons for lack of enthusiasm for EIS by senior executives: (1) lack of computer training and experience, (2) unwillingness to spend time getting such training, and (3) poor keyboard skills. “Ease of use” addresses all three of these issues. Executives should be able to begin using an EIS without any prior training. Robins indicates that ease of use may have been a major factor in the executive being slow in adopting computer technology. Decision support tools followed ease of use in the rating of characteristics by respondents. Since an EIS is a custom-designed decision support system with added facilities, it was expected that the decision support component would rank high. Graphics also ranked high on the list, as did minimum number of keystrokes, and mouse or touch screen operation. Vendors of off-the-shelf EIS software have addressed these issues by creating easy-to-use systems with easy-to-develop and understand colour menus, icons, and graphics. The pictures help keep training to a minimum, and menus are designed so that keyboard interaction is eliminated or reduced to a few keys. The electronic briefing book module and electronic mail package was rated as somewhat important, while the scheduling module was slightly less important.

All users of Comshare's Commander felt that better communication at the management level and access to unavailable information were major benefits. There was also an increased confidence in decision making. It is interesting to note that the three benefits selected by most respondents were consistent among the systems. An additional observation in this analysis is the percentage of respondents indicating increased profits as a direct result of an EIS system.

Watson found, on average, the total costs of developing an EIS are \$365,000, and the annual costs to maintain one are \$208,000. Many researchers indicate that cost/benefit analyses are difficult for EIS because of the difficulty in quantifying many of the benefits [15,25,33]. Furthermore, there seems to be simply an intuitive feeling that the system will justify its costs. Specific benefits and costs savings are often identifiable only after the system becomes operational [42]. These findings were supported by the respondents when asked to indicate whether their company's investment in an EIS was wise. ## 6. Summary

This paper presented data from a study of CEOs of Fortune 500 companies regarding EIS. It shows the major EIS software used, benefits, importance of selected EIS characteristics, and a comparative analysis of the leading EIS software as identified by this research.

Although EISs were originally developed for CEOs and others at the top, this study indicated that Vice-Presidents and Middle Managers use these systems even more extensively than top executives. Better communication at the management level, increased confidence in decision making, and access to unavailable information were the leading benefits attributed to EIS. The following were the most important characteristics: Ease of use, decision support tools, graphics, minimum number of keystrokes, and mouse or touch screen.

EIS technology is evolving rapidly, and future systems are likely to be different from those that are in use today. Developers of decision support technology for executives must be alert to the needs of top executives and the evolution that is occurring with the area of EIS.

As commercial EIS software improves, top executives must utilize this software technology for strategic decision making and managing daily business activities in order to remain competitive.
