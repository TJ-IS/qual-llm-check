# The CEO/CIO relationship revisited: An empirical assessment of satisfaction with IS

Mary C. Jones \*, G. Stephen Taylor, Barbara A. Spencer

Mississippi State University, Dept. of Management and Information Systems, Box 9581, Mississippi State, MS 39762, USA

## Abstract

The necessity of integrating information systems (IS) into corporate strategy has received widespread attention in recent years. Strategic planning has moved IS from serving primarily as a support function to a point where it may influence corporate strategy. The strength of this influence, however, usually is determined by the nature of the relationship between the chief information officer (CIO) and the CEO. Generally the more satisfied CEOs are with CIOs, the greater the influence IS has on top-level decisions. Results of a nationwide survey of motor carrier CEOs and CIOs indicate that CEOs are generally satisfied with their CIOs' activities, and that CIOs perceive CEOs as placing a high priority on strategic IS plans. However, IS does not appear to be truly a part of corporate strategy formulation.

Keywords: Relationship between CEO and CIO; Satisfaction with information systems; Strategic planning; Role of information technology in strategic planning; Strategic information systems planning; Information technology in the motor carrier industry; CEO satisfaction with IS; CIO perceptions of corporate use of IS

## 1. Introduction

Strategic information systems (IS) planning has been a topic of interest to managers and academic researchers. For example, American Hospital Supply's ASAP order-entry system and the American Airlines Sabre reservation system have been discussed in trade papers and management journals for over three decades. Academic interest in IS planning also has a long tradition: “There is an intrinsic linkage of... decision-supporting [MIS] to the organization’s purpose, objectives, and strategy” [19].

Strategic IS planning typically is used in one of two ways: to impact the firm's strategy or to align IS objectives with the firm's overall mission [35]. For the former, top-level IS planning is incorporated in the development of the firm's overall mission [20-22,28]. In the latter, IS planning involves topics such as using IS as a competitive weapon to support corporate strategy. Here the planning process is conducted separately from the corporate strategy planning process [5,14,24,37]. However, both approaches are departures from early uses of strategic IS planning, which were mainly to find better ways to generate information to support the strategic planning process [6,17] and to identify opportunities to improve the IS department [23].

Although many debate the effectiveness of strategic planning [e.g., [10], [12]], there is little doubt that IS has an increasing role in corporate strategy formulation. In many companies senior IS executives serve as the link between corporate strategic functions and the IS function [e.g., [2]]. For example, CEOs at

Kmart and BankOne Corporation incorporate their senior IS managers into the strategic planning process. At BankOne senior IS managers have also been promoted to the level of president of various divisions.

The relationship between the CIO and CEO can be crucial to the success of the strategic use of IS [36]. The CEO must understand what IS can do for the company and be willing to take advantage of IS opportunities when they arise. At the same time, the CIO must understand the goals of the business, over and above those of IS, and be able to recognize appropriate IS opportunities and threats [27,31]. Moreover, and perhaps more importantly, the CEO and CIO must agree on the role of IS in the firm in order for them to work together successfully in moving the company in a unified direction. There is little consensus in the industry about the extent to which the CIO influences corporate strategy. One view is that the CIO has actually become a part of corporate strategy formulation; whereas another view is that the IS function is simply to provide information where and when it is needed.

In this study, we examine CEO satisfaction with various aspects of CIO performance, CIO perceptions of their own involvement with strategic IS planning and corporate strategy planning, their relationship with the CEO, and their satisfaction with the planning.

## 2. CEO satisfaction

CEO satisfaction with the senior IS official was assessed using nine specific and seven general qualities that CEOs look for in a CIO, drawn on work in [11] (see Appendix A). Some of these qualities assess the CIO's general managerial abilities (e.g., is a good manager of his/her people); communication abilities (e.g., speaks the same “language” as I do); and knowledge/commitment to the business (e.g., understands the goals of our business). Also included are the CIO's ability to perform within budget; deliver products on time; prioritize tasks effectively; know which products the firm really needs; and keep the firm's technology up to date.

In addition to having good managerial skills, the CIO also must be able to communicate effectively with the CEO in order for IT (information technology) to be used appropriately. Unfortunately, many IS managers are not highly skilled in communicating ideas to upper management, and few CEOs have much IS expertise. Not surprisingly, increased communication often is cited as a key to improving IS performance [e.g., [15]].

Finally, the CIO's knowledge of and commitment to the firm's business are qualities that many CEOs need in a CIO. CEOs want IS managers who “be-have less territorially about ‘their’ data and who focus more on the firm as a whole” [25]. CEOs also want IS managers who are attuned to the problems of running the company and who can “self-identify with [the] company and its industry” [4]. One of the factors that leads to CIO turnover is their failure to understand the organization. CIOs must be forward thinking, not only about IT, but also about opportunities for the firm.

## 3. CIO perceptions

Key aspects of the IS strategic planning process are the extent to which the CIO feels involved in the process, is satisfied with the time and resources allocated to IS planning $[7]$ , sees a strong link between IS planning and corporate plans $[26]$ , and believes that IS is important to the CEO. These are very much interrelated. For example, despite the increasing role of information technology in business, most organizations allocate a small portion of revenue to IS $[1,34]$ . There is evidence, however, that resource allocation is directly related both to the extent of executive commitment to IS $[13,29]$ and to the extent to which the CEO perceives IS as contributing to the success of the firm $[9]$ .

The amount of time the CIO spends communicating with the CEO, educating the CEO about IS opportunities and threats, and learning about the business should correspond to the CEO's satisfaction with IS. This is consistent with General Food's former IS manager who reportedly divided his time equally between running the IS organization, communicating with General Foods executives about the business, and learning about opportunities and threats outside the company [32].

We also assess the CIO's perception of how involved he/she is in corporate strategy planning and in running the business. One recent study indicates that many senior IS executives serve active roles on corporate strategic planning committees [33]. In fact, some new IS executives spend up to 27 percent of their time helping to develop corporate strategy [3]. However, only 43 percent feel that they are actively involved in the team that sets corporate strategy. The questions used to measure CIO's perceived involvement with strategy making are presented in Appendix B.

## 4. Methodology

A systematic random sample of 750 truckload (TL) and 750 less-than-truckload (LTL) motor carriers listed in the National Motor Carrier Directory was used for data collection in the USA. Competition is intense in both the TL and LTL markets. Entry into each market is relatively easy, and there is a shortage of drivers. Thus freight rates and driver salaries are both highly competitive. In addition to competing with other firms in the industry, LTLs must compete with other types of small load carriers such as UPS and Federal Express, who can often deliver faster and cheaper. Firms focus on keeping costs low and on providing high quality service to customers. To do this, many have turned to IT for variety of applications including scheduling, tracking, and customizing services. Many trucks have computers and fax machines on-board.

Along with a cover letter explaining the purpose of the study, the CEO $^{1}$ of each firm received two different surveys: one was to be completed by the CEO and one was to be given to the senior IS manager in the company. Surveys were coded so that CEO and CIO responses could be matched. (Senior IS managers are given different titles. For ease of discussion, the term CIO is used to refer to the senior IS manager.)

This form of survey distribution probably reduced our response rate. However, the firms in the directory do not list an IS manager, only the CEO, president, owner, etc. Another difficulty in reaching IS managers is that many of the firms in the long-haul trucking sector of the economy do not have IS departments or CIOs: the operations department may be responsible for IT management. To address this issue, we asked the CEO to give the survey to the “senior IS manager” rather than the CIO; on the survey, however, we defined CIO to be the senior IS manager. However, if IT is managed in the operations department, then there may not be an IS manager in the traditional sense. In addition, many of the questions explicitly apply to traditional IS departments. Thus, many non-respondents may have thought that the surveys did not apply to them.

A total of 61 usable CEO surveys and 47 usable CIO surveys were returned; 39 of the surveys were matched pairs. There are several reasons for this relatively low response rate. Some of the firms in the directory were no longer in business. Others replied that their IS usage was not broad enough for them to answer the questions adequately.

Approximately 25% more CEO surveys were returned than CIO surveys. Furthermore, only 83% of the CIO surveys can be matched with a CEO survey. Thus, 17% of the CEOs passed the CIO survey along without returning their own survey. This is consistent with the findings that it is difficult to obtain CEO related responses to IS surveys, and also helps explain the low response rate. Thus, we believe that this response rate is consistent with that in other IS executive related studies. However, because the results are based on responses of firms in which the CEO did pass along the survey to the CIO, findings may be biased toward firms in which the CEO and CIO work more closely together, the CEO places greater importance on IT, or in which the CIO has a greater role in corporate planning. Findings must be interpreted in light of these possibilities.

## 5. Results

The firms in this study range in size from annual revenues of \$1 million to over \$30 million. About 15% of the firms have revenues from \$1 million to \$5 million, 25% from over \$5 million to \$10 million,

16% from over \$10 million to \$20 million, 26% from over \$20 million to \$30 million, and 18% have revenues of over \$30 million. The number of vehicles they operate ranges from 6 to 632.

## 5.1. CEO satisfaction

The means and standard deviations of the sixteen indicators of CEO satisfaction with the CIO are presented in Table 1.

All of the means lie between 3.51 and 4.00 (on a 5-point Likert scale). This indicates that, on average, CEOs are more satisfied than dissatisfied with their CIO. Most of the standard deviations are between 0.90 and 1.00, lending further evidence that CEOs are neither strongly satisfied nor strongly dissatisfied with CIOs. However, most firms have not reached the “ideal” state in which the CIO is actively involved in learning the business and contributing to the firm beyond IS activities. IS is still viewed primarily as a support function, thus CEOs may not be strongly satisfied with CIO contributions. However, responses indicate they are not strongly dissatisfied either.

The typical CEO is most satisfied with the fact that the CIO puts the needs of the company above those of the IS area, does not “talk down” to the CEO, understands the goals of the firm, relates well to other managers, and is a sharp, creative thinker.

The CIOs' seeming inability to separate important from unimportant tasks may be partially due to their background. Many CIOs started their careers in technical jobs (e.g., programmers) and have technical backgrounds (e.g., computer science, information systems). Consequently, most of their training has been technically rather than managerially oriented. Prioritizing tasks is a management skill, thus CIOs may not have been exposed to techniques for developing this skill. The lack of CEO satisfaction with this may also be due to a lack of agreement between the two about what is important. Despite their focus on the business, CIOs may still spend more time focusing on technical issues –issues that the CEO may see as less important. There also may be a lack of a clear vision from top management: the CIO may not really know what is important to the CEO.

The tendency to focus on what can't rather than what can be done may also be related to CIO backgrounds. IS started in many firms as part of the accounting/finance function. Even though many firms have a CIO or Vice President of IS, approximately one-third of today's firms still place IS in the accounting/finance area [18]. Because of the traditional need to compete for resources that have been competitively gained by another area, IS managers have been hesitant to acknowledge problems openly. Problems might detract from IS performance, and budget increases are often perceived as a reward for good performance. For similar reasons, IS managers may be cautious about committing themselves to projects if they are not sure they can complete them –thus the can't do rather than the can do attitudes. This tradition may influence CIO behavior, even in firms that are relatively innovative with respect to the use of IT.

Finally, the dissatisfaction with the CIO's on-time completion of projects is not surprising. IS historically has had difficulty establishing time and cost estimates for projects [8,16,30]. Although IS managers may be cautious about committing to projects initially, after they commit, estimating development cost and time is difficult –particularly in large scale projects. Lack of satisfaction with project due date performance may reflect a growing need for shorter development times –CEOs may want projects completed faster than IS can achieve them.

## 5.2. CIO perceptions

The means and standard deviations of CIO perceptions are presented in Table 2.

The means lie between 3.36 and 4.17, indicating that CIOs are satisfied with some aspects and less satisfied with others. Nine of the fourteen standard deviations are above 1.08, and remaining five are above 0.90, indicating that there is considerable diversity of opinion among respondents.

CIOs believe that the planning process is not more important that the plan itself, there is sufficient time to plan, they are involved in top-level IS planning, the CEO relies on them for input about IS, and the CEO perceives IS as important to remaining competitive. They least agree that they are involved in running the business and that they are active in a corporate strategy planning team. They do not, however, feel that they are satisfied with top-level IS planning efforts, that they receive sufficient resources for IS planning, or that the firm does a good job of linking IS and corporate plans. Despite the widely proclaimed importance of linking IS to corporate planning, it seems that firms fall short when it comes to involving IS in strategy formulation. The CIO is asked for input on how to implement strategic plans that other top level executives have made, rather than to help use IS to shape the firm's future.

It is not uncommon for CIOs to perceive that they are not included in running the business. Companies such as BankOne and Kmart, where CIOs are active members of the corporate planning team and where CIOs have become the top manager of various divisions are the exception rather than the rule.

Strategic planning is an incremental process rather than a one-time event. Strategy is influenced by a firm's internal and external environments, and planning is an ongoing top-level management activity. Because CIOs know more about information technology than other executives, they can help give their firms a competitive advantage by using IS. In today's rapidly changing marketplace, it no longer makes sense for IS to remain a support function. The findings that CEOs are generally satisfied with their CIOs may indicate a “good news/bad news” situation. The good news is that CIOs are expanding their focus beyond the boundaries of IS, and that firms are integrating IS into business planning. The bad news is that CEO satisfaction with CIOs may indicate complacency: CEOs may feel that IS has reached its full potential and are not looking for ways to integrate IS into the business.

## 6. Summary and conclusions

Findings indicate that CEOs are generally satisfied with their CIOs, yet their satisfaction is not strong. CIOs are perceived to be creative, knowledgeable about the business, and committed to achieving the goals of the firm. They also work well with other managers and are able to communicate effectively with CEOs. CIOs appear to be becoming what CEOs say they want them to be: technically competent managers with business savvy. CIOs believe that strategic IS plans are important to the firm and that the CEO wants to use IS to maintain a competitive advantage. They perceive themselves to be actively involved in top-level IS planning and in providing input to the CEO about IS opportunities and threats. They are also generally pleased with the amount of time they are given for planning, although they would like more resources.

Failure to find stronger opinions may reflect a lack of communication between the two executives. On one hand, CEOs may not understand the extent to which CIOs want to contribute to the firm. On the other hand, CIOs not be aware that CEOs expect them to be knowledgeable about the business. In some cases, a CEO may want the CIO to understand the goals of the business so that he/she can contribute to strategic planning when asked, but may not want the CIO to be continually involved in top-level activities. The CIO who has been encouraged to learn more about the business may believe that the CEO expects more CIO involvement in running the company than the CEO actually wants. Thus, we find a CEO who is satisfied with the CIO's top-level contributions, yet the CIO does not perceive himself/herself as highly involved in these activities.

## Appendix A

Questions used to Measure CEO Satisfaction with CIO

Our CIO/senior information systems manager:

1. Rarely exceeds his/her annual budget.

2. Is a good manager of his/her people.

3. Has good working relationships with other managers.

4. Speaks the same “language” I do.

5. Does a good job of separating the important tasks from the unimportant.

6. Understands the goals of our business.

7. Puts the needs of the firm above those of his/her area.

8. Is as open about problems in the IS area as about successes.

9. Is more likely to tell me why we can do what I want than tell me why we can't.

10. Discusses IS issues without “talking down” to me.

11. Keeps our system on the “cutting edge.”

12. Usually recommends only products that we really need.

13. Doesn't get involved in office politics.

14. Is a sharp, creative thinker.

15. Talks to me about our firm's future.

16. Completes projects on time.

Questions used to Measure CIO Perceptions of Top-Level IS Efforts

1. The CIO/senior IS manager:

1.1. Is actively involved in top-level MIS planning.

1.2. Frequently communicates with the CEO about top-level MIS plans.

1.3. Spends time educating the CEO about IS opportunities and threats.

1.4. Spends time learning about the firm's business.

1.5. Initiates many of the changes in top-level MIS plans.

1.6. Is an active member of the management team that sets corporate strategy.

2. Our CEO thinks IS plays an important role in our carrier's ability to compete.

3. Our CEO relies on the CIO/senior IS manager for information about MIS needs.

4. Our CEO involves the CIO/senior IS manager in running the business.

5. The CIO/senior IS manager is generally satisfied with our top-level MIS planning efforts.

6. We do a good job of linking our MIS plans to our corporate plans.

7. We receive the resources we need to carry out our top-level MIS plans.

8. It seems as though we are never given sufficient time to properly do MIS planning. $^{2}$

9. It often seems that the planning process is more important than the plan itself.

10. We have implemented at least part of the MIS plan.
