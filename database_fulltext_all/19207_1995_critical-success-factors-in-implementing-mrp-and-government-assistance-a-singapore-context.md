# Critical success factors in implementing MRP and government assistance: A Singapore context ☆

James S.K. Ang $^{a}$ , Chee-Chuong Sum $^{a}$ , Wah-Fook Chung $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Decision Sciences, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 0511, Singapore $^{b}$ Commercial Union Assurance Company plc, 24 Raffles Place #12-01 / 05 Clifford Centre, Singapore 0104, Singapore

## Abstract

It is generally acknowledged that Manufacturing Resource Planning (MRP) could revolutionize manufacturing operations. Making use of data from a recent survey of MRP practices in Singapore conducted jointly by the National Computer Board (NCB) and the National University of Singapore (NUS), this paper provides a profile of manufacturing companies in Singapore that both have and have not implemented MRP. The Critical Success Factors involved in implementing MRP are identified and the theoretical justification behind each factor examined. Finally, the ways in which the Singapore Government can help local companies implement, operate, and maintain MRP systems are discussed. It is an expensive investment, and difficult to implement, due to its complexity. It is hoped that knowledge and understanding of these factors will assist firms in successfully implementing MRP and enable them to further improve their systems in order to maximize returns.

Keywords: Critical success factors; Government assistance; MRP implementation; User satisfaction

## 1. Introduction

Two factors characterize today's manufacturing environment: intensifying global competition and rapid advancement of manufacturing technology [9]. Developing countries in Asia have made rapid inroads into Western markets, while first world countries such as Japan and America have rapidly adopted flexible manufacturing technology. In this volatile environment, manufacturers are confronted with the need to streamline and improve their operations constantly in order to compete favorably; i.e., automate, emigrate or evaporate.

With rising labor costs, many manufacturing companies in Singapore today face similar problems as those in first world countries. Foreign competitors from countries such as Malaysia and Indonesia, where labor is relatively cheap, are able to produce high-quality products at low cost. Some Singapore companies have shifted production in order to reduce cost (e.g., one local hardware firm, Aztech Computers, has recently set up manufacturing facilities in China). In fact, 5800 manufacturing jobs were lost in Singapore in 1992, mainly as a result of overseas relocation. However, this may not address the root of the problem, which is, more often than not, obsolete production philosophies, techniques, and technologies. Furthermore, shifting operations overseas may even result in technology transfer (this depends on whether the “recipient” learns or just performs “manual” jobs), and this can help create foreign competitors. This could be undesirable from the company’s viewpoint, and ultimately it can erode a nation’s competitive advantage.

Introducing new production methods to improve manufacturing operations can have an equally profitable result and better addresses the long term goals of a firm, since proprietary technologies and applications are then kept within the firm. Advanced manufacturing technologies involving microelectronics and computer technology to enhance manufacturing technology are key component of such improvement.

One key application is production planning and control. It usually encompasses three distinct activities. The first concerns the overall direction of the company, and involves developing a management plan stated in production terms (for example, end items). The second includes the detailed planning of material flows and operating capacity to support the overall production plan. Finally, the third involves the execution of the plans developed in terms of detailed shop schedules and purchasing actions.

Computer innovations have allowed traditional manual inventory control methods to be cost-effectively replaced by sophisticated computer-based production control systems. These do not simply automate manual systems but, because of their ability to handle much larger quantities of data and processing, make possible the use of new and better planning and control concepts and techniques. Manufacturing resource planning (MRP) is the most widely used and successful computer-based production and inventory control system

## 1.1. Manufacturing resource planning (MRP)

Today, there is a wide variety of tools and techniques available that will help manufacturing companies produce their products at a lower cost and higher quality. These include Just-In-Time, Computer Aided Design/Computer Aided Manufacturing (CAD/CAM) and Factory Automation. Intuitively, however, one realizes that it is really enough to be extremely efficient in producing goods that are of consistently high quality, if they are not really needed.

Many of the problems manufacturing companies in Singapore face today stem from just this source: top level plans are not being effectively translated into day-to-day tasks that people can evaluate and be accountable in execution. As a result, companies are not making the right things at the right time. The full potential that technology can bring to manufacturing will only be realized when it is coupled with an effective planning and scheduling system.

From its beginnings in the 60s as an inventory reordering tool, MRP has evolved into a method for effectively planning all the resources in a company. In its highest form (class A MRP), it links a variety of functions, including business planning, production planning, purchasing, inventory control, shop floor control, cost management, and capacity management. MRP also integrates operational planning (in units), and financial planning (in dollars) and has the ability, using simulation capability to answer “what if” questions [20].

The physical workings of an MRP system have been extensively documented in the operations management literature. Numerous “how-to” books exist. The benefits derived from a successful MRP system have also been well-documented. Surveys of MRP users $[15,17]$ have shown that a successful MRP system generates both tangible and intangible benefits. Tangible ones include better inventory and raw material control, reduced clerical support, and reduced lead times, while improved communication and better integration of planning have been cited as examples of intangible benefits.

While computer hardware and software are essential elements of MRP, it is important to realize that it is not just a computer system. Many of the determinants of its success (like handling initial resistance to change and degree of organizational commitment) are directly related to the human factor $[14,18]$ . Implementing an MRP system involves major changes to the formal and informal systems within the company.

## 1.2. Critical success factors

Critical Success Factors (CSFs) can be defined as those that determine success or failure for a business unit or organization [12]. They represent those areas that management must carefully consider in order to bring about continued good performance.

The prospect of identifying and concentrating on a limited number of important priorities is obviously very attractive in implementing complex information systems. Key managers' attention can be focused on the most important aspects of the implementation. Teamwork is enhanced by working towards similar goals.

## 1.3. Objective

The objective of this paper is to identify the CSFs in the MRP implementation efforts of local Singapore companies. Knowledge and understanding of these factors will assist local firms in successfully implementing MRP, and also enable those that use some form of MRP to further improve their systems and maximize returns. The ways in which the Government can help companies implement, operate, and maintain MRP systems is also discussed.

While a formal scheme for classifying MRP systems has long existed $[19]$ , its definition still differs widely among practitioners. Small companies that participated in the survey reported using a full-fledged MRP II system, while some big companies did not even think their system have achieved class A or B MRP. As a result, it is not meaningful to separate the different MRP classes. Thus here the term “MRP” refers to all versions of MRP.

## 2. Literature review-implementing MRP

Implementing an MRP system is not easy. A large literature exists on difficulties in its implementation $[3,6]$ . MRP typically involves a very steep learning curve, and considerable time before full use, perhaps up to 24 months $[16]$ . In some well-documented cases, spectacular results have been achieved $[2,4]$ . Failures on the other hand, are disruptive and expensive, and account for as much as 75% of all systems. What is more, failures are much less extensively documented. As a result, pitfalls to be avoided tend to be less well known. When one considers that it does not cost more to be a class A company as per Wright classification, the importance of the correct implementation method becomes apparent.

Singapore companies are just being starting to introduce MRP concepts. They started applying MRP in the early 80s. Operating in the turbulent and competitive business environment of the 90s, we neither have the luxury of time, nor the resources to absorb implementation mistakes. Identifying CSFs relevant to local manufacturing is one way to increase the chances of a successful local implementation.

Though Burns et al. [5] identified the CSFs involved in implementing MRP systems in 238 companies in the south-eastern United States, they were really studying were the determinants of MRP II success. They divided the factors into two categories: environmental and implementation.

Instead of using their list as a benchmark, we met with several local academics, consultants, and practitioners to seek their assistance in identifying what they perceived to be factors that are important to the success of an MRP implementation in Singapore. The final list of 13 factors include both human as well as technical factors. They are:

1. Top management support

2. Clear goals and objectives

3. Inter-department cooperation

4. Inter-department communication

5. Visibility of implementation

6. Staff training and education

7. Staff motivation and commitment

8. Staff's level of MRP knowledge prior to implementation

9. Vendor knowledge of MRP

10. Vendor Support

11. Suitability of hardware/software

12. Data accuracy and integrity

13. Company expertise in IT

## 3. Survey methodology

Most MRP studies have been case studies (e.g., [11,13]). These usually focus on successful implementations. Failures are seldom documented, probably since it would be embarrassing for the companies. Moreover, case studies are context-sensitive. The lessons may not be applicable in other instances.

Empirical research has been limited. Notable surveys were conducted by Oliver Wright Company and Associates [8] and by APICS (American Production and Inventory Control Society) and the University of Minnesota Center for Operations Research [1]. The results from surveys converge in several areas. First, a properly implemented MRP system does improve operations significantly, for example, by reducing inventory levels and production lead time. However, most MRP users have not been able to realize the full potential of their systems. Education and top management involvement are also important in surveys.

The survey reported here was prompted by the growing interest of the local manufacturing industry in using MRP to manage and improve their companies' operations. Conducted jointly by the National Computer Board (NCB) and the National University of Singapore (NUS), the survey aimed to reach as many MRP companies as possible: hence, mail surveys were used.

While the exact number of companies having at least basic MRP modules in Singapore is not known, discussions with consultants and vendors suggest that the number is around 150, reflecting its infancy in Singapore. The sample for the study was compiled from various sources knowledgeable about the current users. These include government agencies, manufacturing associations, directories, vendors, etc. Due to the confidentiality agreements between the consultants and vendors, questionnaires were sent to both consultants and vendors, to be redirected to their clients. Hence it is not known if some of their clients were already on the mailing list. Also, the actual number of questionnaires sent out by each consultant or vendor is not known. As a result of this, there may be some duplication in the responses. The exact number of companies who received the questionnaire is not known, 750 being a reasonable estimate.

The survey questionnaire was pre-tested by practitioners, consultants, and academics before it was mailed to participating companies. Care was taken to ensure that each question was clear and easily understandable and that the definitions of potentially ambiguous terms (for example, MRP, may mean different things to different people) and special technical terms (for example, pegging) were included in a glossary in the questionnaire.

Data were collected on the following topics:

1. The fundamental characteristics of the company: fixed production assets, number of employees, products manufactured, and manufacturing process used.

2. MRP system features: the hardware and software, the amount of financial investment, and the degree of computerization of each of the major MRP modules.

3. MRP benefits and costs: concrete goals for the MRP system prior to its implementation.

4. MRP implementation: the actual implementation process, including the initiator of MRP in the company, the source of expertise and information, the problems encountered, and, of particular interest, the success factors involved.

5. Non-implementors: data on companies without MRP.

The questionnaire was completed either by a materials manager, a production and inventory control manager, a master scheduler, a MIS manager, or a production manager. Thus, the study is comprehensive in terms of sample size, and the breadth and depth of the survey instrument.

There were 128 responses, resulting in a response rate of 17%; 59 of the respondents had implemented MRP systems, which represents a significant proportion (39%) of the MRP users. It compares favorably with the survey conducted in 1987 by Yeo et al., which had a total of 26 respondents, and a response rate of 29%.

The measuring instrument for success factors was a five-point Likert scale. Respondents were asked to identify the degree of importance of each of the thirteen previously identified success factors and to record any additional factors they considered relevant in their case.

## 4. Survey findings

The major benefits that companies obtained from their MRP system included better delivery dates, better production scheduling, and better ability to meet volume and product changes.

However, an MRP system is expensive, as shown in Fig. 1. 42.9% estimated that they have invested more than S\$500,000 into the system. 44.7% forecast substantial additional investments over the next three years of at least S\$100,000. On the other hand, companies that have not yet implemented MRP do not seem to be aware of the high costs. The vast majority (89.4%) do not expect to spend more than S\$200,000 for an MRP system! Not a single non-MRP company is willing to spend more than half a million dollars, which suggest that a major information gap exists, especially for the 20.9% of non-MRP companies that intend to implement it in the near future.

A measure of MRP implementation success (i.e., perceived user satisfaction) was also collected. While this is a subjective measure, it is generally accepted as a valid indicator. Respondents were asked to indicate how satisfied (score “1” for “low”, ..., “5” for “high”) the company was with the present system. The mean score was 3.33. Most companies (86%) gave their system a 3 or 4 ranking. The results, presented in Fig. 2, suggested that improvement in operations had been observed, and that companies were relatively happy with their MRP systems.

Data were also gathered on companies that have not implemented MRP. Respondents answered a separate portion of the questionnaire. Lack of company expertise in MRP emerged as the main reason for non-MRP companies not implementing it.

It appears that top management support is commonly found in local MRP implementation. In fact, top management played a major role in introducing MRP in 67.8% of the companies. 47.4% of MRP companies set up a formal steering committee to oversee the implementation.

Companies seem to realize that employees contributed greatly to the success of the system. Because it is a recent introduction, there is insufficient knowledge of MRP within most companies. Many respondents cited lack of internal expertise as a significant problem. At present, however, there seems to be a shortage of avenues (for example, consultants, vendor, or Government bodies) to obtain information about MRP.

Apparently there is a pool of qualified vendors offering quality MRP modules locally. Problems encountered could be due to companies not being familiar with MRP technology and purchasing modules not suitable for their organization.

Staff's level of MRP knowledge before implementation can be effectively overcome by a properly designed education program. Since most companies only recently implemented MRP, they may not yet have reached the stage where they have to perform substantial maintenance and upgrades to the initial system, a stage where vendor knowledge becomes extremely important. This would explain the low need for vendor knowledge.

Data accuracy aside, the most important success factors are human rather than technical. This seems to support the contention that human factors are the important when implementing advanced technologies.

With close cooperation between the public and private sectors in Singapore, and the Government's proven track record of leading the economy, it is not surprising that the bulk of the survey respondents expect the government to take the lead in introducing MRP into local industry.

In fact, the infrastructure necessary for any government assistance is already in place; since the beginning of the 80s, the Economic Development Board (EDB) and the National Computer Board (NCB) have been actively promoting the use of IT as a critical tool in support of a company's operations, especially manufacturing and business support IT applications.

## 5. Conclusion

MRP is relatively new in Singapore. However, the local manufacturing sector is really only at the beginning of its learning. Just like for any other large scale systems implementation, failure rates are relatively high; often, however, this is due to mistakes in implementation. The critical success factors approach can perhaps provide a path for the MRP implementor.

It must be emphasised that this is a descriptive paper. Only the most basic statistical instrument, the mean, has been used. Even so, certain factors, like data accuracy, top management support, and employee motivation, stood out as being critical in local implementation. Hopefully, this paper can serve as a useful guide for the manager who is in the process of implementing MRP.
