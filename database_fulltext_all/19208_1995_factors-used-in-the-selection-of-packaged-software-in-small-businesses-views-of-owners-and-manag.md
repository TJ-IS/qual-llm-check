# Factors used in the selection of packaged software in small businesses: Views of owners and managers

Patrick Y.K. Chau

Department of Information and Systems Management, School of Business and Management, The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong

## Abstract

Due to their limited human, technical, and financial resources, small firms usually purchase packaged software to meet their organizational needs for IS. The selection is usually made either by the owner or a manager. A key question is whether they use the same set of criteria. This study examines the question, and finds that owners and managers of small businesses have different views of the importance of the factors in their selection decision. Owners tend to take a more strategic view of software package selection, weigh technical factors more heavily, take more factors into consideration, and consider those factors in a more serious manner than managers. Therefore, different frameworks may be needed in the software package selection process by owners and managers of small businesses.

Keywords: Packaged software; Selection criteria; Small businesses

## 1. Introduction

It has been argued that, like large corporations, small business organizational performance can benefit from computer-based information systems (CBIS) and small firms should use CBIS throughout their organizations [21]. Compared to large organizations, although small businesses have been found to be lacking in expertise with technology, software, and experienced in-house IS/EDP resources [5,6], firms with more CBIS perform better [1,3] and can increase their competitiveness with larger businesses [17].

A number of studies have been conducted on the general use of computers by small businesses [10,18]. The surveys investigated applications, benefits, and problems of using computers. Other studies have extended findings in large organizations to smaller firms, and have attempted to define the key factors contributing to the success of CBIS in small businesses. Important studies (e.g. [23,24]) have examined the relationship between organizational factors and CBIS success in small firms. It was found that the proportion of internally developed applications, presence of in-house processing capability, number of administrative and on-line applications installed, and the organizational level at which their CBIS function was placed were important factors of CBIS success. A survey of 56 small businesses [15] found that user satisfaction and the use of CBIS increased when formal systems analysis, design and implementation procedures, internal technical personnel and/or hired consultants, and extensive vendor services were employed. Montazemi [22] examined the relationship

between organizational characteristics and end-user satisfaction in small businesses and concluded that the number of internal IS professionals, the level of user involvement, the intensity of information requirement analysis, the level of computer literacy, the proportion of interactive application systems, and the degree of decentralization were factors important to end-user satisfaction. In another study of 93 manufacturing firms [8], experience with computers, ability to plan and control computer use, management knowledge and involvement, personal acceptance of computers, use of external support, level of training, and type of computer use were found to be factors affecting the success of IS in the small business environment. More recently, an empirical study of key factors associated with CBIS success in small businesses was conducted by Yap et al. [28]. Their main findings were that a combination of IS experience and knowledge about small business was essential to the CBIS success in small businesses. In particular, the significant factors were consultant effectiveness, vendor support, IS experience, sufficiency of financial resources, CEO support, and user participation.

While the findings of the above studies are important to success in small businesses, none of the studies have looked at how the CBIS was selected and what factors affected the decision. Due to their limited human, technical, and financial resources [7], small firms usually purchase packaged software, such as accounting and inventory control packages, and the amount of investment involved is relatively small. The owner or the manager (of the department in which the packaged software is intended to be used) always plays a preponderant role for collecting relevant information, talking to potential software vendors, doing the software selection analysis, and finally making the software selection decision [25]. Traditional capital budgeting techniques, such as net present value, internal rate of return, cost benefit ratio, and payback period, are generally not used during evaluation of their purchase. Instead, descriptive variables, such as those related to the software, the vendor, and the opinions given by other concerned parties, may be important factors in the owner's/manager's mind while choosing the packaged software [2]. Many studies have suggested that the success of CBIS is dependent on the owner's or the manager's involvement in the development of computing in the organization [4,9,13,19,20]. Therefore, a key question is whether or not the owner and the manager uses the same set of criteria when they select the package. Our research examines whether different factors are used by owners of and managers in small businesses. The study concentrates on the selection of software because small businesses often have only limited resources available for using computers and are unlikely to develop applications on their own. They tend to use their computers mostly for accounting and word processing [17], and rely more on packaged software than the larger organizations do [11,14].

## 2. Factors used in the selection of packaged software

Selecting the right software application package is becoming more important. The biggest advantage of purchasing software is that it provides economies of scale, while reducing the risk of implementation [26]. Although formal procedures designed solely for ensuring the proper fit of the application package to the organization's problem do not exist, the literature does provide guidelines for selection of software packages [27]. For example, the following four steps are generally found in any selection process: (1) study the existing needs; (2) have the vendors demonstrate possible solution; (3) contact other users of the package; and (4) interview some of the future users. The main purpose of this process is to maximize the fit of the package to the application with minimal modification.

Many studies have described the difficulties faced by small businesses in adopting packaged software. One potential difficulty is the lack of in-house IS/EDP resources; small businesses generally do not have any formal selection process. Prior research has suggested many factors that may be important in the owner's/manager's mind with regard to the choice of the packaged software. These can be categorized into three main groups: software, vendor, and the opinions of other concerned parties. In each group, items can also be sub-divided into technical or non-technical nature. Table 1 lists the 21 items identified from the literature and used in this study.

## 3. Methodology

To determine if different factors are used by owners and managers of small businesses when selecting packaged software, a structured questionnaire was developed and sent to 500 small manufacturing businesses in Hong Kong. Small business is defined here as an establishment with less than 50 employees. Based on this definition, about 95% of the manufacturing establishments in Hong Kong in 1991 were small businesses [12]. The addresses were randomly selected from the Members' Directory of the Federation of Hong Kong Industries 1991/92. The questionnaire was sent with a covering letter explaining the study and addressed to the “responsible person” listed in the directory. In the covering letter, this person was asked to direct the questionnaire to the person who was responsible for the company's software selection decision in the most recent packaged software purchase if he or she was not the one and provided that the organization had purchased some packaged software in the past twelve months. 122 usable responses were received (a 24.4% response rate); of these, 68 had purchased software in the past 12 months. This sample was used for analysis.

## 4. Results and discussion

Using a 5-point Likert scale, with 5 being “extremely important”, 3 being “important”, to 1 being “not important at all”, average scores of the 21 factors of the two groups (owners and managers) were computed. In the “owners” group, the scores range from 2.72 to 4.62, while in the “managers” group, the scores range from 2.62 to 4.52. Although the ranges are quite similar, detailed data analysis reveals significant differences between the views of the two groups.

## 4.1. Factors related to the packaged software

As shown in Table 3, both groups generally showed factors related to the technical side of the software package important. All the average scores were greater than 3.00. Among the four factors in this group, the owners treated the hardware/software integration issue as the most important (average score: 4.19) whilst the managers viewed compatibility with existing hardware/software most important (average score: 3.62). Relatively speaking, the integration issue can be regarded as strategic and the compatibility issue as tactical/operational. Therefore, this difference is interesting as it indicates that the owners take a relatively more strategic view of the software package selection than the managers. Two-sample t-tests further showed that the differences between the two groups with regard to these two factors were statistically significant at the 0.05 and 0.001 levels respectively.

The ease of use/user-friendliness of the software package was viewed as an important factor by both groups (owners: 3.98; managers: 3.48). This was not surprising. First, most software packages used in small businesses are accounting or word processing-related. Second, most users generally are not regarded as “sophisticated”. Third, in-house technical support is generally inadequate in small firms.

Both groups, but particularly the owners, rated the availability of the source code of the software as important. This was rather unexpected. To a large extent, the availability of source code is needed for future maintenance, modification, or upgrade of the software package to meet a changing need. Small firms seldom perform those tasks in-house. Concern about obtaining external assistance might explain this high rating. Even though the owners (or the managers) understand that they might not have enough in-house expertise to perform those tasks, they might obtain assistance externally.

The two groups diverged in the way they viewed the importance of price and popularity of the software package. The owners indicated that these two factors were somewhat less important while the managers rated them as “very important”. Both differences were statistically significant at the 0.001 level. Apparently, managers tend to emphasize those factors that would “safeguard” their decisions.

Thus, owners and managers have very different views in assessing the suitability of the software package to their organizations.

## 4.2. Factors related to the vendor

The literature suggests that small businesses generally lack in-house IS/EDP experts and generally rely on the vendor to provide technical support $[16]$ . Both owners and managers rated the importance of vendor support when selecting packaged software as of the highest importance. (In fact, it was the most important factor of both groups among the 21 factors). Also, both groups shared similar views of the importance of user training provided and technical skills during product demonstrations.

Gaining information regarding experiences of (current) users of the software package, as obtained from other people or from experience of using other products developed by the same vendor, was seen as important.

When asked to evaluate the importance of factors related to the non-technical side of the vendor, the owners consistently rated the four factors (reputation, business skills, references, and past business experience with the vendor) more highly than did the managers. The largest difference was their views on the importance of past business experience with the vendor.

In summary, owners considered factors related to the vendor more seriously than did the managers.

## 4.3. Factors related to opinions

Among the four “technical” sources of opinions/advice on different software packages, the owners rated in-house IS/EDP experts as the most important, followed by external consultants and potential vendors/sales representatives. Computer/IS trade magazines or software product leaflets, though still considered useful, were rated as the least important technical source by the owners. On the other hand, the managers gave equal importance to the opinions given by in-house experts, external consultants, and potential vendors.

Other than getting opinions/advice from technical experts, the owners/managers may seek suggestions from their subordinates, end-users of the packaged software, and outside personal acquaintances.

The findings suggest that both owners and managers put a higher weight on the opinions given by internal sources, including in-house experts (if available), and end-users than those by external sources.

## 4.4. Overall ratings between owners and managers

A closer examination of the 21 factors reveals that the owners rated 16 of them higher than did the managers. This seemed to indicate that the owners took the selection decision more seriously: they tended to take more factors into consideration and consider those factors more seriously.

Of the five factors that the managers rated higher than did the owners, the price and the popularity of the software were the only two showing significant differences.

## 5. Conclusions

This study has found that owners and managers of small businesses did have different views on the importance of factors related to their selection decision. Key differences are:

\- the owners took a more strategic view on software package selection than the managers;

\- they considered the factors related to the technical side of the software package much more important than those related to the non-technical side; the managers took the opposite view;

\- the price and the popularity of the software package were considered the least important factors by owners but they were considered among the most important by managers;

\- the owners took significantly more serious consideration of the vendors than did the managers, particularly with their business references and any past experience with them;

\- the owners rated opinions given by in-house IS/EDP experts most important, followed by external consultants, and potential vendors/sales representatives; the managers, however, gave equal importance to their advice;

\- the two groups diverged in their view on the importance of opinions from subordinates and outside personal acquaintance: the owners rated subordinates' opinions higher;

• overall, the owners put more emphasis on factors of a technical nature whilst the managers weighed factors of a non-technical nature more heavily.

Thus, the study has found that even within the context of small business; as the owners and the managers may have very different views on criteria used in the software package selection process, two different frameworks may be necessary. The owner should be aware that if he or she delegates the responsibility of selecting packaged software to managers, they may use a different set of criteria in the evaluation. Also, computer/IS firms may need to use different marketing strategies when dealing with customers from small firms.

There are at least three limitations to this study. First, the data was collected using a mail questionnaire aimed at one industry (manufacturing) in a particular geographic location (Hong Kong). Whether the results can be extended to small business in other industries is a matter of speculation. Also, as Hong Kong is mainly a “Chinese region”, owners/managers of small businesses in another geographic location may look at the issue differently, owing to a different cultural background.

Another limitation is the lack of a theoretical model that involved variables to measure the success of the process. Thus, one could not determine whether the owners' or the managers' selection criteria was better.

Third, the study did not look into such characteristics as IS/computer knowledge and experience in using/purchasing packaged software. A review of the respondents' backgrounds showed that although respondents of both groups received similar amount of IS/computer training, the owners did have significantly more experience in using/purchasing packaged software than the managers.

Computerization is a process that begins with awareness of needs and culminates in actual implementation of a computer system. Small businesses usually purchase packaged software, thus factors affecting the decision of the package of choice is very important to them, in acquiring what they need. Previous research has emphasized the factors affecting the success of implementation, and has neglected the relationship between the decision maker and the choice of package. The question investigated in this study is not an isolated issue but closely relates to others such as CBIS success in small businesses and CBIS growth behaviour in small firms.
