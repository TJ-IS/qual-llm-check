# Applications

# Application of a matrix approach to estimate project skill requirements

Anthony C. Nelson $^{a,*}$ , Kailash Joshi $^{b}$

$^{a}$ University of South Florida, 140 Seventh Avenue South, St. Petersburg, FL 33701, USA $^{b}$ School of Business Administration, University of Missouri, 8001 Natural Bridge Road, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA

## Abstract

The first step in starting an IS project should be to determine the skills that it demands. After this, an analyst whose skill set matches the needs profile can be selected to lead it. This paper specifically focuses on developing an approach to assist IS managers in determining the lead analyst skills that are best for a specific project. The approach and its application to a real project in a large sales organization is presented. Finally, the usefulness and limitations of the approach are discussed.

Keywords: Skills; Training; Project management; Skill assessment; Personnel

## 1. Introduction

According to a recent survey, project management is a critical issue for IS managers $[39]$ . One key need within project management is to assign a systems analyst to lead the development of a new system. Due to downsizing, many organizations face this problem with a smaller pool of lead analysts. Specifying the right skills is therefore a crucial issue facing IS management. In their study of failed systems projects, Walsh and Kanter report that a lack of the right skills at the right time was a major reason for failure $[41]$ .

## 2. The matrix approach

The special skills that are required for a specific project should be determined before taking any staffing actions.

## 2.1. IS project characteristics

It has been noted that different design approaches may be appropriate for different projects. For example, prototyping and life cycle approaches are possible alternatives and their appropriateness will depend on the uncertainty and complexity of the project [6].

Therefore, we have identified core project characteristics that are useful in the assessment of the overall skill requirements. However, while IS projects can vary along many dimensions, we have attempted to identify a parsimonious set of basic characteristics.

## Number of users

Number of users contributes to project complexity $[25,26]$ . There are three categories of users: a few in one department; many in one department; and some in many departments. If many users are involved, there must be people skills. Moreover, if users come from different departmental units, political skills are particularly important because of the potential conflict on the information requirements. Interaction between units also requires skills in organizational understanding and the complex reporting relationships and communication patterns. On the other hand, if a Decision Support System (DSS) is being designed with very few users, then people, political, and organization structure skills may not be so significant.

## User task comprehension and knowledge

User understanding of tasks and applications are factors that influence the uncertainty associated with a project $[7,32,1,19]$ . We consider three categories for user knowledge: novice, intermediate, and expert. The lack of user understanding may need an analyst with high skills in organizational structure and the specific business tasks. The analysts may have to interpret vague suggestions and nebulous ideas and use the skills to independently develop a system solution. However, when users are knowledgeable and know what they want, they may take the lead in specifying the solution; then the analyst need not be so highly skilled in business and organizational structure.

## Degree of structure

We shall consider, three degrees of project structure: fully, moderately, and unstructured. If a system is well structured, the development tasks are relatively well defined; the scope and definition and user requirements are also clearly understood [7]. Furthermore, the project specifications are unlikely to change significantly with time (e.g., a payroll system).

On the other hand, information requirements for unstructured projects are difficult to define. Furthermore, the design may be subject to frequent changes. In such an environment, analysts' skills become vital for successful design and implementation of the system.

## Degree of change

A new or revised IS introduces a certain degree of change in the users' work environment. Such changes can be viewed as incremental (slight), moderate, or radical [8]. When they are incremental (e.g., improvement in a report) no significant user resistance may be encountered. However, when the change is radical, significant resistance may be faced [9,23]. Whether a change is incremental or radical will have significant implications for user adoption.

Another characteristic of change is compatibility [10,34]; this is the degree to which a new or modified IS is perceived as consistent with the old system.

When the degree of change is high, significant people and political skills may be needed to persuade users to cooperate in the change process $[22,28,38]$ . Moreover, knowledge of structure and business functions may be needed.

## Project size

Project size also may be relevant in the determination of analyst skills for a project. The project size (small, medium, or large) must be taken into consideration. Large projects are inherently more complex and generally involve more subsystems and job positions. A successful developer must be able to understand the interrelationships between these subsystems and job positions. Therefore, systems development task and organization structure skills are needed for such projects.

## Degree of integration

The degree of data integration (low, medium, high) among various (sub)systems is another important dimension. When the IS involves the integration of data stored in several locations and formats, a higher level of technical skill is generally required. Systems that attempt to integrate the activities of different functional units are also likely to demand higher technical skills and business knowledge.

## Other dimensions

Other dimensions include the type of system: transaction processing (TP), management reporting and control (MRC), or decision support (DSS). However, we feel that characteristics defined by the project type are already reflected in the other five dimensions (e.g., a TPS generally has multiple users and is fairly structured, while a DSS has fewer users and is usually less structured).

An organization may prefer to use additional project characteristics that are considered important. Although we have identified a core set of characteristics based primarily upon the literature, the approach in this paper readily incorporates additional project dimensions.

## 2.2. Lead analyst skills

The organizational system consists of several components: organization structure; politics; task; technology; and people $[21,4,34]$ . As analysts develop systems, they come into contact with and affect the social components of the organization $[5,29]$ . As agents of change, they fill the technical and social gap and provide a communication link between the MIS function and the user organization.

## Organization structure skills

Organization structure pertains to the broad based objectives, goals and policies, the formal authority and reporting relationships, and the system of communication [40]. It also refers to the corporate environment, such as growth rates, revenues, corporate culture, management style, and competitors [29].

IS often alter the culture, relationships and patterns of communication, authority, and control [12,18,19,27,32,37]. Analysts must possess skills in this area.

## Political skills

The development of an IS can be viewed from a political perspective [35]. Organizational politics refers to the degree of conflict, power, existing coalitions and resistance that may affect the development of the proposed IS. Politics in IS development may cause behavioral problems. Several issues may complicate the development effort, such as, multiplicity of goals, unpredictability of uses, unstructured relationships, and lack of clear criteria for evaluation. Proposed changes to the amount of control, the division of labor, the distribution of power, the organizational culture and goals, autonomy, or evaluation regarding personnel may be received with extreme resistance [16].

As a result, an effective analyst must possess knowledge about the informal structure of the organization. Skills such as mediation, selling, persuasion, or coercion are important to resolve key issues.

## Task skills

Task skills involve knowledge about local work procedures and specific policies and procedures of systems development.

## Business task skills

These assume a general knowledge of the production of goods and services within the group, department, or organization. They also include an understanding of the objectives, procedures, and problems within the work unit. The analyst must interpret and provide such information to the technical people $[11,31]$ .

Several authors stress the need for analysts to combine technical skills with business knowledge; indeed this has been discussed since the early sixties, in conjunction that analysts too often do not have such skills.

## Systems development task skills

Systems development task skills require knowledge of systems analysis and design, problem solving, IS policies and guidelines, and project management techniques. This also includes a general understanding of existing IS and how they are used in the business unit.

## Technology skills

Technology skills include the knowledge surrounding the machines, computer hardware and software required to develop and implement the new or modified IS. As change agents, analysts introduce innovations in the form of new technology to the business units. Although information processing executives believe that less emphasis will be placed on technical skills $[2]$ , both technical and social skills are necessary for the analyst $[24]$ .

## People skills

People skills help analysts to interact with individuals involved with the IS development project. In general, they include experience in interpersonal behavior, interpersonal communication and group dynamics as well as special human behavior in organizations, such as absenteeism, turnover, conflict, training, attitudes and productivity.

Effective analysts have the ability to ask questions and avoid misunderstandings during user dialogue $[15]$ . The analyst must also round-off these skills with a bent towards human relations $[3,13,16]$ .

## 2.3. Matrix approach

Here we describe a matrix approach for assessing skill requirements from the IS project characteristics. It is presented in Table 1.

First, the IS manager must specify the detailed project dimensions by selecting one of the options under each project characteristic. The six systems analyst skill categories are listed at the top. The extent to which each skill is needed for each project characteristic must be estimated as either essential (2), desirable (1), or unnecessary (0) for each project characteristic.

The individual scores for each skill category are summed to derive an overall score, which represents estimated skill requirements for the project. The IS manager can use these to find and select the most suitable analyst.

The manager in charge of systems development was commissioned to develop an IS to rectify these problems. The new IS would need to provide contact information about potential customers, and to help monitor customer sales and inventory activity. The project team consisted of members who were responsible for hardware procurement, programming, help desk management, communications, and training.

The IS development manager had to select a lead analyst who would oversee and coordinate the development effort. Prior to making the selection, several necessary skills were identified. These were: technical skills to evaluate software and hardware purchased from vendors. Systems development skills to identify and document information requirements, and project administration skills to estimate, track, and coordinate project activities.

A lead analyst was selected. However, political and interpersonal problems, erupted between the project leader, the users, and technical personnel. As a result, the project leader had to be replaced.

The author asked the IS development manager to estimate the skill requirements for the same project using the skill matrix approach. A written description of the project characteristics, the analyst skills, and the overall skill matrix approach was provided prior to the meeting. During the completion of the matrix, the author recorded the scores and rationale of the manager. The matrix (Table 2) was completed in approximately twenty-five minutes.

Number of users. The project involved interacting with many computer illiterate sales representatives in remote locations. In order to create a cordial working environment and handle sometimes contradictory requests, political and interpersonal skills were essential. Organization structure skills were believed to be desirable to identify and understand the reporting relationships among the sales representatives and the sales managers.

User knowledge. The sales representatives were very knowledgeable about their business tasks and could provide the lead analyst with general business requirements. Therefore, it was not necessary for the lead analyst to be highly proficient in the business function. However, it was somewhat desirable that the lead analyst be able to interact with these sales experts to learn about their organization structure and business tasks. As a result, organization structure and business task knowledge were seen as desirable. On the other hand, the sales representatives were not proficient in the area of systems development. They were changing from an entirely manual system to a computerized system and were generally computer illiterate. Therefore, systems development task and technology skills were essential for the lead analyst, while people skills were rated desirable, in order to interact with and assist them.

Project structure. Although sales personnel requested an automated system, they could not specify detailed information requirements. Thus, systems development task skills were extremely important to identify requirements, etc. and keep the project from “creeping” beyond its initial scope. Political skills were seen as essential to handle the potential conflict surrounding user requirements and alternative solutions of technical personnel. Interpersonal skills were necessary to handle the many meetings and to coordinate the efforts of various team members plus in obtaining the requirements from sales personnel.

Degree of change. The project involved a radical change from a completely manual system to an automated IS. However, it was difficult to determine whether the new IS would radically change the existing procedures, values, and culture. Furthermore, it was not obvious whether the technical solutions would represent a radical change for the technical people. Therefore, political, systems development task, technology, and people skills were rated essential.

Project size. The project was very unstructured, and therefore, had the potential to increase in size. Thus project management skills were seen as essential. Also, people skills were again rated essential.

Data integration. The IS manager did not think that the project would interact with other major systems. However, much information had to be shared between job positions; e.g., sales personnel needed to update and access information located at district offices. Because of the networking issues, the IS manager rated technology skills desirable.

Added project characteristic. The IS manager found it necessary to consider the number of IS team members. These people were also somewhat diverse in skills and experience. Team members consisted of a help desk supervisor and two help desk staff members; a communications expert; hardware procurement personnel; three trainers; a junior level programmer, and a steering committee of eight to ten individuals. Because of these facts the IS manager rated people skills as essential. Political skills were also needed (essential) to handle the potential disagreements and resistance among the coalitions: mediation and selling skills would be necessary.

People (i.e., interpersonal) skills received the highest ranking, while political and systems development task skills came second.

## 4. Conclusion

This paper has presented an approach to estimate the lead analyst skills which are required for a project. We have included a case study showing its use by an IS manager to a real life project. The manager felt that the matrix was a very useful way to determine project skill requirements. The manager indicated that the initial approach used to identify the skill requirements was unstructured. The matrix approach, on the other hand, provided a more methodical and rational approach.

After completing the matrix, the IS manager indicated that the results could be used to make “conscious tradeoffs” between candidates with different strengths; i.e., the completed matrix provided the manager with a means to trade off the skills of potential available lead analyst.

Also, the matrix approach identified skill areas in which a candidate may have to be trained.

The IS manager indicated that the new lead analyst was interacting effectively with the users and project team members, being a much better match than the old analyst, given the characteristics of the project.

During the application of the matrix approach a potential limitation was noted by the IS manager: The project characteristics of some projects may initially be unclear and this may make it difficult to complete the matrix. However, as the project scope becomes better defined, the matrix can be used.

A project characteristic was added to the list: the number of IS personnel involved is likely to have an impact on the skills necessary for the project.

Anthony C. Nelson is an Assistant Professor of MIS with the University of South Florida in St. Petersburg and Tampa. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Pittsburgh's Katz Graduate School of Business in 1991, and B.S. in Management from North Carolina A and T State University in 1980. He has held IS positions with several large manufacturing and banking firms, and has performed consulting work for city governments and small

businesses. His current research interests include IS personnel issues, CASE technology impacts, and IT implementation and diffusion. His papers have appeared or are forthcoming in the Journal of Information Technology Management, Interfaces, and Information and Management.
