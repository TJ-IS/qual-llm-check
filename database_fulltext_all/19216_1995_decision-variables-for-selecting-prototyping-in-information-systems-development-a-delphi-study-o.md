# Decision variables for selecting prototyping in information systems development: a Delphi study of MIS managers

E.R. Doke \*, N.E. Swanson

Department of Computer Information Systems, College of Business Administration, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, MO 65804-0095, USA

## Abstract

The use of prototyping in the information systems development process is frequently advocated by both practitioners and academics; however, the decision variables that must be assessed when prototyping is considered have not been well defined. A literature review produced nineteen variables that potentially matter. Then a Delphi panel of MIS managers from Computerworld's Premier 100 firms, who are currently using prototyping, identified and ranked variables they consider when deciding whether to include prototyping in their systems development project. After three Delphi rounds, ten important decision variables emerged as most important, including one added during the process by the panel. Seven variables frequently cited in the literature were ranked low and hence are considered unimportant.

Keywords: Prototype; Prototyping decision variables; Information systems development

## 1. Introduction

Prototyping of IS development projects has become very popular. This is due to several factors including the need to improve systems development efficiency and effectiveness, increasing user influence in these projects, wider availability of tools, and the success stories being reported $[2,6,8,13,17,19,20,22,23,33]$ . However, the literature does not state when prototyping should be employed.

The MIS manager's objective in IS development is to identify a project's goals, environment, and alternate development strategies, then to evaluate the alternatives and thus select the approach that will best deliver the system. This is a complex problem that influences the procedures and work styles of everyone involved. Also, various people involved have different perceptions of needs and are naturally biased toward familiar approaches [7,10,34,43].

An early objective of the study was to establish an appropriate definition. The following was synthesized from the literature and reviewed by several MIS managers and then revised:

Prototyping is a process that produces a model of a system or part of a system and requires user involvement. The prototyping process quickly creates a working model for user interaction and evaluation.

The primary purpose of this research is to answer the following questions:

1. What variables are used by MIS managers when deciding to include prototyping in a systems development project, and what is their relative importance?

2. How much agreement is there among MIS managers about the relative importance of these variables?

3. Which of the stated prototyping decision variables are not considered important by MIS managers?

## 2. Background

The research literature suggests that there are three categories of prototyping variables: (1) system and project properties, (2) developer and user attributes, and (3) resource availability.

## 2.1. System and project properties

Project risks are a concern during IS development. They involve the uncertainty about meeting installation and budget targets, attaining system objectives, and gaining user acceptance of the new system. These risks increase with the complexity of systems requirements, project size, project time frame, and unclear project goals. In addition, the type of system being developed influences risk. Generally, the literature suggests that prototyping be used to minimize the effect of these variables.

The need for prototyping is also greater when requirements are detailed and complex. Complex system requirements is thought to influence the decision to prototype $[25,35,41]$ .

Project size, structure, and technology familiarity are obvious risk variables $[12,28,29]$ . Project size is defined by development time, cost, and number of people in the project team [e.g., $[30]$ ]. Project structure measures the problem and solution comprehension. Large system development projects with unfamiliar technology are seen as high risk. Conversely, low risk projects are small and use a familiar technology. In this context, prototyping can be employed to reduce risk $[32]$ . Project time frame and need for rapid development are also viewed as factors that call for prototyping. Project time frame is an estimate of the time needed to complete the project, while the need for rapid development indicates a short project development is desired.

The specific type of system being developed, such as transaction processing or management reporting, has been identified as a variable impacting the prototyping decision $[5,11,24,42]$ . Although similar to system type, interactive application processing technique was identified as a separate variable [1,18]. The lack of clear project goals also increases project risk.

System commonality is the extent to which others might use the same system for their problem; impact is the degree to which the system will affect the organization. Systems with high commonality and broad impact are expected to benefit from prototyping.

## 2.2. Developer and user attributes

Several developer and user attributes have been identified as important. These include user task comprehension [14] and the user's ability to contribute to the development process, the developers' ability to accurately understand the users' requirements [3,4,39], the existence of unfavorable user attitudes [36], user and developer relationships, and familiarity with prototyping. Finally, the developer's familiarity with the technology being employed has been suggested.

## 2.3. Resource availability

The availability of software development tools is obviously highly important. These tools enhance the systems developer's ability to quickly construct prototypes for user review. [21,26,38]

The availability of the data necessary to develop a prototype is also necessary.

## 3. Methodology

The research required a methodology that would identify the variables and aid participants in ranking and selecting important variables. Because consensus about the relative importance of variables was an objective, the Delphi technique was selected. It parallels the methods employed in previous IS and general consensus research $[9,16]$ .

## 3.1. Delphi overview

Delphi is a group process that may use written media to solicit and aggregate information from a group of disjoint individuals [31]. Delphi provides a means to obtain, refine, and communicate the informed judgments of experts. It has been used in numerous general applications in corporate environments [27]. Participants voice their opinions anonymously through a series of linked questionnaires; normally there are three or four rounds. The first round focuses on the exploration of the subject and participants contribute additional information. The middle round or rounds involve the process of reaching an understanding of how the group views an issue; during this phase, some consensus will be occurring. In the last round a final evaluation occurs when all previous information has been analyzed and results have been provided for participant evaluation.

## 3.2. Delphi rounds

The prototyping literature provided nineteen decision variables for Round One. These variables, with a brief explanation (see Table 1), were presented in alphabetical order in the Round One questionnaire. The managers were asked to: (1) rank the variables in order of importance, (2) add decision variables they believed to be important, and (3) mark variables that were not considered important. There were five variables added by the respondents. These are listed in Table 2.

The Round Two questionnaire began the process of achieving agreement. Responses from Round One were analyzed and the summarized results, including variable mean rank and the number of times a variable was marked unimportant, were included. Variables were listed in mean importance sequence. Managers were again asked to rank the decision variables they considered important, and to mark those that were not.

The Round Three questionnaire provided the managers with Round Two results. It listed the variables in mean importance sequence from Round Two and again showed the number of times variables were marked unimportant. The managers were asked to review Round Two results and to reconsider the importance of the decision variables.

The goal of Round Three was to reach consensus on the relative importance of the variables selected from Round Two. Following this round, an analysis of mean rank, percent of managers ranking a variable in the top 10, and standard deviation, indicated a sufficient level of consensus had been attained, therefore no additional rounds were conducted.

## 3.3. Participant selection

The MIS managers that participated in this research were selected from firms listed in the Computerworld Premier 100 most effective users of information systems [40]. Telephone conversations with these organizations identified 41 firms with MIS managers whose responsibilities included deciding whether or not prototyping should be employed in systems development projects. The organizations represented diverse industry categories: public accounting, banking, chemical, communications, data processing services, energy, insurance, manufacturing, and transportation.

Of the initial 41 MIS managers who agreed to participate, 31 responded to Round One, 29 responded to the Round Two, and 27 to Round Three. Results are based on the MIS managers that participated in all three rounds.

## 4.1. Movement toward consensus

As already discussed, the Delphi method was used. The round-by-round data shows the movement toward agreement.

Changes in mean variable rank between rounds is one indicator of movement toward consensus. Fig. 1 illustrates how the mean rank for the Important variables changed over the three Delphi rounds. In most instances mean rank scores moved consistently toward agreement.

Another method for evaluating variable ranking consensus is the percentage of the panel ranking a variable in the top 10. These were above the 60 percent level in the Final Round. Fig. 2 shows how the percentages for the Important variables changed. The rise in percentages after Round 2 suggests that the MIS managers were moving toward consensus. The declines in the percentages of variable 7, Need for Rapid Development, and variable 10, System Structure, from Round One to Round Two are attributed to the introduction of five new variables in the second round. Variable 5, User Availability, was introduced in the second round.

To further analyze ranking consensus, standard deviation was examined. A decreasing standard deviation indicates increasing consensus in the variable rankings. Fig. 3 illustrates how the standard deviations for the top ten variables decreased.

Kendall's Coefficient of Concordance (W) was used to determine if the level of agreement on the final rankings were different [15]. Analysis of the final rankings resulted in a W of 0.54, which is significant at $p < 0.001$ , and this indicates the final rankings of the decision variables are statistically different.

## 4.2. Important variables

Important variables have a final mean rank of 9.6 or higher and were ranked in the Round Three top 10 by at least 60% of the managers. Nine of the Important variables were identified in the literature; however, User Availability, was added by the managers. Five of the Important group variables are System and Project Properties while the remaining five are Developer and User Attributes.

The first four variables: Clarity of Project Goals, Developer Understanding of Requirements, User Task Comprehension, and User Contribution had final mean rankings of 4.3 or better, with strong agreement among the managers about these ranks as indicated by standard deviations below 2.4. Significantly however, these variables were referenced somewhat infrequently in the literature; e.g., the number one variable, Clarity of Project Goals, was referenced only twice.

Variable 5, User Availability, was added by the managers in Round One. Although it was not referenced directly in the literature, over 85% of the managers placed it in the top 10. Perhaps researchers assume users are available, yet project managers know they are not.

Complexity of System Requirements was ranked only sixth but referenced 12 times in the literature.

Variable 7, Need For Rapid Development, was referenced five times in the prototyping literature. The level of consensus for this variable was generally lower than for the other top ten variables. Seven of the panel members did not consider it to be important enough to rank; however, sixty-three percent ranked the variable as one of their top ten. The importance of this variable is probably the most controversial of the ten highest ranked variables.

System Impact, the degree to which the system being developed will affect the organization was cited only once in the literature, yet was ranked eighth by the managers.

Overall, the project risk variables, System Structure, System Development Project Size and Technology Familiarity were ranked 10, 14, and 20 respectively. These rankings are somewhat surprising, given the argument that prototyping may be used to reduce risk. More specifically, these variables are frequently cited (11 references) as influencing the need for prototyping. This suggests that managers feel that they can add the technological familiarity if they need to do so. Moreover, a complex system is a way of life in many IS Departments and may not be seen as a factor for this reason.

## 4.3. Moderately important variables

Moderately Important variables were ranked in the Round Three top 10 by between 10% and 50% of the managers. Three of the four came from the literature.

It is quite a surprise to see System Development Project Size, which is referenced seven times in the literature, ranked fourteenth overall. This variable was included as one of the ten most important variables by four of the MIS managers and viewed as not being important enough to rank by four. This suggests that managers seldom consider a project's size when deciding whether to prototype.

## 4.4. Unimportant variables

The 10 Unimportant variables were ranked in the final top 10 by fewer than 8% of the managers. Three of these were added by the respondents in Round One.

Of the 19 variables identified in the literature, the managers concluded seven were Unimportant. Significantly, several of these seven are frequently cited. Perhaps continuing advances in software and hardware technology have reduced the magnitude of data and software availability problems. For example, software that enhances the systems developer's ability to prototype, such as database management systems and fourth generation languages, is much more readily available today.

System Type is often thought to impact the prototyping decision. This view is evidently not shared by the MIS managers in this study; it was ranked 21st in the final round and five of the MIS managers did not consider the variable important enough to rank.

The level of consensus for all the unimportant variables, measured by standard deviation, was low.

## 5. Summary

Variables influencing the decision to include prototyping in a systems development project have been identified from the literature and their importance ranked by MIS managers. Significantly, several of the variables frequently referenced in the research literature appear to have little importance to the practical prototyping decision.

Of the five variables added by the managers in Round One, one was finally rated Important (User Availability), one Moderately Important (Identification of Processes and Flows) and the rest were Unimportant.

Seven of the nineteen literature variables were ranked Unimportant by the managers. Even some of the frequently cited variables such as System Type, Project Time Frame and Software Availability were in this group. Also Software Availability may no longer be important.

Those variables ranked most important to deciding whether to include prototyping in system development projects were: Clarity of Project Goals, Developer Understanding of User Requirements, User Task Comprehension, User Contribution, User Availability, Complex System Requirements, Need for Rapid Development, System Impact, User/Developer Relationship, and System Structure.
