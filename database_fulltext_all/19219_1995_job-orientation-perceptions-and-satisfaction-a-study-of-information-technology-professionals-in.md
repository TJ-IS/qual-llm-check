# Job orientation, perceptions, and satisfaction A study of information technology professionals in Singapore

Lawrence Loh $^{a}$ , Chetan S. Sankar $^{b}$ , Wee Yong Yeong $^{a,*}$

$^{a}$ Faculty of Business Administration, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 0511, Singapore $^{b}$ Department of Management, 322 Thach Hall, College of Business, Auburn University, Auburn, AL 36849, USA

## Abstract

In this paper, we develop a conceptual model for job satisfaction vis-a-vis technical or managerial orientation of the information technology (IT) professional's job. We suggest that the linkage is mediated by perceptions of the professional on the “micro-environment” of the job (occupational pay fairness and occupational advancement prospects) as well as on the “macro-environment” of the job (organizational progressiveness posture and organizational cohesiveness). The model was supported empirically by testing a sample of 140 IT professionals in Singapore. We found that a high degree of technical orientation results in the perception that moving into management would bring better pay and advancement prospects; this then leads to job dissatisfaction. Further, a technical orientation brings a perception that the organization is a less progressive place and is held together by an emphasis on procedures rather than on accomplishments. On a broader perspective, our findings would be useful for Singapore policy makers in national IT manpower planning as well as in fine-tuning its implementation of the current vision of Singapore as an “intelligent” island by the turn of the century.

Keywords: Information technology professionals; Information infrastructure; Information technology policy

## 1. Introduction

Reward systems are a fundamental determinant of professional performance [5]. Organizations are increasingly recognizing a very basic business maxim that “what you pay is what you get.” Yet, the failure to acknowledge the importance of appropriate motivational mechanisms is still a serious problem in the management of technical professionals. Such a predicament is exacerbated by the uniqueness of this group of professionals that is characterized by a strong pride in conducting technically-oriented creative work [23].

The dilemma is discussed in a recent study: technical professionals perceive that moving into management would result in significantly higher pay and career advancement [26]. In contrast, the managers of these technical personnel believe that their rewards reflect responsibilities assumed and that their jobs have adequate promotion prospects. In addition, the absence of a parallel technical career ladder that allows technologists to move upwards in the organization is an underlying concern.

The literature on the specific role of occupational reward systems for the management of technical professionals has a rich tradition. Deci [9] found that technologists are motivated more by “intrinsic” rewards, directly mediated by themselves, than by “extrinsic” rewards, mediated by other parties. More recently, Saleh and Desai [25] empirically discovered that the top source for occupational stress were problems with the organizational reward systems.

In another study, Mainiero [19] interviewed career scientists and engineers, and obtained evidence suggesting resentment towards management, feeling of exploitation, perceived lack of opportunities, and a general absence of motivation; in fact, these attitudes of technical professionals differ from other work groups in the companies; in these groups, more favorable dispositions are present. Interestingly, about 80% of the technical professionals studied by Badawy [4] indicated an aspiration to be a manager within five years. Despite perceived barriers in the transition from technologists to managers, most technical professionals actually felt pressured to move into managerial positions [27].

Within the IT profession, the strife between technical and managerial positions also exists. In a study of technical professionals at IBM Corp., management constraints are a critical factor influencing the quality and quantity of the professionals' performance on technical assignments [7]. Further, these managers indicated serious concern about the ability of professional staffs to conduct current and future technical assignments effectively. In another study, Igbaria and Siegel [14] showed that general satisfaction with the job, as well as specific satisfaction with progress, promotion, pay, and status are all negatively correlated with turnover intentions.

Our study adopts an integrated approach in examining the critical relationship between job orientation and perceptions and its final impact on job satisfaction. We build a conceptual model that dichotomizes the perceptual job environment of the IT professional into: (1) a micro-environment (i.e., occupation), comprising pay and advancement; and (2) a macro-environment (i.e., organization), consisting of posture and cohesiveness. We argue that the degree of technical orientation is positively related to perceptions that managers earn more pay and enjoy greater advancement prospects; in addition, it is positively related to perceptions that the organization is less progressive in its posture and draws on procedures rather than results. The ultimate consequences of these beliefs would be a reduction in the level of job satisfaction. Empirical results were drawn from a sample of 140 IT professionals across four large organizations in Singapore.

The importance of this research is three-fold. First, given the emerging importance of professionals in the building of an effective organizational information infrastructure, our results would be valuable in developing sound human resource strategies. Second, for the management community, this study presents an empirical analysis pertaining to job orientation and satisfaction in a focused professional group; our specific consideration of IT professionals would contribute to the validity and reliability of existing evidence in more generalized job settings.

Third, and most notably, our research results would assist Singapore policy makers in central IT manpower planning and in building a national IT infrastructure that will realize a fully “intelligent” nation by the year 2000. While it is necessary to assess the effort toward this vision using broader indicators, an in-depth study of IT professionals per se would aid planners in developing more specific measures to achieve its national IT objective.

## 2. The Singapore context: an IT2000 vision

The efficient and effective deployment of IT has been heralded as a critical enabler for Singapore's economic restructuring at the turn of the 1980s. A high-level ministerial Committee on National Computerisation (CNC) was tasked with the responsibility of ensuring that the nation can leverage the benefits of computerization. This committee made four major recommendations; the first of these was to develop sufficient numbers of IT professionals to help in spearheading Singapore's push into the information age.

In the mid 1980s, the emphasis of IT policy shifted toward the development of a national information infrastructure. A committee was formed to establish a National IT Plan. It proposed a strategic framework comprising seven building blocks, where the first is “IT manpower;” it was considered essential to develop a corps of IT professionals and experts to exploit Singapore’s IT [21].

As a consequence of these two IT planning efforts, there has been a significant increase in the size of the IT profession. In fact, the number of such professionals has grown from 850 in 1980 to 14,000 in 1991. With a such base of IT professionals being established, Singapore was ready to pursue an even bolder masterplan. Codenamed “IT2000,” this new initiative was a more fundamental approach to attain global competitiveness [22]. Indeed, the ultimate vision is to transform Singapore into a fully intelligent island through a national information infrastructure and the development of industry-wide strategic inter-organizational applications. The aspirations and requisite capabilities would only be possible with the continuing drive to develop IT professionals, not only in terms of quantity, but also quality: the professionals must be technically competent.

The success of the IT2000 vision rests on maintaining a competent base of IT professionals with strong technical capabilities. If a career bias against technically-oriented IT jobs exists in Singapore, there would probably be incentives to move into management, or even, leave the profession completely. The end result may be a shortage of experienced personnel in the more technically-oriented positions. This could impact Singapore's plans for an intelligent island by the year 2000.

## 3. Conceptual model

In our study, we define “technical personnel” as those who design and control the technical functions of the business; their technical skills may cover research, development, analysis, design, and/or implementation of complex systems. On the other hand, “managers” are those who lead and coordinate the organization to achieve its objectives.

Our conceptual model is built around the six research constructs shown in Fig. 1. Essentially, we propose that the linkage between job orientation and satisfaction is mediated by perception constructs operating in the occupational environment (i.e., pay fairness and advancement prospects) and the organizational environment (i.e., progressiveness posture and cohesiveness basis). Presumably, job orientation per se would not affect satisfaction but it exerts influence through several perceptions of the environment.

3.1. The micro-environment of the job: occupational mediators

Traditionally, evidence has shown that technical professionals assume that managers are paid higher. Sankar et al. [21] documented differences in pay distribution as perceived by technologists and managers. In fact, the comparatively favorable pay for managers is arguably an outcome of the political, social, and economic influences associated with these personnel compared to technologists [6]. With the greater rewards, it is understandable that technically-oriented professionals have an incentive to strive to be managers.

The specific linkage between the perception of pay difference and job satisfaction draws on the theory of distributive justice $[10]$ , which refers to the perceived fairness of the pay amounts received compared to other employees. McFarlin and Sweeney $[20]$ found that the lack of distributive justice indeed leads to a lowering of job satisfaction. In the technical fields, Dailey and Kirk $[8]$ have also argued and tested the relationships between distributive justice and its impact on job satisfaction and intention to quit the job.

On a more general note, Saleh and Desai [25] obtained evidence that problems with the organizational reward systems could lead to stress and dissatisfaction. Further, a large-sample study revealed that perceived fairness for compensation accounted for a significant amount of the variance in job satisfaction.

Thus, our first hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 1(a): Technical orientation of the IT professional is positively related to the perception that better pay lies in moving into management.

Hypothesis 1(b): This perception is negatively related to job satisfaction.

Next, we posit that technically-oriented IT professionals would perceive that career promotion prospects are enhanced by going into managerial positions. Conceptually, this is related to the greater pay. However, such rewards are less direct and have longer time horizons. Although many research-based organizations have devised dual ladders for technologists and managers [1], a clear path for career advancement seems to be lacking for the technically-oriented in the IT profession.

In fact, while scientists and engineers are better off than their counterparts a few years ago, the same cannot be said for analysts and programmers. Since their technical content is recognizably critical to the organization, human resource management policies have to be improved to retain them in their technical positions.

Mainiero [19] documented the anger and resentment of engineers and scientists pertaining to the lack of opportunities for moving up into management. Sankar et al. [26] examined the need to advance to management positions amongst technologists and managers and found that: first, technologists feel that they are not valued when they do not advance into management; second, they feel that they are actually expected to move into management at some point in their career. Within the IT profession, Igbaria and Greenhaus [13] demonstrated that career advancement prospects had positive effect on job satisfaction. Overall, we thus believe that technically-oriented IT professionals do not feel that they have adequate career upgrading opportunities, and this in turn will result in a lack of overall job satisfaction.

Our second hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 2(a): Technical orientation of the IT professional is positively related to the perception that better advancement opportunities can be attained by moving into management.

Hypothesis 2(b): This perception is negatively related to job satisfaction.

## 3.2. The macro-environment of the job: organizational mediators

Technically-oriented professionals are a separate breed within any organization. They are inspired by the challenges in the actual work that they do; in fact, they anchor their careers on the technical field or functional area more than anything else and actually identify primarily with their profession and secondarily with the company [24]. Studies have also shown that technologists are motivated strongly by non-monetary rewards, such as praise and recognition. Arguably, it is also true that creative personnel thrive best within a progressive organizational climate characterized by dynamism, enterprise, competition, and expansion.

Since the need for a progressive work environment is higher for a technologist than for a manager, we would expect that higher technical orientation in the job of an IT professional gives rise to a feeling that the organizational climate is always insufficiently progressive. As Badawy [3] put it: “An organization with a more decentralized and less formal structure with a variety of opportunities for communication, interaction, and participation should be designed. More positive and enthusiastic responses to new ideas… and a reasonable degree of freedom and autonomy are some positive ingredients of a creative organizational climate.”

We suggest that the final impact of these perceptions of lack of progressiveness by technical professionals would be a decrease in job commitment and satisfaction. In a study of Singapore engineers, updating of technical skills was shown to be strongly affected by environmental variables, such as organizational policy and collegial relationships $[2]$ . A recent survey of members of the Institute of Electrical and Electronic Engineers (IEEE) in the United States showed that the most important element in job satisfaction was the opportunity to be creative $[28]$ . Thus, the work environment is a crucial determinant for commitment and satisfaction of technical staff; if professionals think that there is an unfavorable job climate, morale is affected (see $[11]$ ).

Our third hypothesis therefore is:

Hypothesis 3(a): Technical orientation of the IT professional is negatively related to the perception that the organization has a progressive posture.

Hypothesis 3(b): This perception is positively related to job satisfaction.

The final part of our conceptual model deals with the effect of technical orientation on perceptions of organizational cohesiveness and job satisfaction. Organizations are bound by various means. On the one hand, they could be “glued” through an emphasis on behavior-based compliance with formal procedures. On the other hand, the fabric could be outcome-based; in other words, the focus is on task accomplishments and innovation.

Technically-oriented professionals are an organizational group that works best within a culture that stresses results (see $[15]$ ). In research organizations, technical personnel, such as scientists and engineers, find a conformance-rooted climate dysfunctional to professional performance and fulfilment. Likewise, for IT professionals, we extend the argument and propose that analysts and programmers are motivated and derive satisfaction chiefly by the organizational recognition of tangible outputs. Our argument is supported by the findings of Woodruff $[29]$ who showed that task-oriented factors explain variance in job satisfaction.

On a more general vein, the conceptual linkage between technical orientation and the perceived basis for organizational cohesiveness and its impact on job satisfaction can be justified from a study by Harvey [12]: the relative distribution of power amongst stakeholders such as managers and professional staff was shown to have a strong effect on organizational cohesiveness and professional satisfaction.

Hence, our fourth hypothesis is:

Hypothesis 4(a): Technical orientation of the IT professional is negatively related to the perception that the basis for organizational cohesiveness is due to an emphasis on results rather than on procedures.

Hypothesis 4(b): This perception is positively related to job satisfaction.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1.Data

A survey questionnaire was administered by mail to IT professionals in four large Singapore-based organizations. The profiles of these organizations are as follows: (1) a leading local manufacturing company that is listed on the Stock Exchange of Singapore; (2) a major multinational manufacturing firm that has its parent corporation in Japan; (3) a prominent local financial institution that is one of the largest banks in the country; and (4) an established nonprofit educational institution in Singapore. We believe the spectrum of respondents obtained through these organizations is representative of our IT profession. The final sample consisted of 140 IT professionals. This represents a return rate of 70.0%, since 200 questionnaires were given to the directors of the IT functions for distribution to their professionals.

## 4.2. Operationalization

We needed to develop measures corresponding to the six research constructs: (1) job orientation; (2) occupational pay; (3) occupational advancement; (4) organizational posture; (5) organizational cohesiveness; and (6) job satisfaction. The questionnaire items for these measures are shown in the Appendix. These items are consistent with existing similar studies in the U.S., adapted and pretested in the Singapore context.

For the first construct, job orientation, we defined a continuous measure that considers the proportion of technical work in relation to the total spent on managerial and technical works. This measure departs from the traditional binary indicator for technologists versus managers. We believe our present approach is better as it reflects the continuum of technical versus managerial work undertaken by IT professionals more effectively than any dichotomous distinction.

In the case of the other five constructs, we used a 5-point Likert scale for each. Such items were also appropriately reverse coded to maintain the same directionality for the construct. Each of the perception constructs was computed as the simple average of the items associated with it.

A confirmatory analysis was then carried out through two sets of regressions. In the first, a multivariate multiple regression was performed simultaneously for the four perception constructs simultaneously as dependent variables and with job orientation as the independent variable. In the second, a univariate multiple regression was conducted with job satisfaction as the dependent variable and the four perception constructs as independent variables. Both sets of regressions were repeated with the inclusion of the following control independent variables: (1) job level (i.e., degree of seniority); (2) age; (3) gender; and (4) graduate (i.e., whether the respondent has a university degree).

## 6. Discussion

We have demonstrated that job orientation affects an IT professional's perceptions of both the occupational environment (i.e., pay fairness and advancement prospects) and the organizational environment (i.e., progressiveness posture and cohesiveness basis). In other words, when the job of this professional is more technically oriented, he or she would tend to perceive that moving into management brings better pay and advancement opportunities; further, he or she would perceive that the organization is a less progressive place and is “glued” together through an emphasis on procedures rather than results.

In addition, we have shown that the level of job satisfaction is lowered when technically-oriented IT professionals perceive that better pay and advancement lies in managerial positions. We have also found that, perceptually, having a less progressive organizational posture leads to a reduction of job satisfaction.

Overall, our results are consistent with those obtained for job orientations in other professional groups, such as engineers. In fact, we have an expanded model pertaining to technical orientation and its effects on the micro- and macro-environments of the job. We have also tested and verified the ultimate impact on job satisfaction.

Throughout our research, we discerned a consistent tone of discontentment amongst the more technically-oriented IT professionals. The value of entering management is summed up appropriately by a respondent who wrote about her greatest challenge: "For furthering my career, I am taking an MBA course through distance learning and finding it very rewarding. I believe management training at the age of late 20's and early 30's is most beneficial." This respondent is a 28 year old systems analyst with a university degree (B.Sc.) and spends $80\%$ of her job on technical work.

The lack of prospects facing a technical professional is also evident from another respondent, a systems programmer who is highly technically oriented and in his early 30s with an engineering degree and a graduate diploma in computer technology. He suggested: “More recognition should be given to the technical person in terms of long term career path as it is impossible that everyone becomes manager.” Indeed, yet another respondent, when asked to comment on the career in IT, replied, “Maybe I should consider going into another line.” This rather discouraging remark was made by a 25 year old computer science university graduate who had spent barely two years in the technical segment of the IT profession as an analyst programmer.

From an organizational perspective, the IT function has indeed undergone major transformations over the last decade. Whereas the traditional focus of structuring an IT department has been on the internal aspects, such as decentralizing to end-user departments (see $[16]$ ), recent emphasis has been on the external dimension such as in outsourcing to vendors $[17,18]$ . Our results pointing to the lack of incentives to stay in a technical position would mean that more IT responsibilities would be shifted toward line users or outside vendors. The impact may then be an IT department that risks losing its technical capabilities for delivering value to the organization. Arguably, the end result may be a loss of IT-based competitiveness by the organization within the industry (e.g., see $[30]$ ).

On a policy perspective, our findings have major implications on how the IT profession can be structured. In the specific context of Singapore, as the country embarked on an ambitious IT policy to build a national information infrastructure, our study would be useful for government planners to further develop a strong and motivated IT professional corps. While our results may have established a state of the IT profession that is, in fact, similar to the feeling of other technical groups internationally, we can still improve on the human resource aspect of the IT infrastructure. Indeed, this softer aspect should not be neglected over other components, such as computers and networks.

We thus believe that the state of the IT profession needs to be re-evaluated and actions must be taken to address their current concerns. We offer four policy prescriptions that would hopefully elevate the effectiveness of IT professionals.

(R1) Remunerate the professionals equitably. The importance of distributive justice in wage determination cannot be understated even for the professional personnel, who is often motivated by a passionate liking for the nature of the job. The response to the quantum difference in the pay will be critical; thus, the technically-oriented professional's commitment to the job is likely to be influenced largely by the relative difference between the individual's pay and that of an equivalent position in management. Hence, the design of an equitable compensation package may be the single most important challenge facing the profession. Ultimately, organizations should devise sound pay policies for technical professionals, although this process could be achieved through an appropriate central planning authority.

(R2) Recognize the role of technically-oriented professional. The concept of a dual ladder for technical professionals, especially scientists and engineers, is a traditional response to the perceived lack of career prospects for professionals who are technically-oriented. Within the IT profession, however, this parallel track is not distinct: the shape for an organization's IT function is usually a pyramid wherein the higher levels are always managers. In fact, job titles like analysts and programmers refer to entry-level and middle-ranked personnel. A dual ladder allows the promotion of technical personnel to jobs such as chief scientists and executive engineers. The IT profession should formulate a corresponding advancement path. Only then would these professionals be able to add value through continued technical contributions.

(R3) Reposition the organization to be more progressive. Technically-oriented professionals prefer a dynamic environment that is competitive and growing. To maintain a productive stream of outputs, it is essential that the organization demonstrates a progressive posture. Top management must therefore take concrete actions in nurturing a organizational climate and culture that could encourage IT professionals to remain in their existing technical capacity. For instance, a corporation can forge a shared vision to be the “best-in-class” for a given technology or product-market and then rally the entire IT organization toward achieving this quest.

(R4) Redesign processes to emphasize accomplishments. Technically-oriented professionals work best in an organization in which the basis for cohesiveness is cerebral (task accomplishments and innovation) rather than one of applying formal procedures. The traditional organization is characterized by a compelling drive toward behavioral conformance with complex routines and norms. On the other hand, IT professionals flourish in an organization that focuses on results. Hence, it is critical for top management to initiate a “reengineering” of organizational processes that precisely address the shift toward an output-based work environment; e.g., the incentive system can be redesigned to reward on final performance and less on continuous compliance.

## 7. Conclusion

We have developed and tested a model of the impact of job orientation on perceptions and its ultimate effect on satisfaction. Empirical results derived from a sample of IT professionals in four Singapore-based organizations generally support the model. These findings are critical to national IT manpower planning especially as Singapore embarks on its quest for realizing the IT2000 vision.

It would indeed be very costly for the country if technically-oriented IT professionals do not have favorable perceptions on the micro- and macro-environments of the job. Even worse, if job satisfaction is low, the result may be a dilution of organizational and professional commitment. This may then have adverse effects on turnover and departure for these technically-oriented professionals.

Without doubt, retaining the technically-oriented in the IT profession is a crucial factor for success for the IT2000 plan. These professionals are the “engine” of the human IT infrastructure. We cannot afford to have a cadre of IT professionals who invariably aspire to move into management because of perceived inequity of rewards and prospects. The critical priority now is to reshape the profession to reflect the invaluable contributions of these personnel.
