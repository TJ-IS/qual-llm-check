# An assessment of the job prospects of MIS doctoral students in the academic marketplace

Vincent S. Lai $^{a,*}$ , Edward T. Chen $^{b}$

$^{a}$ College of Administrative Science, The University of Alabama in Huntsville, Huntsville, AL 35899, USA $^{b}$ College of Business, Southeastern Louisiana University, Box 350, SLU Station, Hammond, LA 70402, USA

## Abstract

A survey was conducted on 322 new MIS doctoral graduates in the USA to investigate their profiles, qualifications, research and teaching interests, and job placement during the two years of their graduation. Several important findings emerged: the MIS candidates are found to be more experienced in teaching and productive in research publication than previously; more candidates are found to be interested in teaching MIS, Systems Analysis and Design, and Decision Support Systems; Artificial Intelligence and Expert Systems are found to be the most intensive research areas of recent MIS graduates; the average salary for new MIS hires, on a 9-month basis, was found to be slightly above \$50,000; and finally their degree stage, gender, proceedings publication, and DSS/AI/ES specialty are found to be significant factors in affecting job placement.

Keywords: MIS education; MIS career

## 1. Introduction

The new hire market for Management Information Systems (MIS) faculty among academic institutions is the result of the interaction of supply and demand in the labor market. In the past, the demand for MIS faculty has exceeded the supply. Therefore, most MIS doctoral graduates could have multiple academic offers before their graduation. However, the market has had a recent growth in the number of new MIS doctoral programs $[4]$ and increased numbers of MIS doctoral degrees granted. With the continuation of progress towards maturity of the MIS discipline $[3,6]$ and continued interest of students, the supply and demand of MIS faculty had become more balanced. A survey conducted in 1991 [5] found that there was already an over-supply of MIS doctoral graduates (by a margin of somewhere between 0.7% and 2.3%) compared with the available MIS positions. Most of these excess graduates will probably re-apply for academic positions later, causing more graduates to be absorbed by the job market. Also, because of the hiring-freeze policy of many institutions, there has been some shrinking in the total number of MIS positions in recent years.

Thus MIS doctoral students are facing a competitive job market. What is their best strategy for finding a job? How do they prepare their qualifications to match the hiring school's requirements? These important questions have to be answered in anticipation of job placement. The objective of this paper is to examine the placement of MIS candidates.

The results of our work should be of interest to MIS graduates and students who are or will be in the job market. The paper also has implications for universities that are hiring MIS graduates.

## 2. Research methodology

The relevant market and demographic data were gathered from the MIS candidates listed in the Decision Sciences Institute (DSI) and International Conference of Information Systems (ICIS) placement booklets. As we were interested in the trend of MIS placement, a two-year comparative analysis was conducted of the graduates during 1992 and 1993. To ensure the homogeneity of the survey data, the candidates were classified into two categories: new MIS Ph.D. graduates and candidates who are already an MIS faculty. Only new MIS graduates were surveyed in this study: the employed MIS faculty who reentered the job market were excluded. Out of 423 candidates listed in the DSI and ICIS placement booklets in the two-year survey period, 322 (171 in 1992 and 151 in 1993) were new graduates without a faculty position. These candidates thus became the survey base of our study.

The questionnaire in this survey was adapted from Bertin and Zivney [2] and modified to suit our objectives. The instrument was first pretested by its administration to seven MIS Ph.D. students at a major university in Texas. This process resulted in some adjustments to the format and semantics of the questionnaire. After validation, the modified questionnaire was mailed to the candidates in early May of 1992. A follow-up mailing six weeks later was made to those candidates who had not yet responded. The same processes were repeated in the second year.

In the first year, 82 candidates responded to the survey, while 55 candidates responded in the second year. This constitutes a total of 137 returned questionnaires. Of the 322 questionnaires mailed, 12 were non-deliverable, producing an overall response rate of 44.2 percent. After reviewing the responses, it was found that some questionnaires were not valid. Most of the invalid responses came from those who either withdrew from the job market or took a non-academic position. The removal of these invalid responses resulted in 126 (75 in 1992 and 51 in 1993) usable questionnaires.

It is plausible that the survey is susceptible to response bias. The candidates who were moving to new academic positions were most likely to respond and were eager to provide the requested information while candidates who failed to obtain an academic position may have had little interest in this survey. Therefore, the reported statistics should perhaps be adjusted downward.

## 3. Findings and discussions

## 3.1. Profile of respondents

The MIS new hire profiles are shown in Fig. 1. The data indicated that a majority of MIS candidates were male (79.4%) and United States citizens (63.5%). An overwhelming portion of them (93.7%) had at least defended their proposals. 17.5 percent of them had been awarded doctorate degrees. An evaluation of the academic majors revealed that all MIS candidates were either MIS majors (80.2%) or minors (19.8%). Out of the total of 126 responses, 32 candidates (42.7%) in 1992 and 28 candidates (54.9%) in 1993 had obtained job offers.

Teaching at the college level, either part-time or full-time, may provide MIS candidates with an edge in the academic job market. To train MIS doctorates as proper teachers, most doctorate-granting schools offer their students a teaching slot during their final semesters. ## 3.3. Interests of respondents

## Teaching

Teaching interests of candidates suggest where they could make most contribution in the hiring department and with faculty. Matching a candidate's teaching interests to prospective schools has become an important consideration in the job search and screening process. MIS candidates must show their teaching interests when seeking the support and resources provided by the hiring school. The hiring department must use the teaching interests of candidates to balance the overall course coverage and augment the strengths of their existing faculty. In Fig. 2, responses of MIS candidates are shown, based on their teaching experience in some areas, which include MIS, System Analysis, Decision Support Systems, and Systems Design. Programming Languages and Data Communications were found to be the least appealing to MIS candidates.

## Research

Research interests of the candidates provide prospective recruiting departments with possible research expansion. The candidate's research interests can also help the recruiting school in determining its possible new research directions. Hence, the process of matching research interests and needs may impact the placement decision. As indicated in Fig. 3, Artificial Intelligence and Expert Systems (AI/ES) was the most intensive research area in both years. The interest in Systems Analysis and Design dipped in 1993, but a longer trend analysis is required to determine whether this is a trend. Data Communication, Database Management Systems, and Strategic Information Systems have long been considered to be three of the most important research areas in MIS. However, the data show that very few candidates expressed their interests in these topics.

## 3.5. Job selection considerations

MIS candidates were asked to rate the importance of their selection of a University post for twenty-four factors using a Likert scale (1 = unimportant; 7 = very important). The top five and bottom five selection factors are presented in Table 4. The overall results in 1992 and 1993 were rather different. Only two factors, salary and general quality of faculty, were ranked in the top five factors for both years. In 1992, the candidates were more concerned with the quality of the hiring schools and its benefits, while in 1993, the candidates seemed to be interested in the research opportunities of the hiring school. They wanted decreased teaching loads, better research support and higher quality of faculty for joint research.

The least important job selection criteria also changed from 1992 to 1993. In 1992, the new hires were less concerned with the opportunities available (such as fringe benefits and summer support) than issues concerning their families. However, in 1993, the new hires were less concerned with factors that would affect the welfare of their family members. A possible explanation is the increase in number of single, unmarried candidates from 12.5% in 1992 to 25% in 1993.

## 3.6. Success factors in job placement

Stepwise multiple regression was used to assess the impact of various demographic data (age, gender, citizenship, and marital status) and candidate characteristics (e.g., degree stage, teaching experience, publication records, and specializations) on job offers. In order to determine the validity of the regression model, scatter plots and residual plots were developed to examine for peculiar patterns. They did not indicate any violation of the assumptions on which the regression procedures were based.

The results of the regression model are summarized in Table 5. The findings indicated gender, number of conference proceedings, database management systems (DBMS) specialty, and decision support systems (DSS) specialty had a significant impact on job offers at the .05 level (one-tail test); while candidate's program stage had a highly significant impact at the .01 level. The dependent variable, job offer, is a binary variable of values 0 or 1.

Therefore, the interpretation of these significant coefficients can be explained in percentages.

Female candidates had a fourteen percent higher chance of receiving a job offer than their male counterparts. Apparently many institutions are making efforts to hire female faculty to fulfill AACSB's equal employment opportunity and accreditation requirements. The ratio of female to male faculty members in higher education is currently still too low. Therefore, the trend of hiring more female faculty may continue for the next few years.

The candidate's current stage in the doctorate program is also a significant factor in determining his/her chance for job placement. Of the sixty candidates receiving a job offer, $98.3\%$ were at least All But Dissertation (ABD). Only one candidate was found to have only completed the course work. This suggests that the best time for doctoral candidates to join the job market is at the ABD stage.

The number of proceedings in which a candidate had published influenced the probability of receiving job offer. The candidates had roughly $5\%$ greater chance of receiving an offer for each additional conference paper. However, surprisingly, no significant correlation was found to the number of journal publications. Apparently, hiring institutions are still relying on conference papers to measure the candidate's research potential.

Our study also indicates that candidates with DBMS and DSS specialization have a better chance to place in the job market. As demand direction changes, other MIS specializations will become more desirable.

## 4. Conclusions

This study provides comprehensive data on the opportunity offered in the new Ph.D. MIS market during 1992 and 1993. Important results regarding MIS candidate profiles, qualifications, research and teaching interests, offer profiles, and relevant factors affecting job placement were identified. Knowledge of these findings should be valuable to MIS graduates attempting to join the job market and hiring institutions recruiting potential faculty.

MIS is still a young discipline. It is difficult now to predict how its growth will affect the supply and demand of Ph.D. graduates. However, many researchers believe that progress hinges partly on the proper balance between the supply and demand of doctoral graduates. In computer science, annual market surveys have been conducted to review the production and employment of Ph.D.s and their employment as university faculty members. In accounting, academic and salary surveys are conducted on a triennial basis. MIS professionals have not yet performed regular evaluations of the employment opportunities for their doctoral graduates. Some part of this survey might therefore be valuable if replicated in future years. A longitudinal approach would prove benefits in tracking historical development and identifying trends and growth of the discipline. A cross-disciplinary study might then compare and analyze the performance of labor market, doctoral education, and salary structures of varied academic disciplines.
