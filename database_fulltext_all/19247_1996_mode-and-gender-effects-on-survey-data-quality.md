# Mode and gender effects on survey data quality

Donna L. Mitchell $^{a}$ , Gary Klein $^{b}$ , Joseph L. Balloun $^{c,*}$

$^{a}$ Department of Management and Marketing, College of Business Administration, Louisiana State University in Shreveport, One University Place, Shreveport LA 71115, USA

$^{b}$ School of Business, University of Texas of the Permian Basin, 4901 East University Boulevard, Odessa TX 79762-0001, USA $^{c}$ Department of Management and Marketing, College of Administration and Business, Louisiana Tech University, P.O. Box 10318, Ruston LA 71272, USA

## Abstract

Affordable and user-friendly computer technology has made it possible to administer surveys on computers. However, even though computer administration of surveys is becoming a popular mode of data acquisition, little is known about the effect that this method may have on the quality of data collected. Gender differences are known to exist with regard to cognitive dimensions of computer literacy, but reports of gender effects on various modes of data collection are conflicting. This study examined the effects of mode of acquisition on data quality and the interaction effects of mode and gender. Computer-administered questionnaires and pencil-and-paper questionnaires served as the modes of administration. Omission rates, response distortion, completeness of the answer, and psychometric scale reliability were the measures of data quality. A student sample of 400 respondents was surveyed, and mode of administration did not impact data quality alone or in interaction with gender.

Keywords: Computer assisted questioning; Data quality; Computer interviews; Computer testing; Gender differences; Impression management

## 1. Introduction

Gender, computers and data quality are important to research involving the use of surveys. Gender is known to affect response patterns when the data is elicited from individuals [26]. The effects of a computer administered mode, as opposed to other modes, have been studied but the results are conflicting and inconclusive. Data quality can vary tremendously based upon gender, the methodology used for data collection, and other experimental aspects [3,25,27]. Only a few studies explore the relationships among these concepts. No previous study has been found by the authors that explicitly looks at formal data quality as a function of both gender and mode of administration.

For many years organizations have had an interest in computerized data collection over the paper-and-pencil mode [23]. Computers are now major tools used in conducting organizational surveys and psychological tests, applying assessment instruments, and screening job applicants [12,18,19]. Computerized interviewing has a long history in the medical field, while its use in the marketing research and political polling arenas is on the increase. It is thus evident that computer-based data collection is here to stay and can no longer be considered a novelty.

The rising popularity may be a result of several advantages. Computer-based surveys allow flexibility by incorporating techniques such as help screens, question branching, and required entry of needed sections. Due to the elimination of transcription from paper, this method is probably faster and more accurate. Respondents report greater enjoyment on the computer and perceived the questionnaire to be shorter and better at preserving anonymity $[20]$ . Additionally, large samples can be rapidly collected from remote locations $[31]$ .

In spite of these advantages, the computer mode has problems $[30]$ : even when using pre-existing software, development costs are high; it is possible to commit coding errors that researchers fail to detect $[16]$ ; a computer is impersonal, and some people become anxious when dealing with it; respondents may lack needed skills, such as working a keyboard, that limit the use of open-ended questions; researchers must be willing to make fundamental changes in their data collection procedures; hardware and software failures may result in lost data; and different populations may treat the computer differently $[13]$ .

It has been found that communicating via a keyboard and screen is different from that with paper and pencil, and thus these two modes present different task requirements $[21]$ . Many researchers caution against the assumption that computer assisted data collection will provide the same results as paper-and-pencil collection. Additionally, respondents' attitudes may adversely affect the validity of the data. Subtleties, such as formatting differences between the two modes, may also affect the test performance $[11]$ even as enhancements available to the computer, such as sound and color, may alter the quality of the responses.

On the whole, there has not been sufficient research to determine whether the two modes substitute for one another $[1]$ : in fact, results of existing studies have been contradictory $[14]$ . Certain studies found that the computer administered approach allows for more candid answers, and others found that individuals are less candid in their responses $[6]$ . Still other studies conclude that no major difference exists between the two modes; however, the majority of these suffers from a lack of controls and often draws conclusions from a very small sample.

Confounding several of the studies is an omission of gender considerations. Gender difference, when communicating through a computer, is well documented $[15]$ ; apparently there are gender differences due to the effects of computer literacy $[9]$ . Perhaps some conflict may therefore be explained by gender differences. As an example, one study found no difference between the two modes of administration based on a sample population of all males $[2]$ , while another study that found a difference had a sample population of 70% female $[17]$ .

## 2. Research methodology

## 2.1. Subjects

The sampling frame consisted of students enrolled in classes in a business college at a commuter university in the southern USA. Two hundred males and two hundred females were randomly assigned to either answer questions in a computer or paper-and-pencil mode. These survey questions were answered during their normal class times. The average age of the students was 26, and this matched the average reported by the institution; the self-reported academic classifications of the sample were: 5% freshmen, 19% sophomores, 30% juniors, 40% seniors, 4% graduate students, and 2% non-matriculating. Participation in the survey was voluntary; however, each student was given a ticket for a \$50 lottery upon completion of the survey.

## 2.2. Instrument

A group of 19 students participated in the pilot study using the paper-and-pencil format of the instrument. Except for the lottery incentive, participation at all levels was voluntary. Both the computer and paper-and-pencil versions of the survey needed to be revised as a result of the pilot. Faculty members examined the revised computer version to check that all of the programmed instructions and help screens were clear and self explanatory.

The final survey consisted of 84 questions. The first 40 questions contain two scales that assess impression management (IM) and self-deceptive enhancement (SDE) [24], both of which are components of socially desirable responding (SDR), which can be described as the tendency “to stretch the truth in an effort to make a good impression” [19]. IM is the deliberate tendency to overreport desirable behaviors and underreport undesirable ones, whereas SDE is the tendency to give honest but overly positive reports. Sample items from the 20-item IM scale include “I always obey laws, even if I’m unlikely to get caught” and “I never cover up my mistakes.” Sample items from the 20-item SDE scale include “I never regret my decisions” and “My first impressions of people usually turn out to be right.” Two questions were open-ended, non-sensitive questions that asked why the respondent was attending the university and what the respondent would have liked to have known before attending. Ten questions were of a sensitive nature, and example questions include “How many times, if ever, have you cheated on a class test or assignment?” and “How many different sex partners have you had over the years?” Four questions rated feelings about the university, seven assessed the student’s computer experience, five collected demographic data, two asked about the student’s American College Testing (ACT) score and grade point average (GPA), and the last fourteen assessed the respondent’s reaction to the survey. A complete copy of the instrument can be found in Mitchell [20].

## 2.3.Procedures

Participants were asked to sign a statement giving permission to use the data in an aggregate study. Social security numbers were used to provide the link to corresponding institutional data. Individual answers remain confidential; and identities were not revealed; in fact, social security numbers have not been retained on any copy of the data.

A script provided uniform instructions to each class of students. Results of the paper-and-pencil surveys were entered into the computer for analysis, and the data was verified for accuracy. The computerized survey had the same set of questions as the paper-and-pencil survey but used direct keyboard input, thus bypassing the need for later data entry. The computer program was designed to present one question on the screen at a time. The computer mode survey software was developed using the Ci3 System for Advanced Computer Interviewing by Sawtooth Software [28].

The last digit of each student's social security number was used to determine random assignment to a mode of survey administration. As each class took the survey, the number of males/females and computer/paper-and-pencil modes (four cell combinations) was updated. When a cell reached 100, any additional students who had been assigned to that cell were dismissed. Students were allowed to take the survey only once; a total of 22 classes were involved in the survey that was administered over a two-week period.

## 2.4. Measures

The independent variables were mode of administration and sex of respondent. The respondents were therefore split into four categories for statistical analysis, with an equal number of starting observations in each. The categories were: 1) Computer Male, 2) Computer Female, 3) Paper Male, and 4) Paper Female. The dependent variables were: 1) scale reliabilities, 2) item omissions, 3) completeness of answers, and 4) response distortions.

Scale reliability is the internal consistency of scales. Five composite scales were included in this study: (1) SDE, (2) IM, (3) Attitude toward the University (ATT), (4) Computer Experience (COMP), and (5) Number of Sensitive Questions Omitted (OMITQ).

Cronbach's coefficient alpha measures internal consistency of a composite scale defined by adding together a set of variables designed to measure a construct [4]. The computed alpha varies between zero and one and is applicable to the composite scores of a total sample of respondents. In this study, we are interested in how individual differences in measurement error are related to the independent variables. A jackknife procedure created individual pseudo-values which represented the influence of each observation on a sample statistic [22]. This created individual coefficient alpha statistics for each of the five composite scales in the study.

Item omissions usually occur when questions are sensitive or threatening in nature. Passive omission of items is not permitted on most computer-based surveys; and even when it is allowed, the respondent generally has to press a key (or several) to skip a question. The paper-and-pencil mode allows a question to be omitted (in fact, there is no way to insist that any question be answered).

Each additional omission reduces the Fraction Present by .0025; therefore, the Fraction Present must be carried to four decimals.

As suggested in Cureton and D'Agostino [5], omitted data variables in the surveys were studied by recoding the 84 individual questionnaire items, which included the self-reported GPA and number of courses failed for each student in the sample of 400. For each question, if an observation was present, it was given an “omitted” code of zero, while an observation that was not present received an “omitted” code of one. The fraction of respondents with zero codes for the omission variables appears in Table 1. For example, Question 6 (variable X6) had only one respondent not answering that question out of 400 respondents, thus the Fraction Present equals .9995 (obtained by dividing 399 by 400).

These coded omission variables were analyzed by principal components. Here the first component was used to determine the questions contained in the measure of omissions, because it (a) contained the items expected to be sensitive and (b) had a high alpha coefficient, .68 for raw scores or .80 when its omitted variables were standardized to mean zero and standard deviation one. The second component had an alpha of .50 or .53, respectively, and contained only three items before varimax rotation. The number of answers to the questions in the first factor became the measure of omission.

Completeness of answer is measured by the number of responses, or direct answer listings, given to open-ended questions. An earlier study $[14]$ of the same two modes of response found no difference in this measure. The only other studies reporting on this measure utilize other modes; e.g., the comparison of telephone and mall intercept modes provides similar completeness measures, while face-to-face interviews generate more complete answers when compared to telephone interviews $[8]$ . The two open-ended questions in this study require a written or typed response, the unit of “answer” being a complete thought $[7]$ .

Response distortion is deviation from institutional records. No cause is attributed to the distortion here, but it is often due to one of four factors [29]: (1) memory – information may be forgotten or remembered incorrectly; (2) motivation – respondents may falsify responses due to fear of the consequence or hope of self-enhancement; (3) communication – subjects may not understand the questions; or (4) knowledge – feel compelled to answer though they lack the facts. Moreover, clerical errors, recording biases, or inconsistency over time may degrade the accuracy of the institutional records.

When students report their grades or the number of courses they have failed, there is reason to suspect that they may unconsciously or consciously “inflate” their past performance in a “socially desirable” direction. Similarly, one might expect student respondents to under-report the number of courses that they have failed in the past. The extent of such biased responding might be simply indexed by either the difference between students’ reports of GPA or the number of courses failed.

However, while simple difference scores look highly plausible as reporting bias measures, they may cause technical problems. First, institutional records are not always reliable, valid, free from bias or readily accessible. For example, students' grades are not always stable over time or always consistent across the courses taken in one academic term. Besides these problems, difference scores per se create special psychometric problems. They tend to cause low reliability, create an illusion of discriminant validity, may produce multicollinearity, and can result in heteroscedastic or variance-restricted distributions, i.e., where the error variance is not constant over all the observations. Fortunately, the main psychometric or statistical challenges to difference scores can be reduced or eliminated. Here we controlled for some possible artifacts by regressing the self-reported values against the institutional values and using the residuals as the measures of distortion.

## 3. Hypotheses

The null hypotheses are that neither mode nor gender will have a significant effect on data quality. More formally:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} H _ {o}: \mu_ {\text {(paper and pencil)}} & = \mu_ {\text {(computer)}} \\ H _ {o}: \mu_ {\text {(male)}} & = \mu_ {\text {(female)}} \\ H _ {o}: \mu_ {\text {(paper and pencil} \times \text {male)}} & = \mu_ {\text {(paper and pencil} \times \text {female)}} \\ & = \mu_ {\text {(computer} \times \text {male)}} \\ & = \mu_ {\text {(computer} \times \text {female)}} \end{array}
$$

The alternate hypothesis is inequality for each case. The hypotheses are tested using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). Number Cruncher Statistical System (NCSS) [10] was used as the statistical analysis software.

## 4. Results

An examination of the correlation matrix for the entire sample (Table 2) reveals few surprises. Mode of administration of the survey (MODE) exhibits no significant correlation with any of the other variables. Gender (SEX) shows significant correlation with the completeness of answer variables for the open-ended questions (Q52 and Q53) and with several of the reliability measures (prefixed by “A”). The correlation of completeness of answer to gender is readily understood from the raw data: Q52 asks positive aspects of the university and Q53 asks negative aspects; it has been observed that females tend to provide more positive responses and fewer negative responses than males. The correlation of gender with reliability measures is most likely a result of mean and standard deviation differences in scale responses between the sexes and correlations between the scale reliabilities. This premise is borne out by the raw data (Table 3).

The negative correlation between the completeness of answer variables (Q52 and Q53) is explained by the reversal of a positive to negative form of the question. A significant correlation between the first factor of the omitted responses (OMIT) and the reliability of the omitted responses (AOMIT) should be present, and it is. Several of the reliability measures (prefixed by “A”) show significant correlation with other ones; others show little or none. Again, this is expected since the three items exhibiting a relationship (AIM, ASDE, and AATT) are all involved with Socially Desirable Responding, while the other two are not.

The other significant correlations are between the completeness of answer variables (Q52 and Q53) and the response distortion variable of number of courses failed (FAIL). This relation merely indicates that the subjects most likely to deflate the number of courses failed (who are those with the most failures) tend to have fewer positive comments and more negative comments about the university. The final significant correlation is between the completeness of answer variables (Q52 and Q53) and the reliability of the self-deceptive enhancement scale. This relation was not anticipated and is not readily explained except, perhaps, through the common relationship with gender.

The first MANOVA results were obtained by considering both dependent variables and the full set of independent variables. The summary results from this are in Table 4. As can be seen, the null hypothesis regarding mode of administration and interaction cannot be rejected, while the null hypothesis regarding gender is rejected (alpha = .05). This is good news because computer interviews did not detract from the quality of the data on any measured dimension. Furthermore, the differences in previous studies cannot be explained by gender and mode interaction effects as expected. However, gender still makes a difference due to the impression management characteristics.

## 5. Conclusions

This study compared paper-and-pencil surveys with keyboard-entered computer administered surveys. The main question was: Does the mode of administration adversely affect the quality of the data as a solo effect or as an interaction effect with gender? The data quality measures considered included item omission, scale reliability, completeness of answer, and response distortion. The results of a MANOVA revealed no effects of mode of administration or mode interaction with gender on four different data quality measures. Effects were observed for gender, albeit small and perhaps not as large as those of other individual differences.

The convenience sample of students at a commuter university, located in the southern United States, limits the conclusions. However, tight controls, a large sample size, use of data quality measures, and a rich questionnaire increases confidence in any generalizations. The results indicate that the use of a computer in the collection of survey data is appropriate for many situations. This should comfort the growing number of organizations using computer-based survey systems.

Issues remain: Other populations may have different results; individual differences may be influential; and response rates may vary for other than convenience samples. If any population differences are located, they must be quantified to allow for future corrections. Different media also need to be studied rigorously, including telephone interviews and other non-keyboard data entry devices. Measures of data quality also need to be examined, refined, and expanded, especially because the data collected in this study exhibit no general common factor.
