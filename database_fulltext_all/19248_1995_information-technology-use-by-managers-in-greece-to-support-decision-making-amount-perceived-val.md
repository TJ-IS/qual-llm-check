# Information technology use by managers in Greece to support decision making: Amount, perceived value, and satisfaction

George E. Vlahos $^{*}$ , Thomas W. Ferratt

Department of MIS and Decision Sciences, School of Business Administration, University of Dayton, Dayton, OH 45469-2130, USA

## Abstract

This study developed a profile of managers of corporations in Greece, focusing on the amount of IT use and the value of and satisfaction with computer-based information (CBI) in making decisions on planning, controlling, and operating. A detailed descriptive analysis of survey responses indicated that: (1) these managers used information technology (IT) as a valuable, everyday tool; (2) of Mintzberg's four decision roles, respondents rated the value of CBI highest for the resource allocator role; (3) all levels of management rated CBI the highest in supporting the evaluation step in decision making; (4) the highest use of CBI was for short-term decisions; (5) computer-based information systems (CBIS) were more valuable to the manager's mental model for guiding planning, controlling, and operating decisions than for forming or revising the manager's mental model of the corporation; and (6) first line supervisors and information systems (IS) managers were most satisfied with CBIS.

Keywords: Decision making; Managers; Information technology use; Greece; Europe; Managerial work; Information technology value; Information satisfaction; Mental models; Decision support; Executive information systems; Computer use

During the last two decades, information technology (IT) has become increasingly available to support managers in making decisions for planning, controlling, and operating their corporations. Studies from several countries have added to our understanding of the kinds of IT managers use and the amount of time they spend using it. However, information on IT use by European managers, the value provided by IT, and how satisfied managers are with computer-based information systems (CBIS) is limited. Our study examines not only the amount of IT use, but also the value of CBIS in supporting the decision making of managers in Greece; furthermore, it assesses the satisfaction of these managers with CBIS.

Before the proliferation of microcomputers, the cost of installing and maintaining mainframe or minicomputer CBIS was very high, such that only large corporations, mainly in industrialized countries, could afford them. The availability of cost effective and powerful microcomputers and advancements in telecommunications have made it economically and technically feasible for managers in less industrialized nations to introduce both stand-alone and networked IT to their corporations. User friendly software packages, combined with managers' needs for better and faster information, have led to the use of IT by an increasing number of smaller corporations worldwide.

Several studies have shown that the most commonly used computer software is spreadsheets, word processing, and data base management $[2]$ . Purposes for using software reported in one study of North American managers and professionals $[11]$ included planning, assisting in decision making, budgeting, forecasting, communicating with others, looking for trends, controlling and guiding activities, and finding problems. More specialized use of IT has been reported in a few studies; e.g., Nunamaker, Applegate, and Konsynski $[16]$ showed that business and government executives found the quality of deliberation and decision making by electronic brainstorming to be at least as good as that resulting from boardroom meetings.

The reported hours of IT use range from 1.8 hours per week to 2 or more hours per day (i.e., 10 or more hours per week). The accessibility of hardware varies across these studies with the low amount of use coming from a study of knowledge workers (including managers) in Saudi Arabia [18], where participants shared personal computers. The high use was reported in a study of managers and non-managers in Taiwan [10] where about $50\%$ of respondents used a microcomputer for two or more hours per day; the non-managers used IT more than managers. A study in the mid-range of use [12] reported that various levels of U.S. managers spent from 4.4 to 7.9 hours per week using the computer.

Although several studies have reported on the types and amount of IT used, there is little information about the value of various types of CBIS in supporting managerial work. An early study $[20]$ of top executives indicated that executive information systems (EIS) were valuable in supporting managerial work in the areas of planning and control. Another study $[7]$ of managers who frequently use IT concluded that many of these managers “strongly believe they are getting valuable support for their managerial work from their direct use of IT.”

Despite these trends, the majority of managers are not using IT as much as might be expected. One study $[1]$ reported that 79% of the installed and frequently used management support systems relied on transaction processing applications to monitor daily activities and produce fixed periodic or exception reports. Managers participating in the study indicated that most installed computer systems were designed to support lower level business functions and that their informational needs would have been better served by flexible inquiry and analysis systems.

A more recent study [19] of executive support systems (ESS), concluded that: (1) ESS are just emerging from the pioneer stage; (2) many executives believe that ESS are a significant tool whose effectiveness depends heavily on their use; (3) ESS are not for everyone in the next few years; (4) office support, especially electronic mail, and planning and control, will continue to be prime uses; (5) the development of richer, more up-to-date, and sharply defined “mental models” of the manager’s business environment is the real – but usually unspoken – driving force behind their development; and (6) the spread of ESS is inevitable.

## 1. European economic and IT environment

Many international corporations have been formidable and effective competitors in a global economy. However, competition between European, U.S., Japanese, and other corporations may be fiercer since the European Union (EU) has begun to eliminate all trade barriers among its member countries. The fall of communism in Eastern Europe and the unification of Germany has opened the door for other European nations to join the EU.

European corporations, to prepare themselves for the challenges of a barrier-free European Union, have been making drastic organizational changes. As noted by Liscio [13], “A united Europe becomes by far the largest trading block in the world: 320 million people with high per capita income and a Gross Domestic Product (GDP) that is roughly the same as that of the United States.” He also claims that Europeans have a real lead in software technology and have chosen not to compete with the U.S. and Japan in the expensive race for hardware development.

The level of use and the degree of CBIS sophistication varies significantly among European countries. While most Western European countries have been using CBIS for the past two decades, smaller, less industrialized nations have only recently begun to introduce IT in their corporations. In a survey in

Finland [9], small businesses were found to use computers as tools rather than as a communication medium. In a survey carried out in the spring of 1987 [17], IS managers of the 100 largest companies in United Kingdom showed that planning and developing is to support corporate strategy was their prime concern. While U.S. corporations have yet to decide on which technology to adopt for human interface and communication, the EU partners have been using Integrated Systems Digital Network (ISDN) to transport all forms of information (text, data, image, sound, etc.) across their borders even though ISDN was adopted before the technology had sufficiently matured [22].

Some writings suggest that Japanese and European corporations may not have a problem with IT failing to meet managerial needs. One prevailing view is that the Japanese concentrate on changing the organization first rather than imposing new IT on old operations. As Strassman [21] put it, “They are continually innovating organizational forms. Then, when they need to support those new forms with technology, they buy it. In the United States, we have advanced technology but have lagged in developing the management process necessary to harness it”.

## 2. Greece

This study focuses on managers of corporations in Greece. The use of IT in Greece has additional contextual factors. A survey [6] found that Greek IS managers were much more concerned about the location of the IS function within the organization than were managers in the UK. This shows a lack of organizational structure in Greek organizations that other industrialized nations have long overcome. Because of its geographic location in southeastern Europe and the Middle East and its full membership in the EU, many European and other international corporations have subsidiaries and regional headquarters in Greece. These corporations bring advanced IT from their parent organizations but have a problem finding trained managers and personnel. A study found that training was a major problem and many respondents expressed dissatisfaction with the training and advice provided by vendors. Yet another study of general managers and IS managers in Greece [8] showed that they believed that their information systems were mainly used for low-level processing of pre-identified business transactions.

## 3. Specific objectives of this research

European managers are an increasingly important group of information users whose use of IT should be examined. The purpose of our research is to develop a profile of managers of corporations in Greece that describes their use of CBIS and the value of this technology in supporting their decisions vis-a-vis planning, controlling, and operating their corporations. Our study expands our limited understanding of the role IT actually plays in supporting managerial decision making. It also provides a baseline for comparisons with managers from other European countries, the U.S., Japan, and elsewhere.

Given the purpose of our study, the profile for the sample and its subgroupings should include:

1. the amount of IT use and its value relative to managerial roles and the decision making process;

2. types of IT components used and the relative importance of various types of CBIS; and

3. satisfaction with the present CBIS and planned changes.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Sampling procedure

Copies of the survey instrument were sent to approximately 1,000 managers from 200 middle-to-large size corporations in Greece. A cover letter (in Greek) explaining the purpose and importance of the survey was also included. Fifty-five managers responded. Given economic and time constraints, no follow-up survey was conducted. The respondents represent twenty-nine organizations, and at least 41% were employed by foreign corporations in Greece.

## 4.2. Survey instrument

The data for the study were gathered via survey questionnaires. The questionnaire includes items dealing with demographics, amount of use of IT, value of use of IT, types of hardware, software, and IS, satisfaction with existing CBIS, and plans for changing these systems. A preliminary version of the questionnaire was reviewed by experts in IT. It was also tested with several groups of MBA students, most of whom were working managers. The comments and suggestions of the researchers and managers regarding the clarity, validity, and consistency of the questions were incorporated into the final version.

We chose to use English for the questionnaire rather than translating it into Greek. English is a common business language that many international managers read and understand. Although translation of the questionnaire into the language of each country is a common recommended research practice, such translation can also result in interpretational differences, leading to non-comparable data for studies in more than one country. An instrument written in a common language provides a common stimulus, increasing the reliability and validity of responses.

## 4.3. Measures of variables

Measuring Amount of Use. Participants indicated the hours per week they used various types of hardware. The hours for each type of hardware were combined to yield total hours per week. Similarly, the hours for various types of software were combined to yield total hours of software use.

Each of these totals was recoded to match the categories of response for an overall question that asked the actual use of the respondent. The categories and recoded values were: a. >16, recoded to 20; b. 9–16, recoded to 12.5; c. 5–8, recoded to 6.5; d. 1–4, recoded to 2.5; e. <1, recoded to .5; and f. None, recoded to 0. The recoded values for the overall question, the sum of hardware hours, and the sum of software hours were averaged for each respondent. Although the recoding of values greater than 16 truncates the upper end of the distribution of responses, it prevents outliers from skewing the mean, resulting in a mean closer to the median (8.4). Cronbach's alpha (coefficient of reliability) was .91.

Measuring Value of CBIS. Value was measured for specific aspects of managerial work, managers' mental models, and a broad view of value. Measures of the specific aspects of managerial work include those for Mintzberg's [15] decision roles – entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator, specific decision steps – identifying problems or issues, generating alternative courses of action, evaluating the outcomes of each alternative, ranking the alternatives and choosing one, and implementing the chosen alternative, specific decision making tasks – long term (strategic), mid-term (tactical), and short term (operational) decisions. Two items measured the value of CBIS for managers' mental models. A broad view of value was measured by an overall importance question.

An overall value index was developed from these questions with several factor and scale reliability analyses. Two items asked about the CBI used to support long-term and mid-term decision making. For computing the index, responses to these items were recoded to a seven point scale by dividing each by 5, multiplying by 6, and adding 1. Another pair of items asked about the value of the CBIS compared to the mental model. The fifth item was the overall importance question. The Overall Value Index for each respondent was the mean of these five items. Cronbach's alpha (coefficient of reliability) was .84.

Identifying Types of Applications Used and Relative Importance of Various Types of IS. Two listings of types of IS were presented to respondents. The first consisted of both fixed and flexible systems; the second of commonly known applications, e.g., Information Reporting Systems, Decision Support Systems, and Transaction Processing Systems. Respondents ranked the listed sources of CBI in order of importance for their decision making, with 1 the most important. The percentage of respondents ranking an item is reported. In addition, ranks were recoded to yield weights from 1–7 with 7 being high (1 recoded to 7, 2 recoded to 5, 3 recoded to 3, 4 through 7 recoded to 1, and no rank recoded to 0). These weights were then averaged across respondents to yield another measure of relative importance.

Measuring Satisfaction with CBIS and Identifying Planned Changes. Overall satisfaction was based on a single item. The identification of planned change focused on whether further development of the CBIS was to support informational needs of managers or not.

## 5. Results and discussion

## 5.1. Research objective 1: amount and value of use

Table 1 shows that use of IT varied across levels of management and functional areas. The average is at the upper end of use reported in prior studies. Even allowing for errors in recall and self-reported estimates, the amount of use indicates that IT is an everyday tool for managers in our sample.

An examination of participants by management level reveals that first line supervisors or managers have the highest use, about twice that of mid-level or top level managers. The high use of IT by top level managers is rather impressive. One explanation may be that many businesses in Greece are managed by their owners. Another study $[5]$ concluded that since 1989 these managers/owners had passed the stage of fascination/terror with IT and were actively implementing useful systems. An examination of our data by functional area revealed that manufacturing and IS managers had higher use than all others, including accounting, finance, and marketing.

Roles. Table 2 shows the value these managers placed on CBI in three areas. In the first area, respondents rated the value of CBI for each of Mintzberg's four decision roles: entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator. For the whole sample the resource allocator role received the highest rating. Additional analyses showed that the resource allocator role also received the highest rating by each level of management, but there were differences across functional areas. For example, although the resource allocator role had the highest rating for three of the five functional areas, the entrepreneur role had the highest rating for information systems managers and accounting and finance managers. Further analyses across managerial levels showed that higher level managers' ratings of the value of CBI for each of Mintzberg's decision roles averaged from .8 to 1.3 higher than the ratings of lower level managers and from 1.0 to 2.0 higher than the ratings of mid-level managers.

Value of IT Support for Steps in Decision Making. In the second area respondents rated the value of CBI for each of five decision steps: identifying problems or issues, generating alternative courses of action, evaluating the outcomes of each alternative, ranking the alternatives and choosing one, and implementing the chosen alternative. For the whole sample the value of CBI was highest for the evaluation step. Additional analysis showed that the evaluation step also received the highest rating for each level of management.

Value of IT Support for Short-Term, Mid-Term, and Long-Term Decisions. In the third area, respondents indicated the percent of CBI (compared to non-CBI) used for each of three decision making tasks: short term, mid-term, and long term decision making. For the whole sample the percentage of CBI was highest for short-term decisions. Additional analyses showed that they also received the highest rating by each level of management, and long-term decisions received the lowest. However, short-term decisions did not receive the top value for manufacturing managers and marketing managers, who had the top percentage in long term and mid-term decision making, respectively.

Value of IT Support for Mental Models. Table 3 shows the results of responses to two questions on the help and value of the current CBIS relative to the mental model (i.e., image, idea, or conceptualization of the organization) that respondents used for planning, controlling, and operating purposes. The value the CBIS provided to the mental model in guiding planning, controlling, and operating decisions was greater than that in forming or revising the mental models. This result held for the whole sample. Another study reported that data management and control were relatively more important for Greek than UK IS managers. This may help explain why first line and manufacturing managers considered IT to be of very high value.

5.2. Research objective 2: types of hardware and software applications used and relative importance of various types of IS used

Types of Hardware Used. Table 4 shows the percent of respondents using various types of hardware and the average number of hours per week they used it. The results show that stand-alone personal computers were used by the majority of respondents and for about the same amount of time as all other hardware combined. A personal computer or other terminal connected to a local area network (LAN) was hardly used by this group. The predominant use of stand-alone PCs agrees with the findings of other researchers and the fact that most corporations in Greece are smaller and have just recently begun to computerize using PC-based systems.

Types of Software Applications Used. For software the results show that spreadsheet/financial report preparation was the only software used by the majority of respondents and for the most time (4.1 hours/week). Word processing, data base applications, and running other packaged or developed programs all ranked about next in use with similar percentages and time. The use of other packaged or developed programs was heaviest among the manufacturing and IS managers.

These findings are in agreement with those of another study. The IS managers of Greek corporations in that study indicated that more than 80% of the companies used traditional applications, such as general accounting, sales and purchase orders, payroll and inventory; more than 50% of them used standard software packages (spreadsheets, databases, and word processing), and less than one third used electronic mail, desktop publishing and bar code processing.

Comparing the findings to those from IS managers in the UK, an industrialized and economically advanced country, the application priorities were rather different. Executive data support and office automation were the top priorities of the English managers followed by electronic data interchange, international communications, internal administration and accounting, electronic point of sales and CAD/CAM manufacturing.

Relative Importance of Various Types of Information Systems. Table 5 presents the relative importance of different types of information systems in supporting managerial decision making. The first classification of systems was based on Alloway and Quillard's fixed monitoring compared with more flexible query and analysis systems. The results of rankings within this classification show that regular (hourly, weekly, monthly, etc.) reports received the highest ranking. For the second classification, based on commonly known applications, the results show that IRS received the highest ranking.

In another study, the 40 Greek IS managers that were interviewed claimed to believe strongly in the strategic importance of IS for their organization. Our sample of 55 managers did not, however, see on-line reports and EIS contributing to their strategic decision making as much as regular reports and IRS. Other studies agree with our findings that Artificial Intelligence and Expert Systems received a relatively low importance rating.

Information Reporting Systems (IRS), which provide regularly scheduled reports, dominated the types of CBIS used by this sample of managers. Nevertheless, 75% of these managers reported that they were using systems developed mainly to support managers; only 25% reported they were using systems developed mainly for reasons other than supporting the informational needs of managers.

Viewed from one perspective the dominant use of

IRS stands in agreement with previous results obtained with U.S. managers: fixed monitoring systems were the most prevalent even though not ideal. However, viewed from another perspective, a combination of two findings – (1) IRS are the dominant CBIS and (2) the systems these managers use were predominantly developed to support managers – contradicts the previous characterization of flexible query and analysis systems as ideal.

These results suggest alternative explanations. One is that adoption of IT by organizations in Greece has lagged behind that of organizations in the U.S., perhaps because of different economic or cultural conditions: limited use of LAN technology and high use of centralized, less flexible IRS provide some support for this. Another explanation may be that managers perceive IRS as designed to meet the informational needs of managers.

Further analysis shows that the importance of IRS consistently ranked ahead of all other types of IS for all management levels and all functional areas. The weighted rank for IRS in all functional areas other than IS ranged from 5.1 to 6.0. The rankings of the IS managers show IRS as having the highest rank at 3.8 compared with the second highest rank of 3.5 for TPS. Thus, the two sets of rankings consistently show the higher importance of regularly scheduled reports relative to more flexible kinds of query and analysis systems.

5.3. Research objective 3: satisfaction with CBIS and planned changes

Table 6 shows that overall satisfaction was 4.4 on a scale from 1 to 7. First line supervisors were most satisfied, while top level managers were least satisfied. When considering the manager's functional area, IS managers were most satisfied.

The percentage of respondents describing their current CBIS as being developed to serve one of four mutually exclusive purposes were: (1) mainly to support the informational needs of managers/decision makers (67%); (2) especially for the respondent's informational needs (8%); (3) mainly for reasons other than supporting the informational needs of managers (26%); and (4) other (0%). Respondents also indicated that 78% intended to initiate changes to their CBIS within the next twelve months. They indicated the intent of the planned changes relative to one of the four purposes. The percentage of those planning to initiate change in their CBIS to serve one of these purposes follows: (1) mainly to support the informational needs of managers (50%); (2) especially for the respondent's informational needs (24%); (3) mainly for reasons other than supporting the informational needs of managers (12%); and (4) other (14%). The percentages of current CBIS and planned changes to CBIS that focus on supporting the needs of managers, including respondents, were similar (75% and 74%). The intention to support the respondent's informational needs was greater in the planned changes to the CBIS (24% vs. 8%).

## 5.4. Relationship among value, satisfaction, and amount of use

Besides satisfaction, Table 6 also presents a summary of these managers' ratings of the value of their current CBIS. Both measures were on scales ranging from 1 to 7. The overall value index was higher than overall satisfaction for the whole sample and all levels of management. A notable result is that manufacturing managers had the highest overall value index but the lowest overall satisfaction. One further notable result is that across managerial levels first line managers had the highest overall value index and overall satisfaction, while top managers had the lowest. The relationships among hours of use, overall value index, and overall satisfaction were summarized by Pearson correlation coefficients. The correlation between the hours of use and overall value index was .17, for hours of use and overall satisfaction it was .27, and for overall value index and overall satisfaction it was .40. The last is the only one significant beyond the .05 level.

Thus, correlation analysis indicates the amount of use was not necessarily related to the perceived value of that use nor satisfaction with the CBIS; however, satisfaction was related to the perceived value of the CBIS, where perceived value is based on how well the CBIS supports the manager's mental model for planning, controlling, and operating decisions, particularly long and mid-term decisions. Thus, a manager who used a CBIS frequently was likely to perceive as much value from the CBIS in supporting managerial work as another manager who used it infrequently. The perceived value could have been low or high for the frequent user just as it could have been for the infrequent user. Furthermore, a manager who perceived that a CBIS provided value in supporting managerial decision making was more likely to be satisfied with the CBIS than one who did not.

## 6. Conclusions and contributions

In trying to understand why some managers use IT and perceive value in support of their work while others do not, we considered three alternative explanations. One is that IT is not a tool that can be adapted to support some managerial work. That is, some managers perform managerial work that is inherently not supportable by IT $[3,4]$ . This explanation is unlikely since some managers perceived value for various kinds of managerial work. Second, investment in CBIS that support managerial work varies within and across organizations; this variation could be based on different strategic choices, the availability of IT for specific managerial roles, decisions, or functions, or different rates of diffusion of IT that are not readily discernible. The variations in perceived value provide some support for this explanation. The third explanation is that some managers have a clearer understanding or vision of how IT may be used to support their work and have used this to incorporate

IT into the way they work. Once again, the variations in perceived value provide some support for this.

## 6.1. Contributions of this study

This study includes a mix of Greek, European, and other international corporations. Given these characteristics, this study provides new knowledge and information regarding the use of IT by international managers. However, given the size and geographic limitations of the sample, the results may not be generalizable to managers of international corporations. Nevertheless, the study does make important contributions.

First, this study provides the following profile of managers of corporations in Greece:

Amount and value of IT use:

1. these managers use IT as a valuable, everyday tool, with an average use per week of 9.6 hours;

2. they rate CBIS value highest in supporting the resource allocator role, the evaluation step in decision making, and short-term decisions; and

3. CBIS are more valuable in guiding decisions based on the manager's mental model of the corporation than in forming or revising the mental model.

Types of IT used:

1. stand-alone PCs are the dominant hardware used, while LANs are hardly used;

2. spreadsheet/financial report preparation is the dominant software used; and

3. IRS dominate the types of CBIS used. Satisfaction and planned changes:

1. first-line supervisors are most satisfied, while top managers are least satisfied with their CBIS;

2. most managers view their current CBIS as predominantly developed to support managers; this and the previous highlight raise questions about the prior characterization of flexible query and analysis systems as ideal for managers; and

3. although value of use and satisfaction with the CBIS are correlated, value and amount of use are not.

Second, a research instrument was developed and shown to have reliable self-report scales for amount and value of IT use. The scale measuring hours of hardware and software use has a reliability of 0.91.

The scale measuring the value of using CBIS to support a manager's work has a reliability of 0.84. The survey instrument could be used as is or form the basis for developing a new instrument for other countries.
