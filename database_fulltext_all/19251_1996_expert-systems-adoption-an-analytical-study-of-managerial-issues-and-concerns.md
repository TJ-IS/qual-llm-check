# Expert systems adoption An analytical study of managerial issues and concerns

Anand S. Kunnathur $^{a,*}$ , Mesbah U. Ahmed $^{a}$ , Ranjit J.S. Charles $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of ISOM, University of Toledo, Toledo OH 43606, USA $^{b}$ Macola Corporation, Marion OH, USA

## Abstract

The experience of introducing expert systems technology into six automotive component manufacturing organizations in the mid-western United States is described. The domain of expert system application as well as the procedural and organizational hurdles that were encountered are also documented. An overview of relevant literature shows the shortage of studies of this kind. A synthesis is provided of the study observations and implications for better management of expert systems adoption in manufacturing.

Keywords: Expert systems; Knowledge-based systems; Technology adoption; Management of technology; Technology infusion

## 1. Introduction

Faced with the need for revitalization, many American firms have been exploring the use of new technology to stimulate their performance. In recent years, expert systems (ES) have emerged as powerful tools for the design and operation of many business systems.

Expert systems have generated considerable interest, as they aid not only the problem solving process but also help explain and justify the results. Although most early applications were in the medical or engineering domains, manufacturing applications have become increasingly popular recently.

The adoption and diffusion of advanced manufacturing technologies, such as computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) and of information technologies by business and governmental organizations has received considerable attention $[10,14,15,13]$ . In contrast, similar research efforts in ES appear to be inadequate, perhaps due to the infancy of the technology. While there is abundant literature explaining the benefits of ES $[5,7,12,19]$ and giving “cook-book” approaches to developing ES, very little attention has been paid to the organizational issues that favor and nurture the diffusion of this technology in competitive manufacturing environments. The existing published research is largely theoretical, with a notable shortage of case studies. There are single company case studies dealing with user perceptions of knowledge-based systems $[1]$ and of the need for effective management of the process of technology diffusion $[3]$ . There is very little evidence of the factors that influence ES adoption and deployment decisions particularly in manufacturing environments. The reluctance of many organizations to divulge the nature and scope of expert systems usage and even to admit their experimentation with the technology may explain this literature gap.

The objective of this study is to gain a better understanding of critical success factors in ES adoption and implementation based on the ES experiences of six manufacturers in the automotive component industry.

Literature in information technology in general, and more specifically in the area of ES whenever available was used to focus the field-study to a particular set of issues from ES inception to the current state of use or disuse by the participants in the study. A detailed description of the cases and extensive discussions of the findings can be found in Charles [8].

## 2. Literature review

Adler and Shenhar [2] have classified technology into 4 categories: (1) base – common to everyone in the industry with no competitive advantage because all the players have equal access to them, (2) key – that can provide a competitive advantage, (3) pacing – those not currently deployed but probably capable of replacing one of the key or base technologies in future, and (4) emerging – those on the horizon, as yet unproven, but potentially important. ES fall into the pacing or emerging technology categories. They argue that organizations with different technology bases will need quite different strategic approaches when adopting a new technology.

Many ES have failed due to the poor transition or institutionalization $[9,16]$ . In the context of ES innovation, adoption and implementation $[4,11,16,18,20]$ are noteworthy. Benders and Manders $[6]$ have discussed organizational adoption patterns.

Sharma et al. [20] suggest that practical application of ES requires prior evaluation of suitable technology for appropriate tasks. They theorize that the quality of an expert system is governed by interaction of the organizational culture, the technology applied, people support, and the nature of the task. Furthermore: (1) When competition is intense, an organization is more likely to turn to technical solutions. (2) An organization with no experience in computer support is not going to adapt easily to a sophisticated technology. (3) A high-tech innovative culture is the most conducive for ES adoption and deployment. (4) A standardized and formalized structure for decision-making facilitates exploration of expert system problem solving. These postulates are based on the assumption that certain environmental conditions already exist. However, these postulates are purely theoretical. Their research focuses on what should be rather than what is.

Meyer and Curley [17] proposed a “success model” with knowledge and technology complexity as contingency variables; they tackled the ES adoption issue from the perspective of technology management. The difficulty in fielding successful ES is not due to a lack of technology but rather to lack of management understanding and practice. ES are in fact substantially different from conventional IS and new management frameworks complementing existing IS development are needed. They contend that the most dynamic and successful ES address some aspect of the “core” of the company. Also, senior management should understand the reasonable benefits that accrue from ES and have a clear assessment of the fundamental business needs and the critical skill areas of their company.

The research frameworks have been either theoretical or have failed to address the ES adoption issues specific to any industry prior to generalization. Our study attempts to answer the question, “Could a management paradigm for ES in manufacturing be extracted from the experience of manufacturers in the automotive component industry?”

## 3. Case studies

The companies studied were manufacturers in the automotive component industry using ES technology. These companies are located within 250 miles of each other in midwestern USA. These companies were selected because they had already made an ES adoption decision. Information on these companies was gathered primarily through personal interviews with either the person who played the key role in developing the ES or the person responsible for initiating the ES project. Telephone interviews were also conducted to clarify certain information obtained during the field trips. Pseudonyms are used to maintain the confidentiality of the companies: some have patents pending on parts of their ES ventures. Therefore, only essential product and technology information is disclosed. The profiles of the companies are shown in Table 1.

## 3.1. Description of the cases

## 3.1.1. Autowerk International (AUI)

Autowerk International is a large manufacturer of automobiles; its core competence is in the design and manufacture of light and medium size vehicles. Autowerk International has a Technical Research Center (TRC) and a highly skilled manufacturing systems development group exposed to new technologies through the Technical Research Center.

Motivation: The lead time between the design phase and the release of a process plan to manufacturing was close to five years. A system was needed to reduce the lead time and look ahead at the plant capacity, machinery, tooling, and other constraints of the design. The project was justified on the basis of profit margin. Both internal and external customers' requirements had to be met. It was also important to assess the propriety of extending this tactic of ES implementation into other areas as part of the organizational strategy.

Objective and domain: The ES venture was undertaken in engineering design, where the design engineer's subjective decision making expertise was to be captured to incorporate manufacturing and plant equipment constraints.

Initiation process: The project was initiated by middle management at the TRC (R&D), who had attended the “AutoFact” technical fair. The TRC established a team comprising members from the engineering department and the technical center, to identify the dollars that could be saved through the implementation of this project.

Committee: The “Red Team” monitored the entire justification process. It consisted of managers from Engineering Design, Technical Services, Accounting and a regional manager. It took them 9 months to agree that this venture satisfied both tactical and strategic requirements.

Investment: Over a quarter of million dollars per year were expected to be spent over a period of five years starting in 1989. Programming and other hidden costs were included.

System specification: An object oriented ES shell was used for development. The ability to match existing designs to customer provided specifications and also to enhance the designs to accommodate new requirements was built into the system.

Knowledge acquisition: The ES was developed for one product with nearly 40 sub-components. The process began with the identification of experts in the various areas. The knowledge engineer was a mechanical engineer with sufficient design experience and exposure to ES technology through seminars. Design and manufacturing rules were collected for seven modules from 7 design and 10 manufacturing experts. When there was a lack of consensus among the experts, the manager of engineering broke the deadlock. The user interface was designed with input from the design engineers (the intended users).

Organizational issues: Any project that involved new technology had to under go a formal justification process. It was also required to become a part of the corporate “C $_{4}$ Technology” umbrella integrating CAD, CAM, CAE, and CIM. Once a project met these requirements, it had to be tested to see if it passed both the tactical and strategic requirements for new projects.

## 3.1.2. Comfort Ride, Inc.

Comfort Ride, Inc. is a manufacturer of automotive struts and dampers. It is a subsidiary of one of the larger multinational automotive component manufacturing corporations. It houses a small R&D department and a small MIS department. CAD and Finite Element Analysis (FEA) are used in engineering design. The MIS department has been operating with a skeleton staff of seven; mainly maintaining existing systems.

Motivation: Much of the data required for the design of the dampers was to be obtained from manufacturing. Manufacturing's problems were traced back to inconsistencies in the design procedures and the lack of manufacturability consideration. Algorithms used by the design engineers had been coded in FORTRAN and other high level languages. Based on individual experiences, designers used different approaches and sometimes even different data sets, leading to a variety of design procedures.

Objective and domain: The ES venture was initiated to capture the expert knowledge, heuristics, and subjective decision making process of the design engineers.

The ES was to provide the manufacturability perspective that was lacking. Decreasing the repetitive tasks would enable engineers to spend more time with complicated designs.

Initiation process: The ES approach was initiated in late 1989. The venture was championed by the Vice-President of Engineering. Other key people who were involved in selling this project to top management included the Engineering Director and the MIS Manager. A working prototype was developed to expose management to this technology.

Committee: There was no formal steering committee to oversee the project. Those who were involved in trying to sell the ES idea were an informal committee.

Investment: An initial commitment by management was made through the purchase of an ES shell. There was no further commitment in terms of financial or human resources allocation after this.

Organizational issues: Management assigned a low priority to the project; it was felt to be more technical rather than administrative in nature. Also, at about this time, the champion of the project quit the company – unhappy with the lack of commitment. The highly political climate within the company made even the identification of experts a problem. The lack of standards also made matters worse, as too many subjective decisions were made by design engineers. It was hard to identify which was the right method as the design engineers could not come to any consensus. Right from initiation, the design engineers felt that their job security was threatened. Insufficient priority for the project resulted in its losing impetus. As this project was not going to be on the list of priorities in the MIS department it was further removed from the MIS department and transferred to the design engineering group.

Current status of the project: It was realized early that consensus on the expertise would never be reached if the design engineers did not agree on the development procedures. The company decided to form a standards committee to formalize the process. To demonstrate the company's commitment, the chairman of the company became a part of this committee, along with a number of engineers from design, manufacturing, materials, and other related functions.

This venture now has a higher priority in the hope that it will be completed. Management has started to rethink their adoption approach. They have realized that their technology base must be established before such ventures can be initiated. Internal procedures are being established. This seems to be the first step in preparing to adopt ES technology sometime in the future.

## 3.1.3. Circular Spinners

This large automotive component manufacturer had 94 employees in their information system department; 70 were involved in the maintenance and development of corporate business systems, while the rest were in the technical center (R&D) involved in developing new systems for engineering and manufacturing. The raw material for their product is prepared by mixing various chemical compounds. This mix depends on the expected properties of the finished product, specified by the customer.

Motivation: Compounding the chemicals was considered to be extremely complex and was claimed to be more of an art than a science. Once the chemist prepares the compound recipe, Engineering builds a prototype and tests it to check if it has the desired properties. This is a trial and error process where a number of manufactured components are wasted before the final product is shown to have the desired properties and passed into production.

Objective and domain: The ES venture was initiated to capture the knowledge of the chemists in the compound mixing process and predict the properties of the product prior to it being built and tested.

Initiation process: The project was initiated in 1987; it was the second attempt, the first in-house attempt having been to optimize the schedule of the hot presses. This project was terminated, as the project “champion” left the company. Unfortunately, there is no formal documentation of this failure.

In 1989, a new project was contracted out to a consulting firm. Lack of skill level and manpower were cited to be the prime reasons for hiring a consulting firm rather than attempting the venture “in-house”.

Although, some of the people involved in the second project were different from those in the first, there was some overlap. Management believed that a great deal had been learned from the scheduling project and that the technology would work, even though the first attempt was a failure.

Chemical compounding was chosen as the ES testing area since it had a degree of subjectivity and “fuzziness”. These chemical compounds are a major part of the finished product. The venture was expected to reduce testing costs because fewer finished products would need to be physically tested.

Committee: There was only an informal committee consisting of the head of the Technical Computer Center, the Vice-President of Technology, Programmers, and Engineers.

Investment: Approximately \$100,000 was budgeted for hardware and software. Two person-years in programmer time was also budgeted.

Knowledge acquisition: Most of the knowledge engineering process was carried through contact with 3 compounding technicians (experts) in the mixing area and one in-house computer programmer. The computer programmer was the liaison between the compounding experts and the consulting firm who performed the knowledge engineering. The programmer had a background in chemistry as well. He was transferred in to help with the ES project.

System specification: The system was developed on UNIX workstations. Familiarity with the existing system and its integrative features justified the choice of the existing platform. The ES was developed using commercially available software. Neural networks were trained and included as part of the ES.

Current status of the project: This system was tested in all functional aspects and termed a success by the users. Management feels that Circular Spinners is the pioneer in the industry and that the ES will give them a competitive edge. They estimate the cost saving from testing alone to be nearly 40% of the present costs. Management feels that the venture has been a success.

## 3.1.4. Finishing Systems

Finishing Systems is a manufacturer of surface heat treatment equipment for the automotive industry. They service a number of automotive component manufacturers. Their products vary in size and are customized to satisfy specific requirements; the price range is between \$20,000 and \$400,000.

Motivation: Currently, customers in the heat treatment field use a Metals Handbook which provides them with different heat treatment settings for various component shapes and metals. This is complex, voluminous and requires knowledge of metallurgy. Client companies call in to describe the part characteristics required to get the furnace settings determined.

The client firms maintained a lean infrastructure often with no metallurgist. Thus client support requires both the information from the Metals Handbook and metallurgical expertise. Also foreign customers need to have American metallurgical standards translated to fit their local environments.

Objective and domain: To remain competitive by providing improved customer service, through a user friendly computer version of the Metals Handbook to be used by the furnace operator.

Initiation process: A forerunner project was initiated in 1987 to support the day-to-day operations of Finishing Systems's customers. It was to help reduce furnace wear by recommending alternate settings. Unfortunately, this attempt was shelved as it was considered to be too far ahead of its time in its industry.

Management now feels that the market is ready for such a system. It has initiated the ES venture to provide the customers with recipes of a particular component, its heat treatment, inspection, loading, and testing.

Committee: There is no formal steering committee. The informal committee consists of the Director of Technology, the Product Manager, and two programmers (engineers).

Investment: About \$100,000 has been spent each year for the past three years and a similar budget is expected for the next five years.

Systems specification: So far there is a semi-completed database version of the Metals Handbook, which will be the foundation for the ES. Object oriented programming software had been used to provide the graphical interface to the system developed in a third generation programming language. A number of different software platforms are expected to be used. Only WINDOWS environment will be used.

Current status of the project: Although far from being completed, management and developers are happy with the progress. Modularization has allowed measurable results. The venture is on schedule.

## 3.1.5. Exhaust Systems, Inc. (ESI)

Exhaust Systems, Inc. is a manufacturer of exhaust equipment for US, and Japanese automakers. It is a fairly large company under the umbrella of a larger multinational corporation. They sell to both original equipment manufacturers and the maintenance market.

Motivation: To stay competitive, their exhaust systems need to keep pace with the automobile models. Contracts from large automakers are awarded on competitive bids. Since large volumes are involved, reducing prices by a dollar per unit could enhance the bid. Synchronous manufacturing principles are necessary due to manufacturing constraints during the design. Two ventures were therefore initiated:

## Project I

Objective and domain: To organize the diverse product design information by material, labor, equipment, tooling, and other relevant costs and prepare a competitive bid rapidly. The Manager, Engineering Systems joined the company in 1987. He had attended a number of ES and AI seminars and initiated the ES project for competitive bidding.

Initiation process: This project was completed in one year. It was not accepted by the MIS department, as they felt that there were other projects with higher priorities that had to be completed first.

Committee: Although, there was a corporate level committee to explore new technology related issues, there was no committee within ESI to steer the project.

Investment: A budget of \$125,000 was allocated and spent. The Manager, Engineering systems, had the political clout to channel the resources. He was also willing to take the risk.

System specifications: The system was developed on a micro-computer using an ES shell. The MIS department had absolutely no involvement; the entire project was carried out by the Manager, Engineering Systems. Development on his micro-computer platform gave him more control and also allowed implementation on a smaller scale. Most of the product information required for a bid was already available on the mainframe system. This was downloaded to the micro-computer and used as part of the database associated with the ES shell.

Knowledge acquisition: The system consisted of approximately 100 rules. The knowledge was collected from 4 engineers. Where there was a lack of consensus, the Manager, Engineering Systems (an engineer, who had been in design for a number of years), designated the expert.

The system has been in operation for three years and has lived up to its established expectations. The utility of the system has been recognized and installed in the other departments.

Project II

Objective and domain: To automate the design process, including manufacturing constraints and developing a process plan for manufacturing (synchronous engineering).

Initiation process: The success of the first ES venture created a general awareness of technology. The committee that assessed technology at the corporate level approved this second venture based on this success.

The proposal projected the benefits using soft numbers for intangibles. Successful development and implementation should initiate transfer of this system to other companies within the parent corporation – both within and outside the United States.

Committee: The Technology Assessment Committee at the corporate level consisted of engineering upper management, the systems development group, manufacturing, and R&D from a number of plants under the corporate umbrella. These committee members met once a month to discuss common issues on technology. The Manager, Engineering Systems, was on this committee.

Two committees, the Executive Committee and the Worker Bee Committee, were formed within ESI. The first consisted of V.P. Engineering, V.P. Manufacturing, Plant Controller, Manager (Engineering Systems), Director of MIS, and the Financial Adviser. The other committee consisted of Manager (Engineering Systems), Design Engineers, Manufacturing Engineers, MIS Project Manager, Systems Manager, a Vendor representative and a project leader.

Investment: An amount of 10 million dollars was estimated to be spent over the project life of about 5 years.

System specifications: The system is to be developed on a microcomputer workstation. Since ESI did not have the needed resources, they decided to use a contractor for the development.

Knowledge acquisition: The Worker Bee committee was the Knowledge Engineering group. A systems development approach is provided by the MIS department and the software vendor/consultants.

Current status of the project: The project is into its first year of development and has resulted in a working prototype. This prototype will form one of the modules of the larger system. As each automaker's requirement is different, a customized module is planned.

## 3.1.6. Computer Engineered Systems

Computer Engineered Systems is the technology and product development center for the drive shaft division of a larger corporation. R&D and customer assistance activities are undertaken at this center.

Motivation: A system was needed to improve customer service and provide more flexibility in making a product match. A typical customer request will involve an inquiry about a particular type of drive shaft (usually specifying a required diameter, length, torque). This is usually directed to the application engineers; it is a time consuming process involving routine and laborious calculations.

In addition, a system was needed to perform the mundane activities while answering a customer call, and of using non-experts to answer customer calls.

Objective and domain: Better customer service and faster response time was needed. Some flexibility was also desired while trying to match customer specifications to the existing product line. There were a number of judgmental decisions that were made by the application engineers.

Initiation process: The project was initiated by the engineers. One of them wrote a computer program in BASIC to aid the function. At about this time, there was a product line change and this person left the R&D Labs. The department was then faced with the option of either rewriting the BASIC program for the new product line, a major overhaul, or start on a fresh development. An ES approach was used because one of the center's managers had been exposed to this technology.

A small working prototype was developed to show to top management. This was developed in the area of customer support; it helped telephone operators answer preliminary technical questions. The more complex “exception” call was directed to the concerned technical support personnel. This was a relatively small application but it served to convince upper management that the technology would work.

Committee: The committee that conducted the feasibility and requirements analysis included two application engineers (users), the Chief Engineer, the Group Manager of the center, and the Group Manager of engineering.

Knowledge acquisition: A modular development approach was taken. Organizational issues: The company disseminated technology to other plants under the corporate umbrella. While technology R&D and applications are targeted for the corporation, it may not be the sole customer. In fact the center behaves like a separate consulting firm, marketing cutting edge technology and it has therefore to remain competitive.

Current status of the project: The users seem very happy with the progress on and the potential of this project. ## 4. Summary of observations

An overview of the case studies is presented in Table 2. A number of special development features are identified:

\- The knowledge engineering entries identify the development vehicle as being internal or external. The methodologies identified include the Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC), Prototyping, and Advanced Manufacturing Technology (AMT) adoption, which is of recent vintage. AMT adoption incorporates procedures that address strategic, planning, and implementation issues dealing with new technology adoption.

\- The entries in the Organizational Environment column indicate the climate for new technology adoption. Specifically, the entries include:
1. Degree of top management support.

2. Presence or absence of formal committee structures.

3. The availability of well established technology adoption procedures.

## 5. Conclusions

We believe that the choice of firms in the same industry, their size, and their diversity in policies and procedures, allows us to make observations that apply to one type of industry.

The desire to be ahead of the competition was a consistent motivational underpinning of all of the firms. Two firms expressed this goal explicitly, while the others implied it by seeking shorter product design cycles, precision in costing of items for contract bidding, etc. All firms indicated that the preservation of subjective expertise was important in choosing the ES route.

The initiation of the project was usually by those closest to the activity, such as Engineers, Manager (Engineering Systems), etc. and rarely by the specification of a strategy by top management. Utility of the technology had to be “sold” to resource allocators/top managers in five of the six cases. This is not unusual for a new, emerging technology; management is unlikely to be familiar with it.

The education of top managers may be the single most important factor in gaining organizational acceptance and support. In fact, the initiators of ES projects themselves in all firms had been exposed to the possibilities of ES technology use through vendor seminars. However, the champions did not always have an organization wide perspective, nor an organizational agenda for ES introduction.

The role of champions in introducing leading edge technologies is well known. However, it should not be the exclusive vehicle for the introduction of ES technology, if the organization as a whole is to benefit.

Given the focused nature of ES technology application, prototyping of ES would appear to be the natural choice for exploring this technology within organizations. The absence of formal prototyping emphasis can be attributed to the lack of familiarity of ES initiators with IS prototyping technology and the significant absence of MIS participation in the adoption and development process. In fact the MIS group was unwilling to participate in the ES venture in most of the firms. This may be a cause for concern.

Further, contrary to the Meyer and Curley premise, useful ES ventures do not always address core functions, nor do they need to. Therefore, active MIS participation in ES development becomes important to provide coordination and prevent duplication and fragmentation around the organization. A possible reason for a less-than-enthusiastic response of MIS to ES deployment in manufacturing may be the unfamiliarity of the MIS management with the details of the manufacturing domain.

The description of the case studies of six firms in the automotive component manufacture industry helps to fill a gap in ES adoption literature.
