# Swords and plowshares: Information technology for collaborative advantage

Thomas W. Ferratt ${}^{a,*}$ , Albert L. Lederer ${}^{b}$ , Stephen R. Hall ${}^{c}$ , Joseph M. Krella ${}^{d}$

$^{a}$ Department of MIS and Decision Sciences, School of Business Administration, University of Dayton, Dayton, OH 45469-2130, USA

$^{b}$ Department of Decision Science and Information Systems, Carol Martin Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, USA

$^{c}$ MedAmerica Health Systems, One Wyoming St., Dayton, OH 45409, USA

$^{d}$ Greater Dayton Area Hospital Association, 34 N. Main Street, Suite 1441, Dayton, OH 45402-1942, USA

## Abstract

Competitors who view information technology exclusively as a strategic weapon for competitive advantage risk missing a valuable opportunity: the use of information technology in conjunction with competitors for collaborative advantage. Collaborative advantage is defined as the benefit gained by a group of participants as the result of their cooperation rather than their competition. The development of a community health information network by seven competing hospitals in Dayton, Ohio illustrates how information technology can provide such an advantage. It also provides lessons for information systems and other managers who might consider such a use of information technology for their organization. Finally, it suggests further research on the subject.

Keywords: Information systems; Competitive advantage; Collaboration; Cooperation; Hospitals; Community health information networks (CHINs); Case study

## 1. Introduction

Two dramatic movements in business and health care practice in the United States have motivated the examination of the development and use of information technology (IT) by competitors for collaborative advantage. First, business leaders no longer strictly seek to gain a competitive advantage independently, but also look for opportunities to collaborate with their competitors (e.g., see Hamel et al., 1989). Indeed, where competition alone does not meet the larger social good, one could argue that business and political leaders should promote collaboration among competitors. Perrow's (1992) provocative discussion of the significance of networks of firms that both compete and collaborate with each other suggests such an argument. Sharing information (e.g., on markets, technologies, and pay scales) is a characteristic of these firms.

Second, business and political leaders are anxiously searching for solutions to the skyrocketing costs of health care (Enthoven, 1993a). The cost of health care and how to finance it are worldwide concerns (Hurst and Poullier, 1992/1993). In the United States, health care reform has been high on the Clinton administration's agenda. However, business and health care leaders and others involved with the health care system have not been idly waiting for the government to enact its reforms (e.g., see Enthoven, 1993b; Shortell et al., 1993; and Christianson and Moscovice, 1993).

One critical requirement for the successful implementation of various health care reforms is the exchange of information among participants in the health care delivery system. Community health information networks (CHINs) (e.g., see Zinn, 1994, and Bergman, 1994) have been touted as a vehicle for exchanging clinical and financial information between hospitals, physicians, insurers, businesses and customers as suggested in Zinn's definition: “A CHIN is an integrated collection of computer and telecommunications capabilities that facilitates communication of patient clinical and financial information among multiple providers, payers, employers, pharmacies and related health care entities within a targeted geographic area” (Zinn, 1994, p. 28). In reality, CHINs of competing hospitals are a rare phenomenon. CHINs today predominantly consist, instead, of one or more affiliated hospitals and related physicians.

The development of the Greater Dayton Area Community Patient Health Information Network provides a rare opportunity to study the initial stages in the development of a CHIN of competing hospitals. It provides an opportunity to learn lessons that could potentially help competitors in other industries seeking ways to use IT for collaborative advantage. Although intra-industry partnerships have jointly developed IT or investigated its use (see the brief descriptions of a few such efforts in Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990), their experiences have not yielded a base of knowledge that could help guide competing organizations seeking to use IT for collaborative advantage.

This article next describes a vision of the use of IT for collaborative advantage. It outlines a research approach and then elucidates the development and accomplishments of an advanced communications network for sharing patient medical information among seven competing hospitals in the greater Dayton, Ohio, area. It presents lessons learned from this project. It offers caveats for IS and other executives contemplating a similar project and concludes with questions for future researchers.

## 2. Supporting collaborative advantage: A new vision for using IT

The vision of the role of IT in the organization has continuously evolved. In the 1950s and 1960s, this vision encompassed its uses for increasing operational efficiencies. In the 1970s, the vision was expanded to include its use to support managerial effectiveness through decision support systems. (For example, see Gorry and Scott Morton, 1971, and Bonczek et al., 1982.) With the advent and growth of personal computers starting in the 1980s, the vision of IT as an enabler of organizational efficiency and managerial effectiveness was expanded to include its use for individual and group productivity. (For example, see Kroenke and Hatch, 1994; Ellis et al., 1991; and Zigurs and Kozar, 1994.) Another vision of IT's role at the organizational level, its use as a strategic weapon for competitive advantage (e.g., see Ives and Learmonth, 1984; Johnston and Vitale, 1988; Miron et al., 1988; King et al., 1989; and Konsynski, 1993), augmented the previous visions beyond the improvement of the internal functioning of the organization to include the improvement of relations with such external entities as suppliers and customers.

The two movements described in the introduction – the need for collaboration among competitors and health care reform – suggest a further expansion of the vision of IT in the organization to include its use for collaborative advantage among competitors. Collaborative advantage is defined as the benefit gained by a group of participants as the result of their cooperation rather than their competition.

Using IT to support collaborative advantage among competitors is a relatively new phenomenon. Organizations have previously formed strategic alliances with others in their value-chain (e.g., see Johnston and Lawrence, 1988), and they have used IT to support these alliances (e.g., see Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990; Venkatraman and Zaheer, 1990).

More recently a variety of alliances have been categorized as joint marketing, intra-industry, customer-supplier, and vendor-driven (Cash et al., 1992). The new vision described herein has similarities to some of these alliances but does not fit cleanly into any of the four categories. For example, it is unlike joint marketing ventures such as airlines reservation systems in which one airline owns the system and permits its competitors to buy services from it. In fact, such systems are competitive weapons that have resulted in court action alleging that the owner used the system for unfair advantage. The dispute between Frontier Airlines and American Airlines as described by Cash et al. illustrates this. It is also unlike intra-industry partnerships such as the CIRRUS banking network where a separate corporation manages the transactions sent by rival banks to each other (Gifford and Spector, 1985). What distinguishes this hospital alliance is its focus on sharing customer information among direct competitors in a non-strategic partnership where the competitors themselves can access extensive, historical information about each others' customers on each others' own computers.

This new vision prompts questions about how IT may support such collaboration and how organizations may acquire the IT to obtain collaborative advantage. The next sections address these questions based on the development of the Greater Dayton Area Patient Health Information Network.

## 3. The research approach

The methodology used in this study includes elements of application description, action research, and case research (e.g., see Benbasat et al., 1987; Lee, 1989; and Susman and Evered, 1978). Three of the authors thus had responsibility for the development and implementation of the network. Two of them are health care executives who have been involved since the inception of the project. The third, a participant-observer, joined the project on a part-time basis soon after by attending meetings and preparing and reviewing documents. He subsequently joined the project full-time with responsibilities for coordination, evaluation, and documentation. He conducted interviews of about one-hour in length with 18 key participants at the beginning of this full-time involvement. The fourth author is an independent observer.

The authors reviewed and discussed the development and implementation of the network based on project recollections, documents, and notes. As a result, they were able to conceive and agree upon a number of lessons learned from the project.

## 4. A brief history of the case

In late 1992, the chief financial officer (CFO) at a Dayton hospital asked the medical records director if it were possible that doctors in the hospital could check on their patients in other local hospitals by using an electronic medical record. The medical records director discussed this question with the vice president for information systems (VP/IS) at her hospital. The medical records director and the VP/IS also discussed it with the president of the Greater Dayton Area Hospital Association (GDAHA). In January 1993, GDAHA's president brought the medical records directors and heads of information systems in six of the 19 competing area hospitals together to discuss the status of a computer-based patient record (CPR) in their hospitals and the possibilities for sharing access to CPRs across hospitals in the future as requested by the CFO.

Although everyone at the meeting foresaw the eventual implementation of a CPR, no one expected to develop a CHIN in the near future because it would require not only the participation of hospitals but also of physician offices, insurers, and others. A CPR shared among hospitals alone, however, would still allow more timely access to patient information than then available via fax, phone, or courier. Care providers, expected to be primarily physicians and nurses, would use a CPR to support their decisions about patient treatment, thereby leading to improved quality of patient care. For example, a CPR would support a substantially faster diagnosis and treatment, thus saving lives or promoting faster recoveries. At the same time a CPR would reduce health care cost by reducing duplicate or unnecessary testing. Cost reductions would also result from shorter hospital stays and recovery periods, which would arise from faster treatment due to the more timely CPR information.

However, operational and economic conditions were seen as limiting the feasibility of developing a CHIN in the near term. For example, the high cost of starting and operating such a network reduced the incentives for participants while the difficulty of quantifying its benefits increased their risk.

At the same time, hospital executives were not initially inclined to view a network to share patient information with competitors as a strategic initiative. Other investments were seen as typically more strategically attractive. Indeed, the idea of sharing information with competitors is usually considered particularly unattractive. As a result, hospital executives were not predisposed to provide resources and incentives to subordinates to build a CHIN.

In March 1993, however, Ohio Bell (now Ameritech) asked the vice president of information systems at one of the Dayton hospitals if he had any potential projects that Ameritech could use to demonstrate the value of a fiber optic network. The VP/IS proposed a large project involving six local hospitals sharing patient medical records. Ameritech offered to support this project by installing a fiber optic network to connect the hospitals and by funding the communication costs to use the network during a two-year demonstration project. Ameritech's interest stemmed from its desire for regulatory changes and its related commitment to the governors of the Great Lakes states to upgrade the states' communications services. It also saw the project as an opportunity to develop new business. Nevertheless, even with its willingness to invest in the transport facilities for the CHIN, the remaining time and equipment requirements were beyond the hospitals' budgets.

Therefore, the VP/IS who made the proposal to Ameritech approached IBM, a major technology supplier to five of the six hospitals, to interest it in the demonstration project. IBM then proposed to provide IT to support patient medical information sharing. Specifically, IBM would at least initially provide its Medical Records Plus/400 image processing hardware and software solution to store and display patient medical records. IBM viewed this contribution as an opportunity to establish market leadership and develop new business.

The Ameritech and IBM proposals were thus brought to the group discussing the CPR. The group concurred that the project would lay the groundwork for a CHIN by linking the hospitals. Learning about advanced communications and image technology was attractive to many of the participants. The project would also provide considerable technology and services at little out-of-pocket cost to the hospitals. The Community Patient Health Information Network task force was thus formally established under the auspices of GDAHA with approval of the hospitals' chief executive officers (CEOs), who agreed to participate only if the network were competitively neutral.

The partnership expanded slightly beyond the original six hospitals and two vendors as it began its collaboration. In May 1993, the University of Dayton joined the demonstration project. The University saw its early participation as an opportunity to conduct research and community service and to develop support for subsequent participation in the project. USAF (United States Air Force) Medical Center Wright-Patterson, a regional military medical center and member of GDAHA, became a part of the project. The parties committed approximately \$5 million for the project. They then began collaborating to design the Greater Dayton Area Patient Health Information Network. Planning continued in the latter half of 1993. The actual development of the network began in early 1994.

## 5. Project accomplishments

Achievements since January, 1994, include (1) interconnecting the seven hospitals (i.e., the original six plus Wright-Patterson Medical Center) over a leading edge fiber optic (SONET DS3), ATM(Asynchronous Transfer Mode)-switched network running through five Ameritech Central Offices in the Dayton area, (2) jointly developing and agreeing on a community-wide confidentiality policy for patient information and developing procedures to assure that patients can effectively exercise their right to maintain the privacy of their data, (3) installing IBM's image-based Medical RecordsPlus/400 computer systems in the seven hospitals to share images of patient medical records, (4) designing, developing, and implementing an interactive network query and retrieval function for easy end-user access to patient information by physicians and other authorized staff, and (5) sharing patient information among the seven participating hospitals.

The achievements of this project enable health care providers to have computerized access to a brief history of all patient encounters at any of the seven hospitals. With only two workstations installed in each hospital, the system permits end-user emergency physicians (who may practice in more than one hospital) and staff in emergency and medical records departments in the seven participating hospitals to access patient data. With the initial loading of patient encounter information, access to up to three years of patient encounters is possible. Starting in November 1994 (for most of the hospitals), a broad set of medical information (including, but not limited to, patient demographics and encounter description, history and physical, discharge summary, lab reports, radiology reports, and post operative reports) from each inpatient encounter is stored in an optical image database at the hospital where the encounter occurs. If the patient subsequently enters any of the seven hospitals for treatment, the network thus makes a virtual longitudinal medical record available to aid in treating the patient when an authorized end user queries the distributed brief encounter index and medical information databases in the seven independent hospitals.

The unique nature of these accomplishments is evident from recent (1994) CHIN conferences (e.g., in Phoenix, Atlanta, and Columbus, Ohio). Conference participants have talked considerably about building CHINs, but presented hardly any implementations. The few CHINs that have been implemented tightly constrict the meaning of “community” since they involve a single hospital or affiliated hospitals rather than competing hospitals. Focusing on the initial stages of the development of the CHIN in the greater Dayton area should be of value not only for those in other communities interested in developing CHINs but also for IS and other managers interested in the initiation of multi-organization information systems projects among competitors in general.

## 6. User reactions

The system is new and its use is voluntary. Thus it is enjoying a slow but enthusiastic adoption. The reactions from the first physicians to use it have focused more on its potential impact than its current impact. They have stated that the information from it has affected their medical decisions and although it has not yet saved any lives, it will. One physician noted to the authors that in a case of undocumented pain, it revealed that the patient had recently visited several hospitals and thus altered his diagnostic process. Another noted that it helped him by informing him that a patient had been treated for the same problem four times previously in the past year. A third called the system a “great gift” and did not want its slow adoption interpreted as an indication that his peers did not appreciate having the system. One physician commented that the system is so marketable that every emergency department in the country “would die for it!”

Because the system is so new, the currently accessible historical data is still limited. Still, some physicians confidently stated that the system will save time, reduce unnecessary tests, and improve treatment once data are available. Some also expressed their belief that with increased success in finding the accessible data, their peers will use the system regularly. One physician noted that this may take two or three years.

Use of the system will also increase with minor modifications to the system's design and with decreased downtime. While one physician noted the system design is fairly simple and even computer illiterates can use it, another said that navigating the screens can be difficult. Minor modifications to the design continue to be made.

To increase peer usage, physicians have requested more training. They have also asked for more historical data, particularly both inpatient and outpatient electrocardiograms (EKGs) to help treat chest pain. One physician asked that workstations be made more accessible. Some physicians have even expressed the vision of a staff fully dedicated to accessing network information for them.

Besides physicians, medical records staff use the system. They have expressed unexpected benefits to the authors. For example, a clerk can quickly print missing documents from the system rather than waste time trying to track them down. The staff in one hospital were reported to the authors to be “ecstatic” about this time savings.

Thus users are slowly but surely adopting the system. This represents an important accomplishment. However, probably the greatest accomplishment is that competitors were able to develop a collaborative system in the first place.

## 7. Lessons learned

The success of the Greater Dayton Area Community Patient Health Information Network confirms that IT can play a key role in enabling organizations to achieve a collaborative advantage. In doing so, it provides several lessons that focus on developing IT for such an advantage. We now identify and elucidate each lesson. These lessons apply not only to hospitals but also to other organizations, as noted at the end of each lesson.

## 7.1. The competition lesson

Competitors can find collaborative advantage in acquiring the IT to share information provided doing so gives no competitive advantage to any subset of collaborators acquiring the IT.

Hospitals compete for patients based on price and non-price factors. Non-price factors have been the dominant basis. Examples of non-price factors include convenience, product differentiation (e.g., specialized versus comprehensive service offerings), hotel services, and advertising (Higgins, 1989). Hospitals also compete for patients by competing for physicians. Physicians who find it attractive to practice in specific hospitals refer their patients to these hospitals. Finally, hospitals compete for patients by cooperating with organized delivery systems such health maintenance organizations (HMOs) and preferred provider organizations (PPOs).

Competition is important to the Greater Dayton Area Patient Health Information Network hospitals. They agreed to participate in the project only if none achieved a competitive advantage over the others. This agreement underscored their belief that making patient medical information accessible did not put them at a competitive disadvantage. The belief stemmed in part from the view that aggregations of data from other hospitals' patient medical records – the only information that might provide a competitive advantage – would still be expensive to develop and of questionable competitive value.

If any participant believes that another participant is using the acquired IT for competitive advantage, a collaborative project among competitors risks losing collaborators or being cancelled. The competitive nature of the participants suggests that this risk is ever present. All businesses are typically reluctant to collaborate in an activity that might give their competitors an advantage.

## 7.2. The customer lesson

Competitors can have a collaborative advantage in acquiring IT for sharing customer data if service to customers is improved through sharing the data.

Each time a patient (i.e., customer) is treated at a medical facility, a record of the treatment is generated. A patient may be treated at a variety of hospitals and other medical facilities. Access to a patient's comprehensive medical history of such treatments allows a hospital to provide better service to a patient by permitting physicians, nurses, dieticians, and therapists to make better decisions. In non-emergency situations the hospitals, which own the data and have a responsibility to protect patient confidentiality, have established a policy of obtaining patient permission to share patient data; in emergency situations the hospitals may share the data without prior approval, as they have done with paper records, to facilitate treatment decisions. Better service also includes less patient time for completing forms, fewer tests needed, and quicker identification of appropriate treatment leading to faster recovery. Such improvements can attract patients to participating hospitals.

Hospitals currently use postal service, couriers, fax, and telephone to respond to queries from other medical service providers regarding patient medical records. Limitations in the method of requesting the data, the timeliness of the data, and the ease of using the data under such conditions reduce customer service. IT can improve this service, but it is costly.

Without collaboration among competing hospitals, significant portions of a patient's medical history would be unavailable; thus, hospitals desiring to improve customer service through the use of information owned by competing hospitals are highly motivated to collaborate. Improvements in customer service reach their maximum when all medical care providers collaborate to share patient medical history. Any advantage over non-collaborators disappears if all competitors collaborate, but the collaborative advantage from better service (e.g., faster patient recovery and lower health care cost) for all competitors over their initial position remains.

In the case where all competitors collaborate, the advantage can still translate into increased customers for all competitors if improvements in customer service make the product or service provided by these competitors appealing to individuals who had not previously been customers anywhere. Service to customers today has become so important to virtually all business organizations that more and more might be willing to risk sharing customer data to improve service.

## 7.3. The supplier lesson

Competitors can have a collaborative advantage in acquiring IT for sharing data if collaboration provides suppliers (e.g., physicians) with data that will help the suppliers better perform their jobs.

Unlike nurses or dieticians, physicians are generally not employed by hospitals. They act more as suppliers, vendors, or independent contractors. By practicing in competing hospitals, they are similar to manufacturers' representatives who sell products for competing firms. A hospital that makes it easier for the physician to access and use more complete patient medical data at the time of treatment will help the physician work better, thereby providing better service to patients.

Hospitals that collaborate may have an advantage over those that do not if they provide physicians with more extensive patient medical histories. Physicians may be more inclined to refer patients to those hospitals that make it easier for them to make treatment decisions.

In other competitive organizations that choose to collaborate, providing suppliers with accurate and useful information can be a source of collaborative advantage. The customer-resource life cycle has been used to illustrate numerous examples of efforts by individual organizations to gain an advantage through information exchange with suppliers (Ives and Lear-month, 1984).

## 7.4. The purchasing lesson

Competitors can find collaborative advantage in the acquisition of the IT for sharing information through reduced purchasing costs.

Major IT components for this project consist of the ATM switch Ameritech installed at a central site, fiber optic cable to connect each hospital to the switch, the medical records imaging hardware and software IBM installed in each hospital, and the software for querying patient medical information across hospitals. Such IT may be obtained at a lesser price through the negotiating power of the combined collaborators. Furthermore, collaborative acquisition may eliminate costly components that would be unnecessarily duplicated if acquired independently or by subgroups.

In fact, the cost for the components of an advanced information and communications network may be so high that network development is infeasible without collaboration. Collaboration with enough competitors allows the fixed costs of a network to be spread over the collaborators, thereby bringing down the cost for any individual organization to a level that makes the network feasible. In fact, the cost of large-scale information technology is sufficiently great that many organizations would probably be pleased to reduce their own costs through joint purchasing agreements with other organizations.

## 7.5. The incentives lesson

Where the incentives for collaboration to share information do not exceed the incentives for competition, project initiation is more likely if those desiring collaboration provide incentives for it.

When strategic analysis suggests putting capital investments into competitive projects rather than collaborative ones, executives are more likely to fund the competitive ones. While sharing patient medical data among competing hospitals has value, its competitive value is not particularly visible. Other projects may have greater priority. Requiring each hospital to invest hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars to build this collaborative network would have doomed it.

Those interested in building an IT network that supports the sharing of information among competitors will more likely achieve the benefits they desire if they provide incentives for collaboration. Internal visionaries, vendors, government policy makers, or foundations may share such an interest. In this case, vendors saw their investment as an opportunity to achieve their business objectives. This investment significantly lowered that necessary from the hospitals. Medical records and information systems directors saw the development of the project as a step toward developing a CHIN, which they believe is important. Hence, with their interest and the vendor investment, they had incentive to seek from their CEOs the relatively small amount of internal funding needed to begin the project.

Hospital administrators are certainly not alone in needing incentives to take the risk of an investment in a new information technology. Other organizations frequently attribute the success of their initiatives to the project champions who can identify such incentives.

## 7.6. The leadership lesson

With no central authority in a collaborative IT project, project success is more likely if each competitor provides a project leader with a common understanding of the project and a willingness to allocate resources to it.

In single-organization projects, managers typically have the authority to allocate resources, monitor and control their use, and resolve conflict. In a multi-organization project with resources distributed throughout many organizations, no manager wields such authority.

In this project, each vendor had a project leader responsible for monitoring and controlling its part of the project. Each hospital had its own medical records and IS directors as project leaders who assigned individuals to serve on project teams. These leaders served together as an overall project steering committee with an elected chair. The president of the hospital association, who also served on the committee, set up meetings of the committee and also helped keep hospital chief executive officers informed.

All of these leaders had a common vision and understanding of the goals and major phases of the project. The project also appealed to the personal, professional, and organizational goals of the medical records and IS directors. The history of each hospital's activities toward mutually agreed upon project goals and schedules suggests the need for such project leaders from each organization to keep the project on track.

At the same time, certain leaders – the vendor project leaders, the steering committee chair, and the hospital association president – emerged as project champions with broader project responsibilities. The history of their activities, including advocacy of the project to acquire the needed resources and maintain a common project vision and timely movement toward project goals, suggests the need for such project champions to represent the major organizations sponsoring the project. As with the Incentives Lesson, most businesses value the leadership that such champions can provide for their projects.

## 7.7. The priority lesson

If a collaborative project has a relatively low priority for a competitor, project leaders in that firm can benefit from outsiders' help to keep their team members committed.

Since the demonstration project had a relatively low priority for hospital CEOs, the project leaders in each hospital may not have monitored and controlled the project as actively as a higher priority project. As a result, the expectation of meeting the project schedule may not have been as great among the staff assigned to it. Even though leaders had incentives to participate, they also had other commitments with greater priorities.

No particular monitoring and control system within each hospital helped keep the hospital leaders on schedule. The project champions, whose overall project leadership responsibilities included expectations for them to place a higher priority on this project, helped keep the hospital leaders and their staff focused on the schedule. Some of this help came from monthly meetings of all the hospital leaders. These meetings of outsiders and peers from competing organizations discussed issues that arose between meetings and reviewed progress toward implementation.

The testing and training committees further illustrate that project leaders may need help from others outside the organization to keep team members committed to the project schedule. When some hospitals fell behind schedule for testing and training, the expectations of peers from competing hospitals provided an extra incentive for them to return to the schedule. In other business organizations, it is not unusual to learn about the new information technologies that competitors are implementing. Professional association meetings facilitate discourse on the subject. As such, peers can influence the plans and priorities of their competitors.

## 7.8. The $R\& D$ lesson

Competitors can find it easier to initiate a collaborative project seen as a research and development system than one seen as a production system.

Participants have different expectations about a research and development (R&D) project than one that will immediately serve a business need. For example, they expect an R&D project to have less demanding requirements, a looser schedule, and fewer unfavorable outcomes if it fails. As a result, an R&D project is less threatening or risky to initiate whether the setting is competitive or collaborative.

Collaboration among competitors may be seen as threatening or risky regardless of the project. Anything that adds to the threat or risk should probably be avoided. Thus, a project that promises to be R&D may help reduce the perceived risk of collaboration. In this case the project was presented as a demonstration project with opportunities to learn about working together and new technologies. In many businesses, expectations for the success of an R&D project may be lower but potential payoffs may be much greater. As such, the R&D Lesson can probably be quite applicable to them.

## 7.9. The history lesson

Collaborative advantage can be easier to obtain after competing participants first learn to collaborate.

Competing hospitals in the Dayton area have had a history of collaboration through GDAHA. The areas of collaboration have included purchasing, recruiting, and training. Medical records directors have had a history of meeting together as have information systems directors. Working together on this IT project may be easier given this history. Perhaps other communities have not yet implemented CHINs to achieve collaborative advantage because of the difficulty of first learning to collaborate.

Hospitals, moreover, are not the only competitors who collaborate. Pharmaceutical manufacturers collaborate to produce new drugs. Automobile manufacturers collaborate to research new vehicle designs. Even arch-competitors, IBM and Apple, have collaborated to develop a new PC chip.

## 8. Conclusion: caveats and questions

Collaboration among competitors is not a common phenomenon. Still, it is not unheard of. Competing manufacturers collaborate to conduct research for and to develop new products. Competitors join professional associations and share their knowledge. Even charitable organizations compete for contributions but still manage to cooperate in sharing them (e.g., through the United Way).

Collaboration in health care can be especially fruitful. Price competition, though a powerful rein on rising costs in other sectors, is not viewed as a promising means for reducing the rising costs of health care. The use of IT is. To deliver lower cost service to patients, e.g., by avoiding costly duplicate tests, current competitors must share the patient's fragmented medical record to achieve a collaborative advantage.

However, the vision that IT can support collaborative advantage among competitors is a relatively new one. It represents an expansion of the evolving vision of IT's role. This new vision requires new understanding and new lessons on how to develop these systems. This study has provided a rare opportunity to develop lessons from experiences in building a community health information network.

The nine lessons presented cover a variety of areas. They suggest antecedents for the development of IT for collaborative advantage, e.g., that the system be competitively neutral, that the competitors have a prior history of successful collaboration, and that those wanting to develop the system provide appropriate incentives. They suggest that collaborative advantage through the use of IT may be obtained in several ways, e.g., through providing better service to customers, helping suppliers perform their work better, or lowering the cost of IT acquisition. Finally, they make suggestions for managing the development of multi-organization systems, particularly with regard to the need for leadership from multiple sources, maintaining movement toward project goals when faced with competing projects, and positioning the project as a low risk research and development project.

The multi-organization project serving as the basis for this study is a rich setting for developing this variety of lessons for IS and other managers who might consider using IT for collaborative advantage in their own firms. As a single case, however, its lessons must be qualified. Specifically, these managers should compare and contrast some salient characteristics of the hospital project with their own potential project before generalizing the lessons to their organizations. For example, they should consider the external and internal factors motivating a collaborative project, the necessary investment, the project's priority, its risks, and its expected benefits.

In addition to stimulating thoughts about collaboration for IS and other managers, the lessons derived from this case present an opportunity for academic researchers by suggesting questions for further study, such as: In what other industries is IT currently used for collaborative advantage? In what other industries do opportunities for the use of IT for collaborative advantage now exist? What constraints – behavioral, government, and otherwise – may limit the opportunities for the use of IT for collaborative advantage? For what other projects or other conditions are the lessons learned in this study valid? And finally, how can organizations generate ideas for projects using IT for collaborative advantage?

Thus, as a result of managerial practice and academic research, information technology can evolve beyond its role as a competitive weapon. This evolution does not mean that organizations will abandon the use of information technology as a competitive weapon. Instead, it means that in some areas organizations may find it to their advantage also to “beat their swords into plowshares” (Isaiah 2.4) through the use of information technology in its new role as a collaborative tool.
