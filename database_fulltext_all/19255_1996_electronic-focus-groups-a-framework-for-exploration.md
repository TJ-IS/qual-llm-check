# Electronic focus groups: A framework for exploration

Danial L. Clapper $^{a,*}$ , Anne P. Massey $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Marketing and Management Information Systems, Clarkson University, Potsdam, NY 13699-5795, USA $^{b}$ Department of Business Management, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, NC 27695-7229, USA

## Abstract

As computers and computer communication networks become an increasingly common fixture of public and private lives, they open up new possibilities for changing the nature of interpersonal communication. This paper is concerned with a particular type of group meeting: a focused group interview or focus group. While a review of the literature suggests that focus group practitioners and researchers are aware of and beginning to use technology to enhance their meetings, there has been very little research exploring electronic focus groups. Here we review the existing literature, suggest important dimensions in understanding the impact of technology, and then apply some of the insights gained from Group Support Systems and computer-mediated communication research to this specific type of meeting. Finally, we present a tool called Computer Mediated Dialogue (CMD) which was developed to enable exploration of electronic focus groups.

Keywords: Focus groups; Computer-mediation; Small group dynamics; Impact of technology; Group support systems; Group environment

## 1. Introduction

In the past, a meeting implied a face-to-face gathering of individuals, but now new technology is allowing groups to “meet” electronically, either in the same or at dispersed locations. A focus group has individuals who were brought together to discuss a particular topic. The group is not expected to reconvene and it is assumed that they have not have acted as a group in the past. Focus groups are, therefore, one instance of non-repeating group behavior [45]. The informal discussions, usually no longer than two hours, involve 8 to 12 individuals and are focused by a skilled moderator [38]. Generally, these sessions are observed by others and recorded on either audio-or videotape.

Focus groups are a common qualitative tool in a wide range of disciplines, including public and private policy making, business decision making and academic research $[15,17,23,30,33]$ . They may be used as a self-contained means of data collection or as a component of a larger program. Their usefulness covers a myriad of purposes: orientation to a new field, generating research hypotheses based on participants insights, stimulating new ideas and creative concepts, studying differences between populations of participants, developing questionnaires and surveys, and interpreting previous quantitative and/or qualitative results. For example, focus groups are frequently used to learn how consumers perceive a product or service. Recently, they have been explored as a group knowledge acquisition tool by expert systems developers. In this domain, focus groups of domain experts are formed to explore underlying motivations and approaches to problem-solving $[14,27]$ .

If successful, the transcripts of the group discussion reflect participants attitudes, opinions, experiences and perspectives. Morgan [33] suggests that while focus groups are useful for investigating what participants think, they excel at uncovering why participants think as they do. For a focus group, one is interested in establishing an environment that facilitates group interaction in order to uncover underlying beliefs [14]. In this sense, focus groups share a number of significant similarities with brainstorming groups [11]. Although brainstorming tends to discourage interaction and criticism, while focus groups rely on members' abilities to elicit information and assist each other in elaborating difficult-to-express thoughts, both are ultimately concerned with generating new ideas and encouraging creative expression.

## 1.1. Costs of focus groups

One of the most significant drivers for using technology to allow dispersed focus groups is cost. Typical costs can include: moderator salaries, rental of research facilities, payments to participants, travel, and tape production and transcribing. These costs can be substantial, particularly if one must hire an outside firm to run the focus group and conduct the subsequent data analysis. Only larger organizations may be able to afford these costs. The number of people can vary anywhere from 3 or 4 to 10 or more depending on the topic, the composition of the group, and the purpose of the research.

In addition, there are circumstances where the desired focus group participants are dispersed or hard to reach. Bringing together 8 to 12 busy individuals for a two hour face-to-face meeting – which may or may not include travel and its costs – is a significant barrier. Increasingly, technology seems to offer the ability to lower these barriers to conducting focus groups.

## 1.2. Current uses of technology for focus groups

The most common use of technology for focus groups has been teleconferencing – a conference telephone call – used as a substitute for face-to-face meetings [1]. Meeting via phone lines avoids travel costs. Videoconferencing is also used to allow meetings of dispersed focus groups. For example, Focus

Vision Network (of Santa Ana, CA), has developed videoconferencing technology specifically for focus groups [25]. However, due to the costs – \$3500 to \$5000 for installation of videoconferencing reception facilities and \$1450 for 21/2 hours of videoconferencing time – its use has been limited to high volume users, e.g., advertising agencies and major firms. Global Focus (developed by Clarion Marketing and Communications), also aids companies in implementing dispersed focus groups. FocusVision (of Newport Beach, CA) [31,34] also provides a commercial video product linking marketers and advertising agencies to focus group participants. While the videoconferencing market has shown exponential growth [16] and manufacturers are aggressively pursuing market share, its use has been limited primarily to those organizations that can afford to purchase it.

With the increasing prevalence of such commercial systems as Compuserve, Prodigy and American Online, as well as the increasing awareness among business people of public networks, computer-based systems hold a great deal of promise as a cost effective means for enabling dispersed focus groups $[2]$ . An example of using this type of system is provided by Boone and Kurtz $[3]$ , who described the use of such a system, based on Compuserve, by a cable television network (Nickelodeon) to compose a focus group of children. This was done to evaluate a new program that they were in the process of developing.

## 2. Technology and the dynamics of focus groups

The purpose of focus groups is to generate ideas, opinions, attitudes, and perspectives. Inputs of one member can prompt quick and sometimes innovative responses in another, and a genuinely creative outcome may occur. However, success is dependent on the creation of a non-inhibiting, synergistic environment in which group members are comfortable sharing ideas. There are a number of problems that can impact and determine a group's effectiveness: What role could technology have in overcoming these problems? Maybe the fact that the technology can provide anonymity to participants and change the dynamics of the group.

The ability of a GSS to provide anonymity to participants of a meeting is probably one of the most widely cited features to improving group performance. DeSanctis and Gallupe [8] suggest that anonymity reduces the inhibitions of the participants. Jessup, Connolly and Galegher [21] build on the deindividuating research of Festinger [12] as an explanation for the more uninhibited behavior. In their empirical investigation of this phenomena, they found that anonymity in an idea generating task yielded more solution clarifications, more questions about solutions, more total comments and more critical comments than groups working in non-anonymous conditions.

The tendency of some communication media to strip away non-verbal cues and information which is available in a face-to-face situation – and the impact of this tendency on the participants – has been the focus of a number of researchers [10,36,43]. Clapper, McLean and Watson [6] found that even when participants were not anonymous, the use of the GSS resulted in a decrease in the power of the group majority to influence a member. Information richness is defined by Daft and Lengel [7] as the ability of information to change understanding within a time interval. Media richness involves the media's capacity for immediate feedback, the number of cues and channels utilized, personalization, and language variety. A lean media is the opposite of a rich media. Current GSSs typically require participants to communicate via the lean media of a keyboard to public screen. Research has shown that this “textualization” of meeting communication seems likely to impact the nature of the ideas.

## 2.1. Technology and group composition

Small group literature suggests that the composition of a group can significantly impact the degree of interaction and thus the quality of group discussion. Intrapersonal variables include demographics (e.g., age, gender, race, etc.), physical attributes (e.g., height, general appearance, etc.), and personality. Such variables affect an individual's interaction propensity and impact others' perceptions of the individual. For example, research on gender differences suggests that men are more aggressive in group settings than women; women conform more to group pressure; women are more sensitive to emotions; and men are more confident about their perspectives [13].

Heterogeneous groups have been found to outperform homogeneous groups on complex tasks [35], produce more diverse ideas and a broader view [32], but at the same time they can experience group process difficulties [37]. Heterogeneity seems to promote the generation of ideas and productive discussions. However, communication difficulties can arise between diverse individuals. For example, wide gaps in socioeconomic background or lifestyle have been found to impact face-to-face interaction negatively [33,38]. An individual's beliefs about the characteristics of others, and expectations of how other members will act can significantly impact their behavior. Oftentimes group members may perceive each other to be different without exploring whether they really are. These perceptions can negatively impact the willingness of the group to discuss a topic together.

As such, the degree of homogeneity or heterogeneity of the group members has traditionally been a major factor in the selection and recruitment of participants for a focus group. It is generally composed of individuals with an attempt at “homogeneity in background” – with the hope that there exists a sufficient diversity of attitudes. This has led to focus groups being primarily constructed with homogeneity in mind to avoid negative dynamics. But this has resulted in the need for running multiple groups for each category of participant – increasing costs and time.

In addition, their are some positive factors associated with heterogeneous groups. Many problems facing organizations, both public and private, today are complex. Understanding and solving these problems requires bringing multiple individuals with various interests and perspectives together $[5,22,24,39,41,42,44]$ . In these situations, a heterogeneous group crossing boundaries of gender, race, and status may be necessary to gain other perspectives in order to develop effective policies and plans.

For example, IBM recently organized several groups for interactive brainstorming on the computer to understand employee requirements and concerns related to planning IBM's virtual office program. The groups consisted of individuals from different staff levels and status within the organization. Because employees were assured that their contributions were anonymous, the organization was able to encourage the staff's involvement in planning and respond to concerns that might not otherwise have been raised [18]. In a different setting, an author – detailing her experience of an online electronic forum – described exactly how this impact of the technology led to dialogue (and friendships) between individuals who would probably not otherwise have even spoken to each other [40].

## 2.2. Technology and controversial topics

A different problem for the focus group researcher is when the topic is controversial, sensitive, or ethical in nature. Electronic focus groups may provide a way of dealing with these controversial issues. Members of the group can challenge one another, generate new ideas, and potentially resolve conflicts. However, bringing a number of individuals with different perspectives together does not provide any guarantee that they will be comfortable sharing their ideas with a public group – particularly individuals they have never met before. Individuals may in fact be very apprehensive about communicating their ideas in a face-to-face setting. Obviously, some topics could invade the privacy of participants. Therefore, in order to gain access to a breadth of perspectives it may be necessary to form multiple focus groups with each group representing a separate category of participants or stakeholders.

In a recent study, Massey and Clapper [26] compared electronic brainstorming to face-to-face brainstorming. Groups composed of seventeen and eighteen year old undergraduates at a University discussed the issue of AIDS on college campuses. Results strongly supported the use of the GSS, as subjects said that they felt more comfortable suggesting ideas with the group, were less worried what others thought of them, felt more comfortable discussing sensitive issues, expressing strong feelings and negative feelings toward other group members when using a GSS.

Jablin and Sussman [20] suggest that presenting ideas orally can inhibit members of a group. Communication apprehension (CA), a broad construct originating from the communications literatures, would seem to offer promise as a means of understanding individual reluctance to contribute ideas in a group session. CA is defined as an individual's level of anxiety associated with either real or anticipated communication with other people [28]. Individuals with a high level of CA will avoid communication. And Jablin, Seibold and Sorenson [19] found that individuals with low CA produce more ideas than individuals with high CA. Although originally conceived as a personality trait, CA was later reformulated as a continuum with Traitlike CA and Situational CA marking the two extremes [29]. Traitlike CA is defined as “a relatively enduring, personality-type orientation toward a given mode of communication across a wide variety of contexts.” Situational CA is “a transitory orientation toward communication with a given person or group of people”.

Situational CA is of particular interest because it is closest to capturing the particular spirit of an interacting group at a particular time. According to McCroskey [29] and Buss [4], the causes of situational CA are novelty, formality, subordinate status, conspicuousness, unfamiliarity, dissimilarity, and the degree of attention from others. CA is expected to be higher in novel situations due to uncertainty about correct behavior. Both formality and subordinate status result in highly constrained acceptable behavior, and thus tend to raise CA. Unfamiliarity and dissimilarity with others tends to raise the CA. Finally, according to McCroskey, none of these factors can increase CA as much as being conspicuous in the environment.

The causes of situational CA are all of heightened relevance to focus group researchers. The groups, with no history or future, focus on a very specific topic for a short period of time – thus, members may feel more conspicuous than with repeating groups. In addition, participants are usually unfamiliar with one another and may be dissimilar.

Computer-mediated communication seems likely to ease the key causal factors for CA, including: subordinate status, conspicuousness and degree of attention from others. Even when a session is conducted in a non-anonymous manner, having the member's name appear on a screen next to an idea appears to be less likely to invoke a high degree of apprehension than when orally communicating.

## 2.3. Technology and the role of the moderator

The degree of structure imposed and the level of involvement by the moderator is generally a function of the topic and the purpose of the meeting. The moderator is key to establishing a creative, synergistic, non-inhibiting environment for the session. He or she stimulates discussion by encouraging interaction and maintaining focus on the topic without biasing the responses of the participants. It may be necessary for a moderator to follow-up on nonverbal cues (e.g., stopping in mid-sentence, eye gazing, and facial expressions) by probing signals which may indicate that a participant has more to say.

At the low involvement end, the moderator plays a minor role – the group “manages” itself. This approach is particularly useful for exploratory topics. At the high involvement end, the moderator attempts to control the discussion and the dynamics of the group-leaning toward a structured group interview. Sometimes moderators must put participants at ease, encourage interaction, and diffuse highly emotional interactions. While heterogeneous groups increase the likelihood of a variety of ideas, they also increase the possibility of conflict. The most apparent problem of high involvement is that the moderator may bias the group discussion, particularly for smaller groups. Conversely, the most apparent problem of low involvement is that these groups can be relatively disorganized. However, the blend of moderator and the group may be important, e.g. when dealing with children as participants, groups of girls have been found to be more comfortable with female moderators. Conversely, while boys are generally comfortable with female moderators, certain topics (e.g., male hygiene) lend to more open discussions with male moderators [38].

The anonymity and lean communication media provided by computer-mediated communication has the potential for changing the nature of influence dynamics in a small group, and hence the role of the moderator in managing those dynamics. Deutsch and Gerard [9] made a distinction between normative and informational influence. Normative influence is the process leading to conforming to the expectations of others, while informational influence implies that the members are influenced by the merit of the ideas being examined. Technology would seem to make it less likely that participants are swayed by normative influence. This should increase the likelihood that ideas will be evaluated based on their merit. Thus, it would seem that a role for technology in facilitating group interaction would be to aid the session moderator in encouraging the sharing of views potentially different from the group position while minimizing the negative results to the people who suggest these new ideas. However, what impact does the loss of nonverbal cues have for the moderator and other participants? Do other factors outweigh the loss of nonverbal cues? These issues have not been empirically explored in the specific context of focus groups.

## 3. A tool for exploring the impact of technology on focus groups

CMD is a tool designed to lower the barriers for researchers interested in exploring electronic focus groups. In keeping with the design goal of simplicity, the interface was designed to require very little training. Fig. 2 shows the screen that would be in front of each participant in the session. The bottom window is the individual input area where users can compose and review their idea prior to submission to the group by pressing the RETURN key. The CMD software running on each of the PC's will then detect, capture, and display it in the larger, upper "Public" window on the screen.

![](/api/attachments/5V9TAE7E/fulltext/images/944b0a3a2561643017c369f97a9d732912e90bb55d2d7166aebf16339f1df255.jpg)  
Fig. 2. CMD interface.

Ideas are displayed as participants submit them. Thus, CMD can be used for both face-to-face and distributed groups. All participants are able to view the most recent contributions as well as scroll back to view earlier contributions. The Question window provides the means for the session facilitator to ask the group a series of questions and lead it through the focus group process. Only the session facilitator has access to this window.

## 3.1. CMD features

To accommodate the researcher interested in exploring the differences between anonymous and non-anonymous participation, CMD can be used in either mode. In the anonymous mode, ideas are displayed with nothing identifying the originator of the idea. Another feature of CMD is the ability to conduct more than one session at a time, indeed up to ten independent sessions simultaneously – either in the same room or distributed.

CMD was also designed to allow the experimenter to capture both the time each idea was contributed as well as the source of the idea. This is possible even when the group is using CMD in anonymous mode. If a logfile is desired, the experimenter can choose between Identified and Non-Identified options. Whether each user's copy of CMD generates a logfile or not is determined at the startup of the program. So it is possible for the researcher to setup all the users to be without logfiles but for the facilitator to have one.

## 3.2. Requirements to run CMD

CMD requires IBM compatible PC's with minimal processor requirements, little memory and only one floppy disk drive. It requires the computers to be on a Novell Netware local area network. CMD should work well with any LAN hardware that runs Novell Netware (i.e., Ethernet, Token Ring, etc.).

If desired, CMD can be operated solely from floppy disks - it requires no installation on the server in order to be used and does not require users to be logged on to the network with userids. The advantage of this approach is that it allows this tool to be used on a local area network without requiring special userids on the network or a file server installation. This greatly reduces the complexity of installing and administering the software.

## 3.3. Availability

CMD is available via anonymous ftp from site omnigate.clarkson.edu in the /pub/gsstools directory.

## 4. Conclusion

Focus groups are a common means of data and information collection. If successful, these groups can provide a wide range of perspectives and creative ideas that can be used to formulate organizational policies and strategies or generate hypotheses and plans for further study. However, the successful use of these focus groups is dependent on the creation of an environment that facilitates group interaction.

We have examined the forces that work against creation of this non-inhibiting, synergistic environment and how technology may impact these dynamics. As a result, we have identified three dimensions that are useful in exploring the impact of technology on focus groups: the Composition of the Group, the Degree of Topic Controversy, and the Involvement of the Moderator. While technology support has been widely explored for other group methods, such as brainstorming, very little research is available on technology enhanced focus groups. Applying insights from the GSS and computer-mediated communication research, we suggest that computer-mediated technology will impact all three of these fundamental focus group dimensions.

Although communication technology may open up new possibilities, we do not propose that computer-mediated focus groups will or should totally replace traditional face-to-face focus groups. Rather, we would argue for the use of electronic focus groups as part of a repertoire of techniques. Technology may, in fact, change the nature of the focus group process and hence the outcome.
