# Exploring the effects of some display and task factors on GSS user groups

Choon-Ling Sia $^{*}$ , Bernard C.Y. Tan, Kwok-Kee Wei

Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore, 10 Kent Ridge Crescent, Singapore 119260, Republic of Singapore

## Abstract

This study examines the impact of public screen and task types on consensus change and influence equality in a GSS environment. The researchers employed a controlled laboratory experiment with 44 five-person groups. The independent variables were public screen type and task type. Two kinds of public screens were studied: common and individual. Two task types were examined: intellectual and preference. The dependent variables were consensus change and influence equality. The findings were that a common public screen promotes consensus change with the preference but not the intellectual task. Moreover, individual public screens and the intellectual task encourage influence equality.

Keywords: Group support systems; Task characteristics; Screen types; Group decision making

## 1. Introduction

Group support systems (GSS) provide groups with a repertoire of decision aids that help to remove communication barriers during meetings. Potential benefits that can theoretically accrue from the use of GSS include more optimal use of group member contributions $[20]$ , more equal participation among group members, and better outcomes $[13]$ . However, empirical studies on GSS effects have produced mixed results. Although some studies have reported benefits (e.g., $[6]$ ), others have failed to establish the advantage of them as decision aids (e.g., $[8]$ ).

Two factors that differed across empirical studies and which might have contributed to the disparity in results are GSS features and task type. Huber et al. [14] suggest that small differences in GSS features can contribute to significant differences in decision outcome. Thus, there is a need to examine individual features (e.g., anonymity, public screen, and electronic communication) to determine their impact [16]. McGrath [21] suggests that the task type confronting a group is a key determinant of decision outcome. Therefore, it is important to investigate how task type moderates the impact of individual GSS features.

The public screen is a GSS feature that has received little research attention. Although it plays a potentially important role in synchronising and providing a central focus for group work [3], its impact has not been explored. While the use of a common public screen (CPS) may help to focus group attention on key issues and encourage group consensus, the use of individual screens (IDS) to display public information may permit members to pursue their own trains of thought and alleviate domination by specific people. When confronted with tasks for which consensus is crucial, groups may benefit from the use of a CPS. When performing tasks for which domination is undesirable, groups may gain from the use of an IDS. Therefore, the appropriateness of each kind of screen may be contingent upon task type. Our study explores the impact of two kinds of screens (common and individual) and two task types (intellectual and preference) on consensus change and influence equality in group meetings.

## 2. Public screen

DeSanctis and Gallupe [7] identify the display of public meeting information as a GSS feature aimed at removing communication barriers during group meetings. Public information may be displayed either through a CPS, which is a large screen visible simultaneously to all group members, or IDS, which is asynchronously displayed on the terminals serving each group member. The design of multiuser interfaces, such as this screen has to consider the conflicting needs of the group and the individuals [24], because displays catering to the group and the individuals have to compete for the time and attention of the members.

Chidambaram et al. [4] argue that a CPS provides procedural support and promotes group cohesion. Other researchers contend that it increases group focus and attention on the task. However, a CPS that can be continually activated by dominant group members may not adequately address the demands of others. A CPS facilitates domination, which conflicts with the principle that more equal participation by group members enhances acceptance and responsibility for group decisions [12].

The provision of IDS may alleviate conflicts arising from competition to display information on a CPS. However, IDS have other effects on groups. Although group members using a CPS have been observed to look at each other infrequently, eye contact will be further reduced with an IDS. Group members no longer have a common focus of attention. They are able to complete their task without looking away from their terminal. This reduction in eye contact may increase mutual social distance, heighten depersonalization [31], and lower group consensus [28]. Thus, IDS may be able to promote influence equality only at the expense of group consensus.

## 3. Task type

Poole et al. [22] contend that the task accounts for as much as 50% of the variance in group performance. A task has numerous characteristics, some of which have been empirically studied: task equivocality (e.g., [5]) and task type (e.g., [25]). The latter has been recognized as a key factor affecting group decision outcome in GSS literature (eg. [10]). In a GSS empirical study, Tan et al. [25] found that task type has impact on consensus change and influence equality.

McGrath [21] offers a schema for categorizing task types. Intellectual and preference tasks are two important ones because they are commonly performed by groups. Intellectual tasks are problems with correct answers and require the cooperation of group members to arrive at more accurate judgement. In contrast, preference tasks require decision making on issues without right answers and are based on values and norms. Since group members may possess different values, the resolution of preference tasks is likely to involve conflicts. The contrasting nature of these task types has important implications for the use of different kinds of public screens during group decision making. Intellectual tasks require group members to participate according to their ability to arrive at high quality decisions. A CPS may be utilized by knowledgeable group members to bring group attention to their contributions, thus improving decision quality. On the other hand, preference tasks can be better resolved by eliciting the differing opinions of all group members. IDS may be more appropriate because it helps to prevent specific group members from imposing their values.

## 5. Research hypotheses

## 5.1. Consensus change

During a group meeting, group members usually attempt to win others to their positions [18]. When such influence is effectively used, the group moves towards consensus. Thus, a change is likely to occur as a result of the meeting. However, the kind of screen employed can moderate the magnitude of this change.

A CPS increases group focus and attention on task completion. It also promotes group identity and cohesion. Gray et al. [11] argue that individual group members who control the display of the CPS also control the discussion flow. Thus, the CPS provides a means by which a dominant group member can attract group attention and exert influence. When this happens, the group may move towards consensus at the positions advocated by the dominant group member. Therefore, a CPS facilitates consensus change.

In contrast, IDS are activated separately by individual group members. Its use desynchronizes the group meeting, because different group members may be looking at different information. Dominant group members are likely to have great difficulty attracting group attention to their contributions. With an asynchronous display of information, eye contact between group members is likely to reduce while their social distance is likely to increase. Greater social distance can lead to lower group consensus. Hence, IDS is unlikely to facilitate consensus change.

H1a: Consensus change will be higher in CPS than IDS groups.

Group members are likely to arrive at a decision for an intellectual task, based on shared logical criteria. Thus, the pre-meeting views of group members are likely to be quite similar. Since group members do not have much difference in their views, the group meeting is not likely to bring about significant consensus change. Hence, an intellectual task does not facilitate consensus change. In a preference task, group members use their personal values during decision making. Since different group members are likely to have different personal values, the pre-meeting views of group members are likely to be very different. Consequently, the group meeting provides a forum for group members to resolve their conflicting views. Thus, consensus change is likely to be high. Hence, a preference task facilitates consensus change.

H1b: Consensus change will be higher in preference than intellectual task groups.

## 5.2. Influence equality

Influence equality is likely to vary from group to group. The kind of screen employed may moderate the degree of influence equality. A CPS can be activated by any group member. A dominant group member may use it to exert a disproportionate amount of influence on others. Since meeting time is usually limited, less dominant group members may have little opportunity to exercise influence. Hence, a CPS does not encourage influence equality. Conversely, IDS are activated at the discretion of individual group members. With them, some group members are not able to dominate the meeting by continually projecting their views. Hence, IDS encourage influence equality.

H2a: Influence equality will be higher in IDS than CPS groups.

When group members are solving an intellectual task using shared logical criteria, they tend to be more willing to accept the views of others based on valid knowledge [15]. Moreover, they are more willing to compromise their views when decision making is based on information rather than personal values [27]. Dominant group members are less likely to dominate the group meeting. Thus, an intellectual task encourages influence equality. In contrast, the resolution of a preference task will involve personal values. Dominant members, who are likely to be more successful at projecting their views, tend to sway others towards their views. Thus, a preference task does not encourage influence equality.

H2b: Influence equality will be higher in intellectual than preference task groups.

## 6. Research methodology

A $2 \times 2$ factorial controlled laboratory experiment was employed. All meetings were conducted in a face-to-face setting. The group size chosen was five.

## 6.1. Public and private screens

The CPS was operationalized using a large common display where information was visible to all group members simultaneously. All group members could use this feature to display information from their terminal. A subsequent display on the CPS would clear the prior display. The IDS were individual displays on the terminals of group members. When a group member invoked this feature to display public information on an IDS, similar information would not be displayed on the IDS of other members. Thus, different information could be displayed on the IDS of different group members. Besides the public screen, all group members were served by a private screen on their own terminals, which permitted them to invoke GSS functions.

## 6.2. Intellectual and preference tasks

The intellectual task was Zigurs et al.'s International Studies Program task [32]. It required groups to score a list of competing applicants based on applicant attributes. These attributes included four personality test scores, gender, and prior travel experience. The preference task was Watson et al.'s Personal Trust Foundation task [28]. It required groups to allocate funds to a list of competing projects. The projects were based on six personality components: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, and religious.

## 6.3. Consensus change and influence equality

Consensus change was computed from the difference between the pre-meeting and post-meeting consensus. Pre-meeting and post-meeting consensus were in turn derived using fuzzy set theory $[23]$ , based on data obtained before and after the meeting respectively. Consensus change ranges from 0 to 1. A higher consensus change indicates more movement by a group towards agreement.

Influence equality was measured using geometrical distances [28], based on data collected before and during the meeting. It ranges from 0 to infinity. A lower score indicates a more even distribution of influence among group members.

## 6.4. Experimental procedure

All groups started with a warm-up task to mitigate problems related to the use of zero-history groups. Next, intellectual task groups were given example attributes and actual performance scores of 50 sample applicants. Preference task groups skipped this stage, because their task was based on personal values. After this, all groups were given training on the GSS features. Before the meeting, the groups performed their task in between two similar tasks. This served to reduce primacy and recency effects [2] for the subsequent group meeting. During the meeting, the group collectively performed the actual task. After the meeting, group members repeated the task. They also responded to a questionnaire on their age, sex, and experience working in groups.

## 6.5. The GSS and agenda

The GSS used was SAGE [29]. It consists of two programs: public and private. The private program collected inputs from individual group members at their respective terminals and sent them over to the public program, which either displayed them on a

CPS or on separate IDS. During the meeting, all groups had to follow an agenda that was designed to facilitate group communication [17]. It consisted of seven steps: problem definition, criteria definition, criteria evaluation, alternatives definition, alternatives evaluation, decision definition, and meeting conclusion. Groups were allowed to iterate among the steps. Discussion was allowed throughout the meeting.

## 6.6. The subjects

A total of 44 groups (11 per treatment) were involved. Subjects were information systems undergraduates from a large university. Their average age was 20. Approximately half were males and half were females. Most subjects had some experience working in groups. Subjects were randomly assigned to groups, which were randomly placed in treatments. This minimized the possibility of individual differences affecting the group decision outcome [9]. Subjects were given course credit.

## 7.1. Consensus change

The data for consensus change underwent a reciprocal transformation [30] to satisfy the homogeneity and normality requirements of the ANOVA test. A significant main effect due to task type and an interaction between task type and screen type were found. H1a and H1b were not supported. The interaction was further investigated using simple effects analyses [19]. With the intellectual task, both CPS and IDS groups did not differ significantly on consensus change (t = 0.33, p = 0.57). However, with the preference task, consensus change was higher in CPS than IDS groups (t = 6.4, p = 0.02).

## 7.2. Influence equality

A square root transformation [30] was performed on the data for influence equality to allow it to satisfy both the homogeneity and normality requirements of the ANOVA test. Significant main effects due to both screen type and task type were detected. IDS groups experienced more influence equality than CPS groups. Intellectual task groups also had more influence equality than preference task groups. H2a and H2b were supported.

## 8. Discussion

## 8.1. Consensus change

In intellectual task groups, the shared logical criteria (applicant attributes) provided to group members might have guided them in their pre-meeting decisions, thus producing high pre-meeting consensus (mean = 0.64). Conversely, preference task groups did not have shared criteria. They had low pre-meeting consensus (mean = 0.33). Thus, during the meeting, there was scope for group members to resolve differences.

When performing the preference task, CPS groups achieved higher consensus change than IDS groups. Dominant group members might have effectively employed the CPS to exercise their influence and move the group towards their positions. This would raise consensus change. In contrast, CPS and IDS groups performing the intellectual task did not differ on consensus change.

## 8.2. Influence equality

IDS groups reported greater influence equality than CPS groups. The CPS might have been used by dominant group members to exude a disproportionate amount of influence on others. With IDS, dominant group members would have greater difficulty in exercising their influence and would be more willing to accept the positions of others. Thus, influence equality would be high.

## 8.3. Implications for GSS use

Group consensus encourages commitment to the group decision. Since groups are often employed to solve preference tasks to enhance commitment to the group decision [1], a GSS that supports decision making on preference tasks should be designed to promote group consensus. When a GSS is used with preference tasks, a CPS which facilitates group consensus will be a useful and essential feature. By demonstrating an alternative way to help groups attain consensus, this study builds on the findings of an earlier one that shows how a consensus monitor can be employed to help attain consensus [26].

Groups working on preference tasks must be careful with the use of IDS, because influence equality can be attained only at the expense of lower consensus. Therefore, unless true group consensus is not critical, a CPS should be used rather than several IDS.

This study used experimental groups and an artificial setting. Attempts to generalize its findings to other populations and settings must be made with caution. It reinforces the suggestion by Huber et al. that small differences in GSS capabilities can lead to significant differences in GSS impact.
