# Comparison of end-user computing characteristics in the U.S., Israel and Taiwan

Magid Igbaria $^{a,*}$ , Moshe Zviran $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Programs in Information Science, Claremont Graduate School, 130 East Ninth Street, Claremont, CA 91711, USA $^{b}$ Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University, Ramat Aviv, Tel Aviv, Israel

## Abstract

This study examines the effect of national environments on end-user computing characteristics. Data were collected from American, Israeli, and Taiwanese companies. The participants were 156 U.S., 138 Israeli, and 86 end-users from Taiwan, all holding managerial positions in the financial industry. The results clearly demonstrate the existence of cultural differences among the three samples in terms of adaption and usage characteristics. Conclusions are drawn to guide management action; they emphasize the need for cross-cultural studies in the information systems field in general and in end-user computing in particular.

Keywords: Information systems; End-user computing; Cross-cultural examination; U.S.A.; Israel; Taiwan

## 1. Introduction

End-user computing (EUC) is a generic term for the involvement of end-users in performing the traditional computing functions of designing, programming, installation, and manipulation of information systems (IS) [40], originally motivated by the considerable backlog of user requests for the development of traditional computer applications [6,36]. EUC has experienced rapid growth from the early 1980s [8,23]. Moreover, with the increasing power and decreasing costs of PC technology along with the proliferation of powerful and user-friendly new software, EUC is expected to continue as a fast growing area in computer use, with projected annual growth rate of $50\%$ to 100%, compared to 15% to 20% for traditional applications [28]. Therefore, EUC is considered to be a significant force for change in the way information resources are organized, provided and used within modern organizations.

Since its introduction, EUC has been listed as an IS key issue for both IS and business managers. It has, however, changed from very high to low, as it is no longer seen as a separate entity from mainline computing $[3,5,10,17,24]$ . Nonetheless, due to its potential benefits, along with critical issues, such as data management, end-user training and education, coordination and control of end-user activities, planning, and evaluation and justification of activities, EUC is still considered to be a key IS issue $[2,4,15,38]$ . Moreover, approaches to managing EUC can also be applied to implementing cooperative processing systems, field force automation, and consumer computing $[44]$ . Therefore, IS research has been investigating the acceptance of this technology and assessing the effectiveness of end-user applications [1,14,16,22,39,45].

The tremendous growth of microcomputers and EUC is not restricted to the United States. However, almost all empirical EUC research has been conducted in North America, using American subjects. Yet, the applicability of the findings in other cultures is unknown as theories in sociology, psychology, and organizational behavior suggest that a theory that applies in one culture does not necessary apply in other cultures $[26,27]$ . Therefore, it is suggested that in addition to user, organizational, and system variables, EUC research should also examine cultural differences $[21]$ .

This has been further emphasized in a number of recent studies $[31,34,35]$ , motivated by a recognition that international IS and cross-cultural effects will take on greater importance in many organizations. As technologies have made the world much smaller, global IS became a new issue $[41]$ . However, international issues are discussed only occasionally in the IS journals $[11,12,20]$ . In a recent article, Ein-Dor and Segev concluded that “little is really known about the effects of cultural environment on IS in general and on EUC in particular” $[19]$ .

This study addresses the gap in an attempt to gain more understanding of the EUC phenomenon in different national environments and to identify similarities and differences. It examines three countries: Israel, Taiwan, and the U.S.A.

## 2. Cross-cultural studies in IS and EUC

Cross-cultural research suggests that cultural differences across international user communities may be converging. However, empirical research has found significant differences: Niedleman [42] reported on cultural differences between France, Germany, and the Netherlands in usage of computers in small and medium sized organizations; Couger et al. found major demographic and motivational differences between samples from four countries: Austria, Israel, Singapore and the United States; Tricker [47] conducted a cross-cultural research between Chinese business, operating in Taiwan, Singapore, and

Hong-Kong, and Western management and IS practices. He found that language differences are a critical issue and concludes that “to understand information, one needs to recognize the cultural dimension”. Results of these examples are further supported by Hofstede’s work, he concluded that research in one culture may not be generalizable to another.

Several studies examined EUC environments; e.g., Ghani and Al-Sakran examined computer adoption in Saudi-Arabia; Igbaria investigated microcomputer usage in Taiwan; and Lu, Qiu and Guimaraes examined the use of computer applications in China. All of these focused on one culture and no cross-cultural comparisons were conducted. Only a few studies have addressed the similarities and differences between cultures $[19,33]$ , focusing on the United States and Israel. Ein-Dor and Segev examined similarities and differences in terms of organizational environment (budget, size, number and end-users), EUC environment (distribution of hardware use, packages used and sources, location of data, EUC budget sources), CEO use and level of use. Their study found significant differences between Israel and the U.S. Igbaria and Zviran found similar differences in microcomputer usage and demonstrated that cultural differences exist in terms of manager's adaption to EUC systems and that the success, as reflected by satisfaction and overall use, is the product of a complex network of individual and organizational characteristics and attitudes.

## 3. Research dimensions

This study extends previous research by examining EUC acceptance by individuals, focusing specifically on individual characteristics (demographic variables, such as age, gender and education, and end-user experience and training), organizational characteristics (support, assistance, encouragement by top management, and information center staff), perception and attitudes of end-users about the effectiveness and usefulness of EUC, satisfaction, and finally end-user acceptance.

The propositions are developed on the basis of the Hofstede's Culture's Consequences theory [25], which attempts to be specific about the cultural elements of and the motivation model. He identified four main dimensions: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism versus collectivism, and masculinity versus femininity, from 40 countries. According to him, the American culture is characterized by high individualism and relatively lower power distance and uncertainty avoidance; Israel is characterized by low power distance and relatively higher uncertainty avoidance; and Taiwan's culture is characterized by low individualism and relatively higher uncertainty avoidance.

Several studies have examined the effect of culture on motivation and found cultural differences that warrant attention in investigating international IS issues. For example, Couger et al. found major demographic differences between U.S., Israeli, Austrian, and Singaporean IS professionals; the latter were younger and less experienced. Singaporean and Israeli IS professionals are better educated. Israeli responses were higher on goal clarity and feedback-on-goal-accomplishment, but lower on goal-difficulty and task-significance. Further, Couger found that U.S. computer professionals perceive their jobs to be more challenging than those in Singapore. He also found that while the growth needs for males in both countries are similar, they are measurably lower for females in Singapore.

Demographic, organizational characteristics, beliefs, and usage were the four dimensions in the propositions.

## 3.1. Demographic and individual variables

We, therefore, predict that Israeli and U.S. end-users will be older than their South-East Asian counterparts. Recently, the Taiwanese government developed a six-year national plan to foster the development of software expertise. Also, in 1979, the government sponsored the Institute for Information Industry to promote the use of information technology. As a result, more individuals are entering the IS field (an annual growth rate of 13 percent is expected). They will be young and well educated. They are also less experienced and require more training.

Apparently, a larger percentage of women in Taiwan are in the IT field than in Israel and the U.S. Also, Hofstede states that individualism is negatively correlated with training. Therefore, it is predicted that Taiwanese end-users have a higher percentage who are females.

## 3.2. Organizational characteristics

Organizational support (i.e., management and information center support) was found to have a strong impact on EUC acceptance [30]. The individualistic U.S. and Israeli cultures reflect the way people live and the emphasis on individuals. On the other hand, the Taiwanese culture is more collectivist. These differences have important implications for the introduction of new technology. Taiwanese individuals may try to help each other to 'maintain harmony in society. Further, Americans and Israeli end-users expect the organization to support them. Also individuals from high power distance indicator (PDI) countries tend to be less supported. Therefore, we believe that U.S. and Israeli end-users will expect to receive less organizational support than those in Taiwan.

## 3.3. Beliefs and attitudes

American culture emphasizes individual self-sufficiency as an extreme of the individualistic dimension and most theories of motivation reflect a decidedly individualistic perspective. Individuals in the U.S., therefore, focus on their own future gain and personal utility. Building on Davis' work [13], systems that help individuals perform their jobs are more likely to be received favorably. Therefore, we expect that end-users in the U.S. would have a more favorable attitude and belief toward a system than those in Israel and Taiwan.

## 3.4. Usage

Americans have more access to new technology, particularly to EUC environments than their Israel and Taiwanese counterparts. Since individualism has an impact on modernization and introducing new technology into individualistic countries and American end-users are more individualistic, they are more likely to use a system that helps them in performing their job better. Therefore, it is predicted that the computer usage level in the U.S. will be the greatest.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Procedure and sample

The study was conducted during 1989–1990. $^{1}$ Despite the geographical distance and the very many differences between Israel and U.S., both countries were found to have a great deal in common in terms of the four dimensions, while Taiwan, which has been striving to obtain a greater share of the international information technology market, represents the South-East Asian culture. Managers from ten companies with extensive operations in Israel, 15 companies in Taiwan, and seven companies in the U.S. representing banking and insurance industries agreed to participate in our study of EUC. The decision to address only managerial positions and focus on the financial industry was made in order to reduce a possible effect of occupation and industry. Data were collected by means of questionnaires. Contact persons in the participating organizations were asked to distribute the survey to managers who used computers for their jobs. Participation was voluntary, and participants were assured that their individual responses would be treated confidential. The survey was accompanied by a cover letter from a company official strongly encouraging participation, as well as a postage-paid envelope in which managers were to return their completed survey directly to the researchers or contact persons.

The questionnaire was translated from English into the participants' language (Hebrew for the Israelis and Chinese for the Taiwanese) by professional translators, and then back-translated into English by another professional translator. This process helped ensure the cross-linguistic validity of the survey instrument. It was cross-validated in each of the country samples. This analysis is presented later and indicated that the validity tests and procedures supported the pan-culture validity.

Through this procedure, 240 Israeli end-users from the ten companies, 86 Taiwanese end-users from the 15 companies, and 230 American end-users from the seven companies were selected. All participants had free access to PCs and EUC technology. Returned survey were received from 138 Israeli end-users (57.5% response rate), 86 Taiwanese end-users (100% participation), and from 156 American managers (67.8% response rate). The high response rate among the Taiwanese end-users may be because participants were strongly encouraged by top management to respond to the questionnaire. The exclusion of responses received from non-end-users from Taiwan resulted in a final sample of 379 end-users (138 from Israel, 85 from Taiwan, and 156 from the U.S.). Of these, 104 (27%) were women. The mean age of the end-users was 37.5 (SD = 7.63) and more than 70% of the participants had a college degree. They held positions in a wide range of functional areas, including accounting, finance, information systems, marketing, sales, human resources, and general management. ## 4.2. Measures

Country, age, and education. Country was entered as a pre-coded digit (1 = U.S.; 2 = Israel; 3 = Taiwan) that was part of the employee research number located on the cover page of the end-users' survey. Gender of respondent was coded (1) for men and (2) for women. Age was measured in years, and education, reflecting the highest level of education completed, consisted with three levels: (1) completed high school; (2) Bachelor's degree; and (3) graduate or professional degree. All were included in the background information section of the survey.

Computer experience and end-user training. Computer experience was assessed by six items asking end-users to indicate the extent of experience they had in using different types of computer systems, computer packages, computer languages, building models, and technical and non-technical development of computerized information systems (see Table 5). The response options ranged from “1” (none) to “4” (extensive). The responses to the six items were averaged to produce a computer experience index. End-user training was measured by managers’ responses to four questions which asked them to report the extent of training in using the computerized information systems they had received from four sources: college courses; vendor training; in-house training; and self-trained. The response ranged from “1” (none) to “4” (extensive) [32]. The mean of the responses to these four questions was used as an indicator of computer training.

Organizational support. The measure of organizational support incorporated two broad categories of support: (1) information center, which includes activities such as training, consulting, software evaluation, and troubleshooting; and (2) top management encouragement [29]. End-users indicated their agreement or disagreement on a five-point scale ranging from “1” (extremely disagree) to “5” (strongly agree), with eight items concerning the degree of IC support as well as management support. A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced two factors, as expected, with eigenvalue $\geq$ 1.0 that accounted for 62.5, 60.0, and 65.1 percent of the total variance for the U.S., Israel, and Taiwan sample, respectively. Factor 1, labeled “IC support,” included four items. Responses to the four items were averaged to create a scale tapping the IC component of organizational support. The internal consistency reliability of the scale as used in the U.S., Israel, and Taiwan sample was 0.75, 0.78, and 0.77, respectively. Factor 2 represented the “Management” component of organizational support and included the remaining four items. These items were averaged to create a scale tapping the management component of organizational support (alpha = 0.76, 0.75, and 0.84, for U.S., Israel, and Taiwan, respectively). The two dimensions of organizational support – IC and management – were examined separately in all subsequent analyses.

Perceived usefulness toward EUC. This measure assesses an end-user's perception toward personally using a computerized information system application effectively in accomplishing job-related tasks $[13,29]$ . The items used to construct the perceived usefulness scale were modified to apply to EUC specifically. End-users were asked to indicate the extent of agreement or disagreement with eight statements concerning EUC on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from “1” (strongly disagree) to “5” (strongly agree). The items were recoded such that high scores reflected favorable usefulness perception toward EUC. Factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced a single factor solution with an eigenvalue of 3.83, 3.99, and 5.15, which accounted for 47.9, 49.9, and 64.3% of the explained variance for the U.S., Israeli, and Taiwanese end-users, respectively. Therefore, the eight items were averaged to obtain a measure of an overall index of perceived usefulness toward EUC. The alpha reliability coefficient of the measure was 0.75, 0.82, and 0.92, respectively.

End-user satisfaction was measured by a 12-item scale developed by Doll and Torkzadeh. The scale is a measure of overall EUC satisfaction as well as satisfaction with the extent to which the computer application meets the manager's needs and requirements with regard to information content, accuracy, output, format, ease of use, and timeliness. Each item was measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from "1" (almost never) to "5" (almost always). A factor analysis (with varimax rotation) produced a single factor solution with an eigenvalue of 6.20, 7.25, and 6.92, which accounted for 51.7, 60.4, and $57.6\%$ of the explained variance for the U.S., Israel, and Taiwan end-users, respectively. Therefore, the 12 items were averaged to obtain a measure of an overall index of end-user satisfaction (alpha = 0.92, 0.94, and 0.94, respectively).

End-user acceptance. Three indicators of EUC acceptance were included in this study: (1) frequency of use; (2) number of tasks; and (3) number of applications. Frequency of use was measured by a single items adopted from Igbaria et al., which asked managers to indicate the how often they used the computerized information system on a six-point scale ranging from “1” (less than once a month to “6” (several times a day). The measure of tasks included eight tasks for which the computer was utilized, such as making decisions, looking for trends, planning, and budgeting. The managers were asked to indicate whether they personally used the EUC system to perform these tasks. A yes/no response format was adopted, and the sum of the eight items represents the overall index of tasks. The measure of applications included ten applications or packages (e.g., word processing, spreadsheet, data management, modeling systems, statistical packages, and others). End-users were asked to indicate whether they used these features to perform their jobs. A yes/no response format was adopted, and the sum of the ten features represents the index of features.

## 4.3. Data analyses

As indicated earlier, there were significant relationships between country and demographic variables (gender, age, and education) among the respondents. Therefore, it was necessary to control for demographic variables in all analyses so that conclusions regarding cultural differences were not confounded by differences in their gender, age, and education. Since a major objective of the study was to examine cultural differences between American, Israeli, and Taiwanese end-users, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess whether variations in the culture and language were related to the study variables. ANOVA was also performed to test similarities and dissimilarities in terms of each of the applications, and each of the reasons/tasks by each of the end-users groups (e.g., American, Israeli or Taiwanese end-users). Similarly, we examined similarities and differences in each of the training sources and computer experiences among those three groups using ANOVA. In addition, we also examined the correlations between all the study variables and country (we created two dummy variables: Type 1 (1 = U.S.; 2 = Others), and Type 2 (1 = Israel; 2 = Others).

Based on the results of this initial analysis, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to determine whether the perceived usefulness, satisfaction, and end-user acceptance varied as a function of their cultural environment. The covariates to be controlled were selected on the basis of the initial ANOVA and correlations results. Since, our objective is to examine the acceptance of EUC by multinational environments, it is very important to control for all the predictors of EUC acceptance (demographic variables, computer experience, end-user training, IC support, management support, and perceived usefulness) so that conclusions regarding cultural differences were not confounded by differences in their demographic or background variables. Here, country was the independent variable, the covariates were the predictors, and the dependent variables were perceived usefulness, satisfaction and EUC acceptance.

They were less likely to use fourth generation languages and building models; this may suggest that Israeli end-users rely heavily on the MIS department in building IS applications.

Analysis of covariates (ANCOVA), controlling for demographic variables, were conducted to assess the pattern of variations in background variables (computer experience, user training, IC support, and management support) associated with countries. Results of ANCOVA showed similar results to those of ANOVA. This suggests that end-users significantly differed in their background beyond their demographic characteristics. In other words, in spite of the fact that we controlled for the demographic variables, there are significant differences in the background variables. This also suggests that these differences may be due to cultural and languages differences.

Previous EUC research showed that demographic and background variables are correlated with perceived usefulness, end-user satisfaction, and EUC acceptance ([43] and others). Consistent with our expectations, perceived usefulness is correlated with gender, computer experience, end-user training, IC support, and management support. End-user satisfaction is also correlated with education, computer experience, end-user training, IC support, management support, and perceived usefulness. Furthermore, all

EUC acceptance indicators (e.g., frequency of use, number of applications, and number of tasks) are correlated with computer experience, end-user training, IC support, management support, and perceived usefulness. Also, while age is positively correlated with frequency of use, and education with number of tasks and number of applications, gender is negatively correlated with number of tasks (females use a fewer number of applications than men). All EUC success indicators (e.g., satisfaction and acceptance) are correlated. Similar results were obtained when we controlled for country using partial correlations. In other words, the correlations were not affected by country.

The strength of the correlations was also examined by determining intercorrelations among the study variables for the three samples. Management support and encouragement among Israeli end-users had a stronger correlation coefficient than others with frequency of use, number of tasks, end-user satisfaction, and perceived usefulness. End-user training is also correlated with frequency of use. However, end-user training was strongly correlated among Taiwanese end-users with number of tasks, number of applications, and perceived usefulness. Results of the U.S. and Taiwan samples show that computer experience had stronger effects on frequency of use, number of tasks, number of applications, and perceived usefulness than the Israeli sample. In other words, the EUC acceptance level among Israeli end-users is more affected by management support and user training and less affected by computer experience than the other two samples.

## 6. Discussion

This study sought to extend previous research by examining EUC acceptance by individuals and compare EUC characteristics across three national cultures. The findings clearly demonstrate the existence of cultural differences among American, Israeli and Taiwanese managers in terms of their adaption and usage characteristics of EUC systems.

From the generic EUC characteristics viewpoint, several interesting correlations were found in the combined sample. As expected, organizational support (both IC and management support) was found to be positively correlated with end-user training, suggesting that one facet of such support is to provide end-users with higher levels of training in EUC practices. EUC perceived usefulness, frequency of use and overall user satisfaction were significantly intercorrelated and also correlated with computer experience, end-user training and organizational support, having a better perception of the system, use it more often, and are more satisfied.

Significant effects of country were observed for the individual and organizational characteristics. The Israeli respondents reported a greater level of computer experience and training but a much lower level of IC and management support. The differences in training and experience can be explained by a greater exposure of Israeli managers to computer and technological courses, mostly during their military service. This may also suggest that since the Israeli end-users in our sample were more knowledgeable of computers and information systems and had more computer experience, they felt more independent in their EUC efforts, which was reflected in a lower level of perceived organizational support. The Taiwanese sample demonstrated the highest level of end-user training and organizational support. A logical explanation of these results is that Taiwanese organizations assign great importance to the EUC phenomenon and, therefore, try to encourage EUC through greater organizational support and end-user training. American managers reported the lowest level of end-user training but relatively high levels in computer experience and organizational support.

The effects of country on the perceived usefulness of EUC were predominantly direct as the American end-users scored significantly higher. This probably reflects the case where, because of language differences, packages suitable for local use are not always available to the end-user in other countries. Cash et al. [7] and Tricker [47] lend further support to this assertion as they both suggest that language differences are a critical factor in cross-cultural differences in IS characteristics. Thus, less favorable attitudes, as well as using the systems for fewer tasks and features, may result from a need to use packages and manuals in a foreign language (usually English).

On the other hand, it was found that both Israeli and Taiwanese managers reported a higher level of end-user satisfaction and used the system more frequently than American managers. This can be explained in conjunction with the overall use of the system. Both Israeli and Taiwanese respondents reported higher frequency of use but fewer tasks and applications. This may suggest a different pattern of system usage, where the American end-users try to use their systems for advanced tasks, using sophisticated software packages, but due to a lower level of training encounter difficulties which lead to a lower level of end-user satisfaction. Their foreign counterparts may be still lagging behind in using state-of-the-art software packages and still perform less sophisticated tasks. However, due to a higher level of training, they are pleased with their systems.

The contribution of this study has two major aspects. First, it extends our knowledge of the global EUC phenomenon by focusing on the cultural differences in EUC characteristics and the determinants if EUC success. Second, it examines the pattern of relationships between attitudes toward EUC and demographic variables, individual characteristics and organizational characteristics as well as relationships of all these variables with EUC effectiveness. This examination was separately performed for the American, Israeli and Taiwanese sample as well as then for the integrated database.
