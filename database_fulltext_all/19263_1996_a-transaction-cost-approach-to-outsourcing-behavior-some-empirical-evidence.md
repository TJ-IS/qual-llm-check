# A transaction cost approach to outsourcing behavior: Some empirical evidence

Benoit A. Aubert $^{a}$ , Suzanne Rivard $^{b,*}$ , Michel Patry $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Faculté des sciences de l'administration, Université Laval Québec, Que., Canada, G1K 7P4 $^{b}$ École des Hautes Études Commerciales, 5255, Avenue Decelles, Montreal, Que., Canada, H3T 1V6

## Abstract

Outsourcing of information services is a fast growing trend and is receiving much attention in the IS community. This paper presents the results of a study of the outsourcing behavior of ten large organizations. The analysis used the transaction cost framework to explain outsourcing behavior at two different levels. At the first level of analysis, the role of asset specificity, measurement problem, and frequency in explaining the choice of the outsourced activities is investigated. The second level of analysis focuses on the influence of asset specificity and measurement problem on the terms and on the management of the contract between a firm and its outsourcer. The results presented here support the basic principles of transaction cost and incomplete contract theories.

Keywords: Outsourcing; IS operations; Transaction cost; Asset specificity; Measurement problem; Frequency; Contract management; Case study

## 1. Introduction

The use of information technologies (IT) in support of business activities is the object of ever closer scrutiny [11]. Information Systems (IS) managers are under pressure to demonstrate the benefits of IT and to manage this resource in an effective manner. At the same time, outsourcing information services is seen by several organizations as a way of increasing the efficiency of IT management.

Outsourcing is a typical example of a make-or-buy decision and can be defined as the “significant contribution by external vendors of the physical and/or human resources associated with the entire or specific components of the IT infrastructure in the user organization" ([24], p. 336). Major contracts have been awarded recently at firms such as Bankers Trust, Continental Airlines, Eastman Kodak, Enron, and General Dynamics. Several others have handed over part or all of their information services [37] to outside vendors.

Outsourcing decisions may be viewed as being either efficiency-based or politically driven. The driving force behind the efficiency-based perspective is the assumption that organizations maximize profits or minimize costs when selecting amongst competing strategic options. In this context, Huff [17] and Gupta and Gupta [13] have suggested that cost reductions motivate outsourcing. These result from the pooling of demands – which enables the outsourcer to exploit economies of scale –, or from the utilization of excess capacity in the face of specific demand ([30]). Concentration on core business as a strategy is also seen as a source of increased efficiency and productivity [7,10,17,30]. For instance, specialized firms can more easily attract highly skilled professionals and technicians that are in short supply. Other sources of cost reductions are the elimination of large fixed costs during recessions and the transfer of adjustment costs to the outsourcer when a new technology is adopted.

On the other hand, as pointed out by Lacity and Hirschheim [22], political motivations also play a role. Reponen [34], for instance, found that outsourcing decisions were sometimes motivated by the difficulties organizations had in managing their IS function. Among these are: the lack of efficiency and effectiveness of IS departments, the difficulty of assessing IS productivity and of evaluating tangible benefits of IS investments, and the low quality of the services rendered by the IS departments. Lacity and Hirschheim also identify a number of political motives, where the managers “react to the efficiency imperative, acquire or justify additional resources, react to positive outsourcing media reports, reduce uncertainty, eliminate a burdensome function, and enhance credibility” ([22], p. 258).

Whether the motivations behind its outsourcing decision are political or efficiency-based, the organization that plans to outsource part or all of its IS activities must face two critical issues. One concerns the choice of the activities to outsource and of those to keep in-house. For instance, while the contracts between Eastman Kodak and its outsourcers were very large, they excluded application development activities which were kept in-house. Similarly, General Dynamics decided that strategic information resources, such as the software embedded in the firm's products, would remain in-house, while all other IS activities and services would be outsourced. The other issue pertains to the management of the relationship created by outsourcing activities: the definition of the terms of the contract between the client and the outsourcer and the governance of this contract. For example, the Kodak contract provides for exchanges of employees between the client and its suppliers. The choice of these contract features must correspond to the characteristics of the activities outsourced. A mismatch between the activity attributes and the contract features can lead to costly problems. This was illustrated by the difficulties encountered by the Farm Credit Bank which tried to manage very complex activities using a very detailed contract. The Bank and its supplier ended up spending most of their time haggling over the contract terms rather than focusing on the delivery of IS solutions to its business problems [19].

## 2. Industrial organization economics

Classical economics does not devote much attention to the theory of the firm. The business enterprise is seen as a production function which operates as a “black box”. The existence and scope of the firm are taken for granted. This is so because classical economic theory assumes away most transactional difficulties: uncertainty is low or nonexistent, and prices convey all the relevant information to the contracting parties. Transaction costs are negligible, and the decentralization of decisions through market relations achieves an efficient allocation of resources: Walrasian transactions occur.

Modern organizational economics, starting with Coase [9], recognizes the prevalence and the theoretical importance of modelling transaction costs. These refer to the cost of organizing information, coordinating behavior, safeguarding the interests of the trans-acting parties, monitoring the transactions, inducing the appropriate behavior adjustments, etc.

Uncertainty is at the root of all market failures or transactional difficulties. Yet many markets function reasonably well under uncertainty: they efficiently convey the information needed to the various agents through the price system and induce them to behave in an efficient manner. Detailed contracts can be drawn specifying the obligation of each party and the allocation of costs and benefits in every conceivable state of nature. Insurance contracts and warranties are a good example. Alternatively, the parties can decide to rely on sequential contracts: to wait until the state of nature is known before contracting. The drawbacks of the complete contracting and sequential contracting approaches are the presence of bounded rationality and opportunistic behavior.

Bounded rationality represents the inability of the human mind to find or process all of the information available regarding a transaction. Opportunism is defined by Williamson [40] as “self-interest seeking with guile”. It includes the distortion and hiding of information, shrewd actions, and Machiavellian intentions.

These two conditions, together with uncertainty, render the use of complete and sequential contracting more difficult, and therefore more costly. Bounded rationality makes it impossible to enact and enforce complete contracts. Even if it were possible, it might be too costly to do so. Tirole [36] identifies four factors that seriously impede the use of complete contracts. Ex ante, (1) there may be too many contingencies for an exhaustive list to be drawn, and (2) it may be too complex to estimate the probabilities attached to each contingency. Ex post, (3) the contracts may be too costly to manage, and (4) legal costs may prevent a party from using the courts to enforce a disputed contract.

Faced with so many problems, the parties may decide to leave room for adaptation or to rely on a series of sequential contracts. However, this solution is severely handicapped by the risk of opportunistic behavior: one party may renege on a prior agreement, and parties will have an imperfect incentive to commit themselves, since they are not certain that future adaptations of renegotiations will be in their best interest. This is the basis of the “hold-up problem”: each party worries about making irreversible investments or commitments since it can be forced later to accept disadvantageous terms $[12,38,39]$ .

As the costs of using the market increase and the risks of relying on arms-length transactions become unbearable, the parties consider alternative coordination mechanisms. Relational contracting, as in the employment relation, is one possibility. The agreement between the parties concerns a general framework and a set of criteria to settle disputes. This is the essence of Coase's seminal paper: the firm and the market are alternative governance mechanisms. Society should use the governance mechanism which minimizes transaction costs. Williamson [12,38,39], and Barney and Ouchi [4] later developed and refined this framework.

## 2.1. Dimensions of transactions

Whether economic agents will rely on the market, on long-term contracts, or on relational contracting depends on the dimensions or attributes of the transactions to be realized. Transaction cost theory has emphasized a few key attributes, such as: the specificity of the assets required to support the transaction, the level of uncertainty surrounding the transaction, the difficulty of assessing the performance of the transaction, and the frequency of the transaction.

In the world of Walrasian transactions, transaction costs are nil because there are many transactions, their level of asset specificity is low, uncertainty is nonexistent, and there are no measurement problems. In such a context, most customers may choose from a large number of suppliers (current or potential) in order to perform a routine task in a specific environment. Markets are very efficient, and prices coordinate behavior, convey information and induce efficiency reasonably well. As deviations from this idealized situation develop – as they must in any actual contracting environment – the benefit of using arms-length transactions diminishes.

## 2.1.1. Asset specificity

When an asset is durable and dedicated to a specific transaction, it is “highly specific to the transaction”. Asset specificity is a key dimension and one that impacts heavily on the choice of governance mechanisms [35], because transactions requiring important specific investments in physical or human capital also require a contract or mechanism to protect the investor. In order to avoid being locked in the transaction, the investor will ask for a longer duration of the contract or will build bilateral adjustment mechanisms into the contract. In the context of IS, specific investments may be the know-how which the analyst must develop in order to perform the task adequately or the highly specific software that may be needed to service a particular client.

## 2.1.2. Uncertainty and measurement problems

Writing and enforcing complete contracts is even more costly for highly complex and uncertain transactions. Hence we should expect them to be internalized by the firm or to be accomplished through relational contracting.

It may also be difficult or costly to measure the actual performance of the parties. Unfortunately, the elements of an exchange are often difficult to evaluate, particularly in the case of services.

Alchian and Demsetz [1] have suggested that the inability to assess the individual contributions of agents in a team should lead to the measurement of their efforts, hence to supervision, by a monitor or an entrepreneur. Ouchi [31] and Barney and Ouchi [4] have pointed out that reliance on the hierarchy may also cause problems. They suggest that the solution consists in reducing opportunism itself. This can be done in a clan, that involves a commitment from all parties and equilibrates short-term inequities over time. Clan mechanisms can assist the transaction, so that it does not need to be internalized. Clan-assisted markets institute procedures to balance the interests, goals, and values of the contracting parties. The transfer of employees and the undertaking of joint activities are features of such markets.

## 2.1.3. Frequency of transactions

Low frequency transactions are likely to be organized through market interactions or with the help of any general governance mechanism available in the community (reputation, reciprocity, etc.). When parties interact frequently, it may be more economical to design a governance mechanism that is specifically adapted to a specific situation. Thus internalization of transactions by the firm is only efficient for recurrent transactions.

For low-frequency transactions, the firm will prefer to bear the risk associated with opportunism and uncertainty rather than support the cost of creating a new governance mechanism or expanding an existing one.

Fig. 1, based on Williamson [39], summarizes our discussion. Market transactions should be expected to prevail whenever asset specificity is low: numerous suppliers should then effectively compete. When asset specificity is high, the choice of a governance mechanism depends on the level on uncertainty and on the recurring character of the transaction.

Internal procurement is expected to dominate when uncertainty and measurement problems combine with high frequency: the firm has in these circumstances an incentive to incur the set-up costs of organizing the transaction. On the other hand, when the levels of uncertainty and measurement problems are not as high, relational contracting can emerge: this is the realm of outsourcing and strategic alliances.

Clan-assisted markets fall into this category. When an occasional transaction involves specific assets, the parties can designate an independent arbitrator in advance. This third party is mandated to resolve disputes and the two contracting parties agree to comply with the arbitrator's decision. When the transactions are recurrent and the two parties prefer to remain autonomous, it becomes profitable for them to establish a permanent governance structure, such as a joint-venture or a strategic alliance. These arrangements establish a long-term transactions management system, ensuring an evenhanded sharing of control.

Hence, we expect outsourcing to dominate other governance mechanisms for either low-frequency transactions or for transactions that are characterized by a low level of uncertainty, few specific investments and simple measurement problems.

The transaction cost approach has been used successfully in many areas; e.g., to explain the extent of vertical integration in the aluminum and tin industry [16], in the automobile parts industry [26,27], in the sales force of electronic components [2], and in the coal industry [20,21]. It has also been used in MIS to explain project management strategy [5] and types of relation with IS users [6]. Malone et al. [25] used it to evaluate the impact of information technology on the choice between market and hierarchy as governance mechanisms. Agency theory, a closely related approach resting on fundamentally compatible assumptions, evaluates the costs of governance (cost of monitoring the employee, cost of bonding the employee to the principal's objectives, and residual loss). These costs are linked to the measurement problem. Gurbaxani and Whang [15] integrated transaction cost and agency theories to evaluate the impact of information technologies on the choice between market and hierarchy. Gurbaxani and Kemerer [14] used agency theory to explain the management of information systems and the management of end-user computing. Lacity and Hirschheim [22] used transaction cost theory to explain decisions to outsource IS activities. More precisely, they investigated the extent to which managers were concerned with cost considerations, transaction type, and threat of opportunism when making outsourcing decisions.

## 3. Methodology and sample description

Ten large organizations which outsourced part or all of their IS activities were studied. Diverse settings were chosen in order to avoid the introduction of any industry-specific bias. As shown in Table 1, the sample is quite diversified, including organizations from financial, manufacturing, and distribution industries, as well as public utilities and a government organization. The sample was made up of large firms that employed, on average, over 10,000 employees each. Their assets ranged from \$200 million to over \$40 billion.

In-depth interviews were conducted with the information systems vice-president (or the equivalent) in each organization. Respondents were asked to provide thorough descriptions of their information services and management systems. Information was gathered on both software development and operations. At the time of the study, all ten participating firms were involved in some kind of software development. Eight had large projects under way, one had just ended a major restructuration of its systems, and the last was in a stable IS environment and was simply maintaining its existing systems. The scope of these development activities varied widely. The smallest (Montreal Urban Community) only employed a few people for software development while the largest (Quebec Department of Justice and The National Bank of Canada) employed several hundred. Systems were developed on a variety of platforms, ranging from micros to main-frames. Five firms indicated that they were currently buying software products “off the shelf” and adapting them instead of developing systems from scratch.

The first phases, such as feasibility analysis and information requirements definition, were always conducted in-house (see Table 2). All ten firms outsourced some of their IS operations. As can be seen from Table 2, they used external suppliers to varying degrees. The companies were dealing with more than six large outsourcing firms. Some of them even used several outsourcers simultaneously. Three of the firms only outsourced hardware maintenance. The others outsourced some or all their current operations as well as a significant part of their technical support. Among the activities generally retained were management activities, such as coordination and scheduling (in nine out of the ten firms) and general architecture planning (in all ten firms). The sample, when considering the extent of its use of outsourcing, is comparable to previous findings. In their survey, Arnett and Jones [3] found that while a limited number of firms were involved in major outsourcing, almost all were outsourcing at least some activities.

## 4. Analysis of outsourcing behavior

We now look at the wealth of information supplied by our case studies through the lenses of transaction cost analysis. Asset specificity, measurement problems and frequency are looked at in that order.

## 4.1. Asset specificity

The specificity of hardware assets is generally low, because of widespread standards for both equipment and operating systems. This creates a situation in which suppliers have little to learn about the idiosyncrasies of a firm. They can avoid human and physical asset specificity, since they do not need to make heavy investments in order to manage their clients' operations. Hence, outsourcing contracts are easier to negotiate when economies of scale, expertise sharing, or other cost saving opportunities exist. This absence of specific investment was evident in the Steinberg-IST $^{1}$ agreement in which operations, running on an IBM 3090, were transferred to the supplier's computer without modification.

When Hewitt Equipment decided to outsource its operations, all systems were transferred onto their supplier's computer. The programs ran for a year, without modification, on the outsourcer's hardware. After this, a new platform was introduced (an IBM AS/400), and the systems were adapted to run on it. This new computer was owned by Hewitt Equipment but located at the supplier's premises and operated by its employees. There were many outsourcers who could provide service on AS/400 machines so the client could easily change suppliers. On the other hand, there were several firms that used the AS/400, so the supplier could easily reallocate employees and physical resources if the Hewitt contract was terminated.

The same type of arrangement prevailed between the Quebec Department of Justice and its supplier. Between 1974 and 1989, The Department's applications had been running on rented computers, and between 1985 and 1989, it had been dealing with the same supplier. Then, the outsourcing market was not as developed as it is now. The Department determined that there were only two potential outsourcing suppliers in Quebec. The efficiency of a public bidding process was therefore debatable. The Department recognized that this situation gave the supplier a definite advantage. In 1989, they acquired some computer equipment and building space to take care of its own needs. The bid following this acquisition was limited to the operation of the system. This enabled more suppliers to propose. Many potential suppliers were eliminated, but four qualified against the requirements with respect to size, skills, and guarantees. Since the market was more competitive, the Department was able to negotiate the contract at a lower price and to recuperate its initial investment. The system used standard MVS and for this several suppliers are available. Again, suppliers could swiftly reallocate resources from one contract to another.

The manufacturer situation was similar. The company used its own equipment (IBM 3081), but the computer was hosted and operated by an external supplier that provided a standard replaceable service. At the time of the study, the manufacturer was preparing a move to a more up-to-date platform, the AS/400. The new machine offered a great deal of flexibility.

The National Bank was among the companies with the fewest outsourced activities: most operations were performed in-house. Only those requiring totally standard assets and involving large economies of scale, such as telecommunication services, EDI network and PC installation, were outsourced. The bank could buy these totally standard services from suppliers that sold to a large number of clients. At the other end of the scale, all IS operations at Alcan had been outsourced. The IS executives felt that their operations could be handled by an external supplier. They had a highly standardized and well-defined environment.

The situation was the same in micro-computer environments. The Fédération des caisses populaires Desjardins (hereafter, we shall call this Desjardins) had downsized all of its applications. These ran on a 700-station OS/2 network. They were able to outsource the physical maintenance of the equipment, since there were hundreds of potential suppliers that could replace defective disks or cables. These suppliers needed to learn little about the context of operation; their work was completely independent of the information being processed. The same situation also prevailed at Hydro-Québec. The outsourced operations were always using the most outdated technologies. They were well-known, stable, and predictable. The manufacturer also outsourced the maintenance of its micro-computers. The company decided that the supplier service was good and that many firms could provide it at short notice. Since suppliers pooled demand, they kept a much larger and diversified inventory of replacement parts and equipment and could provide more reliable assistance at lower cost than internal services. This is extremely important when strategic applications run on micro-computers, because service interruption is costly.

At the Montreal Urban Community (MUC), the outsourced micro-computer operations included network installation and maintenance, telecommunication services, and office support systems. The in-house staff was responsible for the coordination and supervision of activities. The MUC, therefore retained the general architecture expertise and know-how in-house, thus ensuring that the systems remained in line with the general structures and procedures. The MUC had learned the hard way that some of its services were quite specific. A few years earlier the MUC outsourced all of its operations; it had no internal IS employees. At the time of contract renewal, a new supplier made an offer at a much lower price, indicating that the current supplier had been extracting a quasi-rent from the MUC. The new winning supplier and the MUC soon learned that a large number of activities required specialized knowledge of the MUC and its environment. Information services were literally paralysed for two months, and this led to the creation of internal IS positions. These employees were then able to retain the required knowledge in-house so as to ensure a future smooth transition to a new supplier. This new capacity enabled the MUC to negotiate better arrangements: it made a supplier change less risky.

Another aspect of human asset specificity was observed in the micro-computer environment. Even if the manufacturer sub-contracted maintenance of its micro-computers, installation was always done by a joint team (one employee of both the supplier and manufacturer). Joint teams allowed the manufacturer to retain the specific knowledge of its configurations and to protect itself in the case of a supplier change. Change could therefore be made without any serious problems or unpleasant surprises. At the Montreal Urban Community, the four employees providing user support worked for an outside supplier, but their supervisor was an MUC employee who was familiar with the architecture of the MUC systems and who could retain and transfer information if the supplier was changed.

On the other hand, the transaction cost framework suggests that activities kept under the control of the firm require more specific human assets within the firm. Excepting Alcan, all the firms retained some activities in house. Operation scheduling was one of these. The firms that retained scheduling in-house argued that the planning and ordering of operations was a complex task that required an understanding of the information being processed. Therefore, these tasks demanded an in-depth knowledge of the firm and were more difficult to transfer to an external supplier. The management of IS data could also be specific. For example, at the Justice Department, many assignments required a knowledge of government structure and routines. The handling of data was linked to the laws, rules, and functions of the Department. Employees performing this work needed to be thoroughly familiar with the government bureaucracy.

Software development activities were often more specific, being less standardized. In a systems development project, different activities would entail different levels of specificity. As we move along the Systems Development Life Cycle, we shift from activities involving more human asset specificity to activities with low asset specificity. Firms often hired consultants in the early SDLC phases, but even when they did hire external assistance, they kept control of the process. At the other end of the scale, the coding phase was most frequently outsourced. MUC, Desjardins, Hewitt Equipment, the manufacturer, National Bank, Hydro-Québec, and the Justice Department all used external suppliers for at least part of their coding. Except for Hewitt Equipment, the six firms that used external suppliers for software development activities outsourced some work as early as functional analysis.

Contract duration is another characteristic linked to asset specificity. The shortest was the micro-computer maintenance agreement at Desjardins. It was an activity for which there was a plethora of potential suppliers. At the other end of the scale, the most complex in our sample was the contract involving the coordination and scheduling of activities at Alcan: this covered a five-year period. The contracts involving outsourcing of operations without coordination and scheduling (MUC, Hewitt Equipment, Justice Department, the manufacturer, and Steinberg), had shorter time-spans (two to three years).

The above discussion shows the explanatory power of asset specificity, particularly of human asset specificity, to understand why software development is generally not outsourced as mush as operations. In both types of activity, operations and software development, asset specificity can also explain the selection of the activities that are outsourced or kept in-house.

## 4.2. The measurement problem

The governance modes appear to be directly linked to ability to measure the information services. Operations are generally outsourced as a unit, while firms tend to keep control on project management. They rely on some formal and undisputable measures: response time, disk space, period without interruption, error log, MIPS, etc. The contracts between the ten firms and their suppliers all include these types of measures, specifying the adequate level of service with explicit provisions (fines, penalties, and even contract termination) if the required service level is not maintained. There are two conditions for measures to be effective: observability and verifiability. The first is the possibility for the principal to observe the performance of the agent. The second is the capacity of the principal to verify observations and supply evidence by measures $[36]$ .

The processes used to outsource operational activities was a reflection of a firm's capacity to measure them. The firms employed detailed contracts that specified all of the activities to be performed. These were both observable and verifiable. At the Justice Department, a complete list of the tasks and of the personnel needed was produced every two years. The firms competing for the contract received all of this information so that they could precisely evaluate the cost of performing the activities as well as the resources that they would need. Such a contract includes very narrow task definitions. During operations, the management software registered all errors occurring, so that it was possible for the contract manager to know if the problem was due to a human error or machine defect. If the error was major, it was immediately reported on a control screen; if it was minor, it was logged on a file. The Department could therefore evaluate the performance of its supplier on a daily basis.

The negotiation process at Alcan was also highly structured and detailed. The contract mandated weekly reports on all aspects of performance. At Hydro-Québec, outsourced operations were managed like internal operations. Evaluation was based on two performance criteria: the reliability of the system (period without interruption), and its availability (total time of possible use). The expected performance level for each criterion was specified in the contract. For one of its systems, for instance, Hydro could tolerate a maximum of two failures a year, with less than four hours of total downtime. Penalties were applied if the required levels were not met. If the performance exceeded the specified levels, the supplier could obtain credits (points) that could be used to compensate for future performance shortfalls. If a supplier failed to meet the required levels three times in one year, the contract could be terminated.

The negotiation process was under way at Blue Cross. An evaluation of all the systems had been performed and detailed information about the needs of the company had been collected. This enabled suppliers to make formal proposals specifying what services they would offer at what price over a five-year period. The service level would be evaluated by formal measures (response time, disk space, MIPS, time between failures, etc.). In this case, however, the volume of activity was uncertain. Although the activities themselves were well defined suppliers still prepared detailed proposals. They were asked to provide cost estimates at three different levels of utilization. The supplier would deal with this uncertainty by providing a given level of service at a fixed price while offering a guaranteed price if that volume was exceeded. The volume measures were indisputable and easy to verify. Such arrangement was consequently viable. Seven monthly reports would be produced to assess performance. Hewitt Equipment also utilized similar measures and used daily reports to monitor the supplier.

At the National Bank, the telecommunications and EDI services bought from outside vendors were easy to evaluate. The use of these services was as simple to measure as that of a telephone for any Bell Canada client. Complete measurability was also found at Desjardins where maintenance activities consisted in replacing defective parts, upgrading equipment, etc. Activities were well defined, and the parties could simply count them. The same situation prevailed at the manufacturer in micro-computer maintenance.

Of course, the firms realized that there were unpredictable elements, and that adjustments would have to be made over the duration of the contract. They therefore arranged for weekly or monthly meetings to correct potential problems. All of the firms in our sample used meetings to improve service and settle unexpected problems with their suppliers. Some suppliers went even further. During the first year of its contract with Hewitt Equipment, IST appointed an employee who was to work at Hewitt's headquarters for three days a week; he was expected to "learn" their business. It seemed that firms and suppliers could clearly specify the service levels they expected and establish the managerial features required to reach them.

Software development activities were outsourced in a very different manner. We did not find any firm that had outsourced its software development to an outside supplier for a guaranteed period of time, even among the firms that had outsourced all of their operations. Software development is much more difficult to measure than operations, and the parties cannot establish an indisputable scale to evaluate such projects. It takes a long time before the results are visible, and it is difficult for the client to know exactly what the consultant is doing. Time spent does not provide an indication of the work, because the efficiency of the developer teams is variable. In one of the firms, the IS manager said that they had been “fooled” in the past by a consulting firm doing software development for them. The development team had consisted in new and inexperienced consultants, although the client was billed on an hourly basis. They were therefore learning their job at the client’s expense. When they had learned enough and finally become efficient, they were transferred to projects where the client paid for completed blocks, thus maximized the supplier’s profit. This is one reason why that firms retain more control over development activities.

The choice of the activities to be outsourced could also be explained through the notion of measure. At the start of the development process, user needs are not well defined and uncertainty over the exact specifications is high while the overall view of the system is clearer as the project neared completion.

The only way for a client and a supplier to establish a predefined, mutually agreed-upon, measure for software development is to know exactly what the system will do.

An elaborate effort to develop well-defined software evaluation measures serves the consultants. They have a strong interest to keep their clients. Such a procedure also lowers risk because both the supplier and the client understand the exact needs and measures.

## 4.3. Frequency

Frequency is the third fundamental variable of the transaction cost approach. The frequency of operations and management activities can be considered infinite, since they are constantly being performed. Hence, frequency has no role to play in operational decisions. However, frequency is a relevant variable when software development activities are considered, since they are discrete activities.

The role of frequency is complex and must be analyzed at different levels. Smaller firms conduct software development only occasionally and therefore have little expertise or qualified resources. If software development frequency is too low, it is possible that the firm will not want to pay for a software development environment. It would therefore rely on external suppliers to develop new applications.

However, this simple view is not normal. Most firms constantly have software development projects under way. They have large teams of developers (numbering hundreds of employees) and yet, they outsource some activities.

Software development projects vary in complexity and area of application. Different systems require different skills. Even if a firm conducts software development projects on a regular basis, it may not have the required skills in its regular workforce: the firm will use consultants whenever it believes that these skills will not be required on a regular basis. Then, “frequency” refers to the use of different skills rather than software development projects. For a given project, the required skills and their frequency of use must be identified. For frequently used skills, the firm should conduct the development in-house, but for special skills, a contract (outsourcing)

should probably be used. This is supported by our findings: seven firms indicated that they were using external vendors to provide expertise that was only needed for a limited period. For example, at Desjardins, external suppliers were also used to provide expertise on a short term basis. When it was expected that the expertise would be needed on a regular basis, the personnel were hired permanently. At the manufacturer, the use of external vendors for software development enabled the company to use different types of resources on a temporary basis.

However, we also have to consider the degree of use of human resources. Continuity and stability in the use of the firm's resources are also needed. Stability enables the firm to increase its resources on a full-time basis but there are situations in which the firm needs more resources than are available for a limited period of time. The costs of selecting, hiring, training, and laying off employees would be much too high if these resources are only needed for a project. By using suppliers, the firm also avoids the costs associated with the creation of supervision and coordination positions. As observed in our sample six of the firms indicated that they were currently using suppliers for the explicit purpose of avoiding fluctuations in their regular workforce. Alcan was using external resources when expertise was needed for a short term project and also when it needed a temporary increase of its workforce. At the MUC, the use of external vendors enabled management to even out peaks and lows in resource utilization. The National Bank had not increased its software development workforce in the past ten years; all fluctuations were absorbed by personnel from suppliers.

This approach to the notion of frequency is linked to the idea of the firm as a collection of human and firm specific assets $[8]$ . With reference to evolutionary theory, Chandler $[8]$ indicates that he takes the firm rather than the transaction as the unit of analysis. Firms try to maximize the utilization of their assets to increase their efficiency.

## 5. Conclusion and managerial implications

Many results from this study are relevant in the management of IS. An increased understanding of outsourcing may help practitioners to select the most appropriate governance mode for their firm's IS activities, depending on their characteristics. The use of the transaction cost framework to examine outsourcing behavior was helpful in understanding the phenomenon.

Outsourcing is rarely a total rendition of the IS department to an external vendor. The strategic decision is not “to outsource of not” but rather “what should be outsourced and how”. The differences between IS operations and software development emphasize the need to manage these activities in different ways. Operations are recurrent and largely standardized activities. Development is a punctual endeavor, often requiring infrequently used skills.

When analyzing the impacts of the various outsourcing decisions taken, the benefits observed are sundry. Outsourcing enabled Alcan to lower its costs significantly while downsizing all its applications over a five year period. Alcan also ensured that the IS services would be transformed along with the major organizational restructuration the company was undertaking. At Blue Cross, outsourcing permitted the modernization of the technological environment and the improvement of the overall system security (both physical and logical). Desjardins had easier access to expertise it did not need to cultivate in-house. At the Justice Department, outsourcing greatly improved the flexibility of the IS services. For Hewitt, outsourcing reduced drastically its IS costs. Its yearly operating costs (strictly for running the computer) went from \$4.0 millions before the deal was signed, to \$2.3 millions two years after contract signature. At Hydro, outsourcing facilitated for-swearing of outdated technologies. The manufacturer used outsourcing to transfer its applications to a newer platform. In this case, outsourcing also eased the problems linked to workforce fluctuations. On their side, MUC had access, through their suppliers, to technical expertise they would not be able to nurture in-house. At National Bank, outsourcers absorbed important workload fluctuations, smoothing the bank's human resources management. Likewise, Steinberg was able to reduce significantly its costs through outsourcing and to migrate from a mainframe environment to a distributed one. All these reveal the benefits that outsourcing can bring to organizations when employed appropriately.

Nevertheless, in order to realize these benefits, managers should be sure that they have enforceable and undisputable measures for the activities they decide to outsource. The establishment of detailed contracts depends on the capacity to develop measures to evaluate the activities. If these measures are not available and outsourcing is still chosen as a governance mode, clan mechanisms should be put in place to enable parties to share information and align goals. They also must avoid lock-in situations, either by avoiding outsourcing of activities requiring highly specific assets, by retaining property of these assets, by keeping key personnel in-house, or by establishing governance features protecting both parties. Such protection mechanisms are essential for long term value to both parties. The ease of finding suppliers and the low level of investment these suppliers have to make are dependant on a lack of specificity.

Firms strive for stability in their activities, and peaks in the utilization of human resources are an additional argument in favor of using an external supplier. Frequency is a major factor in using an outsourcer, but it is the specificity and the capacity to measure the activities involved that define which activities will be outsourced.
