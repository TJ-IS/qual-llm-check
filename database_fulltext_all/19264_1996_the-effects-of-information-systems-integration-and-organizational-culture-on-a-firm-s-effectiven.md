# The effects of information systems integration and organizational culture on a firm's effectiveness

Yaakov Weber $^{a,*}$ , Nava Pliskin $^{b}$

$^{a}$ School of Business Administration, Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Mt. Scopus, Jerusalem, 91905, Israel $^{b}$ Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Beer-Sheva, 84105, Israel

## Abstract

Though the relationship between the investment in information systems (IS) and a firm's performance continues to be important; conclusive evidence that information technology (IT) contributes to a firm's effectiveness is rare. This study tests the relationship between the integration of IS during mergers and acquisitions and their effectiveness. The findings point to a positive relationship between IS integration and effectiveness only when controlling for (a) IT intensity, and (b) organizational culture differences between the joining firms. Thus, managers are advised to take into account IT intensity and cultural differences during the pre-merger negotiations and during the post-merger integration process.

Keywords: Mergers and acquisitions; Information systems (IS) integration; Organizational culture; Performance and effectiveness of the merger; Information technology (IT) intensity; Productivity paradox; Conversion effectiveness

## 1. Introduction

The nature of the relationship between information systems (IS) deployment and the effectiveness of firms continues to be an important subject [17]. Taken collectively, however, studies provide no clear resolution of the question: Does investment in information technology (IT) contribute to a firm's overall performance?

Do IS improve the overall effectiveness of firms? During the eighties, firms, especially in the service sector, invested enormous resources in IT in the hope of gaining significant positive financial return $[29]$ . As a result, the service sector now owns more than

85% of America's installed base of IT [28]. However, the “productivity paradox” of IS is that there is little evidence that this investment has led to an improvement in productive efficiency [2].

In most cases, firms that invested in IT presumed that such investments could improve their performance and increase their competitive advantage by lowering costs, enhancing differentiation [26], or increasing organizational effectiveness and efficiency through interorganizational and intraorganizational interactions. They had hoped, for example, that IS would allow them to increase efficiency and/or to coordinate and integrate strategic resources in similar or complementary activities. Such coordination and integration was expected to help in exploiting scale and scope economies, especially if the strategic resources were common to several markets or industries in which the firm participated. For instance, following a merger or an acquisition, many joined entities were willing to invest in integration or consolidation of IS in order to exploit economies of scale. However, the payoff of such investments is hardly explored in the literature [8].

The goal of this paper is to investigate the potential contribution of IS integration to the effectiveness of mergers and acquisitions (M&As). To this end, the empirical investigation in this study controls for explanatory factors that may influence the relationship between the firm's effectiveness and IT investment. Building on previous research, this study empirically explores the relationships between integration of information systems, organizational culture, and overall effectiveness in the context of M&As.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Information systems in mergers and acquisitions

The primary reason for firms engaging in M&As is to achieve synergy through interrelationships between the two business units to increase the competitive advantage of the merged venture $[25]$ . Synergy occurs when two operating units, divisions, or business units together can be run more efficiently (i.e., with lower costs) and/or more effectively (i.e., with better resource allocation). Thus, after the merger, the acquirer may be able to lower its average cost curve due to integration of functions and processes, and to gain competitive advantage over competing firms by reducing prices and thus capturing a greater market share.

Acquisitions vary in terms of the number of organizational functions and units involved and the degree of integration of each unit. For instance, merged firms are considered highly integrated if most respective units have are integrated to a large extent. Although, in theory, high levels of integration enhance realization of synergistic potential, in practice they may result in realization of negative synergies as a result of increased coordination costs and potential for interorganizational conflict. In managing the integration process, top executives thus have to estimate what functions and units have high potential for value creation in light of the integration costs involved. In particular, an important managerial decision upon merging pertains to the extent of integration of IS of the two firms, in order to be, hopefully, in a better position to exploit synergy and achieve advantage. Although there are many anecdotal reports of successful IS integration following M&As (e.g., [5]), conclusive evidence of improved performance for joint ventures as a result of post-merger IS integration is still lacking [4].

The logic behind investing in post-merger IS integration is consistent with that behind some of the types of IT investments investigated by Weill [41]: investment in transactional IT aimed at cutting costs, and investments in strategic IT to gain competitive advantage. IS integration, particularly in the areas of inventory control, order processing, and other data processing including financial systems, is usually pursued to increase synergy and thus reduce both fixed and variable costs of the merged firm. Therefore, those M&As with a higher level of integration of IS might be expected to exploit more of the synergy potential, leading to higher effectiveness than those M&As that do not integrate their IS at all or integrate them to a lesser extent.

2.2. The effects of industry and organizational culture

Weill suggests that organizations differ in IT “conversion effectiveness,” which is defined as the ability to convert IT investments into productive outputs. He asserts that internal factors, such as top management’s commitment and the extent of political turbulence within the organization, influence the ability of the firm to convert IT expenditures into assets. Similarly, in the context of M&A, internal factors may be critical to the ability of the two firms to convert the potentially positive contribution of IS integration into increased merger performance.

Also in a similar vein, Markus and Soh suggest that the theory linking IT investment to the firm's performance should consider (a) structural factors (e.g., industry type) that create differences in the ability of firms to derive benefits from IT spending, and (b) internal managerial processes (e.g., organizational culture) that influence the degree to which IT expenditures pay off.

Industry. The potential synergy from integration of IS after a merger may vary depending on the IT intensity within the industry. The service sector, for example, is relatively IT-intensive. Within this sector, banks and credit agencies, which have increased their IT endowment most dramatically over the last decade, are especially IT-intensive. “Whereas IT plays a supportive or administrative role in manufacturing firms, it is also a product technology and a process technology in the commercial banking industry” [21].

In IT-intensive firms, the integration of IS can bring high economies of scale in many departments, functions, and processes and also reduce redundant and underproductive capacity. As opposed to the financial service sector, firms with lesser IT intensity may have a significantly lower potential for synergy due to IS integration. Put differently, if two firms each have low investment in IS (i.e., it is a relatively small part of their cost structure), the increase in efficiency and effectiveness due to IS integration is likely to be marginal as compared to IS integration between highly IT-intensive firms. In fact, it is expected that the extent of IS integration in mergers between firms in the service sector will be much higher than the extent of IS integration in a sector characterized by lower IT intensity. Top managers in highly IT-intensive M&As will expect to exploit synergy from IS integration, while executives in other M&As will expect the synergy potential due to IS integration to be small.

Organizational culture. Although the synergy potential due to high integration during M&As is widely recognized, attempts to exploit synergy are often unsuccessful. One of the most important factors to be considered is organizational culture. Organizational culture has also been mentioned as a critical success factor in IS implementation $[1,23,30,36]$ . However, no study to date has empirically investigated how organizational culture and IS integration influence the effectiveness of M&As.

Many researchers agree that culture is a complex factor, especially in very large organizations where several subcultures might exist simultaneously. According to Sathe [33], culture is “the set of important assumptions (often unstated) that members of communities share in common.” Every group, corporate or otherwise, has a unique culture that is shaped by its members’ shared history and experience [34].

Because organizational culture acts as a filter through which members grasp the realities inside and outside the organization, organizational culture affects practically all aspects of the way people of a group interact with each other.

Culture is not easily modified; its full potential can be realized when two autonomous cultures are brought into close contact, as typically occurs when two firms merge. Following anthropological literature, Weber and Schweiger describe the consequences of culture clash in M&As, as characterized by negative attitudes on the part of the acquired management toward the acquiring management [38]. These negative attitudes reduce the commitment of the acquired managers toward successful integration of the merging companies and inhibit their cooperation with the acquiring firm's management. Moreover, Weber and Schweiger assert that when there is intense and frequent contact, such as under high levels of IS integration, cultural differences increase the likelihood of conflict between the two top management teams (TMTs) involved in the merger. Such conflict is likely to have a negative impact on M&A effectiveness because the extra costs associated with culture clash may undermine the ability of acquiring firms to achieve the expected synergy.

Thus, both industry and organizational culture may affect the potential contribution of IS integration to merger effectiveness. In fact, recent articles point out that the effects of organizational culture may be different in service, as opposed to manufacturing, sectors $[6,22]$ . Banks engage in a high level of integration in order to reap the higher potential of synergy from IS integration. This will most likely be associated with more contact between the two top management cultures, setting the groundwork for a stronger culture clash. The negative performance effects of the clash may offset the potential positive effect of IS integration.

Manufacturing firms are often less IT-intensive. Thus, the negative effects of cultural differences are often insignificant. We thus propose that in banks, cultural differences will be negatively associated with effectiveness while IS integration will be positively associated with effectiveness. However, the net performance effect (i.e., the positive contribution of IS integration versus the negative contribution of a cultural clash) cannot be predicted.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Sample

The sample was drawn from the lists of mergers published in the Journal of Mergers and Acquisitions during 1985–1987. Data were collected in early 1988. Thirteen companies were excluded from the sample at their request. Of the remaining 185 firms, responses were received from 73, yielding a response rate of 39%, which is significantly higher than the 20% mail survey response rate predicted by Gaedeki and Tootelian [14]. Four firms were disqualified because their questionnaires were unreliable. The final sample consisted of 69 M&As, 40 of which were banks, and 29 of which were from such industries as trucking, chemicals, food, and entertainment. The high proportion of banks was because the banking industry was then experiencing many mergers due to deregulation.

Potential non-respondent biases were checked by comparing respondent and non-respondent firms with respect to their relative size and time since the merger was announced. Presumably, both these variables influence the emotions and objectivity of the involved managers and, therefore, might influence their perceptions of cultural difference $[19,32]$ . However, t-tests of mean differences used with each of the two control variables indicated that there was no evidence of a non-respondent bias based on size and time.

Using Roach's [28] Technology Intensity Measure, banks in the sample scored 3.1, while other non-bank organizations in the sample scored much lower, from 0.7 for entertainment to 1.7 for trucking. These scores ensure that our presumption about much higher IT-intensity in banks versus other manufacturing firms is a characteristic of our sample.

## 3.2. Questionnaire and procedure

Recently, several studies (e.g., [7,39]) used questionnaires to measure perceived corporate culture differences by focusing on shared top management beliefs and assumptions concerning how they should conduct their business. The questions, mostly using Likert scales, were found to have high reliability and validity. Self-report questionnaires of perceived cultural differences also have an additional advantage: people's behavior and attitudes are determined by their perceptions rather than “actual” or “objective” situations [27]. This is because perceptions involve how members view situations of organizational life and interpret the bounds of acceptable behavior, while the more tangible aspects of culture are the surface manifestations of organizational culture. The consensus of multiple respondents in this study lends credibility to this argument.

Ideally, cultural differences should be investigated before a merger takes place and then compared with data collected after it. However, it is very difficult to gain access to such data in large samples during the negotiation period, since many of these preliminary moves do not result in M&As and some mergers are deliberately hidden due to stock exchange and other reasons. This problem is compounded by another difficulty associated with the measure of culture: many elements are unclear to organization members, because people take them for granted. Dramatic M&A events and the close contact with other cultures, make differences salient [20], especially in conflict situations. Retrospective data can thus be useful in studying the relationships between cultural differences and other variables. It has been observed that perceptions, attitudinal, and behavioral data do not become less accurate over time, even for periods of ten years [13,16]. One should, however, follow the recommendations of Huber and Power [18] for access to top management and the use of retrospective data.

To minimize bias, the questionnaire was pre-tested in preliminary interviews with 11 top managers who have experienced M&As. The interviews were used to review all questions for relevance and wording. This process was undertaken to ensure clarity and relevance and helped in understanding the constructs of the variables to be included in the questionnaire. Following these interviews necessary adjustments were made.

The questionnaire was mailed to M&As for which the names and addresses of their top management teams (TMTs) were available. All respondents were guaranteed anonymity (the only identification in the questionnaire was the name of the acquiring company). The completed questionnaires were returned by mail. Since the unit of analysis was not the individual manager but the top management team, responses were summed and averaged for each variable to arrive at TMT means (see [12]). This procedure allowed one to control for team size variations across firms. Individual bias was reduced by using the aggregation of respondents' perceptions to arrive at group scores [35], as well as by having top managers evaluate their team rather than themselves alone. The multiple respondent evaluations of each team enabled a check on reliability. Responses were aggregated only if a high level of agreement among top mangers' perceptions was demonstrated. To avoid pseudo-relationships between variables by means of methodological and process artifacts, provisions were taken against consistency and priming effects.

## 3.3. Measures

Cultural differences (CUD). The extent of cultural difference between the merging firms was measured on seven cultural dimensions: Innovation and Action Orientation; Risk-Taking; Integration-Lateral Interdependence; Top Management Contact; Autonomy in Decision-Making; Performance Orientation; and Reward Orientation. These have been considered important in the implementation of IS [30] and have been previously employed, with high reliability and validity, in M&As studies [7,39]. The measure of each of these seven dimensions consisted of three to five items, resulting in a total of 29. Each was constructed to elicit responses concerning cultural differences according to a five-point scale ranging from “very similar” (1) to “very different” (5). Following the test for reliability and validity, a cultural differences index was computed by summing the scores and then taking their average.

IS Integration (ISI). The extent of IS integration following the merger or acquisition was derived by averaging two items. The first referred to the extent of integration of IS and data processing functions. The second referred to the extent of integration of the financial systems, which are usually a critical element of the IS. Each item was constructed to elicit responses on a five-point scale, between “no integration” (1), where systems remained totally independent of one another, and “complete integration” (5), where respective systems were combined into one.

Merger effectiveness (MEF). This measure was based on 12 questionnaire items that addressed effectiveness along organizational dimensions, such as operations, production, marketing, research and development, and personnel. The use of key informants to assess effectiveness is common in organizational research (e.g., [9,11]), and the self-report measures provide the respondent with an opportunity to incorporate implicitly non-economic considerations and aspiration levels (e.g., [10]). A five-point scale, ranging from “very ineffective” (1) to “very effective” (5) was used. The final measure was derived by averaging the 12 items.

## 3.4. Reliability and validity

A perceived organizational phenomenon may be ambiguous if “one cannot be sure whether it implies an attribute of the organization or of the perceiving individual” [15]. This is particularly important if the individual’s perceptions are to be used to represent a feeling of group of people. Furthermore, while the use of retrospective data is common in some areas of research, including IS, a question of reliability always exists. It was desirable, therefore, to check for within-group variance among multiple respondents for each TMT in our study.

A minority of firms in the sample (17 out of 69) were represented by single responses to our questionnaire. This moderate dependence on single respondents invariably raises the issue of whether one response reliably captured the attributes of the TMT, the study's unit of analysis, as opposed to those of the responding individual. We assume that each individual's responses reflects the team's attributes for three reasons. First, we phrased each item so as to emphasize the collective perceptions of the team rather than the respondent's personal perspective. Second, we only surveyed top managers who are likely to be well informed about the perceptions of their proximate group and about the integration process.

Finally, the actual data also lends support to the inclusion of single responses. We performed reliability tests by examining those mergers (52 out of 69) from which we received multiple responses, and found very high levels of agreement. Specifically, we used Kendall's coefficient of concordance to assess inter-rater reliability on all questionnaire items. Table 1 shows the results, reporting both the value of the statistic and its significance. Forty-two multi-rater cases (81%) demonstrated significant inter-rater reliability at the 1 percent level, including four and five respondents of the same firm. All of the other ten cases demonstrated inter-rater reliability at the 5 percent level. These results were substantially better than those reported for interrater reliability in recent research. Had our sample been larger, the demonstrated reliability would have suggested that each key informant reliably depicted the study's central constructs at the correct unit of analysis. Thus, we feel it is useful (though perhaps slightly dubious because of the relatively small sample size) to include in our sample those mergers with only a single respondent. For each of the acquired firms with multiple responses, each item was averaged, and the average was used to represent the views of the firm's top management team.

The statistical analysis of the seven cultural dimensions showed that all intercorrelations between dimensions are positive and high (at least 0.61), significant (P < 0.01), and of high internal consistency (Cronbach's Alpha ranging from 0.82 to 0.94). The data showed strong evidence of discriminant validity: Cronbach's Alpha coefficient for each dimension is higher than that dimension's correlations with any of the other six.

The data also provide strong evidence of convergent validity, indicating, as expected, that these seven dimensions are part of the same general construct [31]. Although each dimension measures a unique aspect of the phenomenon, all the dimensions refer to the same content domain, making it possible to combine them all in a single index, a procedure for which prior research provides theoretical and statistical support (e.g., [24]). The combined internal consistency of this single cultural differences index was high (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.97). Finally, factor analysis and a scree test were used to confirm the unidimensionality of the construct.

The intercorrelation between the two IS integration items composing the ISI measure was high (r = 0.77, P < 0.0001), indicating high reliability for the combination of both items into one measure of IS integration. The merger effectiveness (MEF) measure was also found highly reliable (Cronbach's Alpha = 0.92).

Of course, results may show the influence of a ‘survivor bias.’ The literature about executive turnover following a merger suggests that those acquired managers who feel intense cultural difference with the management of the buying firm will eventually leave the organization [40]. Because we surveyed only those top managers who remained with the acquired firm up to three years after the merger, the respondents to our survey may not represent the sample population of acquired top managers, particularly as the time span increased between the merger date and the completion of the questionnaire. In other words, as the time span increased so did the probability that the managers who filled out the questionnaire may be overrepresented by those who have already left the organization. In such case, the probability to find relationship between variables is small and we have conservative test of our predictions. Nevertheless, we tested the relationship between elapsed time and each of the variables and found no significant correlations. This relegates concerns about survivor-effects in the present study.

Finally, perceptions of top managers may be influenced by the tone (i.e., friendly or hostile) of the negotiations before the merger takes place. To control for this effect, top managers were asked to indicate the friendliness of their merger by employing a measure that was used in past studies [7,39]. Only two M&As were found to be hostile take-overs, indicating that in most cases top managers' perceptions were not distorted by acrimonious negotiations.

## 4. Results

Table 2 displays means, standard deviations, and Pearson correlations between cultural differences (CUD), the extent of IS integration (ISI), and merger effectiveness (MEF) for the full sample, for banks, and for non-banks. Comparison of variable means between banks and non-banks reveals that IS integration of banks is much higher than that of non-banks, while cultural difference means are almost the same. A t-test of mean differences of IS integration confirms that the mean of IS integration in banks was significantly higher than IS integration in non-banks (P < 0.01), which suggests that banks are engaged in higher IS integration, as an attempt to realize the potential synergy from such integration.

No significant correlations were found between cultural differences and IS integration, indicating that these variables are independent and no multi-collinearity problems are to be expected. The results, according to Table 2, do not support the main theme that the degree of IS integration following M&As is associated with the effectiveness of the merger. However, they do, as expected, show significant negative correlation between cultural differences and effectiveness for the full sample (r = -0.34; P < 0.01) and for banks (r = -0.58; P < 0.001). Taken collectively, then, these results suggest that it is possible that cultural differences offset a possible positive effect of IS integration on effectiveness, at least for banks.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The findings of this study provide systematic evidence for two important concerns of the IS literature. First, the findings link the value of integrating IS during M&As to merger performance. Specifically, when levels of cultural differences between the two TMTs engaged in M&As are held constant, banks that engage in high levels of IS integration during merger outperform banks that avoid such integration. Second, organizational culture plays an important role in the effective implementation of IS integration. Specifically, for banks, high culture differences between the two TMTs involved in M&As are negatively associated with merger effectiveness: those firms with high synergy potential that opt for exploiting synergy may encounter culture clash. For these firms, internal management processes associated with the level of fit between the organizational culture of the joining firms may determine if investment in IS integration can be effectively converted to better performance.

The results are consistent with those of other studies, which found that IT investment aimed at cutting costs and exploiting economies of scale can be associated with improved performance. However, while other studies investigated manufacturing or service firms, our study investigated banks, which are highly IT-intensive, and other industries simultaneously. Moreover, the results not only support the assertions about the importance of conversion effectiveness in explaining the relationship between IT investment and firm performance, but also provide empirical support for the theoretical contentions about the importance of organizational culture to IS implementation. Finally, the robust evidence presented by the regression analysis in explaining the variance of effectiveness (R-square = 43%) suggests that the observed relationships were not due to chance. Most, if not all, published studies that attempted to explain the variance in overall firm performance arrived at much lower correlations.

One obvious limitation of this study is the use of retrospective and perceptual data. However, perceptions may be better predictors of behavior, especially when assessing cultural differences. Furthermore, the interrater reliability between key informants and the consistent pattern of results suggest this was not a problem.

Another limitation, which does not permit any claims of causality, pertains to the cross-sectional nature of the study. While the use of retrospective data is consistent with recommendations to introduce lags in the estimation procedure, future research might collect data in different time periods. Such an approach would allow the impact of IT investment to be felt, and might have the potential to demonstrate a causal relationship between IT investment and performance.

In summary, the results of the present study highlight the importance of a firm's ability to convert IT investment into value added and, particularly, the importance of organizational culture in such effective conversion. Finally, the findings of the study have practical importance because they show that exploiting the synergy from IS integration is affected by organizational differences.
