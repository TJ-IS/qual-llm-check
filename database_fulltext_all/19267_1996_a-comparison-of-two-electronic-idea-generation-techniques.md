# A comparison of two electronic idea generation techniques

Milam Aiken $^{*}$ , Mahesh Vanjani, Joseph Paolillo

Department of Management, University of Mississippi, University, MS 38677, USA

## Abstract

Much research has compared verbal with electronic brainstorming, but very few studies have investigated the effects of different electronic techniques. Most studies of electronic brainstorming have been based upon the individual poolwriting technique. However, gallery writing may be superior to it in some situations. Here, we report on an experiment involving 88 subjects in nine groups of approximately ten people each. The subjects used both electronic techniques to discuss different problems. Results show that the subjects were more satisfied with gallery writing and preferred it. Although more raw comments were generated using poolwriting, the number of quality comments and the number of unique, quality comments were not significantly different.

Keywords: Electronic brainstorming; Electronic gallery writing; Electronic individual poolwriting; Group decision support systems

## 1. Introduction

Verbal meetings are often plagued by inefficient and ineffective communication and low participant satisfaction with the proceedings [10]. In response to these problems, computer-based techniques such as electronic brainstorming have emerged as tools to improve the productivity of group meetings. However, experimental and field results of using these new computer-based tools have proved somewhat inconsistent [5,21]. The processes and outcomes of electronic groupwork depend on the interaction among four variables: group characteristics, task, organizational context, and technology [9,25]. While much research has been conducted to study the effect of these variables on groups, they have seldom explored the role of differences in the technology.

Although it is not always clearly stated in the literature, the vast majority of electronic brainstorming research has been conducted using the individual poolwriting technique $[13,23]$ while others have used the nominal group technique $[18]$ , electronic blackboards, Delphi $[26]$ , or other methods. Very little attempt has been made to compare these techniques. Rather, researchers often compare their results with other studies using other techniques and wonder why there are differences.

Our research attempts to demonstrate that significant differences exist in electronic brainstorming techniques. Further, our new technique (electronic gallery writing) may be superior to the most commonly-used electronic brainstorming technique (electronic individual poolwriting).

## 2. Background

Research on brainstorming began in the 1950s [24] when several verbal techniques were developed to help in generating creative ideas in discussion groups through the reduction of many inhibitory aspects of group problem solving. Early researchers claimed that groups using this could generate more ideas than the individuals working alone (termed nominal groups). Other researchers, however, found that nominal groups were superior to brainstorming groups [6,22,30] in three ways: (1) nominal groups produced more ideas of better quality, (2) average productivity declined using verbal brainstorming as group size increased, and (3) the total number of ideas produced by verbal brainstorming groups decreased as group size increased.

In the past decade, research has concentrated on computer-based counterparts to these techniques. However, the findings have been mixed. While some studies reported that the use of electronic tools led to improved decision quality, increased participation, increased consensus, or increased satisfaction, others found the opposite to be true, or that the results were inconclusive $[11]$ . These mixed findings influenced researchers to move in a new direction that focused on a specific aspect, such as the effect of group size, anonymity, physical proximity, and parallel communication.

Other researchers have stressed the importance of studying the role of technology in electronic group research [17]. For example, a different design or an improvement in computer technology might change the experimental results [31].

Relatively few studies have investigated the effect of different electronic tools (most have compared an electronic with a verbal technique, for example). In one study, researchers examined three different forms of meeting technology (an electronic blackboard, networked workstations, and a verbal, face-to-face meeting) and found that decision quality was best for groups communicating via an electronic blackboard, second best for networked groups, and worst for face-to-face groups [19]. In another, researchers found significant differences in groups using two different electronic meeting tools [12]. These two studies indicate that the choice of electronic tool can have a major influence on the meeting outcomes. Numerous experiments have shown that electronic poolwriting may be superior to verbal brainstorming for large groups, but very few have used other electronic idea generation techniques.

## 3. Idea generation techniques

The literature lists a large number of group, verbal idea generation techniques, but in general, these methods can by divided into brainstorming and brainwriting variations $[27]$ . In general, brainstorming is used to indicate verbal generation of ideas by a group while brainwriting refers to group methods that emphasize the silent generation of ideas in writing. In a broader sense, brainstorming has been used to refer to any procedure in which spoken interaction is the primary method. Because of the various limitations, verbal brainstorming has been recommended for small groups (typically six or less) while brainwriting has been recommended for larger ones $[4]$ .

## 3.1. Brainstorming

In a brainstorming meeting, a group takes turns contributing comments which are written down on a blackboard at the front of the room for all to see $[14]$ . Individuals are encouraged not to criticize ideas, but simply to contribute as many innovative ideas as possible which will contribute to the solution of the problem. Advantages of this technique are that it accommodates social interaction and encourages a high level of group cohesion. However, disadvantages occur because people must take turns (this is a major problem with large groups), only a short summary of the comments may be written on the board, and the comments are not anonymous (shy people may not contribute or the group may not express what they really think). Therefore, brainstorming groups may have disruptive interpersonal conflicts and unequal participation.

## 3.2. Brainwriting

Brainwriting can be categorized as either interactive (face-to-face idea generation) or nominal (non face-to-face idea generation); it is characterized by silent, hand-written communication. The advantages of this technique over brainstorming are that individuals do not need to wait to speak (everyone can be writing at the same time), all ideas are recorded, and a high degree of anonymity usually is preserved (depending on the system). Brainwriting may be preferred over verbal brainstorming when a skilled facilitator or leader is not available, group members are not skilled or trained in brainstorming, one or a few group members may dominate a brainstorming discussion, or conflict exists among two or more members. Because group members using brainwriting can generate ideas simultaneously (no turn-taking is necessary), they often participate more and produce a greater number of ideas than when using brainstorming. However, brainwriting does not satisfy social interaction needs as well as brainstorming techniques.

Several versions of brainwriting have been proposed, but we will focus on the brainwriting pool and gallery writing techniques. The first (also known as the individual poolwriting technique), uses the following steps [15]:

1. Each person in a face-to-face group silently writes ideas on a sheet of paper and places the paper in the center of the table in the middle of the group.

2. Someone else's paper is taken from the pool and the ideas written on it are used for stimulation of new ideas.

3. New ideas are silently written down on the paper.

4. The process continues until time is called (usually after 20 or 30 minutes). After several exchanges, most of the group probably will have seen most of the comments (although not all).

In the gallery writing method, sheets of paper are attached to the walls of a room and group members silently write down their ideas on the sheets $[28]$ . The advantage of this technique over the other is that, in addition to all participants being able to write comments simultaneously, all participants can view all comments simultaneously, thereby increasing a feeling of group cohesion. However, anonymity is reduced because other participants are able to see group members as they are writing comments.

## 3.3. Electronic brainstorming

The term electronic brainstorming has been used to describe any form of electronic idea generation. However, in the purest sense, it should be called electronic brainwriting.

For example, two commercial electronic meeting systems, TeamFocus from IBM and GroupSystems from Ventana, which represent approximately 45% of the market [7], have a program called Electronic Brainstorming (EBS) which is based upon the individual poolwriting technique [1,2]. Although these electronic meeting systems have other programs for idea generation (such as Topic Commenter, GroupWriter, GroupOutliner, Policy Formation, and others in the case of GroupSystems), the vast majority of the research published in the literature has been based upon the Electronic Brainstorming program from these two systems, and this program is used approximately 72% of the time when the systems are used [3].

## 3.3.1. Electronic individual poolwriting

Electronic individual poolwriting (also known as the electronic brainwriting pool) is based upon the manual technique in which participants write comments on sheets of paper which are then exchanged. The electronic version of the technique simply substitutes disk files for pieces of paper. A group of N people at computer terminals exchange typed comments on $N + 1$ files. Comments are almost totally anonymous, ideas are recorded, and the group can communicate in parallel.

In theory, the major advantage of this technique is that a large number of comments will be generated during the course of the meeting (participants can see a new file of comments only after they have written something). The major disadvantage of this technique is that all of the group are unable to see all of the comments during the meeting, although they can have a complete printed transcript after the meeting [29]. A particular group member may not see all of the files during a meeting or may see a small group of files an abnormally high percentage of the time [16]. In addition, at any one time, everyone in the group is looking at a completely different subset of comments. If someone starts laughing or remarks verbally about what he or she read, nobody else in the group knows what the person is looking at.

## 3.3.2. Electronic gallery writing

The electronic version of gallery writing substitutes one file for the many sheets of paper posted on the wall in the manual version. However, the electronic version preserves anonymity, unlike the manual version. Using electronic gallery writing, participants may submit typed comments and view all other members' submitted comments at any time. Because all information is shared, we have found that people spend more time reading others' comments than creating new ones. Therefore, if the goal of a meeting is to generate the greatest number of comments, electronic poolwriting may be best. If the goal of the meeting is a high degree of information sharing and group synergy, electronic gallery writing may be the best choice.

Throughout the remainder of this paper we will be using the term poolwriting for electronic individual poolwriting and gallery writing for its electronic equivalent.

## 4. Experimental methodology

The following subsections describe an experiment designed to compare poolwriting with gallery writing.

## 4.1. Hypotheses

Because of the unique characteristics of the two electronic idea generation techniques discussed above, we formulated the following hypotheses:

H1. There will be no difference in process satisfaction between groups using poolwriting and gallery writing.

H2. There will be no difference in technique preference between groups using poolwriting and gallery writing.

H3. There will be no preference to see all comments at the same time.

H4. There will be no preference to see all comments during the course of a meeting.

H5. There will be no difference in the number of raw comments generated by groups using poolwriting and gallery writing.

H6. There will be no difference in the number of quality comments generated by groups using pool-writing and gallery writing.

H7. There will be no difference in the number of unique, quality comments generated by groups using poolwriting and gallery writing.

## 4.2. Subjects

Eighty-eight undergraduate students enrolled in a Management Information Systems course participated in the experiment for extra credit. The subjects were randomly placed in nine groups of nine or ten people each; very few of the subjects had previously been involved in an electronic meeting.

## 4.3. Technology and environment

Each group met in an electronic classroom: an auditorium with 55 microcomputers connected through a local area network. The human-computer interface for the two programs was exactly the same (the same screen design, function keys, etc.). The only differences were in the file sharing mechanism (participants shared comments on a single file with gallery writing and used multiple files with poolwriting), and the comment submission mechanism (participants were forced to submit a comment to view other comments when using poolwriting only). ## 4.4. Tasks

For the sake of comparability, this study employed the creative tasks used in previous studies [8,20]. Each group was faced with two tasks: (1) "How can the campus parking problem be improved at the University?", and (2) "How can campus security be improved at the University?"

## 4.5. Experimental procedures

Four of the nine groups first used gallery writing to discuss the parking problem and then used pool-writing to discuss the campus security problem. The other five groups reversed the order of the tasks. An earlier study showed no interaction with the order of tool use and no interaction with the topic [4]. That is, we believe that subjects' satisfaction with a particular tool will not be affected by the topic under discussion and the number of comments generated will not be significantly different because of the topic.

Subjects discussed the first topic with one program for ten minutes and then discussed the second topic with the other program for ten minutes. A post-session questionnaire was then administered to assess the participants' satisfaction and preferences with the two techniques. This questionnaire was based upon those used in earlier studies $[4,12,13]$ .

## 5. Experimental results

## 5.1. Satisfaction and preference

Table 1 shows results from the questionnaire, which is listed in Appendix A. In addition to means and standard deviations, difference of means T statistics were calculated by subtracting the median of the scale (4 = neutral) from each observation to determine significance. Significant differences from the median were found for all measures except for pool-writing satisfaction. Thus, subjects were satisfied with gallery writing and preferred it and tended not to prefer poolwriting. Additional difference of means T statistics were calculated by subtracting gallery writing satisfaction from poolwriting satisfaction and gallery writing preference from poolwriting preference for each observation. Subjects were more satisfied with gallery writing (T = 9.10, p = 0.0001) and preferred it over poolwriting (T = 8.05, p = 0.0001). Therefore, the null hypotheses H1 and H2 are not supported.

## 5.2. Importance of ability to see comments

Significant differences from the median were found for the importance of ability to see all comments at any given time and importance of ability to see all comments during the course of the meeting (see Table 1). Therefore, null H3 and H4 are not supported.

## 5.3. Number of comments

The number of raw comments was counted for each of the 18 sessions (nine groups, one session for gallery writing and poolwriting each) and printed transcripts were made. Two reviewers were then asked to count the number of quality comments and the number of unique, quality comments in each transcript independently: a high degree of interrater reliability was obtained in the ratings ( $\alpha = 0.98$ ). A quality comment was counted as any idea related to the topic of the session, no matter how strange. Examples of non-relevant and therefore non-quality comments were “When is this experiment going to end?” and “ddd.” The latter comment and others similar to it were found frequently in the poolwriting transcripts, because subjects were forced to enter something to get another file of comments. No similar comments were found in the gallery writing transcripts. Unique, quality comments were judged to contain an idea not expressed elsewhere. For example, there are four comments in the computer screen sample (see Section 4.3) related to building a parking facility, and these would count as a single unique, quality comment.

## 5.4. Subjects' remarks on the questionnaire

Of the 88 subjects in the experiment, 23 wrote remarks as requested by item 7 in the questionnaire. As shown in Appendix B, the vast majority of the remarks favored gallery writing over poolwriting (22 to 1); this provides additional anecdotal evidence of the former technique's superiority.

## 5.5. Discussion

Groups using poolwriting generated significantly more raw comments than using gallery writing, but the numbers of quality and unique, quality ideas were not significantly different. This mitigates one of poolwriting's purported advantages (the generation of a large number of ideas during a meeting).

Poolwriting was rated lower than gallery writing because of subjects' frustration with using multiple files instead of one shared file. Subjects wanted to see others' written comments simultaneously (as when one person in the group remarks verbally about a written comment), and they want to feel “included” by seeing all of the comments generated during the course of the meeting. Although subjects were not significantly dissatisfied with poolwriting, they were much more satisfied with gallery writing because of the ability to read all comments simultaneously using this tool. Remarks written about the two tools in Appendix B provide additional reasons why subjects in general preferred gallery writing.

We believe it is very important in meetings for participants to share information widely and uniformly. In verbal brainstorming, all receive the same information, but individuals have problems providing information simultaneously (people must take turns to speak). In manual and electronic poolwriting meetings, all can provide information easily (by writing at the same time), but have problems receiving it (nobody is looking at the same comments at the same time and over the course of the meeting everyone may not be able to see all comments). Gallery writing allows participants to receive and provide information easily. Anyone in the group can at any time see what the group has written and can provide a new comment to the transcript without the need for turns. This also may accommodate social interaction and encourage greater group cohesion than when using other brainwriting techniques.

## 6. Conclusion

Results of an experiment comparing electronic poolwriting and electronic gallery writing indicate that the latter may be the preferred technique. The number of unique, quality comments generated by groups using each tool was not significantly different, but groups prefer to see all comments generated simultaneously and over the course of the meeting (a feature provided only with gallery writing).
