# Trends in computer use in collective bargaining

Marian M. Extejt $^{*}$ , Marc P. Lynn

Department of Management, Marketing and Logistics, School of Business, John Carroll University, University Heights, OH 44118, USA

## Abstract

A survey of union and management negotiators was conducted to characterize the use of computers and other information technology (IT) in collective bargaining activities. Software use, data sources, and attitudes toward the use of computers by both types of negotiators were examined and compared. Results suggest that while, computer use is extensive among both labor and management negotiators, programs in use are generally limited to spreadsheets and database management systems. Negotiators tend to use computers primarily to generate contract packages and conduct cost–benefit analyses. Concerns regarding Information used in decision-making are consistent with those posed in the information systems literature.

Keywords: Collective bargaining; Labor relations; Negotiations; Public sector; Teacher bargaining units; Education bargaining units; Computers in bargaining; Computers in negotiations

Twenty-five years ago, union and management negotiators at a MIT-Ford Foundation conference presented their views on the role of computers in bargaining [13]. Given subsequent advances in information technology, one would expect computerization to have a significant impact on negotiations since then; yet a review of the literature reveals no systematic study updating the results of the conference. This paper presents results of efforts to assess the current Impact of information technology (IT) on collective bargaining, focusing on school district negotiations.

## 1. Background

Studies of computer use in human resource management reveal that, while some functions, such as payroll and personnel records, are almost universally computerized, others like labor relations are less so (see Table 1).

A low level of computer use in labor relations may be surprising, considering the great advances in both the hardware and software. Microcomputer use has been advocated for cost comparison, record construction and maintenance, policy capturing, and training [16]. Databases containing information on grievances, attendance and assignments can be used in both formal negotiations and ad hoc decisions [3]. Decision support systems (DSS) may be used to prepare and evaluate contract proposals [9]; computerized decision tree models can be used to help negotiation teams develop reasonable bargaining positions [17].

On a relatively more sophisticated level, artificial intelligence techniques have been used to develop computer programs that function as a mediator in labor negotiations [10]. Expert system shells, like NEGOPLAN are designed to provide negotiation and strategic decision support. A special class of group decision support systems (GDSS) called negotiation support systems (NSS) have been developed. One, MEDIATOR, supports negotiations by consensus-seeking through exchange of information. The Conflict Analysis Program (CAP) is an NSS that supports third-party arbitration.

Herniter, Carmel and Nunamaker [4] examine how electronic meeting systems (EMS) can be used to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of the negotiation process. The setup of an electronic meeting systems room involves a U-shaped seating arrangement. Each participant has a personal computer; they connect each computer to a local area network (LAN). A trained meeting leader controls the system [1,10,14]. This system allows negotiators to share computer documents, maintain electronic contract logs and bargaining books, and edit proposals. Case studies of two private sector contract negotiations using EMS found that using the systems saved time, and reduced misunderstandings.

Beyond the technological advances, negotiator attitudes have generally been supportive of using IT to facilitate collective bargaining, although union and management differed on how they thought this tool could be used [13].

The power of information in negotiations has been well documented. $^{1}$ Gaining access to information increases power in negotiations for both constituents. IS allows access and provide more efficient analysis of data. Thus, use of IT should enhance bargaining power, an advantage every negotiator seeks.

Why then the low application rate of computers in labor-management relations? The purpose of this research is to examine computer use in labor-management negotiations in public school systems, with special emphasis on application software and use of DSS.

## 2. Methods

Sample: The population for this study, conducted in 1994, was drawn from union and management chief negotiators for school districts in a Midwestern state. Management surveys were mailed to all 323 school district superintendents; state-level union officers identified 161 chief union negotiators (NEA and AFT). Sixty union and 141 management surveys were returned. We did not include four union and 15 management responses in the analysis because either the respondent was not directly involved with the collective bargaining process or the survey was returned with significant missing information. Therefore, fifty-six union negotiators (for a response rate of 38%), and 126 management negotiators (a response rate of 39%) participated in the study. Characteristics of the sample are contained in Table 2.

We selected education bargaining units because they possess two characteristics that should support the use of IT. First, both management and union negotiators work in an “industry” where computer use is common for non-collective bargaining activities. Therefore, access to the hardware should not be an impediment. Second, these professionals are assumed to be less intimidated by new technology; thus, attitude toward technology should not be a factor in its application in labor-management negotiations.

Union respondents had a higher level of negotiating experience (based on number of contracts negotiated) than their management counterparts ( $\mu = 21.4$ , $\sigma = 22.1$ vs. $\mu = 8.05$ , $\sigma = 5.6$ respectively). This may be due to the higher proportion of chief negotiators responding for the union (87.5%) compared with management, where responses were evenly distributed between chief negotiators and bargaining team members. The size of the bargaining unit also varies widely in each group, demonstrating a good representation of the population.

General computer experience (gauged by frequency of use) and personal “comfort” with computers were quite similar for the union and management respondents. Over half the respondents use computers daily (union = 61.2%, management = 59.5%). Of the rest, about 21% in each group indicated weekly use, with only 2% being nonusers.

Procedure: We mailed surveys to union negotiators in two ways. For one union, surveys and a cover letters that showed state-level union officer support of the research were mailed directly. Negotiators associated with the other union received their surveys directly from the union's state office. School district negotiators received their surveys through direct mail to the district superintendent's office.

Survey: The questionnaire was split into three sections. The first consisted of questions about the respondent's use of computers and computer-generated reports and analyses in any phase of negotiations. Specifically, we asked that respondents identify the tasks for which they used computers or computer-generated reports, their source(s) of data, which specific types of software they used, and when they used it in the negotiations process.

The second part of the survey contained items designed to measure attitudes toward the use of computers in labor-management negotiations. One set of items asked whether the respondent was concerned with information issues commonly identified with Management Information Systems [MIS]. Respondents were asked to indicate their personal reaction to each issue by responding on a 5-point, Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = not an issue of concern to 5 = very high level of concern. A second set of scale items designed to measure attitudes toward the use of computers in labor-management negotiations was developed from a review of opinions expressed by both union and management negotiators at the 1969 MIT conference [13]. Respondents were asked to indicate their reaction to each statement by their response on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree.

The third part of the questionnaire focused on demographics and respondents' experience related to negotiations and computer use.

Analysis: We conducted analysis of the data in two parts:

1. First, use patterns were tabulated. These included purposes of computer use and type of software used. Analyses of variance were then conducted to examine whether usage patterns differed between union and management respondents.

2. Second, concerns about information and attitudes about computerization of collective bargaining processes were tabulated and compared using t-tests.

## 3. Results

93% of the union negotiators and 90% of the management negotiators reported using computers and/or computer-generated information sometime in the bargaining process. Electronic spreadsheets are the most commonly used tool. A significant difference was reported in the use of database management software. Few negotiators report using custom DSS. Expert systems clearly play no significant role for either group. See Table 3.

A comparison of data sources shows that management negotiators rely much more heavily on analyses generated by their local staff than do their union counterparts. No significant differences between union and management were found regarding the use of other data sources. See Table 4.

Respondents in both groups cited cost-benefit analysis and package generation as their most frequent purpose for using computers. Both groups used them, though infrequently, for inter- and intra-unit dispute mediation.

While high for both groups, management negotiators reported using computers for record keeping more frequently than union negotiators. Union negotiators reported using computers for contract comparison twice as often as management. See Table 5.

Both groups indicate a moderately high concern for the accuracy, relevance, completeness and timeliness of information, but union respondents were significantly more concerned with issues of information accuracy and relevance.

Overall, cost-justifying the use of computers in the negotiation process was not a great concern. Management did feel more strongly about the added accuracy of computer-generated cost analyses of union demands and proposals and the issue of confidentiality when using computers in negotiating. The union respondents' slightly lower scoring of this issue is in line with their increased concern for accuracy. There was also a tendency for management to see computers as more useful in coordinated and/or coalition bargaining. See Table 6.

## 4. Discussion

In contrast to earlier surveys, we found that computers are used extensively in school district labor relations activities. Considering the tasks involved in negotiations (i.e., costing contracts, comparing proposals), the widespread use of spreadsheets is not surprising. Management's greater use of database management software is consistent with their greater reliance on local staff as a data source and their use of DSS for record keeping functions.

Despite the impressive potential proposed for expert systems, they are rarely used by either group. An expert system program that has received publicity in the popular press, Negotiator pro $^{2}$ , is suitable for all types of negotiations, including labor-management. As stated in its manual “[T]he advantage of using this program is that it inspires more creative thinking about negotiations... There is an expert system that helps you analyze your style of negotiation and that of the other side(s) in a Myers–Briggs type analysis that gives broad categorization of personality types and how to best reach each type of person.” when practising negotiators discover that such programs can help them be better negotiators, acceptance levels are sure to increase.

Management's reliance on local staff for data is consistent with their increased use of database management software. No significant differences were found between the groups regarding the other data sources. It should be noted that the questionnaire did not ask respondents to rank the importance of these information sources, but simply whether they were used.

While both union and management used computers for record keeping, the use by management was significantly higher. In fact, the means for both groups are practically the same as their means for local staff as a data source. Again, this is consistent with management's use of database management software.

The only other significant difference in computer-based information management use involves contract comparison. Union negotiators cited this use twice as frequently as management, presumably because the union must negotiate with many school districts, whereas management is more focused on “local” variables associated with their school district. Unions are also more concerned with external equity among bargaining units; management negotiators are more concerned with internal equity, and satisfying other stakeholders (taxpayers, parents, etc.). The unions have less control over local sources of information because of their lower reliance on local staff. If they only have access to processed results from agencies who must provide general information, e.g., the state, and do not have access to the input, one can see why unions are more concerned.

Union respondents were in stronger agreement with the idea for providing information to the “other side,” probably because they have the most to gain if easier access to custom reports and ad hoc queries are provided. It is interesting that there was relatively strong agreement from both sides when they were asked about planning special analyses.
