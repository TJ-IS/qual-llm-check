# The temporal nature of IS benefits: A longitudinal study

Jaak Jurison \*

Graduate School of Business Administration, Fordham University, 113 West 60th Street, New York, NY 10023, USA

## Abstract

This paper discusses the dynamic nature of information technology (IT) benefits and reports on findings of a three-year evaluation of user productivity and organizational effectiveness following the installation of an integrated office information system (IS). For systems adopted on a voluntary basis, individual benefits occur first, while improvements in organizational effectiveness develop over a longer period of time. This suggests that the timing of IS assessment is important and that longitudinal research designs are more appropriate for IS benefits evaluation than cross-sectional studies.

Keywords: IS evaluation; IS impacts; Longitudinal research; Organizational effectiveness

## 1. Introduction

Assessment of the impact of information systems (IS) is a major challenge to managers. Many senior managers are dissatisfied with their capabilities for evaluating IS impact on organizational performance [16]. Empirical studies have produced mixed or even conflicting results [1]. While some divergence can be attributed to a lack of methodological rigor or the ad hoc nature of the evaluation process, carefully designed studies have produced inconsistent results. Three possible explanations may be:

\- Dependent variables are not consistently defined or measured. There is also a lack of agreement on what constitutes a dependent variable.

\- The intervening effect of time is not explicitly recognized.

\- The complex interactions between technology and its organizational users in the implementation process can lead to outcomes that are not necessarily predictable [9]. As a result, it is difficult to make comparisons and develop consistent theories.

The role of time has received limited attention. An assessment of IS research by Weill and Olson [15] shows an overwhelming preoccupation with cross-sectional surveys in which measurements occur only at one time. A number of IS impact studies also use pretest/posttest designs in which variables are measured before and after system implementation. Although these studies provide important insight into the effects of IS, they are limited in their ability to capture the dynamic changes in organizational effectiveness which Steers [13] describes as contingent, continuous process rather than an end-state or static outcome.

To overcome the limitations of cross-sectional and pretest/posttest data, several researchers have suggested longitudinal studies in which data are collected at two or more times [5,11]. These would be useful because they may reveal patterns of change and establish the direction and magnitude of causal relationships. Despite these recommendations, examples of longitudinal studies are rare.

The primary reason for the dearth of longitudinal studies is their high cost. Because the cost is a function of the duration of the study and studies too often involve graduate students taking courses, few studies have spanned much more than a year. The key questions are:

• What should be the length of such a study?

\- How long should be the interval between data collection points?

The length of a study is determined by the expected behavior of the variables being studied while the time interval between data collection points (often termed “waves”) is primarily a function of the rate of change in the underlying processes that create the variables [19]. This implies that the researcher must have a priori understanding about the behavior of the variables.

The purpose of this paper is report the results of a three-year longitudinal study that helps us understand how IS benefits, both individual and organizational, vary over time.

## 2. Time as an intervening variable

Time is a major variable in the organizational change process. A comprehensive analysis of diffusion of innovations in organizations by Rogers [12] identifies time as one of the major elements. Temporal order is also central to stage theories, which describe the evolution of IS over time [6,17]. The underlying premise in stage theories is that there is a pattern of organizational learning in applying new technology, and that organizations go through several distinct stages before they can really exploit new technology.

Process theories are concerned with outcomes and how they develop over time [10]. The evidence indicates that IS outcomes may vary, depending upon when they were observed.

DeLone and McLean [4], based on a literature review, posit an IS success model that characterizes success as process construct which depends on both the temporal and causal influences of six categories of success measures: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact (Fig. 1). These variables are interrelated and interdependent. It is, however, important to note the temporal order of the various impacts.

## 3. IS impact measures

Success measures of interest to managers are those that can be measured and expressed quantitatively, especially in monetary terms. Such tangible measures are useful because they can be used for justifying IT investment and are universally accepted and understood. In the category of individual impact, a widely used measure is personal productivity improvement, expressed as the number of hours of time saved per week.

Studies of personal productivity improvement indicate a significant variation across job categories. The most striking difference occurs between secretarial and managerial/professional workers (principals) in their use of office information systems. Time savings around 25% for secretaries and 15% for principals are typical.

Efforts to define organizational effectiveness measures for IS have been highly problematic. A variety of measures have been proposed and used, but no single global metric has been found adequate for assessing IT contribution to organizational success $[2]$ . Because of the difficulties in measuring benefits from IT, empirical studies have produced mixed results. Indeed, organizational benefit generally lags behind, often by several years $[3,7]$ .

## 4. A three-year study of IS benefits

## 4.1. Research design and methodology

The development of IS benefits was explored in an engineering division of a large Fortune 100 firm. The study followed the implementation of an integrated office IS from the start of a successful one-year pilot implementation of a commercially available product through three years. The system provided an integrated set of applications: word processing, E-mail, electronic filing, electronic calendar, and decision support (spreadsheets). The use of any of the applications was voluntary.

The system was initially a one-year pilot in the engineering organization with 56 users. After six months, the organization decided to acquire the system on a permanent basis by extending it outside the engineering organization. The total number of users reached 260 during the third year of operation. The system remained relatively stable throughout the three-year period.

The test group consisted of 43 users: 28 principals (managers and professionals) and 15 secretaries. Sixty-seven percent of the group had some prior computer experience. This sample was unchanged throughout the first year of the project. At the end of the third year, 20 randomly selected people were added to compensate for attrition through transfers, promotions and resignations. The final sample consisted of 25 principals and 13 secretaries, with 47% remaining of the original test group. All added participants had access to the system for almost three years. All users had attended training classes, followed by hands-on tutorial exercises. Help desk services were available throughout the duration of the project.

The study was conducted in two phases. In the first, the objective was to measure and analyze system benefits during the year, the focus was primarily on individual user productivity (self-estimated time savings). The second identified and measured long-term effects on personal productivity and organizational effectiveness.

Personal productivity was defined as self-estimated time savings (hours per week) from use of the system. These measurement were made 3, 6, and 12 months from system installation. Organizational effectiveness was much more difficult to measure. Because of the wide variation in the nature of the organization's work, no suitable quantitative metric was found. Therefore it was measured in terms of user perception. Users were asked at the end of the first year whether they agreed or disagreed (on a Likert scale with 1 indicating strong disagreement and 5 indicating strong agreement) that organizational effectiveness had increased. At the end of the third year they were asked to make the same assessment for the previous two-year time frame.

## 4.2. Results

Measurements of individual productivity (Fig. 2) indicate large gains made fairly rapidly by secretaries. Principals, on the other hand, took almost a year to reach time savings of approximately half that of the secretaries. Little further improvement occurred from then with time, though secretaries became more productive.

The estimate of organizational effectiveness in the first year yielded mixed results with only a small positive change reported (mean = 3.58, sigma = 0.98). However, there was a strong consensus that organizational effectiveness had improved in the last two years (mean = 4.35, sigma = 0.69). Users were also asked to estimate the year in which the largest gains were made. The distribution of the responses, shown in Fig. 3, indicates that the majority of respondents reported the largest gains taking place in the second year, with a mean of 1.9 years.

We were also interested in changes to the nature and organization of work due to the system. While 56 percent of users reported some change at the end of the third year, practically all were minor in nature and related to individual work habits (e.g., looking for E-mail messages, better organization of files, etc.). No significant changes in work processes were reported except for the implementation of IS-based cooperative work between two individuals across departments.

## 5. Conclusions

Our study suggests that impact on the individual precedes that on the organization. Much of the IS literature suggests that IT by itself does not create organizational change but that the method of its implementation and use determine the changes that will occur. The results from this study support this view.

Obviously, not all users benefit equally from IT – personal productivity gains vary significantly across users. By integrating these findings with earlier empirical research, we can make some generalizations about the evolution of IT benefits. We can group measurable IT benefits into three categories:

\- The first consists of individual benefits, measured within the first couple of months after system installation.

\- The next set follows the transient phase and represents the first impacts that can be measured with consistency and reliability, but they are short-term impacts at the individual level. As a rule, they can be observed within 6–8 months after system installation.

\- The final group of impacts is organizational in nature, being long-term and observable after one year.

In situations where a pilot system is implemented first and then gradually expanded, achievement of critical mass for mature use may take several years [8].

A central purpose of this paper has been to demonstrate the dynamic nature and temporal order of certain IS impact variables. The findings suggest that managers must carefully consider the timing of their IS assessments. They must understand the difference between short-term and long-term benefits when they develop their evaluation strategies. To gain further insight into the temporal nature of IS impacts, we need more longitudinal evaluations. But they are also time consuming and costly. Therefore they must be planned and carried out with care and used only in situations where they are truly warranted.
