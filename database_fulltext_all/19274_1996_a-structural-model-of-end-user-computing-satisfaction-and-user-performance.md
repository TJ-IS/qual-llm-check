# A structural model of end user computing satisfaction and user performance

Jamshid Etezadi-Amoli $^{a,*}$ , Ali F. Farhoomand $^{b}$

$^{a}$ Department of Decision Sciences & MIS, Concordia University, 1455 de Maisonnouwe Blvd. West, Montreal, Que., Canada, H3G 1M8 $^{b}$ University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, Hongkong

## Abstract

The proliferation of end user computing has necessitated the development of reliable and valid instruments that measure satisfaction in this environment and evaluate its success when used in improving user performance. An instrument for measurement of end user computing satisfaction (EUCS) is developed. Using exploratory factor analysis, six attitudinal dimensions for measurement of EUCS are first identified. Structural equation modelling techniques are then used to explore the relationship between EUCS and user performance. It is shown that the six attitudinal dimensions of EUCS account for a significant portion of the variation in user performance.

Keywords: End user computing; Computing performance; Satisfaction; Measures of satisfaction; Structural equation modelling

## 1. Introduction

For nearly two decades MIS researchers have been preoccupied with the issues surrounding the evaluation of IS success $[25,26]$ . Because of a lack of objective measures of IS success, however, researchers have, by and large, relied on users' perceptions as a measure of the success of IS. These studies suggest that users' attitudes can be used as a surrogate for system quality, information quality, system use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact $[8]$ .

Perceptual measures of IS success have been employed extensively over the years. Such measurement instruments have basically relied on user information satisfaction (UIS) as a surrogate measure of IS success. Miller [22] provides a summary of these instruments and the studies that have used them. The most widely used measure is the instrument developed by Bailey and Pearson [3]. A 22-item version and a 13-item version of this instrument were later developed by Ives et al. [16] and Baroudi and Orlikowski [5], respectively. In spite of the popularity of these instruments, however, several studies have cautioned about some of the methodological and conceptual difficulties associated with them [13,21,27]. One of the difficulties with the UIS instruments has been the blurry distinction between cognitive aspects (beliefs about characteristics of a system) and affective aspects (attitudes towards a system or towards using a system). Goodhue [14] argues that this confusion introduces additional bias or random error into measurements. A more important problem is that UIS instruments rely solely on cognitive/affective dimension of IS success without accounting for the performance-related dimensions. In other words, performance-related goals for which systems are designed are not considered in the assessment process.

The proliferation of EUC in the 1980s has necessitated the development of specific measures of success. Doll and Torkzadeh [9] argue that the UIS instruments are not suitable for measuring the success of EUC activities because they were specifically designed for a conventional computing environment. In this light, they developed an instrument consisting of 12 items measuring five factors. In response to the criticism that their instrument of EUCS excludes performance-related measures [11], Doll and Torkzadeh [10] maintain that since performance-related behaviours may be application-specific, development of generalizable measures of EUC success is difficult. Their sentiment is somewhat shared by Amoroso and Cheney [2] who acknowledge that EUC success should include performance-related variables, but maintain that since these measures will probably be application-specific, generalization may be difficult. They also examined the quality dimension of end user developed applications by focusing on EUCS and utilization. They revised the short-form version of the Bailey and Pearson measure to develop a 10-item EUCS instrument. However, it primarily focuses on the information product component of EUCS, leaving out other dimensions.

This paper is predicated on the premise that we need to focus on correspondence between the original goals for which the system is designed and the actual outcomes of the system (e.g., [1]). Since one of the generic properties of systems is that they are goal-oriented [24], the evaluation of IS success should also take into account the individual and organizational impacts of the system. In this light, we have relied on the model of IS success introduced by Delone and McLean. Here, system quality and information quality are assumed to affect user satisfaction and use. The latter constructs in turn act as antecedents of individual and organizational impacts.

Since the unit of analysis is the user, we circumscribe the domain of the IS success model and apply it to EUC success. Then satisfaction with system quality and output quality (independent variables)

leads to improvement in performance, as perceived by the user (dependent variable).

This model relies on the principle of management measurement as opposed to scientific measurement; it emphasizes the users and their responses to the measure rather than the object measured [20]. Even though different stakeholders may evaluate the impact of a system on organizational performance differently, in an EUC environment, evaluation of a system's success can best be performed by its users in terms of the system's impact on their performance. The EUC success model here assumes that the introduction of a system brings about changes in the users' work environment, and that the success of the system is in turn related to the positive feelings of the users about these changes.

We tested the efficacy of this model via an empirical study of 341 end users. First, we identified the underlying dimensions of EUCS via exploratory factor analysis. We then used structural modelling techniques to explore the relationship between these factors and user performance.

## 2. Instrument development

Based on previous research, 27 items were generated to measure the degree of end users' satisfaction with various aspects of the software application that were used most frequently. The instructions on the resulting questionnaire requested that respondents indicate, on a five-point scale, their degree of satisfaction with each item (2 being very satisfied, -2 being very dissatisfied). The respondents were also asked to indicate the importance of each item on an adjacent four-point scale (3 being very important, 0 being unimportant). This scale was included to assess the relative weights of the underlying factors.

In addition, we relied on the existing literature (e.g., [23]), as well as interviews with several end users during the pilot study to generate four outcome variables to assess the impact of the primary application software on the user's work environment. These items measured the degree of success of application software in terms of (i) improving the user's quality of work, (ii) making the end user's job easier, (iii) saving the end user time, and (iv) helping fulfil the needs and requirements of the end user's job. ## 3. Exploratory factor analysis

Before analyzing the data, we excluded Variable 22 (adequacy of communication links with corporate database) from the study because about 82% of the respondents were users of stand-alone PCs. To determine the psychometric properties of the instrument, we factor analyzed the correlation matrix of the remaining 30 items using SPSS. In order to find the smallest number of factors that would best reproduce the observed correlations and also provide a meaningful or interpretable grouping of the variables, we performed several factor analyses. Both the rule of eigen-values greater than one [18] and the scree test [7] were employed in order to determine the number of factors.

An exploratory factor analysis of the 30 variables revealed that eight factors had eigen-values greater than one. $^{1}$ The solution based on eight eigen-values, however, did not produce a meaningful result. $^{2}$ Based on the scree test, we decided to choose six or

seven factors. The orthogonal (varimax) and correlated (oblimin) solutions for both models were examined for fit and interpretation. The solution with seven factors yielded more meaningful results, as well as a good fit based on the residual correlations. The correlated factor pattern obtained from the oblimin solution provided a better grouping of the variable than the varimax solution. In essence, this model shows the effect of user satisfaction with different aspects of an application program on his/her performance.

We used LISREL VII [17] to test the efficacy of the structural model. In LISREL, the overall fit of a model can be assessed by chi-square statistic, goodness-of-fit index (GFI), adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI), and root mean square residuals (RMR). In general, good fit is indicated when chi-square is not significant, the GFI and AGFI are close to one, and the RMR is close to zero. In practice, a GFI or AGFI greater than 0.8 and a RMR close to 0.05 are considered as indicators of good fit. Since chi-square statistic as a measure of overall fit is very sensitive to both sample size and distribution of observed variables [6,12], the ratio of chi-square to degrees of freedom was used instead. Hayduk [15] maintains that a ratio of less than 3 indicates good fit.

As seen in Table 5, all goodness-of-fit statistics are in an acceptable range. The squared multiple correlations of the variables, which indicate the proportion of variation of an item accounted for by its corresponding factor, $^{3}$ are also shown in Table 5. The factor loadings range from 0.45 to 0.88, indicating high correlations between items and their corresponding factors.

The coefficient of determination for the structural model was 0.530, indicating that the six attitudinal factors together explain about 53% of variation in user performance.

Previous studies have argued that the underlying dimensions of EUCS are orthogonal. To test this, we calculated the correlations between the six factors in our study. As shown in Table 6, most individual correlations among the six factors are statistically significant with moderate to large magnitudes, manifesting non-orthogonality of the factors (p-value < 0.05). In addition, we reexamined the structural model under the assumption of orthogonal factors. Since the orthogonal factor model is a special case of correlated factor, we tested validity of the uncorrelated factor model by calculating the difference between the chi-square values obtained from the two models. The difference between the chi-square values for the two competing models was 265.71 with 15 degrees of freedom, yielding a p-value less than 0.001 for the hypothesis of orthogonal factors. Based on these results, we concluded that the underlying factors of EUC success are significantly related to each other, and thus cannot be considered orthogonal.

## 5. Conclusions

MIS research has been faced with a multitude of challenges related to the measurement of IS success. Progress in this area of research has been slow, largely because IS success is a multi-dimensional construct that encompasses several interrelated and interdependent components. In spite of this, however, several promising studies have recently addressed some of the conceptual and methodological issues. A group of these studies has focused on structural modelling of IS success in order to separate antecedents variables from criterion measures and map the temporal relationships among these variables; while another group has addressed the issues associated with conceptual development of the construct.

In this paper, we focused on EUC in order to reduce the domain of the study to only one context of the computing environment. We addressed one of the major criticisms related to perceptual studies of IS success by including a set of outcome-oriented variables. Using structural modelling techniques, we found that a significant variation in user performance can be explained by six correlated attitudinal factors underlying EUCS. In the absence of substantive theoretical foundation, however, we believe that the results of this study should be treated with care. More specifically, we warn against interpreting the relation between the EUCS factors and user performance in a causal manner. In other words, it is possible that this relation is reciprocal: performance may change a user's perception of application software and subsequently affect the degree of his/her satisfaction. Further research is required to explore this relationship. This should eventually help the development of a model for assessment of the overall success of EUC.

In spite of this limitation, the study has some important practical implications. For example, the average score of items measuring a particular factor of EUCS may be used to assess users' satisfaction with each dimension. This should allow companies to identify major areas of satisfaction/dissatisfaction with the use of a given application software. Similarly, the average score of items measuring user performance should enable companies to assess the degree of success of a given application software in improving work environment.
