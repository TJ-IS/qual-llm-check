# Critical success factors in data management

Carl Stephen Guynes $^{*}$ , Michael T. Vanecek

Information Systems Department, College of Business, University of North Texas, Denton, TX 76203, USA

## Abstract

The purpose of this exploratory study was to determine the critical success factors (CSFs) of data and database administration functions. A field survey was made in 16 large corporations of information systems executives who were identified as having a key role and interest in data management issues. Several important data and database administration factors were identified. Of these, three were found to be CSFs for data administration and four were found to be CSFs for database administration. Two overlapped both areas. The overlapping factors had to be considered from both a technical and organizational perspective.

Keywords: Data administration; Database administration; Critical success factors; Data management; Database management systems

## 1. Introduction

This paper reports the results of a field survey conducted to determine the critical success factors (CSFs) in data management. Authors have offered different views of the objectives and functions of data management and have offered various definitions of database management, data administration, and database administration. As early as 1976, Fry and Sibley [9] introduced several objectives of database management. Other authors have stated that the concept of data administration is an integral part of data management and is vital to the successful operation of the information systems function [2], [13], [28].

Jacobs [15] stated that recent technological trends are having a significant impact on the continuing evolution of data administration. He stated that the IS field is witnessing the movement of the data administration function from its primarily technical database orientation to a more managerial one. The influx of microcomputers, along with the growth of such user-based activities as end-user computing, client-server computing, and decision based systems have stimulated this evolution.

Even though most data managers either perform or oversee very technical operations, many consider their jobs managerial. This functional dichotomy is evident in the evolving, but not universally accepted, split of the data management function into database and data administration. The resultant split has the first group doing technical functions, while the second do managerial or administrative functions. Understanding data management's role and definition is a necessary and important first step in ensuring the efficient operation of that environment.

## 2. The critical success factors approach

Although several authors have published articles dealing with critical success factors or data administration, none have combined the two. This study fills that void by combining both critical success factors and data administration theory and research.

## 2.1. Definition of critical success factors

Rockart [21] defined CSFs to be those functions that must go well in order to ensure success for an organization. CSFs include areas and issues that are critical to both an organization's current operating activities and future success. The approach uses a series of interviews to obtain the information necessary to determine which factors are critical. Rockart further stated that the critical success factor approach can be useful at any level of management, as long as it centers on the information needs for management control rather than strategic planning. Several authors [3], [24] stated that the CSF methodology has proven beneficial in generating high level user involvement and support, and has enabled IS analysts to become business partners with the client business.

## 2.2. Limitations of the approach

Davis [4] raised several questions about the CSF approach. He was particularly concerned about the capacity that humans have to construct simplified models that correctly reflect real-world situations. Davis and Rockart conferred on these issues [5] and concluded that the researcher working with the executive must be aware of Davis' concerns and work around them. If the researcher understands the business he/she will be in a better position to clarify some of the executive's responses and help solidify the critical factors themselves.

Boynton and Zmud [1] asserted that the CSF approach is difficult to use and is not appropriate unless the researcher is skillful in the method. They suggested that the researcher could introduce bias during the interview process. In spite of the potential problems that can arise with this method, they noted that, with proper training, the CSF approach is practicable and potentially useful in planning and requirements analysis.

## 3. The data administration concept

The term data administration has appeared in the literature for many years, but no generally accepted definition has emerged. Sibley [22] stated that data administration is a special form of managerial control, which includes authority over data integrity and responsibility over database efficiency. Kahn [16] said data administration involves the collection, storage, and dissemination of data as a globally administered and standardized resource. Durell [8] said the overall objective of data administration is to plan, document, manage, and control the information resources of an entire organization. Mark Gillenson, of the IBM Systems Research Institute [11] stated GUIDE's position on data administration as:

“Data administration is made up of two components which may or may not be organized as subordinate departments. One of those components, data management, is primarily a planning and analysis type of function. It may be responsible for data planning, accountability, training, policy development, standards setting, database design, and liaison support to application development groups... The other subordinate department, database administration, is responsible for managing the data on a day-to-day, operational level. Its responsibilities may include performance monitoring, troubleshooting, security monitoring, coordination of database activities with systems programming and operations personnel, database design, and liaison support to application development groups.”

## 3.1. Data administration versus database administration

One purpose of this effort is to isolate the critical elements of the data administration function from those of database administration. Although some overlap exists, the focus is different.

Several authors have presented their views on the difference between data administration (DA) and database administration (DBA) activities. George Tillmann [26], of Booz-Allen and Hamilton, discussed what he thought were the differences, and presented a set of activities for each. He found several overlapping activities. For example, the DBA duty of establishing data standards also appears as a DA activity. Conversely, several of the DA duties, such as physical database design, also appear as a DBA activity. Kahn and Garceau [18] present an excellent developmental model of the database administration function, and many of the activities here were drawn from that model.

Durell [7] distinguished between DA and DBA functions by stating that database administrators are only responsible for the “design, implementation, security, and maintenance of physical databases.” He argued that it is the responsibility of the data administration function to establish the contents and boundaries of each database.

## 4. Research methodology

The study was intended to answer four questions:

1. What are the perceived differences between Data Administration and Database Administration functions?

2. What are the CSFs for Data Administration?

3. What are the CSFs for Database Administration?

4. What are the overlapping CSFs for both?

The answer to the first question differentiates between those activities that the participating organizations attribute to each. The answers to the second, third, and fourth questions defines those that are the most critical activities within each category.

## 4.1. Research design

The first part of the effort included both unstructured and structured interviews with several key information systems executives. The interviews were used to isolate their perceptions of the CSFs associated with data and database administration. Upon completion of the interview process the researchers developed a structured questionnaire; this combined those factors suggested in earlier articles with the results of the interview process. The same executives provided the basis of a pilot test of the questionnaire. Other experts from academia and industry also provided insights as to the face validity of the instrument. The input from all the sources was used in the design of the final questionnaire.

The second phase of the research design consisted of delivering the questionnaire to key information systems executives in 16 major national and international corporations. The subjects were requested to complete the questionnaire and participate in an interview process.

## 4.2. Subjects

The 16 corporations that participated in the study represent all segments of industry, including vendor, user, manufacturing, retail, and other groups. Further, the majority of the companies had multinational operations. Many had global responsibilities for the management of their organization's worldwide data resources. Thus, for the purposes of this exploratory study, the sample is quite general and provides the necessary knowledge foundation.

## 4.3. Instrument design

The study used a questionnaire and unstructured interviews to gather systems executives' opinions about data and database administration classification and critical factors. The questionnaire grouped multiple data activities in ten different categories. Room was left for the respondent to add other activities as well as to suggest that an activity be moved to a different category.

The initial categories and activities were based on the results of a review of the literature, especially the works of Gillenson, Kahn and Garceau. The researchers further refined the instrument based on the results of the first phase of the study. The ten categories used were database physical control, administration of data items, DBMS-based application control, hardware related planning, database design, software planning, education, DBMS installation and enhancements, database security, and database standards enforcement. Each data activity factor was assigned to only one category.

## 4.4. Measurements

Respondents classified each function into DA, DBA, both, or neither. They also indicated, on a 9 point Likert scale, how critical they perceived each factor to be. The scale ranged from a ‘strongly agree’ that the factor was absolutely critical to successful management of the data function to ‘strongly disagree.’

The Likert measurement examined the respondents perceptions of each factor's function and importance by itself. However, we also wanted to know the importance of each factor relative to the others.

Hence, this part of the questionnaire required the respondents to rank their selections. Ties were not permitted.

## 4.5. Data analysis

Data analysis consisted of filtering the factors until only the most critical ones remained. This consisted of a multiple step process that combined the information from the Likert and ranked order measures to select those that met both criteria. The result, determined by combining the results of the measures and classification process, along with the intersection of the Likert and ranked factors reduced the initial set of 49 activities to the critical success factors.

We determined the association among the rankings established by the company managers by using the Kendall coefficient of concordance (W). The coefficient of concordance is an index of the divergence of the actual agreement shown in the data from the maximum possible agreement. The coefficient of concordance measure has been particularly useful in prior studies of intertest reliability, and has applications in studies of clusters of variables [23].

The next step was to determine the order of factors based on rankings. Table 5 contains those data administration factors that were in the top five. The strength index is a relative measure of the consensus about a factor's importance. It is the number of times that a factor was selected multiplied by its average rank value. For instance, “Developing Data Definitions” was selected by 15 of the respondents. The average rank value is the average of the rank values given a factor by those respondents who selected the factor. In this example, the average rank value is 16.00 for the 15 respondents resulting in a strength index of $(15 \cdot 16.00) = 240$ .

Kendall's coefficient of concordance (W) for the ranked values was computed to be 0.33 at the 0.01 level of significance. This W value expresses the degree of agreement among the 16 executives in ranking the various data management factors. Hence, we can conclude that the agreement among the 16 managers is higher than it would be by chance. The low significance value allows us to reject the hypothesis that the managers rankings are unrelated to one another. This significant value of W may be interpreted as meaning that the managers are applying essentially the same standard in ranking the factors or activities under study. Their ordering may serve as a “standard,” when there is no relevant external criterion for ordering the items under consideration.

## 6. Implications

## 6.1. Database administration issues

Defining an effective strategy for implementing a new database is a critical database administration task. The data management group must carefully consider the impact that bringing up new databases will have on existing systems. If the implementation strategy is well thought out, it will minimize the potential negative impact that the implementation of a new database could have on the organization and facilitate a smooth installation of the new system.

The study further indicates that it is critical to establish effective policies for database recovery. Most of the executives stated that they spent a large amount of time establishing thorough recovery procedures, because it was good insurance in the event of a system crash. The users should also monitor the usage of those portions of the data base for which they are responsible, and be certain that the database has reliable audit procedures and stable backup and recovery procedures.

Another area that was found to be critical was that of determining data structures. The database administration team must be involved in determining the most effective way to organize the database and establish the physical characteristics of the database. They must work closely with the data administration group when moving from logical data modeling to the determination of the physical data structure. This finding is compatible with the comments of Matthews and Mcgee [19].

## 6.2. Data administration issues

Developing logical data models is one of the critical issues in data administration. Several of the executives stated that data modeling is very helpful if it is used to build corporate databases rather than just to get control of the data. They thought that models should be detailed enough to provide an accurate framework for application development, but simple enough to be understood and completed in a reasonable length of time. This is consistent with the positions taken by Inmon [14].

The interviews indicated that the data administration group, when modeling, should concentrate on the human activity of getting user support and cooperation and avoid becoming too enamored with modeling tools. All of those interviewed said data modeling is highly dependent on the support and cooperation of the users and it is important to let them know that their contributions are important. This reinforces the findings of Tseng and Mannino [27] in their studies of user involvement in the collection of database requirements.

The researchers found that it was critical to integrate the applications logical data models into an overall enterprise data model. Several executives suggested taking a modular approach to enterprise data modeling; managers should be wary of a business plan that expects to implement a strategic data plan encompassing the entire organization. This is consistent with the writings of Due [6] and Thompson [25]. They said that existing systems often represent a large capital investment. If they are still effective, it would not make sense to replace them; moreover the concept of designing the application to meet the business objectives of the organization is sound, but endeavors that are over zealous can result in problems.

Several executives stated that a related aspect of data administration is an organization's corporate data policy. This is consistent with McGoveran's [20] position that one of the major reasons for establishing a corporate data policy is because the underlying data in a database is as important to manage as are the processes that use the data. The executives also verified that educating analysts in data analysis methodology is very important.

The majority of the participants thought that data administration personnel should function as a service provider, be involved in setting user standards, and help to implement them. Several stated that data administration should make data standards and data dictionary procedures mesh with the existing environment and link standards to existing application development guidelines and procedures.

## 6.3. Overlapping factors

The factors that appeared in both the database and data administration set of CSFs were in specifying database access policy and educating analysts in the use of the database. Controlling access is critical from a managerial viewpoint as well as a technical viewpoint. Educating analysts is critical.

## 6.4. DA and DBA differences

Many of the organizations sampled are actively migrating from the technical focus of database administration to the business organization focus of data administration. This is consistent with the reported findings of Goodhue, Quillard, and Rockart [12] in their study of data management. Apparently, organizations will achieve maximum benefit from data administration if they concentrate their efforts on specific business objectives.
