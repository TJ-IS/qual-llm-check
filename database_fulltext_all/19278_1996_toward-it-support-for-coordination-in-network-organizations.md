# Toward IT support for coordination in network organizations

Chee Ching ${}^{a,*}$ , Clyde W. Holsapple ${}^{b}$ , Andrew B. Whinston ${}^{c}$

$^{a}$ School of Accountancy, College of Business, Arizona State University, Tempe, AZ 85287, USA $^{b}$ Department of Decision Science & Information Systems, College of Business & Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington KY, USA $^{c}$ Department of Management Science & Information Systems, Graduate School of Business, University of Texas, Austin TX, USA

## Abstract

A critical issue facing managers of information technology (IT) today is how to respond to the emergence and growth of the network organization that is virtually formed by separate firms, each retaining its own authority in major budgeting and pricing matters, to function as integral parts of a greater organization. What can and should be done from an IT standpoint to support and exploit this new organization form? What needs and opportunities exist for new kinds of computer-based systems devised specifically to facilitate management and coordination in network organizations? A prerequisite for answering these questions in a systematic, definitive way is clear understanding of the variables and constructs involved in coordinating such organizations. An empirically supported model of coordination in network organizations is introduced. Our model development takes a ‘reputation’-oriented framework of coordination within network organizations as a starting point. The model posits that participating firms’ reputation strongly influence coordination decisions in networks, affecting each firms’ initiation into the organization, selection for task execution, and termination from the network.

Keywords: Network organization; Coordination; Reputation; Information technology; Communication; Virtual organization; Alliance; Outsourcing

## 1. Introduction

In order to remain competitive and adaptive to the global markets, more and more corporations in various industries have adopted the newer organizational form: network organization [8,9,12,17,23,29]. This phenomenon has been driven by efforts to achieve greater structural effectiveness and responsiveness in an extremely competitive and turbulent environment marked by increasing globalization, technological change, and technology transfer [25]. As shown in Fig. 1, a network organization is virtually formed by separate firms, each retaining its own authority in major budgeting and pricing matters, to function as integral parts of a greater organization [10]. This relatively new type of organization has been variously referred to as modular organization [28], virtual corporation [4], organic network [20], hybrid arrangement [3,24], value-adding partnership [14], and Interorganizational Configurations (IOCs) [16].

A critical issue facing managers of information technology (IT) today is how to respond to the emergence and growth of the network organization. What can and should be done from an IT standpoint to support and exploit this new organizational form? What needs and opportunities exist for new kinds of computer-based systems devised specifically to facilitate management and coordination in network organizations? A prerequisite for answering these questions in a systematic, definitive way is clear understanding of the variables and constructs involved in coordinating such organizations. An empirically supported model of coordination in network organizations would furnish a sound understanding of the important variables, constructs, and relationships.

Here we introduce such a model as a foundation for designing, managing, and assessing aspects of IT relevant to coordination within network organizations. There appears to be a substantial unrealized opportunity for using IT in support of network coordination activities. In a survey of network coordination activities at over forty computer manufacturers, we discovered relatively little application of information technology to these activities. For instance, the average percentage of partners communicating through electronic data interchange (EDI) is only

15.6%. Our survey also found that much of the coordination activity occurs above the operational level of transactions, involving crucial decision making and important information needs at the managerial and strategic levels.

Our model development takes a ‘reputation’-oriented framework of coordination within network organizations as a starting point [6]. This framework furnishes the central construct for our coordination model of network organizations: reputation. The model posits that participating firms’ reputation strongly influence coordination decisions in networks, affecting each firm’s initiation into the organization, selection for task execution, and termination from the network. The model further identifies two major dimensions of reputation and variables salient to measuring each. The model asserts a correlation between reputation and certain variables mea suring transaction and agency costs of coordination. We conducted a field study to examine the validity of our model, to explore the various aspects of coordination processes in a network organization, and to understand whether and how information technology has been applied to supporting these processes.

## 2. Literature review

A network organization differs from traditional hierarchic firms and markets in many ways. Its functioning stresses complementarity, ongoing relationships, and reciprocity between a core firm and its partners. On the one hand, firms engaged in a network as the core firm's partners are fairly flexible in that they operate autonomously without central administrative control. This contrasts sharply with a hierarchic firm in which relationships among participants are comparatively fixed and inflexible. On the other hand, participants of a network do cooperate with the core firm by complementing each other's strengths. In contrast to an open market where prices are the principal means of coordination, there is some joint commitment among the firms in a network organization: establishing and cultivating ongoing relationships. Further discussion of the distinction among networks, hierarchies, and markets appears in [23,27].

There is a core or lead firm in a network organization. It is the integrator that establishes partnerships with various firms to form a network in a quest for access to resources, markets, technology, and/or economies of scale. To facilitate the emergence of specific operating networks, the core firm needs to identify appropriate partners. The basic alliance strategies include licensing, marketing agreements, joint ventures, franchising, private label agreements, common standards, consortia, and buyer-seller arrangement [2].

There are two major approaches taken by companies when forming network organizations: downward and lateral [5]. Downward networking is usually initiated by a large, vertically integrated company that seeks to reduce its overhead by downsizing and outsourcing [15]. Outsourcing breaks down the company's vertical structure. For instance, rather than making virtually all its critical parts, Ford Motor Company has become the core of a network of suppliers that provide them. The lateral approach, on the other hand, is observed when some specialized firms become engaged in value-adding partnerships in an effort to achieve a strategic alliance. Typically, these firms constitute a value-added chain. For example, the major drug and health care product distributor, McKesson Corporation, has been quite successful in lateral networking that includes manufacturers, distributors, and third-party insurance suppliers.

By not acquiring ownership of other firms, the core firm of a network organization achieves profits at reduced risk. Each of the participating firms can also benefit by being relatively adaptable and responsive (i.e., like a small, specialized company), while simultaneously realizing scale economies (i.e., like a large, integrated firm). Once networks are formed, they require ongoing attention to operate smoothly and effectively as a whole. A core firm directs the coordination in a network, much as a 'channel captain' in a vertical distribution system [28]. It monitors relationships not only with its current partners, but also with potential ones.

The relationship between a core firm and its partners is “a mutual orientation of two firms toward each other” based on exchange processes [13]. Johanson and Mattsson see such an exchange process as “not only a learning process but also an adaptation process.” Participants learn to fit with each other. They adapt to each other technically, logistically, administratively, or even financially in the course of carrying out their collective activities. By sharing information with existing partners (such as how the network runs, or technological and marketing developments), the core firm can help its partners learn. A core firm may also engage in nurturing and disciplinary behavior when encountering partners’ opportunistic behaviors or incompetency. This results in changing boundaries of network organizations. When cooperating with loyal and committed partners, the core firm needs to avoid being passive, due to its strong bonds with its partners. Not to do so may prevent it from terminating unsatisfactory partners and introducing new partners to its network in due time [19]. When dealing with fairly opportunistic firms, it is important that the core firm maintain an up-to-date, unbiased assessment of the value of each potential and existing partner in order to facilitate the entry and exit of firms in a timely and effective manner.

Thus, a network grows, shrinks, and evolves over time. Balancing structural flexibility with the fair amount of commitment among firms is a nontrivial task confronting a core firm when managing its network. Such coordination is essential for sustaining long-term efficiency and effectiveness. We contend that IT systems can be devised to support such coordination, or even enable the existence of networks by making effective coordination possible. Such systems have a potential for usefully supporting adaptive, flexible processes in networks, fostering greater longevity and their more widespread adoption; e.g., Electronic Data Interchange or “electronic commerce” systems are used by many companies to interchange data (e.g., about inventories, orders, payments) via direct computer-to-computer transmissions using standard formats devised by participating firms [26]. However, EDI is largely restricted to supporting certain transaction processes among participating firms. From a long term perspective, the success of a network organization depends on more than efficient transaction processing. It also depends on such factors as participant reliability, compatibility, mutual trust, cooperation, creativity, and prudent evaluation. We assert that computer-implemented processes for network organizations beyond the transaction level are possible, particularly with respect to representing, tracking, and assessing these factors.

## 3. A reputation-oriented coordination model for network organizations

In seeking to determine and formalize the defining characteristics of reputation, we conducted a case study with Cisco Systems Inc., a fast growing and highly profitable company, which attributes its success primarily to its networking strategy [8]. We studied how Cisco coordinates its networking activities and explored the criteria it uses to evaluate its partners [7]. This provided us with insights into the development of the research issues and the material for a questionnaire for the field survey; e.g., we identified a set of criteria for which Cisco evaluates its partners as the starting point of operationalizing the construct of reputation. In addition, we have had lengthy and insightful discussions with the Chairman of the Board and the Manager for the Division of Commodity Management at two other high-tech companies on similar issues. Representatives of all three companies have completed an early pretest version of the survey questionnaire and/or provided valuable comments in purifying it; this led to the version used in this study.

The model advanced here is the result of synthesizing ideas from the literatures of organization science and IT, from our previous theoretical work on reputation as a coordination mechanism, and from a case study conducted with the very successful core firm of a network organization. We use performance review results as a surrogate for the reputation construct. Presentation of the model is partitioned into three submodels, each of which is empirically explored via a survey. The intent of this initial survey was to: (1) determine whether the model was meaningful and plausible; (2) collect further information as a basis for extending the model; (3) help in validating the survey instrument for a larger scale field study; and (4) yield descriptive statistics about the nature of coordination in networks.

The first submodel, M1, asserts that the network participants' reputation can be perceived in two dimensions: contractual and non-contractual (Fig. 2). Formal contracts commonly exist between a core firm and each of its partners. But, our case study revealed numerous non-contractual aspects of reputation. This submodel specifies six indicators for contractual: defects, competitive price, timely response, timely delivery, cost reduction plan, and cycle time; and six for non-contractual: ability to grow, controlled production plan, technology design capabilities, new product introduction, technology transfer capabilities, and electronic data interchange capabilities. Identification of these variables resulted primarily from our case study and literature review.

The second submodel, M2, maintains that a core firm's coordination decisions are influenced by the reputation of its current (and even potential) partners (Fig. 3). This submodel is concerned with three major kinds of coordination decisions: those involving initiation of a partner into the network, selection of a partner for a task/project, and termination of a partner from the network. Although there are other factors possibly affecting coordination decisions, such as partner availability and the frequency with which a partner has performed similar tasks, they are not shown or considered within the current scope of the model.

The third submodel, M3, asserts that reputation is related to coordination costs. It has been established that coordination costs can be represented by the dimensions of transaction costs and agency costs where five variables (contractual, communication, inventory holding, transportation, and search costs) determine the former and two (monitoring and bonding costs) determine the latter [11]. For this study, we selected three that are most relevant to the partner review processes: contractual, communication, and monitoring costs, hypothesizing an inverse association between each of them and a partner's reputation. This submodel is summarized in Fig. 4.

## 4. Research design and the survey findings

In the interest of external validity, i.e., the degree of confidence one has in generalizing the specific relationships found in a study sample with the population at large, it is desirable to select a random sample of core firms. However, lacking such a directory, our data collection could only target core firms in clearly identifiable networks. To help avoid selection bias problems, our questionnaire included a component inquiring about the firm's activities, allowing us to ensure that all usable respondents do indeed qualify as engaging in a substantial degree of networking. The focus is on ensuring internal validity, as it is unlikely that unobserved explanations would produce effects across the entire spectrum.

Our field survey was conducted by contacting computer manufacturers listed in the Ward's Business Directory of U.S. Private and Public Companies, 1993 (SIC = 3571). Forty-one of them participated. The study's scope is confined to production relationships between the core and its partners (e.g., as opposed to marketing relationships). These partners are used in the areas of parts/raw material, fabrication/configuration, work-in-process, assembly, testing, and handling finished products. Our principal research instrument was the questionnaire asking about characteristics of network coordination and the current state of computer support available for this. The questionnaire consists of two parts: Part A to study the three submodels, and Part B to explore the degree of the current IT support in coordination activities conducted by core firms (Appendix A).

![](/api/attachments/ECTZR9M4/fulltext/images/275fe5938b91a4659e212e13f981d0439bceb5ca3771646459f3601959cafd12.jpg)  
Fig. 4. M3: Reputation is correlated with coordinates costs.

The goal of this cross-sectional study is to understand the characteristics of sound coordination processes in a network organization, so we decided to use a key informant method, as it is often involved in measurement of organizational characteristics $[22]$ . The informants, in our case, are people directly in charge of outsourcing, partnering, and/or networking activities in a core firm, such as Production Manager and Materials Manager. Data collection was administered via mail questionnaire to key informants in candidate core firms. The subjects of our study are the core firms that conduct networking activities. To promote a high response rate, preliminary contacts via postcards were made, as well as telephone follow-up to non-respondents encouraging their participation.

## 4.1. Empirical exploration of submodel M1

To examine the validity of submodel M1, that reputation has both contractual and non-contractual dimensions, we asked respondents, on a scale of 1 to 10 (with 1 being extremely important), to rate the importance of each of the following measures for which they evaluate their partners:

Variable Description
X1 defects
X2 competitive price
X3 timely response
X4 timely delivery
X5 cost reduction plan
X6 cycle time
X7 ability to grow
X8 controlled production plan
X9 technology design capabilities
X10 new product introduction
X11 technology transfer capabilities
X12 EDI capabilities

Applying factor analysis to the collected data, we were able to identify two distinct factors, as our model suggested: contractual reputation and non-contractual reputation. Two variables (X5 and X6), however, were found to cross load on both factors (Appendix B). Thus, our study validates the unidimensionality of ten of the model's twelve measures on the two factors; i.e., ten of the indicators measure the underlying construct of interest.

Following Bagozzi [1], the reliability of individual indicators can be computed as:

$$
\rho_ {i} = \left(\lambda_ {i} ^ {2} \operatorname{var} A\right) / \left(\lambda_ {i} ^ {2} \operatorname{var} A + \epsilon_ {i}\right),
$$

where $\rho_{i}$ is the reliability of item i, $\lambda_{i}$ is the factor loading relating item i to its respective construct A and $\epsilon_{i}$ is the error variance of item i. Similarly, the composite reliability of n measures of a construct A, can be calculated as:

$$
\rho_ {c} = \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \lambda_ {i} ^ {2} \operatorname{var} A\right) / \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \lambda_ {i} ^ {2} \operatorname{var} A + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \epsilon_ {i}\right),
$$

where $\rho_{c}$ is the composite reliability. A $\rho_{c}$ greater than 0.5 implies that the variance captured by the construct is more than captured by error components. In our study, the composite reliability, $\rho_{c}$ , is 0.71 for the six measures (X1–X6) for contractual reputation and 0.63 for the other six measures (X7–X12) for non-contractual reputation (Appendix B). These values indicate that the measures for both dimensions of reputation are reliable.

The survey findings show that: (1) all twelve measures of reputation identified in submodel M1 are reliable with respect to the two factors identified by the submodel; (2) most of the twelve variables are unidimensional with respect to those factors; and (3) that there are indeed two distinct factors of reputation as claimed by the submodel.

## 4.2. Empirical exploration of submodel M2

Submodel M2 concerns the relationship between a core firm's coordination decisions and its partners' reputation. Regarding assigning projects/tasks, almost $90\%$ of the respondents consider partners' reputation important or one of the deciding factors. The figure rises to nearly $97\%$ when terminating a partner.

When selecting from multiple candidate partners for a particular project/task, respondents were also asked to rank six criteria (with 1 being the most important and no ties allowed). We first took the means of the rankings for each of these criteria, the one with the smallest mean being considered the most significant coordination criterion. Generalized linear models with multi-comparison tests were used to determine the rankings among these criteria. Scheffe's test result showed that the top three ranked criteria (performance review results, quotes for cost, and project/task specific experience), do not differ significantly from one another; neither do the fourth and fifth (number of years working together and geographical location), or the fifth and sixth (geographic location and whether a partner subcontracts) (see Table 1).

These descriptive statistics from the study offer strong evidence that each of the three types of coordination decisions represented in the model are highly influenced by relative reputation of partners as reflected in performance review results.

## 4.3. Empirical exploration of submodel M3

Submodel M3 depicts the relationship between partners' reputation and coordination costs incurred by the core firm. When examining how the use of partners' performance review results (reputation in our model) may affect the coordination costs incurred by a core firm in a network, we found that for in-process communication more than 75% of the respondents agree or strongly agree that they spend less time in conducting in-process communication with a partner having satisfactory past performance than a partner having a less favorable reputation. More than 80% of the respondents agree or strongly agree that the chances a partner with satisfactory past performance forfeits a contract will be smaller than for a partner with less favorable past performance. More than 75% of them agree or strongly agree that they spend less time in monitoring a partner with satisfactory past performance than for a partner having less favorable performance records. Over 95% of the respondents indicate that they are more willing to cultivate a long term relationship with a partner having satisfactory past performance than a partner having less favorable performance records.

In summary, we found that these descriptive statistics were very much in line with the contention that a negative association exists between partners' reputation and some coordination costs: contractual, communication, and monitoring.

## 4.4. Exploring the existing IT support for coordinating network organizations

Malone et al. have discussed how information technology may lead to a firm's greater use of markets (they did not separate network organizations from markets) to coordinate economic activities and reduce coordination costs relative to hierarchies [18]. Interestingly, results from Part B of our survey show that existing IT support for network coordination is far from a saturation point (for computer manufacturers).

Respondents were also asked to rate the degree of automation/computerization of their companies in accessing information required for conducting network coordination activities. Only about one-fourth of the respondents have more than half of the general background information about production-partners available from computer files or databases. The figure rises to about 38% for production-partners' performance evaluation information; and about 32% for project/task-specific information.

In terms of the degree of automation/computerization of the processes of collecting coordination related information, more than six in ten respondents have “review-data collecting” and analysis functions less than 50% automated. Over half the respondents have their “review-data filing” function less than 50% automated.

Although the current IT support for network organizations in the computer manufacturing industry is still limited, the results for Part A of the questionnaire offer a relevant background for organizations seeking to increase such support. Answers to other questions also helped us understand how companies conducted their partnering/networking activities. For example, in terms of the average length of time that a firm has worked with its production partners, almost 65% of the respondents reported an average of one to five years of relationship with partners. This finding supports our contention that a long term perspective is stressed in the coordination of network organizations.

In general, the potential of IT support for a core firm of network organizations can be considered at three levels: operational, managerial, and strategic. At the operational level, the use of IT should stress the ease and efficiency of communication and document flow between the core firm and its partners and among the participants, such as an extended EDI interorganizational system. As an example, the first EDI-based application implemented in Singapore, TradeNet, has successfully reduced the time required for processing enormous amount of trade documents flowed among twenty government agencies and the traders [21]. At the managerial level, information technologies, such as DBMS and DSS, can be applied to assist managers in finding competent partners and assigning projects in an efficient and less-biased way. As found in the study, more than 60% of the respondents reported having the option of choosing from among two or more partners for at least half of their outsourced projects/tasks, and 67% of them agree or strongly agree that it is important to have several partners from whom to choose for a given project/task. Also, more than two-thirds of the companies indicated no limit on the number of partners considered for each outsourced area of specialty, 80% of them reported no limit on the number of outsourced areas of specialty, and almost 70% of them report no limit on the volume of business a partner can have with them over a certain period. In terms of second tier partnering, only 25% of the respondents reported more than half of their partners further subcontract the projects awarded to them. Although half of the respondents would allow or prefer no further subcontracting by their partners, nearly a third would allow a second tier of partnering, and the rest of them do not think it matters. Therefore, IT support at both operational and managerial level can certainly enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of core firms in networking and increase the degree of network externality, if desired.

Finally, IT support at the strategic level emphasizes enhancing organizational learning. That is, to ensure the collection and analysis of performance review and to increase the accuracy of such feedback about cause-and-effect relationships between organizational actions and outcomes. By updating the reputation of each partner over time, the core firm has a solid basis for analyzing and reassessing its relationship. This feedback will also affect subsequent managerial decisions to be made regarding the selection of appropriate partners for specific projects. We see reputation adjustment and tracking as an integral element of IT support for network organizations, to facilitate firms' exit from and entry into the network based on either an “instantiation change” or “role change” of the network; i.e., a shift in the core firm’s inclination to choose one partner over another as a result of organizational learning, and the condition when the core firm’s demand for products or service has changed, respectively.

In summary, we believe that using IT can lead to greater use of network organizations, making them easier to establish, operate, and grow.

## 5. Future directions

In addition to the three submodels that we have empirically explored in this initial study, the model can be extended to encompass related submodels. Consider, for example, the inclusion of two more submodels, M4 and M5. Submodel M4 asserts a relationship between network performance and the soundness of a core firm's partner review process.

![](/api/attachments/ECTZR9M4/fulltext/images/34f98d8567477868c0adfe9f25c3a9c51116aafc3c55b07d850722ddbc55952b.jpg)  
Fig. 5. M4: Network performance is correlated with the soundness of partner review process.

One approach to measuring network performance involves the core firm's assessment of partners' operational performance and service performance, with their associated indicators as shown in Fig. 5. An alternative gauge of network performance that does not rely on the core's assessment of partners, is to employ economic measures of the performance of the core as a surrogate for network performance. Submodel M5 concerns the nomological (predictive) validity of the reputation construct. It asserts that network performance can be predicted by partners' reputation as illustrated in Fig. 6.

While making a substantial first step in forming a foundation for research into IT support for network coordination, the model and its empirical exploration should be further developed. We believe that results of further studying the model and additional sub-models will furnish an even stronger foundation for both applied and theoretical advances in the design and management of IT for network coordination.
