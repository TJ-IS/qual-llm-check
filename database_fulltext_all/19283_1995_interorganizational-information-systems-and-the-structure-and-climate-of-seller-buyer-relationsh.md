# Interorganizational information systems and the structure and climate of seller-buyer relationships

Sarma R. Nidumolu \*

Department of MIS, College of BPA, University of Arizona Tucson, AZ 85721 USA

## Abstract

A model was developed to show the association between specialized investments in an Interorganizational System (IOIS) and the structure and climate of the relationship between the participating organizations. Four hypotheses were proposed: In a seller-buyer relationship, specialized IOIS investments are associated with: 1) decreased control over decision making for the firm making the specialized investments; 2) increased formalization of transactions; 3) increased vertical interactions; and 4) under certain conditions, a more positive transactional climate. Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) techniques were used to test the model. Data was collected through a questionnaire survey of independent agencies in the property and casualty insurance industry. The findings suggested that the first and second hypotheses were not supported, whereas the third and fourth hypotheses received considerable support.

Keywords: Interorganizational systems; Specialized investments; Interorganizational structure; Interorganizational climate

## 1. Introduction

Research in Interorganizational Information Systems (IOIS) has grown considerably in the last decade. An IOIS is defined as “a system that involves the sharing, between two or more organizations, of information resources such as hardware, software, transmission facilities, rules and procedures, data/databases and expertise” [6]. IOISs constitute some of the most important examples of information systems that have been used by firms to obtain an edge over their competitors [19,41]. They therefore represent valuable corporate resources for the company that is involved in their development [70]. Because IOISs involves sharing of information across organizational boundaries, their successful implementation has important implications for the organization, including a consideration of whether the corporate culture supports such sharing [49].

Information Systems (IS) researchers have described a variety of IOISs that can be developed (e.g., see [6,20]). I focus on one particular type in this study – an IOIS that links sellers and buyers in a distribution channel. The use of IOISs can be considered the most significant technological breakthrough in distribution channels in recent years [56]. Because of their importance, such IOISs have been extensively studied by previous IS researchers. Examples include McKesson's Economost system [23], American Airlines'

SABRE reservation system [25] and IVANS and Aetna's GEMINI system in the property and casualty insurance industry [47,48].

A key concern of IS researchers has been the effect of specialized investments in an IOIS on the relationship between sellers and buyers. Specialized investments in a specific IOIS typically cannot be redeployed towards other IOISs. These investments have important implications. For example, some researchers have drawn upon the Industry and Competitive Analysis (ICA) framework of Porter [61] to describe how a firm can introduce switching costs for its suppliers or buyers by inducing them to participate in its proprietary IOIS, and thus make investments that cannot be redeployed to other IOISs. Greater switching costs therefore lead to increased bargaining power (and hence competitive advantage) for the firm initiating the IOIS [53,58].

Other IS researchers have drawn upon the Transaction Costs Analysis (TCA) framework of Williamson $[73,74]$ to study how investments required by participants in an IOIS can affect the most efficient mechanism for coordinating exchanges between them (e.g., $[50]$ ). In terms of the TCA framework, specialized IOIS investments have high asset specificity, which measures the degree to which an asset cannot be redeployed to alternative uses and users. In exchanges where an IOIS significantly reduces the specificity of the required investments, the most efficient governance mechanism is a market. However, in exchanges where electronic integration between a seller and a buyer increases the asset specificity of investments, an electronic hierarchy (rather than a market) will emerge.

The importance of studying the effects of specialized IOIS investments has been justified by recent trends in industry. There is growing resistance to systems based on proprietary standards; instead, many industries are individually getting together to develop industry-wide standards for IOISs. Increasingly, buyers are becoming the dominant force in the growth of such systems [7]. Attempts to develop inter-industry standards are also under way – the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) has sanctioned a committee, designated X.12, to develop a framework for standards that could be used in various industries [54]. However, given the strategic benefits to initiators of proprietary IOISs, the widespread use of IOISs based on common standards is still some years away. The development of proprietary or standards-based IOISs can be viewed as an issue of competition or cooperation between the initiating organizations [22].

Because research on the effects of specialized IOIS investments can contribute significantly to an understanding of important trends in the industry, proprietary IOISs continue to receive considerable attention (e.g., see [72]). While prior IS research has provided many insights on the effects of specialized IOIS investments on seller-buyer relationships, there are at least two important shortcomings that need to be addressed.

First, IS research has largely been concerned with the effect of specialized IOIS investments on the structural aspects of the seller-buyer relationship. Thus, issues such as centralization of decision making and efficient governance mechanisms have been studied. However, as research in marketing channels suggests, the process-related aspects of the seller-buyer relationship are also extremely important. While the structure of the relationship is described by the patterns of arrangements that affect the flow of goods or services between the seller and the buyer, the process-related aspects may be described by the sentiments that prevail between the seller and the buyer [1]. In practice, these sentiments have a considerable influence on the extent of cooperation or conflict between the two firms; this in turn affects the stability of the seller-buyer relationship. For example, the extent of trust (a sentiments-related variable) present in a relationship influences an actor's perception that its exchange partner will behave opportunistically [12,57]. Perceived opportunism, in turn, has an important influence on the way in which the distribution channel is organized [2]. In this research, I consider both aspects by posing the following research question: What is the association between specialized investments in an IOIS and the structure and sentiments-related aspects of the seller-buyer relationship?

Second, IOIS research has either been conceptual in nature (e.g., see [19,41,53,62]) or mostly based on case studies of specific IOISs (e.g., see [23,25,46–48]). While this research has contributed significantly to understanding the seller-buyer relationships in an IOIS, the field is now at a stage where model development and empirical testing of hypotheses can be undertaken. Recent research has begun to address this issue.

## 2. Describing seller-buyer relationships in an IOIS

## 2.1. The political economy framework

The Political Economy framework developed in marketing channels research is a useful tool for describing the relationship between sellers and buyers who participate in an IOIS. Originating in the work of Benson [8] and Stern and Reve [68], the framework combines efficiency-based and sociopolitical approaches for studying interorganizational relationships. According to the framework, “the fundamental activity in marketing channels is the transaction, i.e., the act of exchange between two economic agents” [63]. Efficiency-based approaches have basically focused on costs and have tried to apply theories from microeconomics and industrial organization to the study of marketing channels. Sociopolitical approaches, on the other hand, have drawn from organization theory and social psychology and have essentially been concerned with power and conflict in marketing channels.

An efficiency-based theory used frequently by researchers is Transaction Costs Analysis (TCA). The notion of asset specificity is central to TCA. The problem addressed by TCA is to identify the most efficient governance structures for transactions that differ in terms of key attributes such as asset specificity. In essence, the theory argues that for transactions with low, medium, and high levels of asset specificity, the most appropriate governance structures are markets, hybrid arrangements such as joint ventures, and hierarchies (i.e., internal governance) respectively [75].

Examples of sociopolitical approaches include the Resource Dependence theory of Pfeffer and

Salancik [59] and the theories on power and conflict in traditional marketing channels research (see [34] for a review). These theories argue that a firm initiates interorganizational linkages primarily to gain control over a critical resource and thus reduce uncertainty in its acquisition. One way of obtaining such control is by achieving power over the firm's exchange partner.

The Political Economy framework considers the sociopolitical and efficiency-based approaches to be complementary in explaining seller-buyer relationships: a social system consists of “interacting sets of major economic and sociopolitical forces which affect collective behavior and performance” [68]. Both power and efficiency-based arguments are therefore necessary for explaining the complex nature of interorganizational linkages, i.e., the structural and the process-related aspects of a dyad (a two-firm relationship consisting of a seller and a buyer of goods or services) each have a sociopolitical and an economic dimension. The economic aspect is given by the extent to which vertical coordination is present – market-like arrangements have the least, whereas hierarchical arrangements have the most. The political aspect is given by the extent to which decision making is centralized in one of the dyad partners.

The dyad's structure in turn affects its interaction processes. The economic processes describe the activities through which the terms of trade are determined. Sociopolitical processes – the transactional climate – describe the cooperative sentiments that exist between the seller and the buyer. In this paper, I focus in particular on the relationship between dyadic structure and transactional climate.

## 2.2. Structure-related variables

Dyadic structure broadly describes the linkages present between the two firms. Although a large number of variables can be used to describe structure, the following are considered particularly important:

1. Vertical interactions: The extent of flows of resources, activities and information between the seller and the buyer.

2. Formalization: The degree to which interorganizational activities are governed by procedures, rules and fixed policies.

3. Centralization of decision making: The extent to which the power to make and implement decisions is concentrated in one of the firms.

## 2.3. Transactional climate

Transactional climate describes the sentiments that exist between the dyad members. At any point in time, the dyad's location on a cooperation-conflict continuum depends on these sentiments; they may be described by the following four variables:

1. Goal Compatibility: The extent to which the dyad members perceive that they can attain individual goals while executing the joint tasks.

2. Domain Consensus: The extent to which there is agreement between the partners on the assignment of functions, territories, technologies, products, etc.

3. Evaluation of Accomplishment: The extent to which one member of the dyad approves of the execution of the interorganizational tasks assigned to the other.

4. Norms of Exchange: The extent to which interorganizational transactions are based on mutual trust and a feeling of affiliation.

Higher values on each of the four variables imply a more positive transactional climate.

## 3. Theoretical model and hypotheses

The Political Economy model used here is based on four key relationships involving specialized IOIS investments: their direct relationship with each of the three structure-related variables, and their indirect relationship with transactional climate. Each relationship is modeled as a hypothesis that can be empirically tested.

## 3.1. Relationship with centralization of decision making

The relationship between specialized IOIS investments and centralization of decision making has been extensively studied (e.g., [19,53]). An IOIS can redistribute power between a supplier and its customer by introducing switching costs for either of the firms. Bakos and Treacy [5] argue that a supplier can provide services that require idiosyncratic changes to the customer's firm and thus increase its cost of doing business with another supplier. Specifically, the IOIS could increase the customer's costs of retraining, modifying operating procedures, or investing in additional hardware and software in order to use an alternative IOIS. Based on this research, I hypothesize: H1: IOIS investments specialized to a dyad will be associated with greater centralization of decision making in favor of the investing firm's exchange partner.

## 3.2. Relationship with formalization of transactions

From a TCA perspective, a firm that makes specialized, or transaction specific, investments increases the ability of the exchange partner to exploit it and appropriate rents from these investments [45]. On the other hand, if the investments are nonspecialized, the resulting ability to switch readily to other partners is the investing firm's primary protection against opportunism by its exchange partner. Where the investments are highly transaction specific, vertical integration is a more efficient alternative than market governance due to its lower transaction costs. Often, however, this is not a viable alternative for the firm, especially when it is small [36]. In such instances, market-based contracts can be devised with elements that simulate some of the protective safeguards of a hierarchy [69]. For example, contracts could have administrative control procedures embedded in them. Complex transactions in particular can be augmented by formally specified routines, even though they are conducted in the market [17].

Formalization of communication between sellers and buyers as a result of using an IOIS has also been pointed out by other researchers (e.g., see [56,67]). Research in organization theory also supports such an argument. The greater the resources committed by a firm to an interorganizational relationship, the more likely is the relationship to be formalized [52]. Formalization of rules and procedures for interactions leads to greater clarity of roles and responsibilities for the actors. Since specialized IOIS investments entail commitment of significant resources to a specific relationship, I hypothesize that: H2: IOIS investments that are specialized to a dyad will be associated with greater formalization of dyadic transactions between seller and buyer.

## 3.3. Relationship with vertical interactions

The relationship between IOIS investments and the extent of coordination of activities between members has also been studied (e.g., [50,56,72]). The TCA framework suggests that nonspecialized IOIS investments which reduce the transaction specificity of overall investments could favor markets over hierarchies. However, where IOIS investments are special to the relationship, the governance mechanism resembles an electronic hierarchy. Moreover, marketing channels researchers have traditionally viewed hierarchical mechanisms as vertically coordinated rather than market arrangements [13,31].

Buyers who participate in proprietary IOISs increase the rate at which they place orders with their lead sellers or give a larger share of their business to them. The economic benefits of electronic integration are predominant in such close seller-buyer linkages. I therefore hypothesize that: H3: IOIS investments that are specialized to a dyad will be associated with greater vertical interactions among the dyad members.

## 3.4. Relationship with transactional climate

Research on power and conflict in marketing channels suggests that the use of power is associated with increased conflict, thus vitiating the climate of the transaction $[15,34,66]$ . These findings are in keeping with sociopolitical approaches to exchanges between actors (e.g., [24,29]), where the exercise of power by one leads to reduced satisfaction for the other. On the other hand, the TCA framework suggests that formalization and vertical interaction should be associated with a more positive transaction climate because hierarchical exchanges permit “convergent expectations”.

In an empirical test, Reve and Stern [64] found that vertical interactions and formalization correlated positively and centralization of decision making correlated negatively with the climate-related variables. Prior channels research suggests that the climate-related variables (with the exception of goal compatibility) can be considered as representing a single underlying construct i.e., transactional climate. An important finding was that goal compatibility behaved differently from the other climate-related variables. John [39] suggests that it is more a structural variable than one that describes the sentiments of dyad members. In this study, I therefore model “transactional climate” as an underlying construct described by three variables: domain consensus, norms of exchange, and evaluation of accomplishment. Goal compatibility is modelled to have a separate positive effect on transactional climate. I argue the following:H4: Specialized IOIS investments that are not associated with increased centralization of decision making will be associated with a more positive transactional climate in the dyad.

## 3.5. The theoretical model

The theoretical model is shown in Figure 1. I represent this as a structural model that considers the simultaneous relationships between the variables. In the language of structural modelling, the transactional climate is represented as a latent, unobservable construct. All other variables are directly observable. The hypotheses describe relationships between some key variables. Although not explicitly hypothesized, I also include a direct relationship between specialized IOIS investments and the transactional climate in the dyad, over and above the indirect relationship via the structure-related variables.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Research setting

The empirical test was conducted in the Independent Agency System of the property and casualty (P&C) insurance industry. This system consists of insurance companies who sell their personal and commercial insurance products through more than 45,000 independent insurance agencies nation-wide [37]. Agents, who resemble traditional retailers, are compensated by commission and can represent several insurers. Insurance companies exercise considerable control over the independent agent [32]. In a typical agency-insurer dyad, the insurer is significantly larger in size than the agency.

There are basically two types of IOISs in the industry. First, some insurance companies have developed proprietary IOISs (e.g., Aetna's Gemini system); they have idiosyncratic standards for messaging, communication and procedures. An agent's computer-based investment for interfacing with such an insurer can only partially be redeployed in interfacing with other insurers. Such investments include physical capital, e.g., computer equipment, and human resources used in learning procedures or entering policy data.

The second type of IOIS occurs in the batch component of an industry-wide network: Insurance Value Added Network Services (IVANS). Agents and insurers participating in IVANS (batch) use standardized messaging and communication protocols. An agent making computer-based investments can redeploy them somewhat more easily for interfacing with other insurers using this network. However, the interactive mode of IVANS requires transaction-specific investments, since no industry-wide standards have yet been developed.

The property and casualty insurance industry was selected for the study because though industry-wide standards have been developed, it is a typical example of an industry where proprietary IOISs continue to be of importance. Moreover, the activities that can be undertaken (e.g., issuing and endorsing policies and settling claims) are important and therefore have considerable influence on the seller-buyer relationship. Finally, there have been quite a few studies in this industry (e.g., see [20,47,48]), allowing comparison and accumulation of findings.

## 4.2. Research instrument and sample

A mailed questionnaire was used to collect data. It was developed through a pilot study of 12 agencies of differing size. The goal of the pilot study was to assess the accuracy of the instrument. A preliminary questionnaire, consisting of items derived from previous research and the trade journals, was refined through interviews ranging from 1-1.5 hours with each of the agency principals or data processing managers. The items were also reviewed by IS faculty and doctoral students at my business school.

The research sample consisted of 800 agencies operating in California. In one group, 400 agencies were randomly picked from the population of about 600 agencies who participated in an IOIS. The population list was obtained from ACORD (an industry-wide body that registers agencies participating in IOISs). In the other group, 400 agencies were randomly selected from Kirshner's directories of Northern and Southern California insurance agencies [43,44]. The questionnaire was mailed either to the principal or to the ACORD contact of each agency. Altogether, 155 questionnaires were returned, giving a response rate of $19.4\%$ . Similar response rates are quite common in studies of marketing channels, e.g., John's $15\%$ [39], John and Reve's $18\%$ [40], Etgar's $19\%$ [31,32], and Brown and Day's $21\%$ [14]. Phone calls to non-respondents suggested that non-response was more due to a lack of time than any systematic variations in agency size, etc. Due to a shortage of resources, only one mailing was made.

## 4.3. Measures for variables

A multi-item measure was used for most of the variables; each was measured on a 5-point Likert-like scale (the appendix gives some details).

The measures were generally discussed with agents in the field and are:

1. Specialized IOIS Investments: a binary variable – the agency accessed its leading insurer's computer most often either through the insurer's proprietary network or through the interactive mode of IVANS.

2. Goal Compatibility: a single-item measure – the agency's extent of satisfaction with its achievement of its goals, given the actions of the leading insurance company. The measure is derived from [64].

3. Centralization of Decision Making: eight items that measured the extent of control exercised by the insurance company on aspects of the agency's business: the broad type of insurance produced, the mix of insurance, the specific class of insurance handled, the mix of customers, the percentage of agency business placed with the insurance company, the record keeping procedures used, the total number of companies the agency dealt with, and the company with whom the agency first sought to place a policy. The items are adapted from [32].

4. Formalization of Transactions: two items that measured the extent of detail in the contract between the insurer and the agent and the extent of flexibility that the agency had in placing its business (reverse scored for analysis). The items are adapted from [39].

5. Vertical Interactions: six items that measured the extent to which the agency undertook joint activities and programs with its leading insurer in: advertising and promotion, sales force training, management training, servicing of customer inquiries, market development, and coordination of record keeping procedures. The items are adapted from [32,64].

6. Domain Consensus: eight items that measured the extent of the agency's satisfaction with the assignment of responsibilities between the agency and the insurance company: issuing of new policies, endorsing of policies, billing policies, settling claims of policy holders, training of sales force, promotion of the insurance company's products, producing a given annual premium volume or growth rate, and mainte-

## 4.4. Validation of measures

Of the 155 returned questionnaires, 139 were usable for analysis (the other 16 had missing data that made them unusable). The tests for reliability and validity of the measures are:

1. Reliability: The reliability of a scale or measure describes the extent to which it is repeatable and stable. The Cronbach alpha coefficient [26] is commonly used for assessing reliability [21]. Nunnally [55] recommends that this value exceed 0.7 for exploratory research. The

Cronbach alpha values for the measures used for each variable are shown in Table 1. In general, except for formalization of interactions, the alpha values for the multi-item variables exceed 0.7. Formalization of transactions has an alpha coefficient of only 0.19. However, each of the two items used to measure the construct has high face validity. Moreover, formalization has a positive correlation (r = 0.23) significant at p < 0.01 with centralization of decision making (see Table 1), which is the expected result in hierarchically organized exchanges. This suggests that formalization has nomological validity. The two-item measure for formalization was thus continued in subsequent analysis.

2. Discriminant validity: This refers to the extent to which a concept differs from others [18]. One way of determining this for a measure is to see if its correlation with other measures is less than its Cronbach alpha coefficient [35]. A comparison of the Cronbach alphas with the correlations indicates that this is true for most measures used in the study.

4. Content Validity: This refers to the extent to which the measurement items are a good representation of the domain of each variable. This is undertaken essentially by judgement [42]. In this study, the process included a review of the measures used by prior researchers, a scanning of trade journals, and consultation with experts and agents during questionnaire development. The questionnaire was also validated through a pilot study.

## 4.5. Statistical analysis

Since the theoretical model considers the simultaneous relationships between a set of variables (including a latent construct i.e., transactional climate), Structural Equations Modelling (SEM) is a suitable statistical technique for testing the model and associated hypotheses. When compared to the direct effects studied under standard regression analysis, SEM is more useful since it also includes the indirect effects of one variable on another. Additionally, SEM differs from traditional regression analysis and factor analysis: it allows a regression structure to be constructed on the latent variables.

The specific SEM technique used is EQS developed by Bentler [10]. It is particularly suitable for estimating a system of structural equations simultaneously while avoiding the confounding of measurement and structural parameters [9]. In undertaking SEM, it is important to consider the adequacy of the sample size because SEM techniques require relatively large samples for analyzing models [4]. Bentler suggests a sample size to number of parameters ratio of 5 to 1 or more for SEM analysis. In my theoretical model, 18 free parameters must be estimated. Since sample size is 139, the sample size: parameters ratio is thus around 7.7 to 1.

## 5. Findings

## 5.1. Correlation matrix

Table 2 presents the correlation matrix for the eight measured variables. Many of the correlations are significant, particularly those involving vertical interactions and the climate-related variables. For example, the lowest correlation among the three climate-related variables is 0.59 (p < 0.001), which supports the modelling of transactional climate as an underlying construct. In general, high correlations among the measured variables is a key requirement for the existence of the unmeasured variable. Also, formalization of transactions and centralization of decision making have low correlations with other variables, except with each other (r = 0.23, p < 0.05).

## 5.2. Testing the theoretical model

In SEM, the fit between data and the theoretical model is usually assessed by looking at its chi-square value. The standard cutoff for the p-value of the chi-square is typically selected to be 0.05. The fit statistics for the theoretical model are shown in Figure 2. The chi-square value for the theoretical model is 27.4, with 17 degrees of freedom. The p-value for this chi-square is 0.051, which just exceeds the standard cutoff; thus the model fits the data. The Comparative Fit (C.F) index can also be used to assess the validity of the model. An index value of 1 suggests that there is perfect fit, whereas a value of 0 suggests that the variables are all independent. For the theoretical model, the C.F. Index is 0.96.

The coefficient for the relationship between specialized IOIS investments and centralization of decision making has a z-value of 1.23, which is not significant at p = 0.05. Agents who make specialized investments in an IOIS do not differ significantly in terms of centralization of decision making from those who do not participate in an IOIS.

Hypothesis 2 is also not supported. The coefficient for the relationship between specialized IOIS investments and formalization of transactions has a z-value of 0.32, which is not significant at p = 0.05. Agents who make specialized investments in an IOIS do not differ significantly in terms of formalization of transactions from those who do not.

Hypothesis 3 is strongly supported. The coefficient for the relationship between specialized IOIS investments and vertical interactions has a z-value of 4.9, which is significant even at p = 0.001. Agents who make specialized investments in an IOIS have considerably greater vertical interactions (relating to their general business) with their principal insurance carrier than agents who do not.

As hypothesis 1 was not supported, we would expect Hypotheses 4 to hold i.e., specialised IOIS investments should be associated with a more positive transactional climate in the dyad. Hypothesis 4 can be tested by assessing the significance of the total effect of specialized IOIS investments on transactional climate. The total effect is given by the sum of the direct effect and the indirect effect (via structure-related variables).

The coefficient of the direct effect of specialized IOIS investments on transactional climate has a z-value of -0.04, which is not significant at p = 0.05. However, the indirect effect of specialized IOIS investments has a z-value of 2.5, which is significant at p = 0.05. This suggests that the total effect too is significant at p = 0.05. Hypotheses 4 is therefore supported by the data at the p = 0.05 level.

## 5.4. Exploratory research

The Lagrange Multiplier (LM) test in EQS can be used to identify parameters of the model that should be freed (rather than remain fixed at zero) in order to improve model fit. Since a free parameter implies a potential effect (whereas a parameter fixed at zero implies independence), the LM test can be used to identify additional effects that could be included to improve the model. Running the LM test suggests that model fit increases significantly if a simple change is made to the model i.e., include a direct effect of goal compatibility on norms of exchange. The chi-square value for this revised model is 18.3, with 16 degrees of freedom. The p-value for the chi-square is 0.30, which is considerably over the 0.05 cutoff; the C.F. Index is a high 0.99. This revised model (shown in Figure 3) is recommended for subsequent research.

## 6. Conclusion

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

The lack of support for Hypothesis 1 implies that specialized IOIS investments are not, on their own, related to centralization of decision making (or power) in the dyad. However, the result may reflect the difficulty in measuring power. For example, should it be measured objectively, or in terms of the perceptions of the actor? Anderson et al., [3] argue that power is essentially a perceived phenomenon. The research reported here adopted a perception-based approach, although others (e.g., Markus and Bjorn-Andersen [51]) prefer a more objective approach.

There is also disagreement whether power should be operationalized as potential or actual power $[16]$ . In keeping with one stream of research (e.g., $[27,28,31]$ ), this study viewed power in terms of exercised control. On the other hand, if operationalization is not the problem, an alternative explanation may simply be that contrary to the popular literature, specialized investments in IOISs do not in reality alter the balance of power in the seller-buyer relationship. In TCA terms, this would correspond to a situation of “mutual hostage taking”. The lack of support for Hypothesis 2 suggests that increased formalization need not accompany specialized IOIS investments. Research by organization theorists (e.g., $[11,38,71]$ ) suggests that many interorganizational linkages are characterised by greater cooperation and trust than recognized by theories such as TCA. A potential explanation for the findings is that IOISs characterized by cooperative behavior may be expected to have more informal exchanges, and rely less on formal, explicit contracts.

The strong support for Hypothesis 3 suggests that the primary effect of specialized IOIS investments is on the extent of coordinated activities in the dyad. The IOIS facilitates joint programs and activities through its ability to store, transmit and process information and speed up interorganizational activities. O'Callaghan et al., [56] found that agents who adopted an IOIS increased the share of their business allotted to the insurance carrier from an average of 23% prior to IOIS implementation to 28% subsequently.

The support for Hypothesis 4 indicates that specialized IOIS investments are associated with a more positive transactional climate in the dyad, if the balance of power is not altered significantly. An inspection of the structural coefficients suggests that this is primarily because of the increase in vertical interactions, and the positive impact (significant at p = 0.05 level) that vertical interactions in turn have upon transactional climate. In other words, specialized investments in an IOIS by the buyer lead to more shared activities with the seller, which in turn improves the climate of the interaction. We also see that centralization of decision making is negatively related to transactional climate, although the effect is significant only at the p = 0.10 level. However, since this effect is weak (and since centralization in turn is relatively unaffected by specialized investments), the overall effect of investments is to improve the climate of the interaction, in keeping with predictions from the TCA framework.

## 6.2. Limitations of the study

One concern with survey research is the validity of “key informant” data i.e., is the person responding to the survey in the right position to make the required judgments? [60]. Factors that mitigate against such a bias are the relatively small size of a typical insurance agency (with general awareness of firm-wide issues expected among employees) and the fact that the questionnaire was directed to the Agency Principal or to the ACORD contact, who presumably are well-informed of the insurance company-agency relationship.

Another concern with such a study is that opinions solicited from one side of the dyad (i.e., the agency) may not match the opinions on the other side, i.e., the insurance company. However, these concerns are mitigated by the fact that we are principally interested in the viewpoint of the firm making the specialized investments. Moreover, the key variables of interest to us (i.e., the power and climate-related variables) are perceptual in nature. For variables such as dyad performance, data from both sides of the dyad would have been necessary.

## 6.3. Contributions of the study

IS researchers have typically not combined the study of sociopolitical issues (e.g., centralization of decision making) with economic issues (e.g., vertical interactions) in studying IOISs. This research considers a broader range of variables than used until now for identifying the relationship between specialized investments in an IOIS and various aspects of the seller-buyer exchange. The task has been facilitated by the study's grounding in a reference framework; i.e., the Political Economy framework in marketing channels.

IS research has paid insufficient attention to transactional climate and associated behavioral sentiments in the dyad. By focusing on how IOISs “lock in” their customers and increase the bargaining power of their sponsors, researchers run the risk of overlooking the deleterious effect that this may have on the climate of the relationship. The results from this study suggest that the effect on bargaining power may not be as significant as expected. Even if it were significant, it would then very likely have a negative effect on the climate of the relationship (as given by the sign of the coefficient in the theoretical model). Although some amount of conflict may be functional, excessive conflict could lead to the dissolution of the relationship.

The most important methodological contribution of this study is its use of statistical techniques such as SEM on large-sample data. In the introduction, it was argued that the field was now at a stage where empirical testing of theories could be undertaken. The results suggest strong support for some of the assertions, whereas a lack of support for some of the others. The use of SEM also ensures that the simultaneous effects of the variables are being considered, rather than only the direct effects that one obtains through traditional reduced-form regression equations. Overall, the theoretical model developed in this study (along with the research methodology employed to test the model) shows promise as a basis for constructing richer models of the relationship between specialized IOIS investments and key aspects of the seller-buyer relationship.
