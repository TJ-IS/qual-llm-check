# Conceptual framework and guidelines for the implementation of mass information systems

Hans Robert Hansen \*

Vienna University of Economics and Business Administration, Vienna, Austria

## Abstract

This paper presents a conceptual framework and implementation guidelines for mass information systems (mass IS). The introduction gives an overview of mass IS, their goals, market potential, and outlines the research focus. In the following sections the environment and basic decisions on mass IS are discussed.

Keywords: Adoption of IT; End-user systems; Environment; Framework; Homebanking; Homeshopping; Home information services; Information superhighway; Implementation; Interactive television; Internet; IS development; Kiosks; Marketing; Mass information systems; Networks; On-line services; Self-service; Social computing; User interfaces; Videotex

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Definition of mass IS

By “mass IS” we refer to systems that support on-line information retrieval and routine tasks by way of self-service for a great number (thousands or millions) of occasional users who are spread over various locations. Delivered through public terminals, the services of mass IS are designed to cater to passer-by types of audiences, such as visitors of exhibitions, spectators at major sports events, travellers at airports and train stations, or users of automated teller machines (ATMs). Participants in homeshopping and homebanking, or private customers of information services (e.g., database services, videotex, Internet) normally use their own home computers with a telephone-line hook-up. In the future the “information super-highway”, or interactive television (TV-I), might become an important carrier.

This paper is focused on the problems of mass IS operators, who provide information or sell products in this way. To simplify matters we assume that the operator of a mass IS is identical with the owner and developer. In reality this is not always the case. We discuss the basic decisions on mass IS from the perspectives of the Marketing Manager and the Chief Information Officer (CIO).

## 1.2. Goals of mass IS

Goals of mass IS development are [2,3,10,22,24,39,41,45,46,59,67,71]:

\- To make profits by new customers or new sales channels and new points of sale.

\- To shrink the pipeline (supply chain).

\- To save time and reduce costs of business transactions (by self-service).

\- To adapt products to individual customer needs or new fields of business in a more flexible way.

\- To offer a better service around the clock and nationwide.

\- To gain public relations benefits.

If a mass IS is not restricted to in-house use, the operator has to cooperate with network operators like telephone or cable companies, and with the operators of value-added services (VANS) like mailbox systems, ATM clearing houses, videotex systems, or television channels.

The target user groups of mass IS are private customers or prospects. They are not very well known to the mass IS operator and they are using the system voluntarily. Motives for the usage are $[23,29,37,56,68]$ :

\- To save time and money of business transactions (by self-service).

\- To get a service at a certain place or a certain time that is not available otherwise.

\- To get a service in the most convenient way (at home).

\- To fulfil a need for personal or anonymous nationwide communications with partners and about subjects (everything, e.g. sex, jobs, sale) that is not pleasant in other ways.

\- To entertain oneself during leisure or waiting time (e.g., in stations, airline jets).

\- To overcome isolation.

## 1.3. Research focus

It can be assumed that the “unknown user” and the lack of knowledge about the special conditions of mass IS implementation are main reasons why so many systems failed (for a general discussion of the reasons for IS failures, see [48]).

Widely discussed examples of such failures are homebanking systems $[58,61]$ and nearly all mass IS on the basis of European videotex services $[8,33,51]$ , with the exception of the French Teletel/Minitel system. Less prominent failures include city information systems, museum information systems, and many Gopher servers of the

Internet. The future success of electronic newspapers is just as unclear as the success hoped for car navigation systems, new homeshopping channels of retailers, or in-flight entertainment systems in airline jets. Trials to offer broadband applications at home in the seventies and early eighties were discouraging, $^{1}$ and results of actual market tests $^{2}$ do not exist yet. In this light we see the present euphoria and the multibillion dollar investments in interactive television on the basis of the planned U.S. information superhighway [26] $^{3}$ with a certain skepticism.

Research objectives in this area should be the development of industry-oriented reference models to guide mass IS investments. Until now, little is known about the success factors of mass IS. The ongoing research on electronic markets $[2,4,15,28,53,54,70,72,74,75]$ is a good theoretical basis for conceptual modelling, but the special aspects of private customers and the dominant home use of mass IS are not covered. Very few empirical studies have reported the influence of some selected factors on mass IS adoption in a certain industry $[35,42,56]$ . However, almost no studies exist with a broader approach. A comprehensive analysis of the environmental conditions and their impact on the basic development decisions of mass IS is still missing. The framework presented in this paper might be a first step in this direction.

## 1.4. Market potential for mass IS

The market potential for mass IS has grown over time, and this trend continues. In 1993, U.S. consumers purchased 2.7 million PCs for home use, that is 19.9% of the total 13.6 million PCs sold in the nation. $^{4}$ 27% of homes in the U.S. now have PCs, up from 20% a year ago. $^{5}$ By 1997 consumer purchases will jump to 5.9 million units or 33.9% of the total 17.4 million PCs sold. $^{6}$ There is also a sharp increase in the number of households owning computers with a telephone-line hookup. $^{7}$ Though there have been sold at most 200,000 personal digital assistants (PDAs) in the first year since the introduction in August 1993, companies like Apple, Motorola, Tandy etc. still expect to sell millions in the next decade. At least 20 other manufacturers plan to announce PDAs in 1994/95. 26 million U.S. households already have a game player. Advanced video game players are powerful multimedia machines that can be used as cheap terminals in future mass IS applications. They have 32- or 64-bit RISC microprocessors, peripherals like CD-ROM drives, and soon they will get operating systems that will enable them to work with widespread end-user tools or to serve as set-top converter boxes for interactive television.

Dedicated terminals are also widespread. There is already one ATM for every 2,500 Americans. All checkout counters in supermarkets accept credit cards and very often bank cards too. 10% of the 200,000 service stations in the U.S. have gasoline pumps that read plastic cards; every new pump sold today has a card-reading device [47]. Multimedia kiosks help customers to find merchandise in retail stores. They dispense tickets for baseball games, garages, and transportation systems. They make the renewal of drivers' licenses easier and they support the registration of courses by students.

The continuing discussion about the information superhighway [26] in the U.S. has pushed on-line services like CompuServe, Prodigy, GE-nie, and America Online [11,33,34,47,64,69,73]. Their total number of subscribers in the U.S. has topped five million; the growth rate has never been higher. The number of Internet users is increasing even much more, some millions per year worldwide [1,9,65,69].

The most booming applications are point-to-point and one-to-many communications systems such as electronic mail and bulletin board systems. In comparison, homeshopping is still in its infancy: US-2.5 billion in sales 1993, just 0.5% of the total U.S. retail sales. But much hope is placed in the future, especially on the basis of interactive television. A big share of the present annual US-12 billion video rentals, US-6 billion video game sales, and US-14-15 billion for broadcast TV advertising in the U.S. is hoped to be shifted to the new call-up TV services. $^{8}$

Similar developments as in the U.S. occur in most other industrial countries.

## 2. Framework for mass IS implementation

Fig. 1 shows our framework for mass IS implementation. Interrelated components are the environment (outer circle), the goals (core), and the basic decisions on mass IS (inner circle). The environment consists of major determinants of the mass IS success that cannot be influenced by the mass IS developer, at least not in a short-range period. Basic decisions on mass IS concern the contents (products, processes, prices, conditions), the users (area, market segment), the points of usage (carriers, terminals), the time (starting time, offer time), and the methods of development and operations. A mass IS is successful when it reaches its goals [19,31,49]. Surrogate constructs for the evaluation are the mass IS usage and the user satisfaction with the various aspects of a mass IS [27,31,49,57,66]. The following sections provide more detail on the environmental factors and the basic decisions.

Aligning IS with the organization and its markets has been a top priority of IS research for decades [e.g., [12,14,19,31,48,49,66]]. Further basis of our knowledge are 15 years of experience with a self developed mass IS which is accessible to over 20,000 students at the Vienna University of Economics and Business Administration (WU). $^{9}$ Supplementary materials have been gathered by WU students in a MBA seminar on the state of the art of mass IS in Germany and Austria in 1993. Last but not least, numerous discussions with representatives and customers of different U.S. on-line services have been very helpful.

## 3. Environment of mass IS

Significant environmental factors are related to the industry, the telecommunications infrastructure, and the particular firm. $^{10}$ Their specific importance, impacts, and relations have to be considered in each individual case.

## 3.1. Industry

Industry factors encompass the market conditions, the competitors, and the customers.

The market conditions consist of the market area, economic growth, competition, market organization, laws, regulations, rules, government support, distribution channels, shop dispersal, shopping hours, traffic infrastructure, transport systems, innovation rate, wage rate, culture, and habits.

The influence of the competitors is determined by their type, number, location, size, power, strategies, market share, position, customer relationships, sales promotion, advertising, sales channels, branch offices, sales stores, IT support of marketing, and their on-line services in use or in test.

Relevant characteristics of the customers are demographics such as location, number, age, race, education, income, and behavioral traits like their values, service expectations, attitude towards IT, flexibility, mobility, supplier relationships, and buying behavior.

## 3.2. Telecommunications

The telecommunications infrastructure comprises the information technology at home, existing and planned networks, and value added network services (VANS).

Relevant components of IT installations in households are the type, number, performance, and quality of telephone/cable lines, PCs, modems, TV equipment, other potential terminals (game players, PDAs), and software. Even more important than the current status is the IT change rate over the intended usage time of the mass IS. $^{11}$

The possible networks differ from country to country, sometimes even from state to state. In many countries the national PTT or Telecom still has an extensive network monopoly, though there is a strong drift from highly regulated telecommunications to an open competitive market. Prices of monopolistic carriers are usually much higher than prices in a deregulated market. Other criteria, influenced by regulations, are the conditions of usage and the adoption of new technologies, especially towards broadband. The area, size, and growth of possible networks are most important [42]. The offered type, number, and quality of connections and the type, speed, and quality of transmission have to be considered as well. Special security features are necessary in particular for homebanking, homeselling, and health care applications.

If VANS like the existing on-line services $[1,6,8,9,11,34,47,64,65,69,73]$ are a possibility, first of all the number of subscribers, their demographics, and their behavior have to be taken into account. The type, quality, image, and adoption rate of services are significant too. The accessibility depends on whether a service is public or private, closed or open for everybody (information providers and consumers). Other factors are the stability, modem speeds, prices, conditions of usage, user interfaces, and the coordination of vendors and services. Very important can be whether advertising is allowed or not ${}^{12}$ and whether ethical guidelines restrict certain message or database contents. The adoption of future developments $[36]$ , e.g. towards multimedia, might also be crucial. ${}^{13}$

## 3.3. Firm

Environmental factors that are related to a particular firm have to do with alternative services, firm structure, management, IS/IT infrastructure, and support.

Alternative services are either personal such as telephone selling or IT/IS such as broadcast television or CD ROM distribution. Characteristics are the optional service types, quality, benefits, and costs.

The influence of the firm structure is determined by the type and location of the business, size, market share, growth rate, financial power, sales, profits, workforce, working hours, labor costs, position in market and society, range of products and services. Organizational criteria are especially the organizational complexity, the degree of decentralization, the number and type of branch offices and sales stores, or direct access to sales channels and customers. Furthermore, logistics and alliances play an important role.

Determinants that have to do with the management of a firm are the mission, strategy, planning system, management style, culture, innovation policy, communications policy, marketing policy, and service policy $[66]$ . The rating of direct marketing is especially crucial. In the short term, the management system cannot be changed normally and the mentioned aspects can be either extremely stimulating or holding back mass IS developments. Top management involvement has been detected as one of the most important factors for innovative IS initiatives and success $[19,49]$ .

Other promotions or restraints are resulting from the IS / IT infrastructure. Indicators are the size and maturity of an IS organization and the sophistication of existing IS. The services offered by the IS department, strategic IS planning, especially the priorities of mass IS, the available resources (personnel, hardware, software, budget), and the CIO support are crucial factors too [17].

## 4. Basic decisions on mass IS

Basic decisions on mass IS concern the contents (what?), the users (who?) or the points of usage (where?), the time (when?), and the methods of development and operations (how?).

## 4.1. What to offer in mass IS?

It has to be decided what products shall be offered at what prices and which related business processes shall be supported.

Products can be information, consumer goods, or services. Normally, the buyers need to see physical products during the transaction process. That means that only goods with well standardized attributes and simple descriptions can be offered without showing and explaining them on a screen or in an accompanying catalog. Suitable are products with frequent, high-volume business transactions [2]. If public terminals (kiosks) are used, the offerings are rather limited. They have to correspond with the available capacities to avoid unreasonable queues.

In most cases the prices of merchandise should be low to medium (usually less than US-200), because expensive articles require negotiations and customer checks. But there is no upper price limit if well-defined tickets or securities are sold. $^{14}$ Processes that can be supported are above all in marketing and sales.

Quite successful products of broadband systems with multimedia presentation have been all sorts of mail-order merchandise, such as jewelry, clothes, linen, toys, appliances, etc. But, at present, such possibilities are only available by in-house systems and television homeshopping channels with telephone feedback. Telecommunications and entertainment industries' hopes are placed on movies and games on demand, called-up by future interactive television [1]. But, as mentioned above, there is no evidence until now how profitable these new services really will be. $^{15}$

Today's dominating systems like CompuServe, Prodigy, America Online, European interactive videotex services, and Internet, are accessed by low-speed telephone lines from home. Therefore, they are text-oriented, and allow only limited interactive graphics applications. Fairly well accepted products offered by such narrowband systems have been:

\- Business information, but only to a very limited extent in a few industries

\- personal data for customers,

\- industry data (e.g., financial information),

\- directories, and reservations.

\- Communications, like

\- e-mail,

\- news, forums (bulletin board systems),

\- file transfer.

\- Announcements - business services for

\- sex,

\- money (ATMs), and securities.

However, the number of failures goes far beyond the number of successes. Flops in narrowband systems have been:

\- Teleselling of any merchandise.

\- Entertainment, perhaps with the exception of a few games.

\- Education (Computer Assisted Instruction (CAI)).

\- Business information, like

\- company data (public relations),

– guides (for cities, museums, restaurants,...).

\- Announcements - business services for

– real estate, rentals, jobs, used cars, etc.

\- homebanking, consulting, and most other personal services.

The marketing support of mass IS on basis of narrowband lines is also very poor. The initial hope of many developers that mass IS will serve public relations purposes has never been fulfilled. Just as unsuccessful are market research, customer relations, sales promotion, and advertising. Advertising in on-line services is a tricky business because it is slowing down already very long response times, it is increasing information overload, and, therefore, it might even result in customer losses. $^{16}$

Sales support by such mass IS can be much better. Suitable are the placing of offers, orders, and confirmations. Requirements are normally that there is no need for an explanation, that the personal user identification can be secured, and that printed receipts are available. Accordingly, a card-reader and a printer are useful devices – at home and as components of public terminals. For payment and the delivery of products that are not for free this equipment is a must. Such products can be money, tickets, software, data files, or the typical goods sold by vending machines. The mentioned sales support functions are the most successful and promising fields of application.

For customer service however, mass IS do not seem to be quite adequate. At least, in most existing systems the use of counseling offers is only rare. Therefore, it happens quite often that questions are answered only after a few hours, days or never [11]. A telephone hotline is more convenient to discuss special questions immediately.

Marketing support by broadband mass IS is much more efficient than by narrowband systems. Multimedia kiosks for description and localization of products are very useful tools for sales promotion in many retail stores. For advertising, broadcast television is the most important mass medium for decades. The telephone feedback of homeshopping channels offers excellent insights into buyer behavior, and strengthens customer relationships. However, such systems do not simultaneously support the on-line placing of offers, orders, confirmations, payment, and delivery at home. It will require another ten or 15 years, until the information superhighway is completed, and interactive television will provide functions like that for everybody.

The shift from one-way communication to two-way will have strong effects on consumer marketing [7,22]. It will be generally possible to identify the individual customers, to learn continually about their needs, and to tailor products and marketing measures to meet those needs. In an environment of “differentiated products in decentralized markets”... “the role of marketing is to manage the simultaneous flows of information – or the relationships – between the firm and its customers” [[7], p.12]. The traditional market segmentation will lose its importance; modular product design and utility programs will support “online self-customizing”. The direct influence of customers on the production process will grow, as well as their autonomy. The tendency towards electronic markets, where the consumers can easily browse through the offerings of many firms, will put prices under pressure [7]. On the other side, differentiated products together with extended information about customers will allow to differentiate prices much more than today, corresponding to specific customer characteristics, i.e. different demand curves.

## 4.2. Where to offer mass IS?

First of all, the target groups of customers or market segments of mass IS users have to be identified. Their needs and willingness to pay determine the product/price and process considerations discussed above. Decisions impacted are the geographical areas for offerings and points of usage, the specification, location and installation of terminals, the selection of carriers and services, and the accessibility of the system.

Possible points of usage or terminal locations of mass IS are:

\- at home;

\- at work (non-business applications may be undesirable, but employees use them sometimes, nevertheless);

\- on the street (places with high numbers of passers-by);

\- on a longer trip (in the car, train, bus, or airplane);

\- in waiting rooms (at railway stations, bus stations, airports, public authorities, medical practices, hospitals, hotel lobbies);

\- at exhibitions, large conferences, and major sports events (Olympic Games, World Championships, US Open,...);

\- at the bank, the cinema, the service station.

It has been proved successful to fully adapt a mass IS to the existing terminal base at home and to actual consumer PC purchases. Most trials of VANS and mass IS operators to offer special hardware failed [58]. $^{17}$ Because of the rapid growth rate of PCs in households and the few dominating market standards, $^{18}$ the decision which terminal types shall be supported is much easier today than it was in the early times of videotex or homebanking systems. But it is still and will always be very difficult to adapt the mass IS design to the capacity and power of the home terminals. Advanced applications with modern user interfaces typically require a performance that is offered by well-equipped mainstream PCs, bought during the past one or two years. These represent at most one third of the existing terminal base. Even much smaller is the percentage of private users whose PCs have high-speed modems. That is why on-line service operators tend to be rather conservative in their terminal specifications. But mass IS developers should not forget the very high innovation rate, the rapidly increasing number of high-power users, and the fact that their remote applications will be compared with the wide-spread modern end-user tools. In the past, outdated interfaces often have been blamed for home systems failures [33,58,61].

We have already mentioned other home terminals that might become important during the next decade, especially when they will get PC-compatible operating systems: CATV sets with set-up boxes for multimedia programming, video game players, and PDAs. To orient commercial mass IS at present towards the uncertain future of such peripherals seems to be a high risk.

Whereas, touchtone telephones already offer cheap access to mass IS from almost everywhere. In homes often more than one telephone is available; cellular phones are used in cars, restaurants, or outdoors. They are very suitable to call up short messages. Compared to graphical oriented systems the voice interface allows only a very small size and complexity of information: not more than five (better three) menu levels, not more than ten alternatives per level. The longwinded access along hierarchical search trees results in very slow response times. Other disadvantages are:

\- Occupied lines or waiting time, when lines are busy.

\- Few <Help> functions, users can get lost.

\- Limited possibilities for different user modes (beginners vs. experts).

\- Short time and as a result poor expressiveness for the description of products and services; TV commercials or printed catalogs are necessary for teleselling.

\- No immediate receipt.

\- No design standards.

If touchtone telephones are the poor man's terminals, multimedia kiosks are for the rich [21,37,38,62,71]. Multimedia systems allow nearly unlimited size and high complexity of information by menu-driven or non-linear hyperlink interfaces (up to 100 alternatives per screen). They offer excellent multilingual product description features: text, animated 2D and 3D graphics, audio, images, and full motion video. But multimedia mass IS require broadband data transmission, or in a slim version at least terminals with an optical disk.

The long development time and high costs of multimedia applications restrict the present use to offerings with no changes or at least a very slow change rate, like movies, games, electronic books, catalogs, maps, etc. The multimedia systems development might become more profitable when interactive television will attract potential audiences of millions of users in the future. But we believe that commercial mass IS in the long term will remain text-oriented for the most part. $^{19}$

An economical way to install kiosks today is with an industry-standard PC in a suitable cabinet that has to be vandal-proof, appropriate for the handicapped, and weather-proof (for outdoor use only). For naive users touch screens are especially suited. $^{20}$ A card reader and a simple ink-jet printer are normally necessary. Approach sensors to pre-activate usage are nice to have. The keyboard should correspond to standard PC keyboards, the cursor control device should be built-in like in notebook PCs. Most important are high levels of availability and reliability.

To summarize: whenever it is possible, the adoption of PC market standards with a view to the future is highly recommended. This applies not only to home equipment and kiosks, but also to terminals in cars $[46]$ , ${}^{21}$ commercial jet airliners, ${}^{22}$ or other mobile units.

Public terminals have to be placed at locations that are most conductive to transaction generation. A suitable method for identifying appropriate locations is value platform analysis, drawn from the theory of retail outlet deployment in marketing science by Kauffman and Lally [41].

The selection of carriers for a mass IS implies decisions on the coverage, transmission speed, type of media (audiotex, videotex, or multimedia), pricing of services, and possible partners. We have already discussed the first issues.

Pricing decisions have also been mentioned, but only for the offerings of a mass IS. Another equally important component of pricing are the services that are used to transmit the offerings. If third-party carriers like commercial on-line services are used, the prices are set by the VANS operators. If a proprietary service is preferred, the prices are self-determined. The options are:

\- No charge: free terminals, free communications devices, free connections, free services.

● Fixed subscription fee for the basic services:

\- Subscribers pay for their own equipment and connection costs.

\- Free or low-priced starter kit (communications software).

\- Differentiated fees

\- for different services, service components or service qualities.

\- for different usage times.

\- for different users or locations.

\- Transaction charges.

At least during the early stage a newly introduced telecommunications service should be free of charge or very cheap. The more it becomes a necessity the higher the prices can be raised. $^{23}$

A key question is, whether a mass IS shall be offered as a proprietary, single system, or whether it is better to join a shared system together with other information providers $[13,42]$ . Pros of single-vendor mass IS are:

\- Competitive advantages (own sales channel changes the basis of competition, raises entry barriers, offers new sphere of action).

\- Specifications can meet exactly the requirements of target group (e.g., offerings, support of high-speed modems, user interfaces).

\- Close customer relationships (increase of switching costs), and flexibility in pricing.

\- Avoidance of the typical problems of multi-vendor systems (see below).

\- No need for negotiations on technological developments and coordination of services.

Widespread problems of multi-vendor systems like nationwide videotex services or Internet are information overload, poor navigation tools, and confusion, i.e. no clear segmentation of services corresponding to different user interests. There is a lack of cohesion between the offerings of the vendors, and many different screen interfaces are bewildering. Bad data quality, i.e. incomplete, inaccurate, out-of-date information, of some information providers might harm the trust of customers in the offerings of others. Restrictive technical features (e.g., modem-speeds, security), advertising, and guidelines of family services possibly don't fit business needs. At last the image of a multi-vendor service might not fit with the company's ideal.

Cons of single-vendor systems or pros of multi-vendor systems are the long development time for the IS and the basic infrastructure $[38]$ . Because of the slow adoption of new telecommunications services $[6,8,51]$ , it can take many years to get a critical mass of users. Consequences are a low capacity utilization of the network and the terminals. High costs for development, operations, maintenance (especially of the network infrastructure), and marketing have to be paid all alone. And last but not least: A company outlet is less attractive for consumers than a mall!

If multi-vendor systems are preferred, main choices are the global Internet, big national general purpose or industry services, and small mostly product-oriented services.

The Internet [1,9,16,65,69] originally intended for defense, research, and higher education, has meanwhile a rapidly increasing number of participants in business and at home. Home users access Internet mostly by local networks of their corporations or schools at no personal expense. The usage of over 20 million people in more than 130 nations concentrates upon communications. Electronic mail is most frequent by far; other popular applications are chat lines, bulletin boards, file transfer, and the real-time connection to other networks (“telnet”). Utilities like Gopher, WAIS, World-Wide Web, or Mosaic support the navigation of users. But because there is no coordination of information providers and no control of offerings, casual users are in many cases overwhelmed by the huge pile of information ${}^{24}$ and bored by data that are often useless or of bad quality. ${}^{25}$ Further hurdles that have to be cleared are the future commercial conditions, e.g. prices, legal problems, and security gaps, that are inadequate for business mass IS. ${}^{26}$

Big national general purpose networks are videotex services [11,33,34,47,64,69,73]. Big industry services have global, international, or national audiences from some 10,000 to some million users per company or joint venture. Most profitable are financial services [2,74], ATM networks [3,13,21,42,50,71], and POS systems [45,50]. $^{27}$ However, pure database services have struggled for success for decades. Many other industry services like homebanking, $^{28}$ mass IS in tourism, $^{29}$ or electronic newspapers $^{30}$ failed in their first attempts in the eighties. But there are still hopes, regarding new trials today. Car navigation systems, airline in-flight systems, and many others are in an experimental stage.

Small mostly product-oriented services have a national or local audience. Company services and joint ventures of a few firms, e.g. for ticketing or technical support, typically have from some 1,000 to some 10,000 users. Interest groups and dedicated services, e.g. for file transfer, have even sometimes less than 1,000 participants [11,64].

In many cases it is a good strategy not to rely exclusively either on a single or on a shared network/VANS approach. In the early days of mass IS introduction in an industry, a single-vendor system can have many advantages for a progressive developer. In a more mature stage, when mass IS have become a necessity for everybody and the systems design has achieved common standards, “shared development and cooperative operations often provide the least expensive means of achieving a desired level of geographic coverage” [[13], p.6]. Even if a single-vendor mass IS is strongly preferred, VANS can offer additional access or additional prospects for low costs. But the marketing support of VANS is usually poor; it is necessary that the mass IS operator advertises the different access possibilities intensively.

## 4.3. When? The right time for mass IS

“Time to market” is a critical success factor also in the area of mass IS. Competition forces early-stage investments in highly risky new technologies. After failures – like homebanking or interactive videotex applications in the early eighties – there are many publications around that explain why. But this doesn’t really help to guarantee the future success of new innovative systems and technologies.

Parallels can be drawn to the market introduction of consumer goods [52]. At least 50% of the newly rolled out products in supermarkets fail! Despite systematic market research and huge marketing efforts the consumers very often do not accept new products or prefer other brands. Mass IS developers and operators have to be aware of that. They address the same clientele; and there are very often other sources of information or services that are cheaper, easier, or more comfortable to use. Wrong products/services and inappropriate terminals or user interfaces can delay a market's take-off for years.

An important advantage of mass IS is that they can be accessed 24 hours 7 days a week. That is true of almost all audiotex systems [43] and videotex systems. However, if much more expensive television channels are used as carrier, it has to be decided on the best time for offerings. At present home shopping channels retailers buy typically one or two broadcasting hours per day once or several days a week. $^{31}$ Future interactive television will offer a free choice to subscribers to call up, i.e. order any offered product around the clock. But there will still be the necessity for suppliers to advertise and promote their products as usual.

## 4.4. How to develop and operate mass IS?

In principle, there are no fundamental differences between the development of IS for employees, for business partners, and for private customers. That means, common methods and tools can be applied to mass IS as well. Special problems occur mainly in three areas:

\- Requirements analysis.

\- User interface design.

\- Marketing and end-user support.

As usual, the user requirements are not well-known. But in the case of mass IS it is especially difficult to study them because of the lack of user involvement in the IS development and the low intrinsic motivation for usage. In analyzing possible IS impacts one has to be aware of the danger of customer losses by reduced personal communications.

Problems of user representation in mass IS development are [44]:

\- Large numbers of users are scattered over many locations.

\- Demographics, attitudes, and behavior of users are diverse and not well-known.

\- Users are changing rapidly over the years.

\- Most users have no interest to work together in development.

\- Users willing to cooperate in development are not representative.

\- User representatives do not see themselves as responsible to or representing the users.

\- User representatives “join the other side”.
Therefore, appropriate methods for requirements analysis of mass IS are very similar to those for traditional consumer market research:

\- Collection, processing, and analysis of market data from secondary and primary sources to identify customer needs.

\- Prototyping.

\- Observation of and interviews with test users.

\- Test markets.

● Statistical analysis of usage data.

\- Periodical polls with questionnaires.

We have already mentioned some aspects of user interfaces for mass IS above, and we cannot deepen it here. Decisions concern interface types (voice, textual, graphical, multimedia), standards, screen design, access paths (menu trees, hyperlinks, database languages), help functions, user identification, etc. Especially in big multi-vendor systems common standards for structuring the offerings, screen design, and information retrieval are of vital interest. To bring it to the point: interfaces have to be so attractive that the voluntary usage of a mass IS is motivated and supported continuously. To offer rapid access to large information bases and multiple services for an always changing majority of untrained novices and equally accommodate experienced users is hard to achieve!

Something else and special for mass IS is the importance of marketing. Normally, the employees are informed and trained during the development process of an IS, especially in the course of systems introduction. In contrast, mass IS announcement starts with the systems availability, and its promotion is never ending. Marketing measures have to be intensive and professional. If IS personnel just copy the existing implementation strategies/messages for a newly developed mass IS, it will be almost certainly a failure. This is rather a task for marketing specialists, not least because very often the offered products and services of a mass IS have to be modified or enhancements have to be developed according to customer demand $[7,22]$ . That is why marketing counseling should start with the beginning of the mass IS development, and it has to be continued through the systems life cycle [52]. Accordingly, marketing costs are extremely high, especially during the first introductory year, and the mass IS operator needs a lot of staying power.

The package of measures may contain advertisements in magazines and newspapers as well as commercials in television and direct marketing actions. Especially in branch offices and in mail-order catalogs the additional on-line ordering possibilities of mass IS can be announced. Printed catalogs are important supplements for mass IS with limited product description features (see above) or a very large number of offered products/services. Customers use in such cases the catalog for their often longer-lasting considerations, and they access the mass IS for placing orders only. Free subscriber kits and free trial offers for normally charged systems usage are the most common sales promotion steps.

Support of a very large number of end-users of a mass IS can be only limited. A free telephone hot-line and on-line advice are usually most effective. Public terminals should at least have a sticker with a customer service telephone number; better is a push-button direct line. Furthermore, if the worst comes to the worst, it has to be announced when and how long the system or certain components are probably out of order. Public terminals have to be inspected at least once a day. Delays in repairing and cleaning them are an impairment of the firm's image.

## 5. Conclusion

Starting from the United States, with a time lag of about two years the euphoria about the promising future of on-line services has spread all over the world. The growth rate of the global Internet has never been higher than today. The latest SRI measurement of July 1994 shows 3.2 million reachable machines. This is an increase of 81 percent for the past year, and represents an even steeper than normal increase over the past six months. Meanwhile, almost all traditional videotex providers offer at least limited access to Internet services. Future versions of widespread

PC operating systems will include standard components for Internet access. Kiosks are proliferating around the globe, ATM and POS systems attract new user groups because of their enriched functionality. In September 1994, the state-owned German Telekom has announced plans for six urban test markets for interactive television. Recently, many other bureaucratic and traditionally sluggish European PTTs as well as the Japanese PTT have announced similar plans.

Again, IT/IS practice is far ahead theory. This applies especially to new consumer and commercial offerings of on-line services. Accompanying academic studies of current tests are needed right now. The development of industry-oriented reference models to guide mass IS investments is an urgent and important research goal.
