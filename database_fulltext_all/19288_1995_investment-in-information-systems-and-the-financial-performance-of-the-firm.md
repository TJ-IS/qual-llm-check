# Investment in information systems and the financial performance of the firm

Hannu Kivijärvi \*, Timo Saarinen

Helsinki School of Economics, Runeberginkatu 14–16, FIN-00100 Helsinki, Finland

## Abstract

The results of this study show that investment in information systems is not necessarily related to superior financial performance of the firm in the short term. However, investment in information systems is associated with the maturity of information systems, which in turn is related to improved performance. This indicates that investment in information systems pays off but only in the long term, because of the long learning and development period necessary to take full advantage of them. This finding is supported by the fact that experienced and advanced users of information systems are able to produce better applications and services with a high impact on organizational processes. On the other hand, investment in information systems will depend on the size and type of the firm and its financial strategies. It is therefore dangerous to oversimplify the problem and give general guidelines concerning the level of investment required in information systems. Heavy investment in information systems does not guarantee profitability. However, if wisely used to support the firm in its strategies they can import a sustainable competitive edge.

Keywords: IS investment; Financial performance; Success

## 1. Introduction

Success stories give impressive examples of the effects of investment in information systems (IS) on a firm's performance and competitive advantage. It is therefore quite natural that information systems have become one of the key areas in organizational investment strategies. Of late, more than one third of the total capital spending in the US has been used in information technology [4,21,23]. At the same time, several empirical studies have failed to find any positive relationships between extensive use of information technology and organizational efficiency, performance and success. Some studies have even found evidence to the contrary $[9,33]$ . It has also been assumed that performance affects the firm's investments in information systems or that the relationship between IS investment and organizational performance is circular $[13,28,34]$ . However, it has not been categorically shown that high-performance firms really invest more in information systems than low performers.

A substantive reason for the diverging and contradictory results might be that the strategic value of information systems varies sharply from industry to industry. Indicatively, a good deal of research in the area is concentrated on individual industries, for example, on the insurance $[3,8,13,14]$ , wholesale $[9]$ , and banking sectors $[33]$ . Another, methodological reason, for the diverging results is the difficulty, especially in empirical research, of defining and measuring the performance of a firm and isolating the effects of IS from the effects of other factors. Of course it is also possible that information and technology are, in general, poorly managed in the companies and they do not contribute to better performance $[5]$ .

In this study, the primary research focus is on the relationship between IS investment and the performance of the firm. We explore the principles of incremental theory building and analyses. Using a four-step process we deepen the research issue from a simple direct relationship to a more complex system of mediating factors. Methodologically, the primary purpose of the incremental approach is to show what the simple, naive research strategies can reveal from the complicated problem, why more elaborated strategies are needed and what their potential value is. First we attempt to determine whether investment in information systems and performance are directly related. Then we extend our research model and try to discover if and how the special relationship depends on some contextual factors like the size and type of organization and the strategic potential of information systems. Furthermore, we try exploratorily to find some potential, underlying ‘mechanisms’ behind the relationship. We attempt to determine any mediating effects of the state of IS in the organization on the relationship between IS investments and the firm’s performance.

On the basis of prior experience with preliminary case studies $[19]$ we rather exceptionally use cross-sectional data from different industries. Three reasons for using cross-sectional data and more than one industry were evident. First, the boundaries of the organizations change so quickly that the usage of the longitudinal data is difficult and in some cases even misleading. Secondly, we wanted to include a sufficient number of organizations in our study that the use of statistical methods was possible. Thirdly, we wanted to analyze the differences in IS investments and their relationship with a firm's performance in different industries. To reduce the deficiency of cross-sectional data we define some ‘quasi dynamic’ variables which embrace the dynamic, long-lasting effects of IS, although evaluated at a single point in time.

We begin this paper by building a conceptual foundation for the study. This part of the paper provides a theoretical framework for the justification and identification of measurable variables and developed hypotheses. Even though this study has some exploratory characteristics, we proceed by following the logic of the traditional testing of hypotheses. A number of potential variables are also used, since we attempt to determine which characteristics of IS investments are associated with performance. The empirical part of this paper evaluates the relationships between IS investments and performance in 36 firms. Furthermore, we analyze 40 applications and their impact on the organizational processes in 29 of the firms. Finally we present a number of conclusions and recommendations.

## 2. Theoretical backgrounds

## 2.1. Symptoms of the problem and open questions

The research literature describes numerous cases where IS have contributed organizations increasing their performance in marketing, production, product distribution, customer service, financial and other operations. Anticipation of universal success is increased by such well known cases as, for example, American Airlines and American Hospital Supply. Still, success on this scale seems fairly unusual and several managers, after years of usage, are questioning the benefits of their IS investments.

Despite the positive experiences and confirmative case reports, some crucial questions have remained unanswered. Even the most critical question has not been completely and indisputably answered:

\* Have IS investments in general any significant effect on the success and performance of an organization?

\* If they have, how is success mediated?

\* Do IS investments create the necessary infrastructure for immediate profit or merely the conditions for organizational learning that later lead to better performance?

\* Is it possible that organizations first learn to develop working applications and only later learn to choose the correct objects for them?

\* Do IS investments merely create the proper conditions for automating the primary routines, which is a prerequisite for more innovative applications?

\* If IS investments have positive effects on performance, how can they be seen, measured, or evaluated?

\* Is the bottom line of the income statement the only possible measure of success, or should some other measures, for example IS impact on the organizational process, be used to evaluate the long-term effects?

These questions concerning IS investments and their effects require answers and have been the stimulus for this research project. We quote Lincoln [22] who reports that in the UK “the main barriers (to the further use of information technology) are the lack of appropriate cost-benefit techniques and the need to consolidate previous investments."

The state of research on investments in information technology (information systems) is thoroughly reviewed and evaluated by Weill and Olson [35], and Kauffman and Weill [17]. There are a few theories in this field and, consequently, most of the empirical investigations have been exploratory in nature. Weill and Olson [35] found that even the concept of information technology is defined and measured in different ways in the relevant literature, although, many authors use the original resource utilization as a starting point [3,13,28,33]. The average investment level is estimated to be between $0.5\%$ and $2\%$ of annual revenues. The investment levels, however, seem to vary substantially from industry to industry and from one business to another. One would expect that measurement of a firm's performance is at a more advanced level, but there are a large number of anomalies in measuring it, too.

The results of the studies on the level of IS investments and firms' performance are confusing. Two studies discovered that high-performers spend more on information systems than the others [13,28]. One study even finds an optimum range for the level of investments [3]. Another study states that the extensive use of information systems is related either to high or low performance [9]. Turner [33] does not find any relationship between the level of investments and a firm's performance.

Kauffman and Weill [17] developed a framework for analyzing the earlier studies and they showed that the studies clearly differ in purpose, approach and theory base, as well as in the focus of measurement, analysis techniques and control of environmental variables. Different operationalizations of the key concepts and varying research settings make it difficult to compare the results across the studies, but there may also be other explanations for the contradictory results. We still have to admit that the distance between IS investments and performance is great, both in terms of causal relationships and of the time needed to convert investments into successful systems which, in turn, might have positive impacts on organizational performance. In the following, we will try to narrow this gap by developing a framework for studying the phenomenon in more detail. Figure 1 collects the incremental evolution of the research models, the variables of the study and the hypothesized relationships among them. Next, each research model will be discussed separately.

## 2.2. Development of the framework for the study

## Direct explanation

The primary purpose of this research is to study the effects of IS investment on the financial performance of a firm. Our first research hypothesis is based on the naive assumption that it is possible to find a direct relationship between investment in IS and the financial performance of a firm. The first research hypothesis explicates the dominant assumption that investments in IS are profitable and pay off.

H1: The level of investment in IS and financial performance are positively related.

Identification and measurement of all expenses related to the investment in IS is not a straightforward task. In addition to the original investment expenses (hardware, software) other direct and indirect costs typically include systems development, operation, maintenance, user training, data input and analysis, management time related to systems development and operation, overheads, etc. It is clear that sufficiently accurate records are not kept concerning the costs in every organization. Especially, the costs of decentralized data processing are usually not recorded in the firms and hence, the information expenditures may be underestimated in certain cases. However, for the purposes of evaluating the overall effectiveness of IS investment the available cost data are generally acceptable. In relevant research the original resource utilization is often used as a starting point for defining investment in IS $[3,13,28,33]$ . The resource use is then proportioned to, for example, revenues, expenses or personnel.

In this study, at a conceptual level, we also use the total amount of information expenditure (data processing costs) to measure the investment in information systems. The information expenditures are measured as they have been recorded at the organizational level by the firm itself. The main concern is the applications in general business administration, finance, and marketing. Costs of the applications of production control, manufacturing, robotics, etc. are not included. At an operational level, in the analysis of empirical data, the information expenditures are proportioned to the net sales, the number of employees, and the number of office workers.

In the literature concerning investments in IS, different kinds of constructs are used to measure the (financial) performance of the firm. Bender [3] measures the firm's performance by a single item only; expenses over the premium-income ratio. Most researchers, however, believe that the performance of a firm is a multi-dimensional construct. In a study of the relationship between computerization and the overall performance of a firm, Cron and Sobol [9] use the following four measures of financial performance: Pretax return on assets, return on net worth, pretax profits as a percentage of sales, and average five-year growth. Other measures used are market share [28], and operating income to total asset ratio [33]. Unfortunately, selections of dependent variables do not coincide in different studies [25]. On the basis of the literature survey and our preliminary study [19], we arrived at a conceptual level in the following three measures of financial performance: growth rate, profitability, and funding position. It is assumed that these three constructs sufficiently reflect the financial performance of the firm. At an operational level growth rate includes net-sales growth rate, personnel growth rate and total investment. Profitability contains gross margin, net income, and ROI. The funding position consists of the gearing (equity to total assets) and debt to net sales ratios.

## Context dependent explanations

Cron and Sobol [9] found that the relationship between computerization and the overall performance of the firm is not straight-forward. They found that “non-users tend to be small firms with about average overall performance. On the other hand, firms owning computers and making extensive use of them in a variety of ways tend to be either very high or low performers." Hence, an explanation for the contradictory and confusing results in empirical studies might be the lack of situational consideration given to the IS investments. Therefore, our second hypothesis is formulated for studying the contextually dependent relationship between investments in IS and the financial performance of a firm.

H2: The relationship between the level of investment in IS and the financial performance of a firm depends on the organizational context.

In order to study this hypothesis in detail we consider three potential contextual attributes – the size, type, and financial strategies of the organization – that might be likely to affect on the relationship between IS investment and the performance of a firm.

Because of the many organizational levels, large organizations have an extensive need for communication and information exchange. Usually they also have more resources and expertise than smaller ones, making them more effective users of information systems. Yap [37] found that “larger organizations are more likely to use computers than small and medium sized organizations.” DeLone [11] distinguishes between small and large firms in their adaptation patterns; large firms are more likely to be early adopters of computer systems than smaller ones. Also the findings of Ein-Dor and Segev [12] indicate that the larger the personnel of the organization, the greater the probability of IS success. Hence, the relationship between IS investments and financial performance seems to be related to organizational size. Organizational size is assumed to reflect the organization structure as well as the communication and information processing requirements. In this study, organization size is assessed by means of both net sales and number of personnel.

H2a: The relationship between the level of investment in IS and financial performance depends on the size of the organization.

In empirical investigations variation has been observed on IS adoption patterns across industries [37]. Consequently, much research on IS investments is concentrated on a single sector of an industry. This approach allows control of many environmental factors potentially affecting the opportunities to use IS. However, it also limits the prospects for analyzing the existence of a general positive relationship between IS investment and a firm's performance. It also limits the potential for analyzing the differences in adoption patterns between industries. For these reasons we selected two different industry-types for our study, manufacturing firms and sales organizations.

Another potential determinant of the organizational type is its information intensiveness. Certain industries, such as, for example, the retail and wholesale sectors may be more information-intensive than, for instance, manufacturing. The relative number of office workers may be an indicator of information intensiveness.

H2b: The relationship between the level of investment in IS and financial performance depends on the organization type.

The importance of IS investments on performance might also be contingent on the strategic value of IS. In the extreme, if the strategy does not provide any role for information systems or, in another extreme, if they are used as a main source of it, this dramatically affects the potential for finding a relationship between IS investment and the performance of the firm.

A potential theoretical background for studying the strategic role of IS can be found from the effect of technology on industrial organization [29]. Information systems can support cost leadership, differentiation, or focusing strategies. Porter's strategies relate the organizations to their competitors by looking at their different decisions on products and markets. However, strategy can also be analyzed on the basis of different decisions on the financial resources available to organizations. The financial institutions and the owners of the firms, in particular, are interested in the strategies that determine the use of financial resources for making profits in the short term, for keeping the firm in shape and ensuring long-term profitability. In this study we look at the financial strategies retrospectively by means of the key indicators of a firm's financial performance. We do not ask the managers what strategies they have used, but try to obtain the answer from the financial figures that describe the growth, profitability and financial position of the organization.

H2c: The relationship between the level of investment in IS and financial performance depends on the organization's financial strategies.

## State meditated explanation

Information systems usually have many staggered, long-lasting effects. In order to catch the effects at full strength two potential methodological strategies appear. First, it may be possible to collect longitudinal data to analyze the effects. Second, quasidynamic variables can be used to capture the long-lasting, cumulative effects at a single point in time. In their review at research on enterprise-level impacts of information systems, Cronston and Treacy [10] argue for analysis of processes between the input and output variables. Cheney and Dickson [6] found that IS functions that were well managed tended to produce a greater degree of user satisfaction. Mahmood and Becker [24] found weak but significant correlation between state of IS and user information satisfaction. These results inspired us to use the second strategy and link the input variables (investments in IS) to output variables (financial performance) at a conceptual level with evolution models and respective state variables. The state concept captures the long-lasting effects of the prior investments in information systems and conveys the effects of IS investment to performance. The state of information systems describes the abilities of the organization to develop new information systems and use them for gaining improved financial performance. Consequently our third hypothesis examines the possible state mediated relationship between investment in IS and financial performance.

H3: The level of investment in IS is positively related to the state of IS, which in turn is positively related to financial performance.

H3a: The level of investment in IS is positively related to the state of IS.

H3b: The state of IS is positively related to financial performance.

In the preliminary case studies, we used Nolan's [27] model to evaluate the state of IS. We learned that the various IS growth processes develop at varying rates and that it is almost impossible to obtain reliable longitudinal data. Secondly, the organizational boundaries of the firms had also often changed (reorganizations at divisional and corporate levels, establishments of new control systems, etc.) and thus evaluation was rather difficult. Because of these problems, we decided to use more aggregate variables and the IS managers' and line managers' own (perceptual) estimates of the situation in their organization. Consequently, at the operational level, we evaluate the state of IS with three variables: experience in data processing, coverage of applications portfolio, and maturity of IS function as compared to competitors.

## Underlying explanations

In order to understand the underlying mechanisms the state mediated effects have to be analyzed in more detail. What makes advanced IS organizations better than others? Is it the service level that can be offered to the users making them satisfied, or is it the infrastructure on top of which highly beneficial applications can be developed, or both? In this study we analyze the effects of the state of IS on the direct outputs of information systems function i.e. both services and delivered applications as well as their impact on organizational processes. The motivation for formulating these hypotheses lie partly in Mahmood's and Becker's [24] promising results showing a weak but significant positive correlation between IS maturity and user information satisfaction. However, we expand the analysis to cover also the impact of applications on the organization, which is an indicator relating the maturity of IS with the output measures at the departmental level.

H4: The state of IS is positively related to user information satisfaction and the impact of applications on organizational processes.

H4a: The state of IS is positively related to the impact on organizational processes.

H4b1: The state of IS is positively related to user information satisfaction.

H4b2: User information satisfaction is positively related to the impact on organizational processes.

At department level the state of IS can be measured by the same three state variables as at organization level: experience in data processing, coverage of applications portfolio, and the maturity of the IS function as compared to competitors.

IS applications can be considered as a special case in organizational investment. From this point of view, the investment literature, or the capital-budgeting literature offers several potential performance measures for investment evaluation and control. By these measures it is possible to merge the outcome of the investment to the objectives of an organization. Most of the traditional investment criteria imply that the initial investment, the incremental cash flows, costs of capital, and the economic time horizon of the investment alternatives are known. It is assumed that all effects of the investment can be traced, measured and transformed into monetary units. Intangible costs and revenues are assumed to be zero. From a strictly theoretical point of view the evaluation problem is, however, simple; the value of the selected criterion from the investment in information systems should be favorable if compared with the other investment alternatives.

Uncertainties inherent in the cost estimates, systems life, and especially arbitrary estimates of systems benefits dilute the use of traditional techniques of investment analysis. Generally, when the investment has indirect, corporate-wide, cross-functional effects, the investment evaluation must be fulfilled under multiple, diversified and often subjective criteria. Two such measures of success are used here: user information satisfaction (UIS) and impact of the system on the organization.

User information satisfaction (UIS) measure $[1,16]$ forms one well formulated and accepted surrogate for success. The UIS construct is not only tested $[2,16]$ and theoretically evaluated but also criticized $[6,15,26]$ . However, it still offers one of the best documented, tested and most reliable surrogate measures of IS success and is therefore also used in this study.

UIS measures how well the information system fulfills the users' information requirements. It can be divided into three subcategories; information provided to the users, quality of related IS services and users knowledge and involvement in IS. Here the following attributes of the UIS are applied [2]: relevancy, accuracy, precision, reliability, and completeness of output information, relationships with EDP staff, communication with EDP staff, attitude of the EDP staff, processing of requests for changes to existing systems, time required for new systems development, degree of EDP training provided to users, users' understanding of systems, and users' feeling of participation.

Another multi-item construct of success in our study is the impact of the system on organizational processes. At conceptual level, by synthesizing the different potential impacts of IS found in related literature $[18,36]$ , we arrived at the following organizational impacts of information systems: decision making and control processes, internal and inter-organizational communication, profitability, improved work processes and use of a system with subsequent organizational changes. At operational level a fifteen-item set of potential impacts associated with the IS applications was produced. For a detailed list of items see Appendix 1.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Pilot study

Due to the exploratory nature of the study and in order to increase the reliability of the data, the research project was carried out in two phases: a preliminary study in four organizations $[19]$ and the main investigation $[20]$ . In the preliminary study, personal interviews with open questions were used, subjective opinions were gathered, and a significant amount of data was obtained concerning the investments, state and effects of the information systems. Many more variables were used, particularly state and output variables, if compared with the main investigation.

The results of this preliminary study with four case organizations supported the assumption that resources and IS maturity develop in tandem. The mutual pattern also conformed with IS maturity and financial performance in two case organizations, but nothing conclusive could be said about the other two organizations.

On the basis of the case studies, we learned that the use of longitudinal data in the main investigation would cause difficulties and thus we decided on cross-sectional data in the main investigation. However, a few variables are defined to include some ‘quasi dynamics’. For example all ‘growth’ variables implicitly include at least two time points and all ‘state’ variables describe the integrated or cumulated effects of IS investments over time. Maturity and coverage of applications, for example, are such variables.

## 3.2. Data collection

In the main empirical investigation, data on information systems was collected from the firms on the list of the 200 largest companies in Finland (Talouselämä magazine). The data collection was part of a larger research project where the success of the key information systems implemented during the last two years was evaluated [30].

The main principle for selecting respondents within a firm was to find persons who have the best insight into the resources used and the effects of IS on the organization. For this reason we determined to use two types of questionnaires in this study; the first one intended for the chief information manager of the entire organization; and the second one for the responsible heads of selected business units or departments. We assumed that the responsible line managers have the widest conception of the different effects of a particular IS on the processes of a business unit. In the survey, 40 percent of the IS managers and 62 percent of the line managers returned the questionnaire. This was achieved after follow-up letters and phone calls.

Data concerning the financial performance of the firms was obtained directly from the list of the 200 largest Finnish companies (Talouselämä magazine). In order to obtain some growth percentages, the data from the previous year was also used.

By integrating these two sources of information and selecting the manufacturing and sales organizations we received complete data from 36 firms and 40 application development projects finished during the last two years in 29 of the firms. Seven of the 36 firms had not implemented new applications during the last two years while eleven of them had implemented at least two applications.

There are some deficiencies in the data obtained. We should note that all companies use computerized information systems extensively in their daily operations and all of them are large Finnish firms. Thus, the firms in the sample may be relatively homogeneous in respect to the investments in information systems and financial performance. The sample size is not sufficient enough to allow statistical partitioning of data in the subsequent analyses. For example, it would be interesting to perform all the analyses across the industries but due to the sample size such analyses would be meaningless. Due to the small, homogenous sample it is difficult to find statistically significant differences among the observations.

## 3.3. Participants of the study

The descriptive data of the sample is provided in Appendix 1. The firms are large Finnish firms representing manufacturing and sales companies. The average personnel was 3,700 and the average net sales was FIM 2.8 billion (1 FIM = USD 0.2). The organizations' average investments in information systems are .76% of the revenues. The standard deviation, however, is .73%, indicating quite large differences across organizations. The average investment per employee was fim 8,000 and this also varies quite a lot, the standard deviation being FIM 7,000. These figures may well be underestimated due to the increasing decentralization of IS and lack of accurate cost records as noted in other studies [13,28,35]. It should be also noticed that investment in IS in Finnish firms across all industries is larger than in this sample. Saarinen and Sääksjärvi [30] included organizations from a wide variety of industries also including for example financial institutions and the average investment in IS was 1.31 percent of net-sales accounting for FIM 11,100 per employee.

Investments in information systems, organizational types, and state of information systems are measured directly by using several single-item measures for each of them. Because the investment measures are not additive we could not apply simple factor analysis to reduce their number. However, for the output variables we developed multi-item scales in order to increase their reliability and validity. The remainder of this chapter discusses the creation and evaluation of these scales $^{1}$ .

## 3.4. Constructed variables

## Financial performance:

Financial performance was defined earlier as consisting of three major constructs comprising eight variables. The values of these original variables were taken directly from Talouselämä magazine.

The values of the eight measures of financial performance were factor analyzed. The results of the factor analysis after varimax rotation indicate that the financial performance of the firm is three-dimensional as expected and that a reasonable separation exists among the factors; The three dimensions account for 77.7% of the total variance of the eight performance measures. Despite a slight deviation from the theoretical expectations the factors are interpreted as Profitability, Funding position, and Growth. Values for the items comprising each factor were linearly aggregated to produce a performance rating (principal component score) for each of the three performance measures. Reliability coefficients (coefficient alpha) for these scales are, respectively, 0.79, 0.84 and 0.51.

## Financial strategies:

Next, we tried to find differences in the strategic behavior of the firms studied. It was assumed that the three dimensions of a firm's performance, profitability, funding position, and growth give sufficient data for such an analysis. The three factors comprising the financial performance were used as the basis to derive the potential, underlying financial strategies of the firms. A cluster analysis was performed to achieve groups of firms that are homogeneous according to their financial performance factors (see a similar analysis in Tainio et al. 1990 in case of bank performance). The cluster analysis was performed by using an iterative relocation strategy and Wilk's lambda (L) as a clustering criterion. The four-cluster solution was chosen because it had a low value on the test variable $(\mathrm{L} * \mathrm{g}^2$ , where g is the number of groups) and it was easy to interpret. The mean values of the original performance variables in each of the four groups are shown in Table 1.

The first group succeeded relatively well in all respects. It is labelled Profit-makers. The second group consists of investment-intensive firms financing their investments through debt. This group is labelled Investors. The third group experiences sharp growth by increasing personnel and other capacity and is labelled Expanders. The fourth group has failed in almost every respect and is labelled Reorganizers (they are in trouble and need reorganization). These four groups describe the strategies toward different stakeholders (employees, owners, creditors, etc.) rather than toward competing firms in the same industry. However, they also clearly separate firms according to their dynamic, strategic behavior.

## User information satisfaction:

Responses of the line managers regarding single items representing each of the earlier described 13 attributes of user information satisfaction were factor analyzed, producing five distinct factors. The factor structure explains 80.3% of the total variation. The first factor represents the Quality of services (reliability of 0.84) provided by information systems in the organization. Precision, accuracy, and reliability of output information load in the second factor representing the Quality of information (0.88) provided for the users. Users' understanding of systems and degree of participation load on the third factor which is labelled Users' knowledge (0.79). The fourth factor represents the Information content (0.70) of output and the fifth factor Reactivity of the IS services (0.73). The factor structure was in relatively good accordance with our expectations.

## 4. Findings

The research models and respective hypotheses set up in section two establish the assumed causal ordering of variables and the order of the analysis procedure. The hypotheses concerning the effects of predictor variables are tested through correlation analyses and variance analyses. First the direct explanation between IS investments and financial performance will be examined.

## 4.1. Direct explanation

The first research hypothesis predicted that the level of investment in IS and financial performance are positively related.

Table 2 presents the correlations between financial performance and the investment. On the basis of the results we cannot convincingly argue that a high level of IS investment is related to high performance. Neither can we argue that high-performing firms allocate more resources to information systems than low-performing firms do. The only significant correlation is found between IS costs/net sales and funding position. No other significant relationships were found.

## 4.2. Context dependent explanations

## Organization size:

The second set of research hypotheses (H2a, H2b and H2c) predicted that the relationship between the level of investment in IS and financial performance depends on the contextual attributes. First, for testing the potential effects of the size of the organization, the sample data was divided into two classes of equal sizes by the amount of net sales and, secondly, by the number of personnel. Then the correlations between the IS investment and the financial performance were computed in each subset of the data. The results are presented in Table 3.

Some support can be seen for the size dependent explanation stated in H2a, as some measures of the IS costs seem to be related to the profitability and growth in larger companies. On the other hand, in small companies, the IS costs seem to have some connection with the funding position; if the gross margin is satisfactory and credit available they invest in IS. It should be noticed, however, that the companies in the sample, even tough of different size, are from the list of 200 largest Finnish companies.

## Organizational type:

Hypothesis H2b predicted that the relationship between the level of investment in IS and financial performance depends on the organizational type. In particular, we assumed that the key relationship varies according to the industry and the information intensiveness of the firm.

As shown in Table 4, IS costs to net sales are related to the profitability of the sales companies. On the contrary, in the manufacturing companies, no evidence can be found of such a relationship. In the firms with a large number of office workers the IS costs/employee are negatively correlated to the funding position of the firm while in the firms with small number of office workers these variables are positively correlated. These findings do not indicate any clear difference between the level of IS investment and firm performance according to the type of the organization.

## 4.4. Underlying explanations

The final set of our hypotheses concentrated on the effects of IS at department and application levels. We assumed that the state of IS is positively related to user information satisfaction which in turn is positively related to the impact on organizational processes. First we tested the direct relationships between the state of IS at department level and their impacts on organizational processes. The results of the respective correlation analysis are shown in Table 8.

The results indicate a particularly significant relationship between the maturity of IS and the impact of IS on profitability, work processes, and the use of IS with subsequent changes in organizational activities. The extensive coverage of IS seems to be mostly related to the profitability, possibly due to cost savings. An explanation for the negative correlations between experience and organizational impacts can be, for example, that the best improvements are achieved at a relatively early phase of IS use.

Secondly, we predicted that the user information satisfaction could be a mediating concept between the state of IS and the organizational impacts. In Table 9 we present the results of the test to determine whether the state of IS is positively related to user information satisfaction.

Surprisingly, the length of experience with IS is negatively correlated with the service attributes of user information satisfaction. This might be an indicator of some negative experiences from the early years of data processing. The resources in the experienced organizations may also be tied down in maintenance of old systems and thus the service level perceived by managers is decreased. Another explanation is that more experienced users are more sophisticated and expect more of their information systems.

If the IS cover a wide range of activities, then the users in general are satisfied. IS services and IS reactivity with regard to new systems development are effective. Also, information quality and information content are evaluated positively. The maturity of information systems, as compared with the respective systems of the competitors, is also significantly related to the service attributes, information content, and IS reactivity.

## 5. Discussion

Any research study can be assessed by a number of theoretical or practical criteria, such as unifying power, consistency with related theories, logical consistency, simplicity, parsimony, testability, agreement with known facts, generalizability, and heuristic power [31]. An important criterion of any managerial study is its practical value.

By the unifying power of a theory is meant the extent to which the theory connects previously unconnected concepts and relations [38]. In this study we have attached some previously unconnected concepts like the state of IS, financial performance of a firm, and user information satisfaction. The postulated new relationships connecting these concepts can be regarded as a strength of this study. This study is closely related to, and consistent with, some managerial theories and theoretical concepts, most of all the theories of industrial organization and investment theories.

We have strived for logical consistency by applying the principle of incremental theory building, as described in Figure 1. Entering the new elements into the model one by one it is possible to evaluate unambiguously theoretical and empirical power for each element. New elements are, however, added parsimoniously and the research model has remained relatively simple. Because the study is – at least partly – exploratory, lots of operational variables are used which has complicated the statistical tests of the model to some degree. We have met also some other problems with the testing of the research models. First, the measurement of IS investment is not a simple and straightforward task. We have to trust the firms own registration methods that might differ considerably from one company to another, gathering only a part of the IS costs, usually only the costs of the centralized data processing. Similarly, the measurement of the financial performance and the state of IS are not unambiguous. Because the IS have both direct and indirect effects on the whole organization the UIS would be evaluated more effectively by a number of respondents including both users and managers, not by a single manager as we did.

These and some other measurement difficulties have caused some problems while testing the relationships of the model. Consequently, we have not specified or estimated any causal equations, e.g. regression models, although our research model clearly implies such a causal modelling. We have remained at the level of correlations in the testing of the hypotheses. We have, however, shown that the model is fully testable, not simply speculation.

One of the most important evaluation criterion of any research is how well the proposed model or theory fits the known facts. Facts include the empirical data collected for the study as well as findings of earlier studies. The support achieved in the empirical tests for the research models, or the hypotheses, is summarized in Table 11. In the table, the level of support gained for each hypothesis is simply based on the percentage of expected correlations adjusted with the percentage of unexpected correlations detected for the studied relationship.

In the empirical part of the study the resources used, the state of the information systems, and the financial performance of 36 firms were investigated. The cross-sectional survey data did not provide any evidence to support the hypothesis relating the investment in information systems directly to the financial performance of the firm.

On the basis of the results, we cannot argue that extensive investment would guarantee high performance or that lower investment would lead to low performance. Neither can we argue that firms with high performance invest more resources in information systems nor that the low performers invest less. We have failed in this study to find a direct relationship between the IS investment and the financial performance of a firm. Such a finding would have been a tremendous result, but during the study process we have been convinced that there exists no such direct relationship.

As suggested by the previous studies, the key relationship between the investment in information systems and the financial performance of a firm is probably dynamic and time-dependent. The dynamic phenomena can be studied with lagged models, or as we have done, by using quasi-dynamic variables which describe the time-related, cumulative characteristics of a system in question. The selected concept of the state of the information systems appeared to be a good choice; the coverage and maturity of the information systems are at a higher level in those firms that invest strongly in information systems, and further, the coverage and maturity of information systems are related to a high performance of the firm. Because the coverage and maturity of information systems capture the long-term impacts of information systems it clearly implies that the investments pay off in long run. Perhaps these relationships are also contingent on the size, type and strategies of the firm. Unfortunately, the sample size did not allow such analyses.

In addition to holistic, corporate-wide analyses we also investigated the key relationships at department level with single information systems. The results at department level confirm the organizational-level findings. The coverage and maturity of information systems are specially significant explanations for the user information satisfaction which in turn is related to a high impact on organizational processes.

Any research effort should be evaluated against all known facts, specially against the findings of previous research. A lot of contradictory findings are evident in previous studies concerning the effects of IS investment on the financial performance of a firm. A major finding of this study is that the relationship between the information systems and financial performance is not as simple and straightforward as might be expected; instead it is mediated through different kinds of organizational processes. The achieved results may, at least partly, explain the contradictions within previous observations.

The value of research always increases in accordance with the capability to generalize the study findings. Studies in the field are usually concentrated only on a few case-companies or on a single industry sector. On the contrary, we have remained at a general level in this respect although we have introduced the industry-type as a contingent variable. Unfortunately, we had to pay a high price for this generalization; the special characteristics of each firm and industry have disappeared. The primary concepts of the study (IS costs, financial performance) are measured by general means that are not dependent on national standards and the study is not limited to some specific information systems but is general in this dimension, too.

One criterion to evaluate managerial research, perhaps the most important, is the value of findings to practice. In this respect this study also has its strengths and limitations. In our opinion, the fundamental strength of the study is that we are dealing with an important problem. The investment in IS plays a significant role no matter what nation, industry or firm is concerned. The importance is independent of the criterion used – share of the investment costs or the effects on revenues. Consequently, even a marginal contribution to solve the original problem is significant. Unfortunately, the results cannot be directly transformed into practical investment strategies or justification schemes. The highest gains are achieved only via the increased quality of IS in an organization, while for searching for immediate, quick victories is hazardous and only occasionally successful. It is dangerous to give any general guidelines for the appropriate level of IS investments, because it still depends on the context.

One criterion to assess a research activity is its heuristic power – ability to discover new directions and raise interesting questions for future research. In general, there is a clear need for additional research of an integrated nature, research that tends towards a collective, systemic picture of the effects of IS investments.

Methodologically the further studies can be improved in several dimensions. On the basis of this study it is possible to limit the number of variables, use larger sample sizes and improve technically the contents of the measurement instruments. This allows more exhaustive testing of the hypotheses and makes it possible to use the techniques of causal modeling. In order to analyze the whole system of relationships, path analysis would be an appropriate method. If the causal modeling and circular relationships appear to be strong then, perhaps, even the methodology of System Dynamics could be applied. The methodology of System Dynamics emphasizes the causal modeling of the whole system and moves the analyses from pure empirism towards understanding the underlying mechanisms of the whole complicated phenomenon. Nevertheless, we need an approach that collects the comprehensive number of findings and integrates them to a fruitful whole.

Substantially, the research model could be sophisticated by refining the postulated relationships. We need to know the mechanisms by which the state of IS is increased by the investment in

IS, how the financial performance is improved after increased state of IS, etc.

Also, the research model could be integrated in the current theories of the strategic management, organization behavior, and other managerial theories by more elegant fashion.

## 6. Conclusion

To conclude, investment in information systems is associated with the coverage of applications and maturity of information systems, which in turn is related to the financial performance of the firm. Thus we can conclude that investment in information systems pays back, but only in the long term, probably as a result of the long development and learning time required to take full advantage of them. While it is impossible to guarantee the high financial performance in advance, knowledge of the underlying mechanisms should moderate the way for managing IS investment effectively, to prevent disappointments, and to understand the hard, long term work behind the success stories of the effects of IS investment on the firm's performance and competitive advantage.
