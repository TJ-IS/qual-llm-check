# The CIM-IS partnership: integration for competitive advantage

David Lei and Marion Sobol

Edwin L. Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, Texas 75275, USA

This paper focuses on the growing need to closely coordinate and link up manufacturing activities with information systems and networks. Computer-integrated manufacturing is helping an increasing number of firms produce higher variety goods in smaller lot sizes. A 4-cell matrix of degree of product complexity and production lot size is formed to study the needs for interaction of Information Systems (IS) tools and computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM) tools. As product complexity grows and lot size diminishes, more integration of IS and CIM is needed to build competitive advantages.

Keywords: computer-integrated manufacturing, information systems, integration, organizational strategy, implementation, training

Over the past ten years, there has been a growing amount of interest in utilizing computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM) and information systems (IS) to revitalize US industrial competitiveness $^{1-4}$ . Although CIM technology can help firms reconcile the conflicting strategic issues of low-cost/low-volume production to achieve faster responsiveness, its implementation in many US firms has stumbled because of difficulties in understanding the technology and organizational impediments $^{5,6}$ . As Saraph and Guimaraes $^{7}$ state, 'Currently, fewer than 100 firms throughout the world are at this level of automation, which may be because manufacturing organizations usually move slowly toward a CIM environment by adopting new manufacturing automation technologies selectively and in increments' (p. 59). In addition, many manufacturing and service firms alike have invested considerable sums in new IS technologies to link up disparate operations and to provide faster communications and responsiveness with suppliers and customers, only to find that similar organizational problems hamper smooth implementation.

All too few firms have even attempted to link up their CIM and IS efforts to build both internal and external sources of competitive advantage. This paper focuses on some of the conditions and problems surrounding the management of manufacturing capabilities, networks and system configurations, and organizational problems that are crucial to the integration of CIM and IS technologies. It is the authors' contention that CIM and IS represent complementary skills and functions that top management often artificially separate because of traditional doctrine and organizational practices concerning manufacturing and information activities. This linkage will help to smooth the problems of connectivity. The implications of merging CIM tasks with IS tasks to build competitive advantage and to design effective organizational structures are evaluated.

## Traditional manufacturing and information outlooks

## Manufacturing's constraints

As opposed to the assembly-line manufacturing of the past, in recent years manufacturing problems have revolved around the conflicting demands of balancing low-cost production with high product variety. In plants using mechanical technologies, low-cost economies of scale always came at the strategic cost of sacrificing fast changeover in product designs, high product variety and high work-in-progress. Today, a growing number of US and foreign firms such as Toyota, Ford, Next Computer and Caterpillar, are able to reconcile the needs for low-cost/low-volume manufacturing through such innovations, as just-in-time (JIT) inventory, modular designs, group technology and cellular manufacturing. Yet perhaps the most significant force that has begun to alter traditional manufacturing thinking and outlooks is that of CIM, which is now providing economies of scope as well as economies of scale. Economies of scope means that a CIM-based factory can produce high levels of variety without the cost penalty of traditional technology; i.e. producing small lots of many different products will engender no higher unit costs than producing long runs of one product type. For example, Allen-Bradley's CIM system helps keep the US firm a strong player in manufacturing industrial controls for motors.

## Information Systems: a line function?

The role of IS technology and skills in many US firms has constantly faced the issue of centralization v. decentralization of operational activities. Under the guise of such terms as 'distributed computing', 'local area networks', and 'information architecture', senior management often remains uncomfortable and unclear about how to manage and leverage the full scope of IS capabilities $^{8}$ . Until the advent of cheap computing power, personal computers and internal networks, management has generally preferred to centralize all IS functions around a mainframe computer system that ties together many staff-related activities in a 'hub and spoke' configuration. More recently, the proliferation of internal networks and personal computers has shifted the focus of IS activities away from excessive centralization. At such firms as Texas Instruments, Ford, General Electric and Unilever, cheap computing power can now link up many different parts of the firm, ranging from overseas subsidiaries in large multinationals to outlying offices and districts in smaller domestic firms.

The calls for greater levels of 'distributed processing' or dispersed processing are indicative of management's quandary concerning how IS fits into the overall organizational framework. On the one hand, IS has continued to provide enormous gains in efficiency and cost savings by centralizing many staff-based functions; yet, competitiveness in many line-centred operations depends upon information networks that often define a firm's competitive advantage and responsiveness. From Campbell Soup to Benetton, IS has helped to shape these firm's strategies for dealing with a more complex and demanding marketplace. Campbell Soup, for example, is among one of the few companies attempting to integrate IS with CIM technology in both the laboratory and on the production line. Benetton has successfully utilized IS as a competitive weapon to manage inventories, control costs and respond faster to customer needs. Thus, the evolution of IS towards a greater involvement in influencing many firms' line operations has effectively endowed this function with many line characteristics as well. ## Manufacturing and information technology interface

## Internal and external linkages

In recent years there has been great pressure to compete in terms of both quality and time. The growing pressures of time-based competition put a great premium on coordinating CIM and IS technologies $^{4}$ . Competitors such as Honda, Apple Computer, Hewlett Packard and AT&T are linking up their design, manufacturing and marketing activities to take advantage of CIM and IS's complementary offerings. It is understood that at present we are not talking of completely computer-integrated manufacturing since it exists in only a few plants. By CIM, we are talking of a number of different approaches to using and adopting flexible automation. As global competition demands continuous innovation and a steady stream of new products and process, linking up CIM with IS can have profound implications for tying together design, manufacturing and marketing. We first look at how CIM and IS can complement each other in the design of firm strategies. Later, we consider the combined impact of CIM and IS on design, manufacturing and marketing.

Although people often associate robots, automated materials handling and machine cells with advanced manufacturing technology, the real heart of any CIM-based system is the information network and databases that link many different multi-mission tools, inventory control and product design specifications into one flexible, responsive system. Robotics, machine tools, and automated controls are the 'hardware' of the CIM system; the 'software' that enhances system flexibility is the underlying information network that integrates designs, materials, process flows. In any case, CIM technology represents a combination of both hardware and software dimensions to produce a responsive and flexible factory, a powerful internal force to build competitive advantage.

IS technology exhibits a similar parallel development with that of CIM. Although information technology helps CIM revolutionize manufacturing internally, IS can also become a platform for developing strategic networks and alliances with external suppliers and customers to build additional sources of competitive advantage $^{9,10}$ . In addition to its contribution to improving staff activities. IS is at the forefront of many firms' efforts to use time as a competitive weapon $^{4}$ . Already, firms such as Ford, IBM and Motorola in the US have begun designing sophisticated information linkages and networks with their most important vendors. These networks help both sets of firms arrange just-in-time delivery of inventory that eliminates carrying costs and improves product quality. Moreover, through these linkages, both the vendor and manufacturer are able to respond to changes in production schedules and mix orders without disruption. On the customer side, sales representatives in the field at many companies are able to enter an order directly into the factory via a personal computer. The order is processed immediately into the scheduling system, which then plans materials requirements, shop floor activities and delivery time. Thus, the impact of IS technologies on improving the firm's cost picture and scheduling activities has already been significant, with time becoming perhaps the most significant competitive weapon of all $^{4}$ .

## Conditions for a CIM-IS partnership

In essence, CIM and IS tasks have considerable opportunities to complement each other in developing and sustaining a firm's competitive advantage. Over the past ten years, two significant factors are especially important in strategy formulation: the fast pace of technological change across most industries, and the growing fragmentation of markets worldwide.

Technological change. The pace of technological change has accelerated in the past ten years as entire industries, ranging from biotechnology to commercial aircraft to consumer electronics, develop and assimilate such wide-ranging innovations from genetic engineering to composite materials to miniaturized circuits. Although fibre optics, telecommunications and computer chips are found in today's simplest to the most complex products, digital and other technologies are reshaping and consolidating many once-different industries (such as radios, televisions, computers and control systems) into one related industry $^{11}$ . On a global and domestic basis, this trend means that firms need to maintain a presence in one industry (e.g. semiconductors) in order to learn the technical skills needed to compete in another (e.g. consumer electronics) $^{12}$ . The paradox facing firms is that as technology becomes the underlying common denominator of many different products, the manufacturing and information-based skills required to compete in any fast-changing industry have multiplied greatly. Product complexity and the ever-faster proliferation of new designs mean that firms must compete along dimensions of high product variety (which is handled by CIM systems) and fast response to new demands of suppliers and customers (an attribute which is handled by IS-based networks).

Market fragmentation. On the demand side, global competition and the growing sophistication of customers everywhere have actually increased market fragmentation, rather than consolidation as predicted by Levitt $^{13}$ . Instead of facing a fairly homogeneous marketplace for their goods, firms are now confronting a series of volatile market niches which demand customized products and high levels of responsiveness, even in domestic markets. A standardized approach to global marketing no longer works. Customers have come to expect world-class quality goods at low prices tailored to them, thus making classical differentiation and market segmentation strategies less useful than in the past.

In effect, the underlying dichotomy of different rates of both technological change and levels of market fragmentation have generated a series of demands on the products and services needed to compete. Faster rates of technological change are likely to involve products of increasing complexity over time. Likewise, high levels of market fragmentation mean that production will need to be based on smaller lot sizes. Thus, as more industries over time face greater technological change and market fragmentation, the greater the need to produce increasingly complex products in smaller lot sizes.

Figure 3 attempts to capture the relationship between technological change and market fragmentation in the linkage of CIM and IS activities. The vertical axis denotes the degree of market fragmentation facing the firm; the horizontal axis refers to high versus low levels of technological change facing the firm's products. Each of the four cells offers a particular alignment to organize CIM and IS activities for building competitive advantage. It is our theory that the more complex the goods and the smaller the lot size, the closer CIM and IS should be integrated.

Companies in Cell 1 (low technological change and non-fragmented markets) tend to be in such industries as construction equipment, home appliances, light machine tools, farm equipment and other similar durable products. Such companies and businesses as Caterpillar, Case and Whirlpool would fit this block. Products tend to be highly standardized and produced in large lots. These companies have invested in automation to reduce costs, eliminate downtime and respond faster to marketplace changes; the functional nature of these products mean customization is not vital to their sales. Industrial products and consumer durables typically are materials-intensive and rely on capital-intensive production. The distinctive competencies for competing in these industries typically emanate from such upstream activities as low-cost procurement of materials, flexible production systems and economies of scale. Moreover, some of these companies have developed a strong IS platform to link up supply activities with their most closely associated vendors to achieve just-in-time inventory cost reduction.

The prototypical example of how a Cell 1 firm has used CIM and IS technologies to full advantage is that of General Electric's Major Home Appliance Group.

After investing over \$US 30 million in a state-of-the-art plant that utilizes flexible manufacturing technology, GE also designed a common set of modular parts for its many different variations of dishwashers for different home sizes and consumer needs $^{14}$ . The Louisville, Kentucky plant produces a broad range of dishwashers and other appliances at low cost with very few quality problems and has helped the company regain considerable market share against other competitors such as Whirlpool and White-Westinghouse. On the IS side, components needed for dishwashers (such as motors, plastic tubs) are tracked through a sophisticated information network that ties in many other GE business units together. These companies have also attempted to use IS to build stronger links with their suppliers.

Cell 2 firms (low technological change, highly fragmented markets) compete in such industries as food processing, consumer nondurables, abrasives and other related products. Firms such as Nestlé, Campbell Soup, 3M, Heinz and Procter and Gamble compete across a series of fragmented and dispersed regional markets worldwide, where constant adjustment to local tastes, regulations and customs are essential for competitive advantage. Although the products tend to be simple, production occurs in small lot sizes. In addition, the food, nutrition products and consumer nondurable industries are characterized by process manufacturing, which have generally lagged batch and discrete manufacturing industries in automating their plants $^{15}$ . Nevertheless, many process-based companies as Johnson and Johnson, Ralston Purina, and Nestlé are investing heavily in new CIM-based manufacturing technologies that can more easily meet unanticipated flavours, mixes and other changes in product offerings quickly. In Cell 1, IS is primarily concerned with inventory. In Cell 2, IS is concerned with product change and suppliers along the production line. At Campbell Soup, for example, IS and CIM are engaged in joint efforts to design new factories and testing sites to simulate actual production and inspection conditions. In 3M's labs, automated manufacturing works in tandem with a growing IS network that serves as a massive database for all of the company's innovations in tapes, substrates and abrasives. Moreover, the IS network is conceived as a strategic weapon for locating unexploited market opportunities around the world. IS becomes a focal storage point for shifting skills from one market to another and drives product changes.

Companies in Cell 3 (high technological change and nonfragmented markets) tend to be in such industries as automobiles, consumer electronics, telecommunications and commercial aircraft. Such firms as Boeing, IBM, AT&T, Sony, Hitachi, Ford and Honda compete globally across a growing spectrum of fast-changing technology-based products. Because markets are fairly large and tastes standardized, production occurs in large lot sizes. These companies could invest in some of the automated and flexible CIM systems to garner the benefits of economies of scale and scope, while also linking up with their suppliers and customers through a highly sophisticated IS system that has become a major competitive weapon. As fast technological change demands a constant flood of new product designs to remain competitive, investment in CIM systems is essential for producing a broad range of products at low cost. Equally important, these firms must also invest in dense IS networks that serve two critical purposes. First, IS networks allow the firm to tie in their design and production efforts with suppliers to provide a division-of-labour across subcontractors. By linking up the vendor's and manufacturer's CAD/CAM networks, firms are able to design, test and produce a much wider range of new prototypes than could be done alone. Second, IS networks are vital to integrating a firm's numerous internal activities across divisions and departments. IS enhances cross-functional coordination of marketing, production and design activities that are essential to fast responsiveness and assimilation of new technologies and skills within the plant.

One firm's efforts that exemplify a Cell 3 strategy is the Toyota Motor Company. Its ultramodern factory at Tahara, Japan is able to produce limitless combinations of different Lexus, Cressida and other auto designs in any random sequence with no disruption or changeover time needed between models. Each model and variation's flow down the assembly line receives instructions from a centralized computer that controls the robot's placement of every part in its correct sequence. Outside the plant, a sophisticated IS network links up the factory with dealers and sales headquarters. Every customer's specific order reaches the plant in under three days. On the supplier side, the same IS network is able to set production goals with vendors three months in advance, while also providing considerable flexibility

Cell 4 companies (high technological change and high fragmentation) compete across a mix of different industries on the frontiers of change. Cell 4 companies are unusual in that they represent a mix of both products and services, including custom computer chips, software, biotechnology, banking and fashion retailing. Unlike any of the three previous cells, companies in Cell 4 must adjust not only to volatile market fragments, but also technological changes that are redefining their respective industries in ever shorter time frames. Cell 4 firms include such well-known names as Benetton in fashions, American Express in financial services, AT&T in custom chips, Microsoft in software and Genetech in biotechnology. Although these firms compete in industries that seem to exhibit less capital intensity in the factory, they correspondingly invest more in IS technologies that help them remain on the frontier of knowledge and market changes. IS becomes the critical competitive weapon that levers time as the one strategic resource to outmanoeuvre larger competitors. At Benetton, for example, this boutique marketer is more profitable than leading department store giants because its IS network is able to track sudden changes in market demand and fashion styles through its sophisticated link-up with wholesalers and suppliers. American Express uses its proprietary IS system to tie in its travel services globally with other company offerings. Software and other high-technology firms use IS networks as a database to store ideas and innovations for further use later, as well as to control new CIM systems.

Each one of these four cells provides different emphases on where it is crucial for CIM and IS technologies to interact according to the technological and market conditions facing the firm. In each cell, IS plays a different role, as does CIM, but the crucial issue is that both functions must work in a coordinated manner to nurture and sustain sources of competitive advantage. Under conditions of low market fragmentation, IS generally plays its most effective role by linking up manufacturers with suppliers in networks that speed up design time and provide low-cost inventory management. Under conditions of high market fragmentation, IS becomes important to focus on customer needs across a variety of market niches and segments. CIM plays a similar, complementary role with the IS function. It can offer firms the strategic vehicle by which to escape from the traditional strategic problem of producing high variety at high cost, especially for Cells 1 and 3.

Cell 2 firms typically engage in extensive new product development that relies heavily on CIM and IS as sources of data and innovations. Because market segments and niches change frequently, marketing activities take on a simultaneous offensive-defensive posture that seeks opportunities to create new segments while defending existing ones. Constant development of product mixes and variety also put considerable demands on the manufacturing function to adjust to very rapid and often discontinuous scheduling. R&D in these firms often involves building a base of new products from very information-intensive databases that can generate an endless flow of new product combinations. Cell 3 companies devote a considerable amount of resources to tracking and monitoring changes in competitors' offerings and new technological developments. In this cell, design, manufacturing and marketing activities work together to use time as a critical weapon to outflank, pre-empt and outmanoeuvre competitors in locating new market niches and delivering new products quickly. Computer-aided design (CAD), computer-assisted manufacturing (CAM) and computer-assisted process planning (CAPP) can be utilized here. As Saraph and Guimaraes $^{7}$ state, 'machine and tools databases must be created and integrated with parts databases. Application programs are needed to supply process loading and unloading instructions. Application programs are needed to transfer product design information to the CAM system' (p. 63). Thus there is a big need for IS and manufacturing to work together to develop products in this cell. All three activities work together in small, multifunctional teams that provide heightened responsiveness and flexibility. IS networks tightly linked with CIM facilities place the firm into a strategic 'nexus' configuration that closely coordinates vendor designs with customer order entries. A nexus configuration allows the firm to closely monitor and direct the flow of designs, components and products to and from suppliers and customers.

Although market niches remain largely stable, the fast pace of technological change places a great premium on design and manufacturing teams to push through a continuous stream of new products and processes to become more responsive to buyer needs. For example, at Motorola, pager and cellular phone customers are linked up to the company's manufacturing plants to enter customized orders directly through a sales representative's personal computer.

The firms in Cell 4 face the greatest difficulty in integrating design, manufacturing and marketing functions because of the tremendous volatility of their technologies and markets. Such firms as Next Computer, Microsoft and American Express must continuously link up operations with IS across every product and market to ensure that customers have immediate access to their newest product innovations first. Because market segments and technologies change so quickly, design activities are likely to drive both manufacturing and marketing. It is no accident that Cell 4 firms will often be leaders in their industries in fully combining CIM (or operations) with IS to gain maximum leverage in using time as a competitive weapon. These firms will need to adopt flexible manufacturing systems (FMS) to integrate material handling, storage retrieval systems and CAPP. This type of integration plus DN, DNC, CAD/CAM as used in Cell 2 will necessitate a wide variety of collaboration between the IS department and manufacturing management to reach full integration. As Saraph and Guimaraes $^{7}$ state. 'In addition to the full integration of the systems factory, transaction data is summarized at various stages of aggregation to produce management reports. AI and expert systems will be used and most commercial applications must be changed' (p. 63). Moreover, Cell 4 firms produce goods and services that either have short product life cycles or are prone to rapid competitor imitation. Biotechnology, software and new machine tools are innovations that blur the distinction between product and process, leading to smoother and concurrent integration across functions.

## Problems in implementing CIM and IS technologies

Ironically, one of the most significant problems facing senior management in exploiting new technologies is that CIM and IS are often unable to cooperate, despite commonalities of the underlying technology and the potential synergy of such efforts. Some of the problems plaguing US firms' implementation of CIM can be traced to the managers' overvaluation of the 'hardware' dimension and downgrading of the 'software' aspects in rebuilding firm competitiveness. IS tasks are often considered to be separate from manufacturing innovations, resulting in numerous difficulties in linking design, manufacturing and marketing tasks. Sobol $^{17}$ notes that surveys of CAD users show designers often spend 30 to 40 per cent of their time searching for drawings they cannot find. In fact, 85 per cent of 'new' parts have already been designed and could have been replaced with existing parts, if only designers knew where to look. This development strongly suggests that effective design and management of databases are high priority items in linking up CIM and IS. Linking design and production data requires an alternative mode of organization, something we discuss in a later section. Jaikumar $^{6}$ notes that US firms adopting FMS technology achieve only a fraction of the system flexibility and product variety of their Japanese counterparts. Other studies point to similar problems surrounding effective leveraging of new manufacturing innovations that are increasingly based more on digital technologies and feedback systems, rather than mechanical technologies $^{5, 10, 18}$ . As computerization pervades the factory, many manufacturing tasks will need new levels of IS-based support.

First, it is thought that many US managers invested in CIM technology primarily to replace high labour costs, rather than to exploit simultaneously higher levels of product variety at low cost. Automation in the minds of many US managers $^{2}$ is associated with achieving greater economies of scale, rather than economies of scope as well. General Motors' US 90 billion effort in automating its plant facilities has still been unable to match its Japanese counterparts' successes in reducing design time and building high levels of product variety without long changeover times. Instead of conceiving factory automation as a new means to bridge once-conflicting strategic goals, many US managers still view it as a necessary capital expenditure to reduce direct labour costs. Using automation along traditional manufacturing lines denigrates its most important potential contribution — faster responsiveness to market changes.

Second, successful adoption of CIM technology requires substantial organizational changes that work in tandem with many IS-based developments (e.g. reporting requirements, databases, networks, protocols, work flows, market research incentive systems and training) that are often slow to come by and laden with internal resistance. For example, it is very difficult to link different machines so that they can communicate with each other. Similarly, databases designed using different software may not be able to be linked. The IS department might well achieve considerable leverage by learning how to do this type of coordination. The most significant and necessary change for successful CIM implementation is the removal of artificial functional barriers between design, manufacturing and marketing that were the hallmark of traditional manufacturing doctrine $^{2,4}$ . On a firm-wide basis, flatter managerial hierarchies that delegate greater responsibilities to managers enhance responsiveness and flexibility as well. The removal of thick management layers also augments cross-divisional cooperation that complements closer functional integration within and outside the plant $^{19}$ . To achieve both integration and consistency of sub-unit actions, information networks can provide one way of linking up the organization, both vertically and horizontally. Since CIM tasks require a tight meshing of all three functional activities to achieve maximum responsiveness and flexibility, a significant underpinning of the link between these three functions is the IS function. IS becomes the bedrock of multifunctional teams that will exert varying roles, as demonstrated in Figure 4.

Third, as design and information systems tasks become ever more crucial to successful CIM implementation, top management still tends to separate its thinking and treatment of CIM and IS as two distinctly different departments. Despite the growing evidence that CIM and IS indeed rest upon similar information technology databases, CIM and IS people have had few opportunities to plan and conduct projects jointly $^{8}$ . Computerization has enjoyed a longer history in improving the efficiency of staff functions than in revolutionizing manufacturing and plant-floor activities, which may account for some of the line managers' bias against IS personnel. Line managers remain fully accountable for their costs and output, despite their efforts to introduce CIM into the plant smoothly. Moreover, IS managers may not fully understand the pressing responsibilities of design and manufacturing engineers, thus further perpetuating the division. In addition, many senior managers who design CIM investment programs do not feature in IS potential role from the beginning. The end result is that the inevitable problems hampering smooth CIM implementation may actually find workable solutions across divisional lines within the firm: different managerial mentalities are perhaps the single biggest impediment to the building of IS-based multifunctional teams across the firm.

## Organizing for a CIM-IS partnership

One of the important challenges facing firms deriving competitive advantage from CIM and IS technologies is to ensure that the two functions work closely with one another and with the rest of the firm. Although CIM and IS technologies are flexible investments, successful understanding and exploitation of these assets depend upon a similarly flexible organizational structure. Firms wishing to succeed in the next century must design incentive systems, vendor relations policy, and management training programs to maximize the use of CIM and IS technologies and, more importantly, to bridge two different mindsets and approaches. Coordination across traditional departmental and divisional lines is essential for smooth implementation. As Hayes and Jaikumar $^{5}$ noted, new technologies cannot grow and thrive in traditional organizations where high functional barriers and thick layers of management impede the fast flow of information.

## Bridging two different mindsets

The traditional functions and tasks which once dictated the structure and style of manufacturing and IS operations have also contributed to the imprinting of two different mindsets and approaches that have become increasingly difficult to reconcile and harmonize. A report in $Datamation^{8}$ noted that 70 per cent of the time involved in manufacturing operations involved moving information around a company, while design engineering controls up to 80 per cent of total costs. While these statistics suggest that information systems tasks and manufacturing operations should have much in common, in fact there is little evidence that IS is closely working with design and manufacturing engineering to provide people with the systems and information needed to accelerate design activity $^{8}$ . This finding is similar to Sobol's $^{17}$ report that considerable amounts of time are wasted in CAD/CAM efforts to locate and redesign existing prototypes because of poor database management.

The underlying problem confronting manufacturing and IS managers is that, despite a convergence of goals, they do not share a common language or mindset. One area cannot empathize with the needs of another. The problem of acutely distinct mindsets hampering smooth coordination is stressed by Kenneth Boulding $^{20}$ :

...recognized that through their professional training in various disciplines (in marketing, engineering and accounting) and through role differentiation across departments, managers develop very different cognitive perspectives — they bring very different information and knowledge to a problem and they place the problem in different contexts. While a discipline is by definition, a 'trained capacity to think in certain ways', it is also conversely, a 'trained incapacity to think in other ways'.

The engineering and product designer may think in a very problem-oriented direction, while the IS specialist may be trained to think of overall interactions to a problem. Consultation between the engineer and systems specialist may not be easy because of different outlooks $^{21}$ .

Clearly, many IS functions have direct applications and equivalent counterparts with manufacturing operations. IS expertise with telecommunications, protocols and databases are needed to implement fully developed CIM systems which revolve around constant sorting and use of product design and market data and the prerequisite connectivity for these tasks. Equally important, total integration of the CAD/CAM area with operations and engineering demands unimpeded flow of information, a task that IS specialists have mastered over the years. What is critical for senior management is to design and implement a balanced mix of incentives and training that enhances both internal integration of manufacturing with IS, as well as with the firm's suppliers and customers.

## Incentive systems

IS and CIM systems that cross organizational lines require reward systems that foster interdependence and smooth coordination. Consequently, as design, production and marketing tasks become closer, (especially in Cell 4), the specific and relative contribution of each function to total value-added will become more difficult to assess. This issue is especially acute as it relates to the amalgamation and coordination of IS and CIM tasks, since many managers conceive of these functions as distinctly separate when they are actually integral parts. Senior managers in many US firms tend to partition CIM and IS roles, thus perpetuating the problem of different languages, mindsets and biases that impede faster integration of CIM and IS personnel and technologies. CIM and IS managers often face conflicting incentives and objectives that reward individual departmental performance, rather than any prospects for synergy. Across both departmental and divisional lines, the impact of every skilled person's role in implementing CIM and IS technologies will be felt over longer time periods as organizational learning and assimilation of new skills becomes an ongoing process, rather than an end in itself. Instead of bureaucratic control processes that emphasize short-term profitability and individual results, incentive systems for the more successful CIM and IS users are more likely to reward group rather than individual efforts and to use less financial, less quantitative data to assess performance. This is already the case in many high technology change (Cell 3 and Cell 4) firms. Kaplan $^{22}$ noted that the use of strict, ROI measures of performance undermines new production techniques and the flexibility of advanced technologies. Coordination is a vague concept that defies easy quantification and measurement, especially when task interdependence grows more complex and reciprocal in nature $^{23}$ ; thus, firms that continue to rely on short-term financial performance measures will undermine their efforts to learn new skills and technologies over time. Shrewd firms have already discovered that IS and CIM technologies represent a similar set of skills and assets that perform best when coordinated together.

## Vendor relations

A significant element of Japanese industry's success with manufacturing expertise rests with their long-term supplier relationships that foster high-quality production, timely delivery and knowledge of each other's strengths and weaknesses $^{4}$ . As more firms adopt IS and CIM technologies, new relationships and data transfer mechanisms must be conceived together with suppliers early on, especially if vendors are to play a critical role in co-designing and co-testing early prototypes. Vendor designs can be transmitted directly over a CAD/CAM network into the manufacturer's CIM system for immediate production. Just-in-time inventory management exemplifies the kind of close vendor-manufacturer relationships that are needed for long-term competitive advantage and flexibility across firms.

A close working relationship with vendors becomes even more critical with each stage of technological evolution and innovation. Increasingly more complex skills are needed to conceive, produce, test and improve new products that must be manufactured in ever smaller lot sizes for more fickle customers. As the number of designs for auto components, computer chips and industrial tools proliferates, so will the need for more intense vendor-manufacturer relationships to provide a coherent division-of-labour across all parties according to their distinctive competencies. Coordination of IS technologies, software and networks across vendors and manufacturers is essential to sustained organizational learning and acquisition of new skills. Jaikumar $^{16}$ and Nemetz and Fry $^{24}$ note that the depth of the vendor relationships must go to the point where vendors in effect have direct and continuous access to the manufacturer's facilities and networks. In essence, the most successful users of CIM and IS technologies not only erode organizational barriers internally, but also externally as well. Ford is one of those firms that have given their most trusted vendors direct CAD/CAM linkups that further eliminate downtime and coordination problems.

Thus, the impact of new vendor-manufacturer relationships means that firms can no longer select suppliers purely on the basis of low cost on a separate project-to-project basis. Vendor reliability, close personal knowledge of managers and ability to respond to sudden design and supply changes are as vital as cost to build strategic flexibility across CIM and IS networks.

## Customer relations

On the marketing side, the impetus of CIM and IS will be to commit greater levels of customer involvement in the design and distribution of the final product offering. In traditional manufacturing firms, marketing generally consisted of advertising, personal selling and packaging, all of which nonetheless still exhibited a 'distant' quality from the end user. Newly redesigned organizations using CIM and IS must involve the customer more closely through such means as computer link-ups, direct participation in product development and testing, and extensive customer training in the use of information technologies to provide faster responsiveness. Peters and Waterman $^{25}$ noted that a key attribute of successful firms is the immediate response to customer needs; IS and CIM are two tools that offer this potential but only when the customer directly participates in learning these technologies as well.

## Management training programmes

Flexible manufacturing and information technologies pose an additional dilemma for senior managers in defining and categorizing jobs and tasks. As production skills become more intermingled with those of IS, very specific job classifications have considerable potential to undermine the inherent flexibility of human knowledge and skills acquired over time. CIM and IS are likely to transform fairly routine and repetitive tasks into a more technical, sophisticated quality. High levels of organizational responsiveness demand significant leeway and fluidity within the firm to adjust each person's skills according to specific projects, not specific classifications. Thus, smooth implementation of CIM and IS throughout the firm will require more generalized job categories, greater worker involvement in understanding the technical process, and constant training programmes to keep personnel abreast of latest innovations. Employment arrangements in many Japanese and leading US firms have already demonstrated the need for continuous training and education that are the hallmark of sustained organization-wide learning.

In the personnel area, it may be wise to employ people who have cross-trained or, alternatively, to provide this type of cross-fertilization. For example, an ideal combination of degrees for integrating CIM and IS might be a BSc in electrical or mechanical engineering combined with a master's degree in information systems, telecommunications or computer science.

## Centralization and decentralization

As firms compete globally, the needs for coordinating headquarter-subsidiary relationships will fall ever more heavily on the IS function. Cross-subsidiary information networks are already making enormous headway at such firms as Procter and Gamble, Texas Instruments, and IBM where subsidiaries are constantly aware of new activities within the firm wherever they occur. The impact of IS technologies, in particular on organizational structures in large multinational firms, is to increase the centralization of decision-making processes that were previously decentralized (e.g. at N.V. Philips), and to increase decentralization of decision-making in firms that were highly centralized before (e.g. many Japanese multinationals). The net effect of IS is to provide potentially faster, more participative and more responsive decision-making across the system, and to balance out many of the issues of centralization v. decentralization.

## Building the CIM-IS partnership

The harsh reality of global competition demands that firms continuously locate and build new sources of competitive advantage that can use technology successfully to meet changes in market demand. Sustainable competitive advantage, however, is based on an underlying set of core competencies that can reinforce the link between advanced manufacturing capabilities with information technologies across the entire firm. The blending of product and process engineering, internal and external networks, and the involvement of suppliers and customers in the design process, create new organizational forms that govern how well firms can devise new sets of future competencies. This is a difficult task that demands considerable managerial understanding and coordination to convert growing and changing interdependence into a solid base of competitive strength. This complexity requires managers from both manufacturing and information functions to redefine their roles and responsibilities in the wake of these changes.

Discussed below are some specific programmes and steps that both manufacturing and IS managers can take to create a workable CIM-IS partnership. Although it is assumed throughout the discussion that managers from both manufacturing and IS functions are closely involved, there are some areas in which one function may be better suited than another to initiate particular types of changes and strategies.

## The new roles of manufacturing managers

The growing emphasis on supplier partnerships to improve quality, innovation and response time has sharpened the need for cross-functional and cross-firm collaboration. Within the firm, manufacturing managers must become active in multifunctional teams that blend engineering, manufacturing and marketing personnel to speed up design time and customer satisfaction. Already at such firms as Procter and Gamble, manufacturing managers play a leading part in designing and integrating sophisticated process controls with the latest changes in surfactant technology that lie at the heart of new detergents and soaps. More broadly, managers in manufacturing need to recast and redefine their traditional perspectives, i.e. focusing solely on physical conversion of work, to a broader concept of operations spreading across design, testing and distribution as well. For example, in GM's Saturn project, manufacturing and IS managers have jointly taken the lead in transforming the nature of their value-adding activities to encompass not only plant-based activities, but also design work and customer satisfaction as well.

Externally, manufacturing managers need to bring in their counterparts from supplier companies to serve on projects that integrate engineering, design, manufacturing and purchasing from their earliest stages. Even more important, creating closer alliances with suppliers and other firms enhances the manufacturing function's role in transforming the firm into a learning organization that can simultaneously create, assimilate and implement diverse sources of technologies faster. This is particularly important for those firms located in Cells 3 and 4, in which producing small lots of goods requires constant technological renewal. Manufacturing managers can thus become the nexus of a highly integrated process that blends project teams drawn from internal businesses, functional departments and external suppliers and alliances. The convergence of different managerial responsibilities, and the opportunity to create liaisons across functions, means that manufacturing managers are in a unique position to blend upstream and downstream capabilities into a more potent and responsive organization.

## The enhanced role of IS managers

Managers from the IS function have an equally critical role to play in redefining the firm as a learning organization. As with their counterparts in manufacturing, the IS managers have a unique opportunity to move from a staff function to a fully-fledged line component of the organization. The need for creating new data links, databases and protocols between advanced laboratories, manufacturing tools and marketing programs means that IS managers are particularly well-positioned to create a new strategic 'architecture' that can make nurturing and utilizing core competencies much faster and easier.

Internally, IS managers can become the vanguard of the firm's attempts to locate hidden and new market opportunities not yet covered by competitors. In addition to complementing the strategic planning function, IS managers can become active players in devising 'strategic thinking forums' in which alternative competitive and technological change scenarios are fleshed out and analyzed. Instead of viewing themselves solely as a data-processing or data-linking function, IS managers can become direct participants in formulating and evaluating strategic alternatives and changes that match the firm's core competencies with new environments. In this way, the IS department can work directly with the firm's marketing arm (or downstream activities) to coordinate the firm's offerings across a host of different product/market scopes. At Royal Dutch Shell, for example, the IS department plays a vital role in sensitivity and environmental scanning analysis that gives the firm an unmatched advantage over its rivals in locating new market and production opportunities. On the operational side, IS managers serve a similar role to manufacturing managers in the creation of a multifunctional team within the firm. With manufacturing and other upstream activities, IS managers and skilled personnel can act as a bridge in which information, ideas and designs are spread quickly throughout the organization, reinforcing a genuine CIM-IS partnership.

Externally, IS managers can fully complement the role of manufacturing managers to learn and assimilate new and diverse sources of technology from suppliers and firms working in close strategic alliances. The merging of CIM and IS necessitates a capability to simultaneously develop technologies internally while learning new ones externally. IS managers are now becoming agents of change, to link up their roles with manufacturing managers internally and externally. At IBM, for example, a special emphasis on building strong supplier and customer relationships with such firms as Motorola, Apple Computer, Borland International and Siemens is leading to some novel practices in which the firm has formed a multiple series of joint development teams, where IS managers become simultaneously alliance managers and customer support partners. IS managers from both sets of companies provide engineering support, proprietary data and new skills to better understand and link up hitherto untested manufacturing techniques and to remove problems of protocol incompatibility early on. The risks in developing new manufacturing and information transfer skills are thus spread across a series of partners. In this way, IBM and its partners engage in a series of cross-learning experiences in which both sides gain access to technical data, and ideas form the basis for new core competencies. These competencies are then further refined for incorporation into new products and capabilities among the partners.

Another instance where IS managers are gaining experience when integrating internal and external operations at IBM, is the new relationship with Eastman Kodak. The photographic and imaging giant transferred its entire data operations to IBM for purposes of consolidation and greater efficiency, thus furthering a natural partnership between two giant firms whose core competencies and technological skills are converging.

To summarize, both the IS manager and the manufacturing manager need to constantly monitor new technologies to see how these innovations can help the organization. Some very forward-looking firms like Texaco in Houston now have future technology groups that constantly search for new inventions to enhance their strategic advantage.

According to the CIME staff report $^{26}$ , ‘the manufacturing enterprise of the future probably will only have five major components — business systems, design engineering, manufacturing engineering, shop floor, and employee/customer/vendor education’. In these areas the IS manager should focus on the business systems and the employee/customer/vendor education. The business systems include the marketing and distribution aspects of the enterprise as well as the financial aspects of running the business. The employee/customer/vendor interactions will require inputs of problems of marketing, purchasing and personnel. The MIS director who usually has a strong business background should be especially important in this area, although interaction with manufacturing is crucial here. For example, purchasing decisions will relate to desired inventory levels specified by manufacturing.

The manufacturing managers will focus no doubt on design engineering, manufacturing engineering and the shop floor. When we take the engineering and IS functions together, there are two large MIS areas which are common to both groups. These are databases and connectivity. Both the manufacturing and the business functions will generate databases which need to be stored correctly so that they can be easily searched, and to be written in compatible languages so that they can be combined if needed. Surveys of CAD users have shown that designers can spend 30 to 40 per cent of their time searching for drawings. The manufacturing manager and the IS manager will have to cooperate on designing and maintaining excellent databases.

Finally, there will be problems of connectivity overall, i.e. enabling 'islands of automation' to communicate. Telecommunications knowledge will be essential for both groups, but it may be that IS managers should specialize in this and enhance their job descriptions accordingly, since dispersed and distributed computing have eroded a major part of the IS manager's role.

## Conclusion

The impact of CIM and IS technologies on redefining firms' competitive advantage will be far-reaching. CIM and IS are already working in a close partnership in several leading US and foreign firms to produce a broader range of new products for more demanding markets. The key to more widespread successful implementation of CIM and IS technologies, however, rests first on acknowledging that these two functions indeed can be effective partners, and second on organizational adjustments that enhance learning and the use of CIM and IS fully. The firms who produce complex goods in small lots potentially have the most to gain from this integration but even firms who produce simple goods in large lots have already reaped benefits from coordinating their IS and manufacturing functions.
