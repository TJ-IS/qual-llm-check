# Stakeholders of strategic information systems planning: theoretical concepts and empirical examples

Mikko Ruohonen

Turku School of Economics and Business Administration, 20500 Turku, Finland

Strategic information systems planning (SISP) requires the participation and involvement of different managerial groups. It is suggested that the key stakeholder groups in this process are top management, user management and IT/IS management. Management education is needed to integrate the different views of managers concerning the use of IT. Successful SISP requires sound communication and the interpretation of these different views. The nature of these three stakeholder groups is defined. Emphasis is placed on the interaction and collaboration of managers and managerial groups. Theoretical reflection is supported by experience of three case examples from different companies. Results provide a wider framework through which to examine intra-group and inter-group relationships in the context of SISP.

Keywords: stakeholder groups, strategic information systems planning

The importance of information technology (IT) has grown rapidly during recent decades. In the 1980s both practitioners and academics began increasingly to emphasize the potential competitive advantages that may be leveraged by IT $^{1}$ . During the same time IS researchers have integrated the study of business strategy and planning with studies of information systems in organizations $^{2-18}$ .

One critical research issue in the IS strategy field is management involvement and collaboration. It has been observed that different interest groups, or stakeholders, view IS planning issues differently $^{14,19}$ . However, little of the research documents differences either in depth or in a real-life setting, i.e. through action research methods. This study attempts to analyze and interpret the SISP process in three Finnish organizations (in which the author participated). The basic objective of the paper is to describe the stakeholders, compare the socio-cultural situations of each case company, and derive theory-laden guidelines for better analysis of organizational life in the context of SISP.

The results suggest that stakeholder analysis is an empirically valid instrument for the SISP process in a socio-cultural context. This study also provides a wider framework for evaluating relationships both inside a single stakeholder group and between stakeholder groups.

The following section describes concepts and the context in which the study is executed. The author then examines the nature of groups and groupwork problems, and provides definitions for the three key stakeholder groups. The research approach is defined and the empirical findings described and summarized. The concluding section offers suggestions for further research.

Definitions of information management and SISP

## Information management

Wilson $^{20}$ defines information management as ‘the effective management of information resources (internal and external) of an organization through the proper application of information technology’. A more detailed proposal $^{21}$ embodies ‘information systems planning and policies, and information systems organization’. Earl $^{13}$ adds ‘the control of information resources’ to the previous definition, so that it encompasses ‘the planning, organization and control of information resources’. This paper focuses on IS planning, and more specifically planning activity which contributes to strategic business planning.

## Information systems planning — four challenges for managers

Galliers $^{22}$ defines information systems planning (ISP) as ‘a management task which is concerned with integrating information systems considerations into the corporate planning process and with providing a direct link between this and, e.g., information technology acquisition decisions and the applications development process’.

He explains ISP very extensively (p. 230) as consisting of a number of complex tasks involving the identification of (i) current and future information, (ii) information systems and (iii) information technology, leading to the discovery of associated requirements and priorities with reference to organizational responsibilities and objectives.

First, ISP entails definition of the IT infrastructure, i.e. data, application, systems and telecommunications architecture under future organizational conditions. This is the technical challenge of ISP leading to technology investment decisions (databases, computers, networks etc.). Second, there is the issue of the identification of IT potential for improving organizational competitiveness, in line with environmental circumstances and organizational strengths/weaknesses. This task emphasizes the integration of business strategy planning and the exploitation of IT to identify realistic opportunities for business development: this is the business competency challenge of ISP. Corporate and/or business strategy concepts should be shifted to the area of technology and back again to be applied to the business area. Third, managers should identify the risks and resource-related implications associated with alternative plans. This demands a view of organization development. This is the implementation or 'change-management' challenge of ISP. Fourth, Galliers points out that all these issues are relevant at the corporate level, as well as at the level of discrete information needs. Corporate level ISP should focus on the issues of synergy, i.e. information resources, which are of most value to people occupying different posts in the corporation when designed as a centralized resource. Experience has suggested that corporate level ISP also requires the design of the IS organization structure and control processes i.e. a renewal of IS management practice. This is the reconstruction challenge of ISP.

The technical and business competency challenges have very often been highlighted in the context of ISP, but unfortunately the implementation and reconstruction challenges are often ignored. The author's view is that successful ISP requires the learning of new or hitherto unrevealed knowledge concerned with IT/IS issues. It demands new insight into business development and should be integrated with business planning. Furthermore it must be managed in everyday routines; it has become both an evolving opportunity and a risk for management. Finally it demands a holistic systems approach in order that the full nature of information management be understood at all organizational levels.

## Strategic ISP — tasks and requirements for managers

The strategic importance of IS can be based either on the intelligent use of information or the wide utilization of the technological opportunities of IT, or both $^{17}$ . SISP is therefore a high level planning activity; it is not well-structured and requires considerable creativity and some risk-taking to be successful. In the main, it focuses on knowledge acquisition and the comparison of competing alternatives. In spite of the ambiguous end-states of IS implementation outcomes, a number of decisions should be made relying on managerial knowledge, insight and intuition. Lederer and Sethi's $^{23}$ view of SISP is as follows: 'the process of deciding the objectives for organizational computing and identifying the potential computer applications which the organization should implement'. Earl $^{14}$ calls this IS strategy formulation and points out that it should be distinguished from IT and Information Management (IM) strategy formulation processes. The author's view is that IS and IT strategies put greater emphasis on the business and technical challenges of ISP, while IM strategy deals with implementation and reconstruction challenges.

Lederer and Sethi give a dichotomous representation of SISP:

1. SISP refers to 'the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and consequently realizing its business goals'.

2. Conversely, SISP can also 'entail searching for applications with a high impact and the ability to create an advantage over competitors'.

The first half of the dichotomy is also referred to as the align mode of SISP (see $^{24}$ for example). The other half of the dichotomy is recognizable from the literature and research on 'IT as a competitive weapon $^{2,3,4,11}$ and can thus be called the impact mode of SISP. In order to master the implementation and reconstruction challenges, a third SISP mode is needed concerned with the analysis of organizational opportunities and constraints (see References 25 and 26, for example). This implies notions of organizational culture, requirements for human resources and capabilities for change management and might here be termed the fit mode, since the use of IT, corporate strategy and the organizational mind $^{27}$ should fit together.

Managers need those qualities which enable them to effectively master the strategic management of information systems. Managers are, however, often unaware of all the opportunities IT offers, or find the use of IT difficult to manage $^{1}$ . Education is therefore required to enable them to cope with this new and challenging task. The main tasks of managers in the SISP field are:

1. identification of IT/IS opportunities, in line with current strategy;

2. decision-making concerning IT investments and IS management practice according to opportunities and constraints; and

3. control of the IT/IS strategy implementation process and further evaluation and comparison of strategy outcomes.

Evolution of IS-related work has created new tasks $^{28, 29}$ . IS planning was previously perceived mainly as a job for those working in the IT/IS field. Since the value of IT/IS to organizations has increased and IS planning has been seen as a matter of business knowledge (due to its effect on business and competition). IS planning issues have also become the responsibility of business managers; IS planning has become strategic. Earl and Skyrme $^{30}$ define a number of competencies\* a hybrid manager should have in this new and challenging situation and surmise that such a manager 'is an almost impossible dream'. Their proposal is to create teams 'whose individuals provide complementary competencies, but which overall provide a good balance.' In addition, creating hybrid managers is not sufficient to develop a meaningful partnership between IS and the business $^{30-33}$ . Similarly, SISP is too complex $^{34}$ as a managerial job to be handled by one person; managerial groups are required. So it is clear that SISP needs:

1. increased managerial learning i.e. we need information management education (IME) for participants in the SISP process; and

2. increased interaction and co-operation between different managers and managerial groups.

First, this requires increased communication between different parties, and second, the interpretation of the different views of managerial groups, i.e. the construction of a social reality and language for SISP. SISP should be executed participatively and co-operatively with a number of organizational interest groups $^{35}$ , the most important of which are top managers, user managers and IS managers $^{36}$ . Top managers should, in the long run, have a view of business IT and evaluate the importance of computers in alignment with corporate strategy. User managers should ensure that IS are properly implemented and used in key business areas. IT/IS experts should be informed and learn about critical business factors and scan the range of IT opportunities. There are other interest groups too $^{37}$ , but they have only minor power in final IS decisions.

Organizational strategies, structures, processes and especially people greatly affect SISP opportunities. Checkland $^{38}$ has emphasized that when dealing with human activity systems a certain kind of learning paradigm is needed — a softer approach, in order to understand more deeply the problems of organization. It is therefore suggested that Information Management Education (IME) (a term proposed by Earl $^{13}$ ) should — first of all — focus on the strategic planning of IS, and should be organized in a parallel fashion with SISP activities in order to

1. integrate learning of new SISP knowledge during planning activities;

2. enable communication and interpretation of new SISP concepts; and

3. use and control that 'SISP learning'.

In our IS strategy studies $^{39}$ we have found that one key element of successful SISP is the alignment of planning and learning. Educational events such as seminars, introductory lectures, workshops and interviews-in-depth are usually organized to support SISP and integrate different views of managers.

The organizational change $^{40,41}$ and managerial learning aspects $^{42}$ are discussed elsewhere, while the interaction and collaboration of managers and managerial groups are under consideration here. Järvenpaa and Ives $^{43}$ noted recently that much of the research into management participation and involvement is based on an individual perspective, and less so on group level observations. The emphasis here is placed on assessing different group situations during

SISP and finding supporting evidence for a theoretical framework. The SISP experiences come from three case companies drawn from different industries.

## The nature of managerial groups in SISP

## The research focus — SISP as a political group process

Individuals and organizational sub-units form groups. When individuals form a group, the following qualities can be recognized $^{44-46}$ : (a) members of the group have at least one common motive or objective; (b) members create a set of group norms; (c) during group interaction a set of stable roles emerges and (d) emotional and mutual relationships emerge. Schein $^{47}$ states that a group creates ‘a pattern of basic assumptions — invented, discovered, or developed by a given group as it learns to cope with its problems of external adaptation and internal integration — that has worked well enough to be considered valid and, therefore, to be taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think and feel in relation to those problems.’ Bostrom $^{35}$ has noted that ‘the parties involved [in IS development] bring different frames of reference to the situation…’. This is one of the basic assumptions of soft systems thinking $^{38}$ . It is also of note that team work and groups are found to be governors of performance. According to Hambrick $^{48}$ : ‘… general managers who want to improve their organization’s performance will work on improving their management teams.’ In summary, people perceive observed reality differently, and collaboration around these views is critical for performance.

In negotiation situations we are dealing with organizational power and politics $^{49-51}$ . Stakeholders' assumptions vary in strategic planning situations $^{27, 52}$ . The use of a coalition of key players, e.g. a steering committee, is usually emphasized due to its property 'to change the nature of a very political process [of selecting MIS projects] by altering the locus of authority and often the personnel involved' $^{53}$ . To summarize, the political process of SISP depends on the organizational and social relationships of managerial groups.

## Interaction problems in group work and behaviour

Group-related problems could emerge as a result of the following factors (Reference 46, p. 11): (a) the objective of the group, (b) the mutual alignment of group members, (c) individual objectives, and (d) the personalities of group members. Problems may arise due to contradictory objectives $^{54, 55}$ or individual preferences. People avoid embarrassment and threat, and use organizational defensive routines $^{56}$ . Managers might not co-operate with each other, while group roles $^{57}$ could be incompatible. Personal feelings or those related to one's position in the company hierarchy could make cooperation difficult. Personalities could also be too similar, leading to lack of innovation, or they may be strongly contradictory when dealing for example with power (positional power v. expert power). Personal level problems could be due to the organizational status of the IS Department, generating frustration for the IS manager. Business managers might unconsciously fear IS issues or object to any changes which require reorganization. To summarize, group dynamics are very complex and must be studied 'inside the process'; the results may otherwise be superficial. Hence, effective group design depends on environmental/contextual and internal structural factors $^{58}$ . There are problems concerning both the tasks of the group, and the organizational context in relation to actors' emotions.

## Critical managerial groups within SISP

As stated above, a successful SISP process requires both increased managerial learning and increased interaction and co-operation between managers and managerial groups (increased communication and the interpretation of different views). The potential value of IS for competitiveness has dramatically changed the situation; organizational and strategy analysis for successful IS development is thus needed $^{59}$ . Managers need to think strategically about information $^{60}$ .

The three critical stakeholder groups in the SISP process — top management, user management and IT/IS management — need to interact and collaborate in planning for strategic IS. However, top managers do not know enough about the strategic use of IT $^{17}$ . IT/IS managers are not familiar with business strategy issues and should learn more about managerial issues $^{61}$ . User managers or business-unit managers should know how to plan and implement strategic IS $^{62}$ . The rationale behind this study is the observed differences between different stakeholders. Galliers $^{19,63}$ has noted that 'it is likely that different stakeholders will have different reasons for wanting an ISP study to take place i.e. they may well be looking for different outcomes from the process'. Earl's findings $^{14}$ corroborate this view: IS directors being mainly concerned with the implementation of strategy, 'perhaps because they are charged with delivery', while user managers are not satisfied with the IS planning process 'perhaps because they seek more influence' and general managers emphasize SISP method issues, 'because they find strategy-making far from easy.'

Both perceptions and knowledge of IS issues are reflected in IS decision-making, for example in IS investment policy-making, priority-setting for IS projects $^{53}$ and human resource planning. In this paper the ontological status and situation of managerial groups is determined by managers' organizational and sociocultural position and by their beliefs about information management. It is proposed that needs and preferences differ greatly according to the status of the individual manager, his/her decision power and expectations of IS services. Each critical managerial group is defined through:

1. organizational and sociocultural position:

2. managers' decision power and responsibility area; and

3. previous IS experience.

The groups are described below (see also Table 1).

Top management groups: These are in the first place responsible for business management and decision-making. They must perceive the mission of an organization. Their area of responsibility encompasses the identification, planning, implementation and evaluation of corporate/business strategy. They are the top level decision-makers and can strongly affect the decision-making process. Typical titles are, for example, General Manager, Managing Director, CEO, Director, President, etc. Top managers have a general view of the corporate strategy of the enterprise being studied. They usually sit on the Board. Their experience of IS is most often limited to management reporting systems. Some of these managers may exploit end-user computing (EUC) applications and electronic mail systems. They are usually concerned with curtailing the costs of IS.

User management groups: These include managers who are responsible for separate business units or for profit-driven departments/divisions. They must report to higher level management, i.e. they are controlled in some way. User managers have a view of their area of responsibility and its development, i.e. they have a business strategy view. They could thus be defined as being in the same sociocultural environment as far as their relationship with both the Top Management Group and the IT/IS Management Group is concerned. Their IS experience is gained through ISD projects, where they have probably participated as business experts — or they have none. They are usually more interested in the service level of IS that is critical to the management of their unit. They are evaluated in terms of business performance and very often are not responsible for IS costs. IS services are usually a general cost for them, and not included in their business plans.

IT/IS management groups: These are defined as those management groups responsible for the information processing/services department or some corresponding function in the company. IT/IS managers have a view of the opportunities and constraints of IT. Those managers similar to IT/IS managers, who are mainly responsible for IS development, should also be recognized although their formal organizational position may not reveal their involvement (many of them are also Administration or Finance/Accounting managers). IS management is required to take account of corporate and business strategies. Hence, an independent company which is only partly owned by the parent-organization and sells external IS services in competitive markets is excluded from the definition, since it has independent operating power. It actually changes the situation into an operational make-or-buy problem. By this definition IS management usually serves the parent organization.

## The research approach

## About paradigm and method

This study is based mainly on social relativism $^{64}$ . The author participated in the SISP process as an active member. The data was collected through participative observation and interviews. It is important to study and gather knowledge concerning the co-operation, interaction and communication of managers during the SISP processes. The research setting is such that Information Management Education (IME) $^{42,65,66}$ is provided during the field studies, in order to help managers to understand the importance of IS and formulate a general perception of the use of IT $^{67-72}$ . The main objective of this approach is to create a mutual understanding between different managerial groups. We must derive a 'working SISP language' and generalized SISP concepts with which managers can deal during the planning work. Performance and the implementation of strategies are related to managers' qualities $^{73, 74, 85}$ : 'top management teams which demonstrate a preponderance of output functional experience, of multiple company employment, and of wider educational training will outperform those which do not, whether this be upon criterion of inter- or intra-industry productivity' $^{75}$ .

Functional experience and previous education thus have a positive effect on the performance and success of companies. Many of the studies (e.g. Reference 73) on managers and their impact on business performance are weak in that they utilize a quite functionalist method: managers are understood as components having objective and measurable traits. Managers should be understood as intentional actors who create their social reality; this reality should be experienced in order to gain knowledge of managerial processes. Objective managerial qualities are only one area of this research. Interaction, personal relationships and contradictions between managerial groups during planning processes are also interesting research issues $^{54, 55}$ —and are not accessible in functionalist studies. It is proposed that not only managers' cognitive skills affect the successful change process, but also that social interaction and the co-operative application of acquired knowledge steer development and change, and should be registered in the research setting. There are a number of contradictions and tensions between managers and managerial groups: in our research projects we attempt to affect, redirect or even change them, with the help of IME $^{42}$ and interactive group-work methods.

## Case descriptions and the objective of the study

The SISP planning process (the EMIS framework $^{39, 76, 77}$ ) was executed in three Finnish companies between 1988 and 1989 (the case examples are referred to as CASEA.

CASEB and CASEC). Further details of the SISP approach used here can be found in References 42, 65, 66 and 78. Each organization was attempting to tackle ISP problems and wished to make an effort to formulate an IS strategy. Brief descriptions of each case company are provided in Appendix A. This paper documents experiences from the starting phase of the SISP process in each case company. The basic objective is to describe and compare the sociocultural situations of each case company, and derive theory-laden guidelines for the improved analysis of organizational life in the context of SISP.

Each of the stakeholder groups previously described was represented in the case companies studied. After brief observation and interview it was quite obvious that political and cultural power relations, behavioural differences and contradictions existed between each of the groups. In CASEA the field of study was applied within one business unit, so the UMG comprised departmental managers. CASEB and CASEC furnished corporate level examples.

## Assessing the relationships of stakeholder groups during the the SISP process

## CASEA

Tensions between UMG and IMG were obvious and explicitly observable. In CASEA managerial relationships had been infected due to the inferior level of IS services. Business departments were fighting over IS services. One departmental (P/II) had maintained a good relationship with the IS Department and thus guaranteed priority for IS services, mainly in relation to production systems.

The other departments had played a minor role in ISD projects; the IS of the Sales and Marketing Department and the Administration Department for example were quite primitive. One of the managers from production department P/I was frustrated by the level of IS service, and felt his department was being treated inequitably. He had commented: 'In order to grow, we need systems to support our growth'.

Office automation was not connected to the transaction processing system and traditional word processing and calculating tasks were executed either by old-fashioned text processing machines or by hand, since office automation was of little interest to the IS Department. For example, the first course in the use of microcomputers was held during the IS strategy planning process. One manager from Sales & Marketing commented: 'The IS Department didn't have a choice, it was forced to organize the first micro course'. The IS Department was against the growing use of micros. The IS manager commented in one of the seminars: 'Micros lead us into disaster'. Department Heads disagreed vociferously with this comment, as micros had offered an appropriate alternative for their information processing needs. This contradiction can be interpreted as a political one, since micros devolve power to user units; the IS Department did not want the volume of work being carried out to go beyond its control. It was overprotective towards the use of the corporate database, fearing data corruption. User support activities now needed to be formally arranged. The situation at the time had led users to ask the R&D Department for advice about operational problems, since it had the most extensive experience of the independent use of micros and micro networks — despite the fact that R&D should have concentrated on strategic product development problems, not on operational IT utilization problems. The R&D Department has generally been very independent and used intra-departmental know-how for its administrative and operational routines as well.

The IS Department naturally offered a great deal of IS service to its host (the Finance and Accounting Department), so traditional administrative systems took up a sizeable share of available resources. A great number of planned development projects were administrative, not operational in nature. Key players in the F & A Department could, socioculturally speaking, be defined as being included in the IT/IS Management Group.

The CEO of CASEA was not using IS in his day-to-day work, but was worried about IS service and said he needed higher quality reports. Being responsible for the whole company he needed to ensure the apportionment of appropriate resources to all its parts. The CEO's main task was to locate business operations according to business strategy, and he had had a Strategic Business Area analysis conducted by a consultancy firm, in order to increase awareness of strategy issues. Lifecycle analysis revealed that the company had two product lines, of which one was at saturation phase or even declining, and the other growing fast. The growing business areas of CASEA were therefore more important than the mature areas. This organizational evolution had brought about a situation where the CEO, Head of R&D and Head of Production Department I (i.e. P/I from the growing business area — see Figure 1) had a good relationship, and therefore a basis for fruitful discussion. Other relationships were not so good. The Head of the mature department (P/II) had more power at factory floor level, which caused tension between growing and traditional departments.

The CEO wanted to change current information management practice and make ISD practice more explicit. He looked to a process of IS strategy formulation for help. The sociocultural setting of managerial groups is described in Figure 1.

## CASEB

CASEB is a large corporate organization in the process of transforming its business activities. The traditional operations model has been centralized and is therefore inefficient, due to the time-lapse between the finished manufacture of goods and their retail, during which time capital lies in store. The application of IT is planned to aid the structural change. The CEO of CASEB has pinpointed this issue in public business journals and is highly committed to the use of new technology.

Business chains — the logical units of CASEB — will in future be more independent i.e. they will be concatenated $^{79}$ . The introduction of new technology also demands new leadership and management qualities $^{80}$ .

In addition to the need for a general IT strategy (i.e. the technological options and solutions of the whole Group) and IM strategy (the overall organization and decision-making practice of information resources), there is also a drive to obtain independent IS strategies for each business chain. Two of the chains already have quite an independent IS (CIV and to a lesser degree CIII), but two (CI and CII) are still dependent on the parent-organization. The parent-organization (CASEB Group) is interested in synergistic IS activities (those of the customer database or logistics service activities), which entail the development of a telecommunications architecture. Business chains are investing heavily in EAN-based POS terminals and back-office systems, in order to become integrated with the basic architecture of the Group. The industry in general is in a turnaround situation.

The managers of different business chains will thus gain more power (and also responsibility) to develop their own IS, despite the need to fall in line with general architectural principles. This phenomenon is referred here as a division of IS power $^{29,62}$ . The relationships between the TMG and the UMGs of each of two different chains (CI and CII) are basically good, due to synergistic activities. These business chains are in fact very dependent for historical reasons on the Group organization, also unfortunately reflecting most of the traditionally inefficient features of business operations in the wholesale/retailing business. Chain III is very independent in its business activities, except for purchasing operations; Chain IV even controls its own purchasing.

The history of IS services revealed some interesting points. The IS Service Unit, which was a separate profit-centre working mostly for the parent-organization, has been unable to serve CASEB efficiently, being unable to offer IS support for day-to-day business operations. The Unit has mainly processed financial data and produced reports and statistics. Now its role needs to be redesigned $^{8}$ , since business chains require a markedly different service than before. The Unit has, most significantly, become estranged from business management groups. The critical problem for CASEB is how it will in future be able to fulfill the IS needs of business chains and 'headquarters'. IS services must be distributed and tailored according to the needs and interests of each chain. One of the most important questions asked at the beginning of the strategy planning process was: 'What will the future IS organization and IT architecture be?', so the reconstruction challenge was there! ## CASEC

CASEC is a production-oriented company, due both to its history of craftsmanship and having a stable market that has not demanded customer-oriented activities. The closeness of markets, the oligopolistic nature of Finnish business and a low share of export sales imply that competition is not very aggressive in Finnish markets. Due to this special craftsman-culture the managers of manufacturing sites are very powerful people, despite corporate management. Their value weighs heavily in all development operations, sales and marketing people consequently receiving insufficient IS services for their field operations. It is very common in this industry for product delivery times to be longer than promised, one reason being ineffective information systems. As a result of interviewing one Marketing manager two important IS issues were pinpointed: 'We have no support systems for customer service . . . production professionals don't bother about our customer-based needs and product specifications.'

In recent years the IT/IS Department has made a great effort to integrate IS within the extremely long value chain of CASEC. This goal has not yet been achieved. The IT Manager commented: 'There is still a gap between production and ordering systems, which must be filled in the future'. This problem had occurred mainly at user management level, while obviously being a critical part of the service process, i.e. an issue to be decided upon at the top level. Top management was, however, more interested in product development.

CASEC was also transforming its centrally managed business operations model into more independent and profit-aware business chains, but this was still at the planning stage and had not yet been implemented. Current IS cannot serve future information needs, since distributed operations require more effective and focused IS. One specific part of CASEC's business is OEM-product sales, the value chain of which extends more or less through warehousing and selling, and not through traditional production-oriented business. A better fit of IS is required to the nature of this growing business area.

During the planning process 'the craftsmanship syndrome' was evident on several occasions. The R&D Department, for example, had a good relationship with the management of Production Site I and with Top Management (the Board), but the dialogue centred mainly around products, not necessarily processes, so 'customer-drive' and the need for an adequate service level (i.e. the interests of Sales and Marketing) were not receiving enough attention.

The IT/IS Department had a good relationship with business departments, although mainly with production departments. The career of the IS manager had actually started in one of the production departments. The IT/IS Department was in general responsible to the Chief Administrative Officer. Board level and actual field operations were quite remote, the organization being layered so that each business chain had its own managers in addition to other functional posts such as factory manager. The critical problem for the collaboration of managerial groups is how to integrate corporate strategy and business strategies and support this process with IS development. Customer-orientation and the reduction of lead-times are top priority goals for CASEC, requiring the collaboration of Sales and Marketing professionals, and Production and IS Managers. IS staff should offer systems able to deliver products and services to CASEC's customers.

IS Management had not received any instructions or guidelines for resource allocation, since there was not at that time any general agreement on business direction. After such an agreement the IT/IS Department could probably participate more effectively in IS planning and implementation.

IMG co-operated well at production site level (PSI and PSII), i.e. with factory automation and related support activities. These good relationships were due to previous IS projects. CASEC's business was changing however, and the 'craftmanship syndrome' did not allow for enough discussion about future business guidelines and related reorganization. Factory managers were powerful and, for example, the Sales and Marketing staff were not taken seriously in their proposals for developing better service. TMG and UMG co-operated in part, particularly concerning product development (R&D). In general, the development of processes was almost ignored, which further affected the IS planning process.

## Summary

This small sample of companies demonstrates that organizational planning and development are multifaceted, such that different organizational and political interest groups locate themselves to defend their positions and preferences (see Table 2).

This study revealed that stakeholder groups and their dynamics should be observed at two levels. First, at stakeholder level, concerning group objectives, individual objectives, members' mutual alignment and personality characteristics (i.e. internal integration $^{47}$ ) and secondly at inter-group level where interaction between groups is studied, i.e. how socially and culturally formed stakeholder groups communicate and collaborate with each other (external adaptation $^{47}$ ). These two levels are mutually linked: if intra-group collaboration collapses, inter-group collaboration is also affected. However, some intra-group problems can be managed through inter-group collaboration. This is a challenge for information management. It is suggested therefore, that both internal integration and external adaptation should be examined in the context of SISP studies.

This study differs in its approach from previous studies: it attempts to capture real experience and real sociocultural situations during SISP processes. Our research approach can take into account phenomena which are not open to direct discussion or observation, trying to reveal the hidden assumptions and understand the highly political process of SISP.

The main findings of this study are:

1. The definition of the SISP stakeholders concept with three groups, top managers, user managers and IT/IS managers, was found to be empirically applicable and supported previous findings $^{14, 19}$ . It is also appropriate therefore for further theoretical elaboration.

2. The stakeholder analysis should be executed in the context of environmental and cultural factors. Managers do not plan 'in a closed room', but learn from previous experience. With no cultural context the reconstruction and implementation challenges of SISP could easily become ignored.

3. As a contribution to further research, this study offers a wider framework for stakeholder analysis, where both intra-group and inter-group issues are considered. Intra-group analysis concerns both objectives and people, while inter-group analysis takes the planning environment into account as well.

In order to be effective such a strategy should be a manifestation of current and, especially, future IS development needs and objectives. These objectives should be planned in an environment of mutual understanding. However, organizational, cultural and political factors affect and even destroy IS planning efforts. In addition to efforts to increase managerial learning we should be aware of opportunities and obstacles to managerial collaboration.

## Appendix A: Case Descriptions

## CASEA Company

In general: CASEA is a fast growing Finnish high-tech company, most of whose manufacturing output is exported. The company is a subsidiary of a large multinational enterprise and it trades in very competitive markets. Its primary competitive edges are high quality products and a high level of R&D investment. It is dependent on IT, for information concerning both product and the production process (Porter & Millar 1985).

Personnel and organization: The personnel have a mostly technical background — they consist mainly of engineers. Research ability is also highly valued, due to R&D dependence. Company organization is a typical matrix with divisions and departments. The IS Department is responsible to the Finance and Accounting Department. Each division has its own management board and each department its own head. The Managing Director coordinates the whole enterprise.

Information processing: Information processing is centralized and includes two minicomputers, a number of terminals and accounting and production software. Office automation and telecommunications seem to be at the diffusion stage $^{82}$ . IT is considered to be a supporting activity $^{4}$ in spite of its important role. ISD is production-oriented, event-driven and constrained by a huge application backlog. Business departments have increased their microcomputer capacity due to lack of IS services. This has created confusion and infected relationships between IS and business departments. IT architecture has grown evolutionarily and become difficult to manage. IS planning is uncoordinated and applications are mainly issue-based, although the company has an integrated database. End-users' operational usage of the company's database is minimal, due to improper communication links and incompatible accounting standards.

Previous experience of SISP and the history of IS: Previous information processing and development problems were recognized in the company. Management had already made an attempt to solve these problems, but had not been able to do so. They did not have a proper approach to ISP, discussions turning too quickly to particular applications and problem issues. Further, they had no general framework with which to investigate the overall situation. The CEO wanted a solution and contacted the author's department asking for a contribution to IS development.

## CASEB Company

In general: CASEB is a large Finnish wholesale/retail corporation, one branch of which purchases goods from industrial manufacturers and then distributes these items and a number of different household goods (clothes, household equipment etc.). It operates in a very stable Finnish market. The focus here is on the household business.

The corporation's annual turnover (at group level) is several billion Fmk (thousands of millions of dollars). Competitors are very similar in nature, it being difficult to recognize any competitive advantage between them. The Finnish wholesale/retail business can be described as oligopolistic. However, the main threat to all Finnish enterprises in this industry is forthcoming economic integration (e.g. 1992), which could bring outside competitors into this stable environment.

McFarlan $^{4}$ has noted, that this kind of industry is usually in a ‘turnaround’ situation regarding the use of IT. That is to say, the current status of IT usage is low, but future IS needs demand a high level of IT. Thus, in Finland, all major companies in this industry are actively developing the application of IT in business operations. They will be dependent on IT in the future.

Personnel and organization: The corporation's managers are mostly business-educated and usually gain experience in different sectors of the company before they reach leading positions. The corporation as a whole is coordinated by its Board.

CASEB is transforming its business operations from a wholesale/retail units format into a vertically integrated business chain-type organization. This means that, as a corporation, it is distributing decision power and responsibility to different business chains which will be more independent in the future, although they may use 'headquarters-services'. The old-fashioned model of a wholesale/retail corporation, in which the wholesale organization purchases goods and then sells them to retailing units will be redesigned. Hence, they are developing their business according to the value chain concept $^{83, 84}$ . As they are finding, it is not important to sell within a business chain (one business partner to other), for customers are the crucial factor. Customer service is emphasized in almost all the major business chains.

Information processing: Information processing is centralized and handles mostly operational routines (purchasing, order processing, sales statistics, financial and accounting routines etc.). Information processing is executed by a separate IS service unit. Nevertheless, some of the chains already have information systems of their own and independent IS development. There is no proper telecommunications architecture for transaction data, which would coordinate the group level reporting: only the electronic mail network is operative.

Point-of-Sales (POS) terminal investment has increased considerably in business units, but in general they lack stock control applications. The increase of POS terminal investment is reasonable, since it is the only point at which data about sales and customers can be collected. IT is considered an activity that will support the change of the business model.

ISD is executed mostly by the IS service unit, although business units participate in the information systems requirements phase. The business units' main focus is on daily business operations. Many of them are not very interested in IS development and operations, except for daily routines (POS-terminals). There is, however, a growing demand for the distribution of IS services to business chains, which need their own management information systems (MIS) in order to make fast decisions effectively and actively steer business operations. Needs are different at corporate level. Reports are required from the whole business (from differentiated and segmented chains) together with product/service information from suppliers and general information about the competitive situation. Synergistic activities of the whole business (marketing, purchasing) are allocated at corporate level. The refinement of the business strategies of each chain is dealt with at business chain level.

Previous experience of SISP and the history of IS: Information processing has not been very cost-effective and its service level has also been low. For example, headquarters do not know what is happening in the field of retailing. Sales reports come too late and do not include sufficiently utilitarian information (for example, sales statistics per article or article group). Marketing functions cannot make use of customer information in planning, since there is no systematic collection of customer preferences, sales history, etc. Managers are ignorant of logistics efficiency — goods-in could stay in warehouses too long and deliveries are not on time. To summarize, the management of the whole value chain — from factory to customer — is not well-designed. There are 'black-outs' of information within the value chain.

Thus, CASEB needs a SISP process, which ought to aim at the development of information systems and management strategy (ISMS) for the whole corporation and different business chains. This strategy would activate the on-going change process, support the business model of the future and explicate critical IS development areas and projects.

## CASEC Company

In general: CASEC is a Finnish manufacturing and sales company dealing in wood-based products. It also operates in a very stable Finnish market, which has been divided between a couple of large corporations and a number of minor companies and entrepreneurs. Foreign competition is growing, especially in the low-price sector. This company begins its production process by collecting the raw material from source — Finnish forests — then sawing, designing and manufacturing, before commencing sales and distribution activities, namely marketing, delivery to stores and selling to customers. The value chain is therefore extremely long and difficult to manage. Thus, its effectiveness is one of the critical success factors for this company.

The competitors of CASEC are very similar in nature. There are basically two groups of customers in this industry; households (i.e. consumers) and public/business organizations. The way in which business operations are executed differs greatly depending on the customer group. Organizations demand high quality products, but also a high level of service. Consumers usually emphasize product price factors more than organizations, although quality is also important. Some CASEC competitors have concentrated on low-price equipment for consumers, which is almost always imported from countries where unit costs are lower (i.e. from Eastern European countries). Some companies in the industry invest to a high degree in design and product development. Nevertheless, foreign competition can change current business conditions. This industry tends to be more cost-efficient in, for example, Sweden.

There is a huge amount of information in the value chain of this company. The management of manufacturing and lead times is crucial to future success. Customer information is important, especially in the public and business organization sector, as it enables good service. Sales of consumer articles must be followed more carefully in the future. Stock levels must be reduced in order to minimize warehousing costs. Delivery times must be shorter than at present. The industry is also transforming its business model. It can no longer be a huge centralized organization, which 'puts products in the marketplace'. In the future, it must listen to the needs of customers, and manufacture goods which match current preferences. Production must be steered by orders and eventually by customer needs. This demands better IS.

Personnel and organization: Personnel in general and most of the company's managers have a technical background (engineers and self-taught craftsmen). There is a strong orientation toward the production function: so strong that marketing and sales activities have played a minor role. In the past, the organization has been a typical manufacturing model, which has grown around its essential function, i.e. production sites. Production is based on discrete step-by-step manufacturing phases and it operates on a line organization. Shell-oriented manufacturing has been under discussion, but a total shift in that direction would require massive changes in the production structure. In the future, the company will operate with a different business model. Business operations are going to be divided into separate profit chains, which correspond to the customer grouping (consumers, organizations, etc.) each chain serves. The IS department is responsible to the Administration Department. Each factory has its own manager and the CEO coordinates the entire company.

Information processing: Information processing is centralized and enacted by minicomputers, a number of terminals and accounting and production software. In addition, there are a number of separate microcomputers. An essential part of the software is the order processing system, which gathers together orders from all over the country. It is not integrated with the production steering system. This causes an operational problem affecting delivery times and customization needs fulfillment. Integration within the long value chain would certainly be a competitive factor for future business.

The role of IT is seen largely from the perspective of production, in spite of the fact that there is also a growing need for IT in customer management. The information systems of retail stores should provide information about delivery times, complex products and customization possibilities, for example. It is supposed that better management of customers would lead to a 'good service image'.

Previous experience of SISP and the history of IS: CASEC contacted the author's department in order to outline IS needs and requirements for the next five years. ISD had previously been event-driven and concentrated mostly on corporate level administrative systems. Now the company wanted to envision future IS operations.

The coming new business models are challenging centralized information systems structure and simultaneously current organizational culture. If every customer group-oriented chain is to be responsible for its own profits, tools will be needed to control and manage operations. Then, however, centralized and inappropriately synthesized information will not offer any relevant message to profit centre managers. They will need customized reports, which tell them how their business is going. Thus, the parent organization of CASEC should guarantee ISD independency and at the same time take care of its own corporate level needs.
