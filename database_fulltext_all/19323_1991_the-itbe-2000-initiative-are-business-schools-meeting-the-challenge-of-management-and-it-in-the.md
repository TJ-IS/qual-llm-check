# The ITBE 2000 Initiative: are business schools meeting the challenge of management and IT in the 21st century?

David Targett

School of Management, University of Bath, Claverton Down, Bath BA2 7AY, UK

Tomorrow's senior managers will face the challenge posed by the current transition from an industrial to an information-based economy. Their ability to perform successfully into the 21st century will depend upon a profound understanding of the business impact of information technology (IT) and information systems (IS). They will be able to delegate decisions and responsibilities but not understanding.

The senior managers of the 21st century, many of whom are part of the existing management stock, are being educated today in a wide variety of business schools throughout Europe. Both business and academic worlds are in agreement that the management of information resources will be one of the key business skills of the future. One of the major findings from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology's Management in the 1990s Research Programme $^{1}$ (MIT90s) was that IT-induced business re-configuration is the new strategic management challenge.

For some time the major business schools have been devoting considerable resources to research in this area. The MIT90s project, involving twelve sponsoring organizations each donating \$0.5 million in cash and probably the same in other resources, is a prime example but not the only example. Research seems to be receiving its fair share of attention, but what are business schools doing to develop new approaches for teaching IS?

Many educators are concerned that past approaches to teaching IS, in which technical issues and process automation were stressed, are at best inappropriate and at worst damaging because they can obscure the strategic challenge of IS. Business schools are adopting a wide range of approaches to the problems but many admit to a real uncertainty about the best way ahead.

There is a vital need to tackle these issues which concern business as much as business schools. Forward-looking organizations, aware of the changing management role, are exactly the organizations which are keenly interested in the supply of a key resource. They wish to find managers who have both business and IS skills and who are also able to take an international perspective.

The ITBE 2000 Initiative, with participants from business and business schools, seeks to provide a forum for the exchange of experiences and the development of ideas concerning IT and business education. This article explains ITBE's objectives and describes the First Annual Meeting held at the Theseus Institute in Nice when leading business schools and business organizations from eleven European countries were represented.

## The changing role of information systems

Rapid advances in the capabilities of IT combined with substantial reductions in its cost have radically changed its role in organizations. Since the 1960s, organizations have used IT to mechanize and automate functions such as the payroll and stock control. Their concerns were whether this automation was being achieved on time and within budget.

Now IT does more than automate processes: it has led to the development of IS which can transform industries, organizations, business processes and management methods. Retailers' use of EPoS systems and travel agents' use of computerized booking systems are just two examples of information systems which give benefits much wider than cost and manpower reductions. Organizations' concerns are now whether their IS investments are gaining them short-term competitive advantage, whether further investments can maintain their lead — and whether their competitors' investments are putting them at a competitive disadvantage.

IS have also had the effect of seeming to shorten geographical distances and enabling organizations to cross international boundaries. Just as the development a century ago of the telegraph and telephone allowed local companies to become national companies, so modern IS improve communications within an organization and promote close alliances between organizations. A European automobile manufacturer can be linked more closely with offices and agents on other continents. It can communicate electronically with suppliers in other European countries and IS help it establish joint ventures with other manufacturers.

Of course it would be wrong to suggest that IS always, or even mostly, lead to success. There have been many disasters but they tend not to be the focus of best-selling books or business school case studies.

Other organizations fall between success and disaster. And many do not know whether they have been successful or not. In some industries it is necessary to spend vast amounts on IS — or cease to compete. Banking is an example. In spite of the large IS expenditures there is no competitive advantage, just competitive disadvantage if you fail to spend or spend wrongly.

## The changing role of managers

In the 1960s the general management perspective on computer operations was that they were a technical matter. The general management role was to check whether projects were on time and under budget, or not. This role did not require general managers to know much, if anything, about computers.

The situation now is very different. There are three inescapable reasons why general managers today need to be closely involved with IS.

\- IS affect strategic issues. If IS can gain, or lose, competitive advantage and if they can radically alter market share then a CEO can hardly regard them as a technical matter only. A pharmaceutical company dramatically increased market share when it put computers linked to an information system about its products on doctors' desks.

\- IS are at the core of business processes. Many organizations could no longer function without IS. A bank which did not offer the customer services that computerization brings would have a poor future. Some business processes have been changed radically by IS, for example the buying and selling of shares.

\- IT accounts for large expenditures. In many organizations IT expenditure regularly runs at 4 per cent of revenue or more. For some banks it is as high as 10 per cent. A recent survey $^{2}$ revealed that 50 per cent of new capital expenditure is on IT.

Even if the strategic and management arguments are ignored it is certain that a CEO will pay close attention to major cost factors. For others the IS revolution has not yet occurred but they have seen how quickly it has struck elsewhere and do not want to be left behind by competitors. Whatever the reasons, IS are firmly on the general management agenda.

## How are business schools reacting?

Business schools are very much concerned about IS: the annual ICIS (International Conference on Information Systems) draws around one thousand delegates but this is primarily a research forum. What about the teaching?

Business schools worldwide have had courses on IS since the 1960s. At that time, very properly, the courses were about technical issues. Today, a survey of leading business schools in America and Europe $^{3}$ has shown substantial diversity in their positions. The findings raised a number of significant issues.

\- Content: Many courses with IT or IS in the title were oriented to basic skills and knowledge. Few appeared to deal with the strategic impact of IS. Although courses had been updated, the changes were often to reflect the new technical boundaries of IS rather than their new business role.

\- Place in MBA programmes: Less than half the MBA Programmes had core (compulsory) IS courses although most had optional courses. There seems to have been inertia in creating new courses to deal with a wholly new issue such as the new role of IS. As one MBA Programme Director said, 'Everyone agrees that IS must feature but no-one wants their own course to be the one left out in order to make room.'

● Faculty: Some schools had substantial IS faculties, whereas others had none. Of course the latter could be the most appropriate response: the IS area had been integrated in existing courses. Or, it could indicate a non-response.

\- Organizational structure: Some schools had a subject grouping devoted entirely to IS. Others incorporated IS within existing subject groupings such as finance decision sciences, management science and business policy. This is the clearest indication of the diversity of business schools' strategies with regard to IS.

Business schools brochures usually work hard at marketing IS as an important dimension in their programmes. Privately, however, business schools admit that they are uncertain how to deal with the new role of IS.

Meanwhile, business organizations are clear in voicing their need for managers who understand the business and IS. They are concerned about the education of their existing stock of managers: that they should be aware of the new situation and know how to react to it.

They are also concerned about future managers, many of whom are now taking part in undergraduate programmes across the usual wide range of subjects. Undergraduates are normally computer literate in the sense of being able to operate word processing and spreadsheet software: the availability of personal computers at school and university has seen to that. But they may not appreciate the business impact of IS. Worse, their indoctrination in the usefulness of computers as a technical tool may blind them to the real issues: appreciation of strategic issues must lead to the appreciation of technical capabilities, not be obscured by it.

## The ITBE 2000 Initiative

All this uncertainty points to the need for a forum in which business schools can exchange ideas and experiences and resolve some of the problems. A group of leading European business schools, backed by sponsorship from the Nolan Norton Group, are trying to do just this.

ITBE 2000 is a network of those concerned with educating managers for the 21st century. It comprises faculty from leading European business schools together with senior managers from major organizations. ITBE 2000 is based on open exchange of information and experience to promote new ideas whose development will often be beyond the capacity of a single organization.

It is intended that the ITBE 2000 network operates through two main mechanisms: annual meetings and working groups. The annual meetings will last a maximum of two days at a European location and their usual style will be of short informal presentations on selected themes followed by chaired discussions and syndicate work. However, a diversity of forms of presentation will be encouraged. The annual meetings will not follow the conference style of presentation and criticism of academic papers.

The working groups will be established with structures and objectives evolving from the annual meetings. It is hoped that one of the tasks will be the development of case material which would be made generally available.

## The first annual meeting of ITBE 2000

ITBE's first annual meeting was held at the Theseus Institute in Nice on April 4-5, 1991. The 26 participants from eleven European countries represented leading European business schools and business organizations.

The keynote speaker was Victor Walling, Head of IT and Strategic Planning for Shell International. He illustrated his theme of 'The Demand from Industry' with examples of the impacts of IS within his own company. At the organizational level he was concerned about the way managers must incorporate IS when re-thinking business strategies and competitive positioning. The benefits of IS must be viewed differently from the past: the issues now were how to measure benefits in logical rather than quantitative terms and how to implement the activities necessary to achieve the benefits. At the individual level he was concerned with how managers can re-design work and business processes. Managers must re-learn the way they approach many of their basic tasks and be prepared for fundamentally different organizational structures and working methods.

A series of short presentations under the general heading 'What Business Schools are Supplying' described the different approaches that were being adopted to the problems being faced. Research $^{3}$ into the extent to which 'Management and IS' was being taught on the MBA programmes of 45 leading business schools in North America and Europe was presented. It showed the variety of approaches being adopted. For example, some approaches involved the infusion of dedicated IS courses whereas other involved the diffusion of IS across other functional areas. Some business schools have launched IS-based MBA programmes which include a substantial amount of IS within a management context.

The conference then broke into syndicates to discuss the issues facing industry and business schools. The major issues defined included:

\- What will future managers need to manage information resources?

\- How can business needs which are not yet defined be met?

● Who will need the education?

\- What role should industry play in the formulation of curricula?

\- How should IS be integrated with other management courses?

● How can already crowded curricula be changed?

● How can teaching quality be improved?

After discussion further syndicate work led to a series of action points being proposed. The action points fell under four headings. The first two deal with the changing role of management and the second two with improving the response of business schools.

1. Envisioning the future. The conference agreed to embark on a process to look at the changing management role. Questionnaires will be developed to collect views and opinions which will be assembled to form scenarios of management in the next century.

2. Trends in management skills. The conference agreed to set up a task force to identify emerging trends in management skills and thereby to define the skills and competencies of the manager of the future. The task force would collect information from a wide audience.

3. Develop integrative skills. For management and management teachers alike, integrative skills were thought to be a key issue. Although this has been on the agenda for some time it was felt that little progress was being made. Two working conferences/workshops were proposed. The first would bring together industry and business schools with a view to getting a representative view of what industry was expecting of business schools. The second would bring together IS teachers and strategy teachers from business schools with the objective of considering the implications of the growing overlap between the areas.

4. Improving the quality of IS teaching in business schools. A working group was set up to consider the different dimensions of teaching quality. Management teachers would be sent questionnaires to solicit views: networks to communicate course descriptions and other information would be established; alternative teaching styles would be explored and experiences compared; case studies would be developed and shared.

All these action points were spelt out in some detail, the responsibilities of those involved were defined, and report-back dates agreed.

## Conclusion

The benefits of ITBE 2000 for business schools are seen as:

● a supportive group within which to discuss and develop teaching ideas;

● information on the progress and experience of fellow management educators;

\- a means of keeping in touch with the thinking and needs of forward-looking business and administrative organizations;

● the ability to develop and maintain a genuinely cross-European perspective on international and IS management issues:

\- a bank of relevant and up-to-date case material combined with first-hand reports of its success in the classroom.

There will also be significant benefits for organizations that will have a means to influence the supply of a key resource. ITBE 2000 will allow them a direct input to the way future managers are being educated and the types of course being offered to the existing management stock. Organizations are rightly concerned about the difficulty in finding managers who have both business and IS skills and who are also able to take an international perspective. ITBE 2000 is addressed at precisely these issues.
