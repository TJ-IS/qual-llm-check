# Information systems management in Greece: issues and perceptions

Georgios I. Doukidis, Steve Smithson\* and George Naoum†

Department of Informatics, Athens University of Economics and Business, 76 Patission Street, Athens 10434, Greece

The changing nature of information systems (IS) management in Greece is examined, based on a survey of IS managers from leading Greek companies. The managers' views and perceptions regarding the key issues facing the IS function are discussed from the perspective of the needs and objectives of the business. The findings suggest that the main preoccupation of Greek IS managers is with strategic management issues, with somewhat less interest in people management and systems development issues, and less interest still in end user computing and technology management. With a few exceptions, these findings are broadly similar to those from the UK a few years earlier. The paper discusses the implications of the findings in the light of current US and Western European thinking.

Keywords: information systems management, strategic management, technology management

Information Technology (IT) is widely recognized to be one of the fastest growing issues in Greek business. Although Greece lags behind the more advanced countries in its application of IT, computing has still developed at a considerable rate and shows no signs of slowing. The use of computers is becoming widespread through most industries and across an increasing field of human activity. Businesses of all sizes, from the biggest banks, offices and factories to the corner shop and the one person firm, and public organizations providing education, health care, services and utilities, are all involved in the adoption of IT. In quantitative terms, the number of installed computer-based systems rose from 850 in 1981 to 56 950 in 1987, while the software market increased from \$4 million in 1984 to \$60 million in 1987 $^{1}$ .

However, the successful introduction of computer-based information systems (CBIS) is not without its problems. Numerous failures have been recorded and these are likely to be just the tip of the iceberg, as many more remain hidden by organizations' discretion and embarrassment. However, most of the problems encountered by organizations in the development and operation of CBIS are very much management problems, where 'management' is interpreted in the broadest sense. The really difficult, perhaps currently insoluble, technical problems are mostly the preserve of universities and research laboratories. Within organizations, many apparently technical problems can be traced to the poor management of the development process or operating procedures. It is the human, social and organizational problems that are typically the most severe in terms of consequences (e.g. resistance to change) but, given a sympathetic environment of senior management commitment and workforce flexibility, they can also be the most tractable. Therefore, this paper focuses on the people and issues involved in the management of information systems.

This paper discusses the changing nature of information systems (IS) management in Greece, based on a survey of a large number of IS managers from leading Greek companies, undertaken in 1989. The objective of the survey was to examine the managers' views and perceptions regarding the key issues facing the IS function. The paper discusses those views from the perspective of the needs and objectives of the business, including the strategic role of IS, its influence on the organizational structure of an enterprise and its utilization in the search for competitive advantage in the market. With the use of IT growing rapidly, the education of senior executives, middle managers and end-users has become an important issue. Furthermore, the spread of IS development methodologies has raised new issues in the management of the IS development process, while the problems caused by software bugs have made quality assurance a high profile activity. Finally, in the light of the large amounts of investment required, the evaluation of costly IS in terms of efficiency and productivity gains remains a difficult issue. The central focus for these issues is the person in charge of the IS function, the IS manager, and the discussion centres on the changing nature of his job.

## Survey methodology

For this survey, we decided to concentrate on leading Greek companies since their IT policies and practices were likely to be relatively advanced. Other Greek companies will probably follow the lead of these 'front-runners' over the next few years. We assessed firms for 'leadership' primarily in terms of their financial performance over the last few years, plus the perceived professional skills of their IS management. At the time of the survey, the firms chosen fulfilled at least three of the following criteria:

(a) they had maintained impressive or steady growth for the last three years;

(b) they had a good reputation;

(c) they were one of the leading firms in their sector; and

(d) their IS managers were highly respected.

The survey was carried out in the summer of 1989. It involved face-to-face semi-structured interviews with 40 IS executives who were members of the Hellenic Institute of Information Management (EIP), an institution created in 1985 within the framework of the Hellenic Management Association (EEDE). The views of these leaders in their fields are likely to be valuable in understanding the present concerns and future needs of the IT function in Greece. Every interview lasted at least an hour, allowing a fairly wide-ranging discussion. While some interviewees preferred to answer the questions in a matter-of-fact, objective way, others were more perceptual and subjective. It should be noted that, of the 40 IS executives, only one was a woman.

The other main criterion for selection, apart from company success, was that we wished the survey to cover a wide range of industry sectors without exceeding our own limited research resources. The final sample comprised: 24 manufacturing companies, five oil companies, four banking institutions, three insurance companies, three consultancies and one retailer. These can be classified approximately into an industrial sector of 29 companies and a services sector of 11 companies. All the companies were based within the Athens area and most were fairly large; based on total assets, six of the manufacturers were ranked within the top ten Greek manufacturing companies. The companies had an average of 980 employees including 17 staff in the IS department. Thus, although there was some variance in the company profiles, the companies are basically comparable.

The questionnaire which formed the basis of the interviews comprised 47 questions, ranging from background information on the organization and its IT budget to more subjective questions regarding the IS manager's views on the problems and opportunities within IS development and the relationship between the IS function and the rest of the organization. The design and content of the questionnaire was mostly based on similar studies carried out in the UK $^{2-4}$ .

Although there is some foundation for a comparison between the two countries, differences in the timing and samples means that great care is needed in drawing any conclusions.

Since we asked the IS executives a number of questions concerning their view of senior management's handling of IT strategy, we tried to arrange interviews with senior managers of the same companies, in order to obtain a balanced picture. These proved difficult to arrange, due to time constraints, and so we mailed them a revised questionnaire. We received only 15 responses from the senior managers but these provided a limited counterpoint to the view of the IS executives.

Comparing the 1989 Greek ranking with the 1987 UK scores $^{3}$ , there is a broad similarity. Interestingly, the top two issues are the same, but in the reverse order. However, there are two issues which seem to suggest major differences of opinion between the two countries. First, the location of the IS function within the organization's structure is ranked third in Greece and only nineteenth in the UK. This may reflect the fact that the UK organizations have already carried out major organizational restructuring, while in Greece the pressure is still building up for similar changes. In most Greek companies, the IT function reports to the finance director, who may have little appreciation of its importance. Organizational concerns may be exacerbated by the small size of Greek IS departments, where responsibilities are vague and duplication common. Furthermore, organization charts are not part of the Greek tradition, which is characterized by a family orientation and informal management style. This 'free-wheeling' produces a poor fit with the needs and aspirations of an IS management which looks towards the systematically organized style of the West for direction.

Second, the UK managers ranked IS planning by top management at third place, compared to sixteenth by the Greeks. This may reflect the UK managers' higher profile within the organization, and their greater desire to work with senior managers on planning. This position is still probably some way off in Greece; perhaps neither IS management nor senior management are ready for such cooperation there. Few senior Greek managers show signs of understanding the importance of IT, probably due to their lack of education and training in the area. On the other hand, the IS managers, whilst technically proficient, show little sign of possessing the management and business skills required to promote IT within their organizations.

Data management and control was seen as relatively more important in Greece, again perhaps reflecting the early stages of integration. In the UK, the education of senior management seems to be considered more important than in Greece, perhaps reflecting difficulties encountered in their interaction during the planning and resource allocation process. In addition, fourth generation languages were seen as relatively more important in the UK; this may be because they are less well known in Greece. The lack of enthusiasm for expert systems and artificial intelligence in both countries is noteworthy.

This ranking is comparable to other recent surveys, although each survey tends to name and delineate the issues in a slightly different way. In the UK, Silk $^{5,6}$ found that the top five issues concerning information systems managers were:

● the impact on the organization (including end-user aspects and training);

● the linking of IS strategy to business strategy;

● security of data;

\- IS management (including the management of IS development);

\- justification of IS investment.

In the most recent Price Waterhouse survey $^{7}$ , the top issue was ‘integrating IT with corporate methods’, followed by ‘meeting project deadlines’. Similar surveys have found that these issues also feature in the concerns of US IS managers $^{8}$ .

We grouped our twenty issues into the following five key areas of responsibility for the IS manager:

● strategic management;

\- people management;

● systems development;

● end-user computing;

● technology management.

The classification of the issues is shown in Table 2. Clearly, any classification of such complex, multifaceted issues is bound to be fairly arbitrary. Each issue represents a blend of human, social, organizational and technical aspects; for example, office automation could easily be placed within other groups but we considered its key impact is in terms of end-user computing.

Table 2 shows the rankings of the issues within each area, as perceived by the Greek managers in 1989, their expectations for 1992 and the UK ranking for 1987. The final column shows the direction (positive or negative), and the magnitude of the difference between the 1989 Greek rankings and those of the UK. A positive difference indicates that the issue was ranked higher in Greece; for example, 'identifying strategic opportunities' was ranked one place higher by the Greek managers, compared to their UK counterparts. The most striking observation is the broad similarity of the rankings. The average difference is 3.8 for negative differences and 3.9 for positive differences. Moreover, if we exclude the two largest differences '+16' and '-13', the average difference becomes 2.77 for negative differences and 2.55 for positive values.

As can be seen, strategic management issues dominate the rankings; they were the top three concerns in Greece in 1989 and in 1992, as well as in the UK in 1987. However, the third placed issue varies between the two countries. This result underlines the importance of strategic management issues. In Greece, in 1989, the top three issues were ‘active’ IS strategy issues: identifying the opportunities, aligning IS with business strategy, and determining the position of the IS function within the organization.

In Greece, people management issues were given fairly high priority at the time of the survey but had slipped badly for 1992. This perception that the people management issues of recruitment and training, and senior management education, would be overtaken by other concerns by 1992 is rather worrying in view of the importance of people in the success of IS developments $^{9}$ . Systems development issues seemed to receive a fairly medium priority, with quality assurance and productivity remaining fairly important in 1992. End-user computing was not seen as an important area in 1989, probably because of its limited impact in Greek organizations. However, one of the issues there, decision support systems, was expected to move up significantly, from twelfth to sixth position. This change, which does not match the views expressed in the UK, may be because such systems and their microcomputer platforms were relatively new in Greece. As such, they were enjoying considerable popularity amongst the pundits, eager to predict the 'technology of the future'.

Technology management shows a curious range of perceptions. At the time of the survey, the most important issue in this area was perceived to be data management; this slips in relative importance by 1992 to be eclipsed by telecommunications, which suddenly leaps from twelfth to third place, presumably based on the expectation that the elderly Greek national telecommunications network will eventually be modernized. However, security and integrity retains its importance. Coping with new technology is expected to become relatively less of a problem, and expert systems and artificial intelligence remain relatively irrelevant. We shall now consider each of these areas in more detail.

## Strategic management

The IS function was traditionally seen by top management as primarily a support activity with little impact on the nature or profitability of the main areas of the business. This view is now changing, helped by the widely publicized success stories of companies such as American Hospital Supply and Thomson Holidays, who incorporated IT as a key component of their product or marketing strategy to gain a competitive advantage over their rivals. Thus it has become common parlance to discuss the impact of IS on earnings, new products, new markets and acquisitions. IS can be used strategically to influence the organization's external environment, through product differentiation and other marketing strategies. In addition, IT can have a major impact on the internal workings of the organization, through improved effectiveness and efficiency, reducing costs and increasing product and service quality. Various theoretical models and frameworks have been used to analyse and explain these strategic aspects, including Porter's model of competitive forces $^{10}$ , the Value Chain $^{11}$ and the Customer Resource Life Cycle $^{12}$ . Such concerns characterize the later stages of Nolan's Stages of Growth model $^{13}$ . The notion of IS strategy is also concerned with the long-term planning of systems, but experience suggests that organizations have many problems with IS strategic planning and its linkages with corporate planning $^{14}$ .

All 40 Greek IS managers that we interviewed claimed to believe strongly in the strategic importance of IS for the organization. Many claimed that they were trying to establish this importance by aligning their departments' goals with the overall business goals. Their concern with strategic management is reflected by their ranking of strategic issues in the first three places of all the issues. Their prime current concern was identifying opportunities for the strategic use of information systems, followed by aligning IS strategy with business strategy. The first concern may be partly due to the widespread publicity currently given to this issue, plus the promotional efforts of a few Greek consulting organizations. The second concern is likely to be reinforced by well-intentioned IS managers eager to develop an IT strategy but alarmed at the general lack of business strategies with which to align it. The changing role of IS within organizations puts considerable strain on the existing organizational structure. This was recognized by the IS managers, who ranked location of IS function within the organization's structure in third place. The IS managers expected these three issues to retain their top three positions in 1992. Whilst it could be argued that these priorities and views are not deeply held but are only espoused ones, excessively influenced by current literature from more advanced countries, it is still significant that the Greek IS managers recognized, at such a high level, the relevance of these issues to their own situations.

The final strategic issue, IS planning by top management, was initially ranked much lower, at sixteenth position, although it rose in importance to eighth place, by 1992. As the IS budget swallows an ever greater share of the organization's investment budget, it becomes essential to ensure that resources are allocated in line with the needs of the business. This calls for the widespread involvement of senior management in IS planning. The relative lack of interest by our interviewees may be due either to a perception that their senior managers lack the necessary interest and expertise for such involvement or that the time is not right for such intervention into IS affairs. They may believe that in the near future such coordinated IS planning will take place and that top management will become sufficiently expert and concerned to play an important part in aligning IS and corporate strategy.

Strategic issues vary greatly between organizations, depending upon such factors as the industry sector, product range, organization size, and corporate objectives. Thus, while some organizations may find valuable strategic opportunities from IT, others may still profit from a low-key approach to IT. These differences are highlighted in the well-known Strategic Grid $^{15}$ , a framework that helps organizations assess the value of strategic IS. The grid (Table 3) consists of two dichotomies: first, the organization's strategic dependency on current computer-based systems and second, the expected impact of future applications currently being developed. The four quadrants (support, factory, turnaround and strategic) roughly characterize the position of an organization with regard to the strategic value of its IS.

This latter belief was more prevalent amongst service organizations, where eight of the 11 interviewed claimed to be in the strategic quadrant, compared to only 14 of the 29 industrial sector firms. However, these responses may be highly unreliable and may largely represent wishful thinking by managers of prestigious companies intent on preserving their company image. On the other hand, we should recall that these 40 IS managers are well respected. Therefore, it is necessary to examine further evidence, in terms of other answers given in the interviews, to validate these assertions.

Current expenditure: According to Nolan $^{13}$ , IT expenditure is an indicator of an organization's IT maturity. Table 4 compares the IT expenditure of the Greek organizations (from our study) with those in the UK $^{4}$ . Non-IS department expenditure largely represents end-user computing or other applications that are not charged to the IT department budget. For the Greek companies, this figure is either included in the other totals or is not included at all, perhaps where the IS manager is unaware of departments' IT activities.

Great care should be taken in drawing conclusions from this data because of the different characteristics of the companies, the small size of the Greek sample (only 19 answered this question either on grounds of confidentiality or the lack of available figures) and the comparability of the statistics. This includes the apparently high proportion of the expenditure by Greek companies on hardware and software, which should be treated with care as it incorporates the non-IS department expenditure. Thus the main difference is that the Greek companies surveyed spent relatively more on staff and less on technology (hardware, software and telecommunications). This is probably due to a number of factors, including a larger proportion of data entry staff and perhaps more in-house development. Nevertheless, it is clear that the Greek companies interviewed do currently invest heavily in IT and 95 per cent of the IS managers reported that IT expenditure had grown over the last five years.

Current applications: Another indicator of progress concerns the portfolio of current IT applications. We asked the IS managers to identify the applications that were currently important to their companies (Table 5). As can be seen, traditional applications such as general accountancy, sales and purchase orders, payroll and inventory, continue to dominate the portfolio, being used by more than 80 per cent of the companies. In the middle ranking, standard software packages (spread-sheets, databases and word processing) are used by more than 50 per cent of the companies. With a lower usage (less than one third), electronic mail, desktop publishing and bar codes have had less overall impact but their application is likely to increase over the next few years. This relatively low-level applications portfolio does not seem to match the claims for the strategic use of IT reported in Table 3 above. The inconsistency reflects an interesting gap between the enthusiasm and awareness of the IS managers and the systems that they actually deliver to their users.

In the UK, the application priorities were rather different: executive data support, office automation and the replacement of outdated systems (each with a score of more than 50 per cent), were followed by electronic data interchange and international communications (33 per cent) and, finally, by internal administration and accounting, electronic point of sales and CAD/CAM manufacturing (each at 25 per cent). Office automation incorporating word processing, spreadsheets, databases and desktop publishing, seems to be of less importance in Greece but its usage is growing. The priority given to executive support systems in the UK confirms the recognition of the importance of improving managerial effectiveness. Although there is currently little evidence of such systems in Greece, the growing interest in databases, fourth generation languages and decision support systems indicate that Greek companies are moving in a similar direction. The relatively new UK issue of the need to replace earlier systems that lack the facilities, flexibility, and relevance needed to meet current requirements, is one that is expected to emerge in Greek IS departments in the near future.

Organizational position of the IS manager: According to Nolan's model $^{13}$ , as organizations enter the later stages of evolution, the IS department should break away from its parent department (of finance or management services) to become an important department in its own right. Similarly, the IS manager is expected to move up the organizational hierarchy to occupy a senior management position with a title like Chief Information Officer (CIO) or IT Director at the vice-president or board level. In the USA and Western Europe, there is evidence of such changes taking place in organizations' structures, especially in information-intensive companies where IS is considered to be of central importance to the way that the business operates. Gradually the traditional arrangement of the IS manager reporting to the director of the parent department (typically the finance director) becomes increasingly unsatisfactory. Finance directors rarely have the expertise or the time required to deal with IS issues. Furthermore, as IS becomes more familiar and its application more widespread throughout the organization, any strong linkage between IS and just one user department (i.e. finance) is likely to become a source of tension. When the IS function is sufficiently well developed, there is a strong argument for giving the IS department the independence it needs to succeed across the whole organization.

In most of the companies we visited there was little evidence of the organizational changes needed to effect IS independence. In most cases, the IS department reported to the finance director. Whilst it is relatively easy for senior managers to claim that IS is/will be a strategic resource, the organizational changes needed to implement that claim may be very painful as other departments and managers may resist the encroachment of a technical upstart into their territory. However, we did encounter two organizations (groups of companies), where the IS departments had been hived off into independent companies, relatively free from the control of their users. These IS departments could even offer their services to competitors of the parent group. The major drawback of such an arrangement concerns the potentially conflicting business goals of the new IS company and its parent.

As the IS function evolves from a narrow, technically-based, subsidiary support operation to become a mainstream function, comparable to marketing or finance, the role of the senior IS manager is likely to change significantly. Rather than just organizing a few technical employees, the CIO will probably join the senior management team, taking part in long-term planning and strategic decision-making, and liaising at a high level with customers and user departments. The CIO should be constantly searching for opportunities for the IS function to contribute to the organization's long-term objectives, as well as efficiently administering a department that will be consuming an increasing share of the organization's precious resources. Thus, the CIO needs a balance of skills, including an excellent understanding of both the business and the technology, as well as negotiating and administrative ability, to become a hybrid manager. The concept of hybrid management is arguably more important to Greek companies because of their small size. The pressures on senior managers of small companies serve to create hybrid managers, and our interviews suggested that successful IT managers already display some of these hybrid skills.

Our study suggested that the average curriculum vitae of Greek IS managers is as follows. The managers had been in their current position for an average of five years (ranging from six months to 16 years) and had an average of 14.5 years' IT experience (ranging from five to 23 years). 64 per cent had been promoted from within the department, where they had worked for an average of nine years, leaving 36 per cent who had been brought in from outside the department. Almost half of the managers (45 per cent) had worked in other departments of the organization, mostly in finance.

In general, the IS managers had a fairly strong educational background but it was largely of a technical nature. Two-thirds of them possessed a university degree, with the most common degrees being in computer science (13 managers), mathematics and physics (six) and economics (six), while only two managers had management degrees, 90 per cent had attended some form of IS education or training course; of these 85 per cent were fairly satisfied with the courses attended. Only half of the managers had attended any management educational programme, although 70 per cent considered it important to do so. Thus there is some evidence that IS managers are less well educated in management practice than is required for their future hybrid role. However, this disadvantage may be balanced by their fairly lengthy experience of the day-to-day practice of hybrid management.

Sprague and McNurlin $^{16}$ propose a five-stage evolution for the role of the IS manager, characterized by the following realms within which they operate:

(i) the IS department;

(ii) interaction with other departments;

(iii) the enterprise;

(iv) interaction with customers and suppliers; and (v) the industry.

In the companies we visited we found that few IS managers had progressed further than the third stage, interacting within the enterprise, and many were still operating within the first two stages.

Earl $^{17}$ proposes a rather different stage model, based on the notions of three leadership stages. In the delivery stage, the IS manager is concerned with the efficient provision of services and concentrates upon the internal performance of the IS department. The second, reorientation, stage sees the manager attempting to steer the IS department towards competitive needs of the business. In this stage he/she spends more time outside the department than inside it. In the third, reorganization, phase the manager focuses on the interface between the IS department and the rest of the organization and can be expected to spend equal amounts of time inside and outside the department.

We asked IS executives where they currently spend their time and where they expected to be spending their time in three years' time: inside or outside the IS department. Table 6 shows our findings, together with the results from the UK $^{2}$ , and the data neatly fits Earl's model. The Greek managers seem to be moving from the delivery stage, where they spend more time inside the IS department, towards the existing UK position, where most time is spent outside the department (reorientation stage). On the other hand, the UK managers appear to be moving from the reorientation stage towards the reorganization stage, where they expect to spend equal amounts of time inside and outside the IS department.

These strategic management issues clearly impact on the preserve of organizations' senior management and so it is opportune to consider the views of the 15 senior managers who replied to our postal questionnaire. All of them claimed to believe in the potential of IS as a competitive force for their company, but only half acknowledged the problems identified by the IS managers. There was some evidence of disagreement or misunderstanding between the two parties regarding the IS role. Only 44 per cent of the IS managers agreed that senior management shared their views about the role of IS in the organization, whereas 60 per cent of the senior managers thought there was considerable agreement. Less than half (47 per cent) of the IS managers were satisfied with the IT knowledge available at board level, although 66 per cent were satisfied with the way that IT was handled at board level. 65 per cent of the IS managers claimed to participate in meetings with senior managers, although 80 per cent thought that they should participate in the future. Senior managers were much less convinced with only 5 per cent in agreement, 25 per cent against such participation and 70 per cent unsure. Thus, there is evidence of a gap between the aspirations of IS managers and the views of the organizations' senior management. This could be narrowed perhaps by the greater use of steering committees for both IT policy and individual projects. Such committees would provide a specific forum for interaction at an appropriate level between IS managers and senior and middle user management. There was some evidence that such committees already exist in a few Greek companies on an informal basis.

## People management

IS professionals have traditionally been largely enamoured with the technical aspects of computer systems; the human aspects have been of much less interest. It is encouraging that the Greek IS managers ranked people management issues so highly. Staff recruitment and training ranked fifth and has become particularly important in the current Greek labour market, where skilled and experienced IS staff are in short supply. The other people issue, the education of senior management, was ranked ninth. Educating senior executives in IT should be the first step towards a more enlightened resource allocation for IS, a more strategic view in planning these systems, and a broader perspective on the evaluation of their implications for the company. It should be a prerequisite for the senior management support and commitment that are widely considered to be crucial to successful implementation.

According to our interviewees, by 1992 these issues will be of relatively less importance: eighteenth and fourteenth places respectively. This relative decline is worrying as it is the people, both specialists and users at all levels within the organization, who are likely to be the key factor in gaining the maximum benefit from the technology. The managers' views might be explained by the enormous growth in IT education and training in Greece in the late 1980s. The number of universities offering IT degrees increased from one to five during the 1980s and there was a profusion of IT seminars offered by public and private organizations. For example, in 1989 the Greek Productivity Centre offered 204 such seminars, covering 25 637 seminar hours, to a total audience of 3 862 participants $^{18}$ . In 1990, the Hellenic Management Association offered 31 IT seminars, covering 728 seminar hours, to 435 participants. Unlike the position ten years ago, technical IT training is currently a flourishing business in Greece. In addition a number of consulting companies have recently introduced new schemes to help organizations recruit IT personnel.

Although the number of IT courses is growing, the staffing of IS departments with skilled and experienced people is unlikely to stabilize in the short-term. Similarly, the education of senior managers in a complex and changing subject area is not going to be a trivial exercise. Thus we find this shift in attention somewhat over-optimistic and rather disturbing. Other questions in the interviews revealed that 62.5 per cent of IS managers regarded recruitment and training as a significant problem, 27 per cent being particularly concerned over their ability to pay large enough salaries to attract skilled IS staff.

In terms of staff numbers, the companies we interviewed had an average of 17 IT staff per company, although there was some difference between the services sector with an average of 24 staff and the industrial sector with 15 people. On average, a little over 40 per cent of the staff had a graduate or postgraduate qualification. Skilled IT staff seemed to be relatively well paid with an average salary for a recently recruited systems developer (of about £8 300 per year) roughly 30 per cent higher than the average salary in Greece.

Although the IS department was generally responsible for development projects, outsourcing was increasing through the use of outside contractors (software houses) and the purchase of software packages. IT consultants are relatively little used in Greece and even then they are mostly consulted regarding individual developments (75 per cent) rather than on matters of IT strategy (25 per cent). The companies that do not use consultants claim that this is mainly because of their high costs and their lack of effectiveness; a belief that may owe much to the traditional inward-looking perspective of Greek IT departments and the fact that Greek companies are reluctant to pay for services or information, which they traditionally regard as a 'free' commodity. However, there is some evidence of this attitude changing through the Western attitudes of the subsidiaries of multinational companies and the new breed of Greek managers educated in foreign business schools, plus the efforts of Greek consulting companies. However, most consultancies still only offer software development and very few can provide a complete, integrated range of advice from strategy to development and implementation.

IT staff training mostly takes place through in-house seminars given by IT vendors and software suppliers. In a few cases, staff are sent to external seminars, and very occasionally senior staff are sent abroad. Table 7 shows the views of the IS managers interviewed, in terms of their ranking of the importance of the various educational programmes.

As can be seen, a one day briefing by an expert speaker is considered to be the most important educational programme, as it is an elite, peer group event of the right time length and with a business perspective. Seminars follow closely behind, because they are topic specific and allow the sharing of experiences. In third position, in-house specially commissioned courses are favoured (especially for the junior staff), because they focus on relevant issues and may use company and industry specific data. IT supplier presentations are not ranked very highly, presumably because of their commercial aspects, although these were the dominant programmes in the past.

Comparing these results with the UK $^{2}$ , the most significant difference concerns conferences, which were ranked first in the UK. Some interviewees mentioned that the recent increase in the number and range of educational programmes implied a need for an education and training specialist within the IT department to give advice on their relative merits.

## Systems development

For more than two decades, the discipline of information systems has been preoccupied by the problems of systems development. The backlog of software development projects continued to grow, as the IS department could not start development because its resources were bogged down with current projects running out of control and with the high maintenance demands of existing applications. Over the years, a procession of tools and techniques have been developed to improve the productivity and quality of IS development $^{19}$ . They mostly fitted into the traditional model of systems development, the systems life cycle. The growing number of disparate tools and techniques, plus the difficulties in controlling development projects, led to considerable interest in information systems development methodologies $^{20}$ , whereby the tools and techniques are linked together by a common philosophy and carefully ordered procedures.

The importance of IS development issues was recognized by our interviewees, who ranked them quite highly. The general problem of project management and implementation was ranked eleventh, slipping slightly to twelfth in 1992. The related issues of quality assurance and evaluation, and the difficulty of measuring development productivity were more highly ranked. The former was placed seventh in each year, while the latter followed at eighth, slipping to ninth in 1992. There was much less interest in fourth generation languages (ranked at nineteenth and moving up to seventeenth in 1992). 70 per cent of the managers agreed that meeting project deadlines was a significant problem and 35 per cent were concerned about their maintenance loads.

In terms of development practice in Greece, our interviews suggest that this lags somewhat behind Western Europe and the USA. Only 30 per cent of the companies used specific development methodologies; those mentioned by the managers included Data Analysis, Ca Promacs, Case Tools, System One, and Stradis. The remaining 70 per cent base development to a greater or lesser extent around the traditional life cycle with standard phases of analysis, design, implementation and maintenance. However, most of the IS managers agreed on the urgent need for a common development method, and showed interest in SSADM. These findings underline the major requirement for effective systems analysis in Greek IS departments. The previous lack of interest and lack of awareness by these departments has resulted in a reliance on ad hoc methods for developing systems. However, a shift towards better grounded and more systematic methodologies requires considerable investment in time, effort and expertise, if their introduction is to be successful.

In the context of the need to justify current and proposed IS developments, we asked the IS managers which methods they used to evaluate their IS (Table 8). They claimed that the very vague 'business improvement' was the major method, although there is no effective way of determining causal links between the two. An improvement in sales or profitability may be due to any number of factors (e.g. changes in fashion, trade tariffs, the national economy) operating in the complex business environment. More specific methods included simple comparisons between annual costs and output (throughput) over a number of years. Again, changes in these statistics could be caused by various factors. The other methods mentioned comprised comparing costs against those of other companies, attempting to calculate savings achieved, and special (ad hoc) investigations of particular systems. These are all as fundamentally subjective as the IS manager's opinion. From the interviews, it was clear that in some companies there was strong pressure from senior management for more and better evaluation of IS. However, only 15 per cent of the managers rated evaluation as a major problem.

## End-user computing

One of the most significant recent trends is the explosive growth of end-user computing $^{21}$ , where users purchase, develop or operate systems relatively independently of the IS department. In Western Europe and the USA, expenditure is shifting from central mainframes located within the IS department to workstations within user departments. In many organizations, end-user computing already dominates IT expenditure. The reasons for this shift lie in the increasing knowledge and confidence of users, the increasing user-friendliness of the technology, and, perhaps more significantly, the experience of years of frustration with an apparently slow, ineffective IS department. The most popular approach to the support of end-user computing is through the introduction of specialized information centres. Staffed by selected IS staff, such centres become a shop window for standard items (hardware and software that the organization supports) and a clearing house for user development projects. End-user computing implies a significant role change for much of the IS department and its staff, as they shift from ‘manufacturing’ to ‘support’ functions.

The management of end-user computing and the development of office automation attracted less concern from the IS managers. Both issues remained rooted in the lower quartile of the rankings and this may reflect two highly doubtful assumptions. The first, suggesting that end-user computing will not require much management, may be misconceived for the reasons given above. The second is more debatable. A relative lack of concern with office automation may indicate a belief that few problems remain rather than that few systems will be installed. Certainly, few technical problems remain with first-generation office automation, which largely comprises word-processing with limited electronic mail facilities, although even these systems may face organizational and human difficulties if the users concerned are unhappy with the implementation. Reaching beyond the first-generation systems towards computer supported cooperative work $^{22}$ , the problems become much more complex and deserve much more concern than was evident amongst the Greek IS managers.

This probably again shows the 'excellence' of the companies surveyed and that they are true IT leaders in Greece.

One IS manager told us that duplication of effort, redundancy of data and also lack of security resulted from this approach. ## Technology management

The issues included here deal with the management of essential IT and although some of the issues are considered to be very important, the IS managers seemed to feel that they could cope quite successfully in general. The general issue of coping with new technology, ranked fourteenth, seems to be generally perceived as of low and declining importance, due to the expertise that the departments have accumulated from their large investments in technology. However, management should be aware there is a difference between managing isolated systems and the provision of an organization's communications and information infrastructure.

As IS penetrate all parts of the organization and become interconnected, data management and control become increasingly important. Data needs to be available to those that need it, without excessive duplication of data entry and storage. This concern ranked fourth, declining to tenth position in 1992, suggesting that managers believe that the main principles can be determined in the short term. This may be rather optimistic as, apart from the technical aspects, the control of data often gives departments considerable status and political power. When this ownership is threatened, organizational conflict may result. The use of external data, from online databases and other information providers, is seen to be increasing in importance, although still not becoming a serious issue. The security and integrity of both systems and data is realistically seen as continuing to be a major problem. There have been numerous well-publicized instances of computer viruses, fraud and unauthorized access, and managers need to remain vigilant. This is especially the case as the systems become more central to the business of organizations but, at the same time, they become more vulnerable through extensive networking $^{23, 24}$ .

Telecommunications and network management were perceived by the IS managers as becoming one of the top three issues by 1992. 60 per cent of those interviewed considered it to be a significant problem. Certainly, networking will be the basis of inter-organizational systems, such as electronic data interchange, as well as providing the backbone for local and long-distance internal systems. Network management includes the strategic issues of network planning and development, as well as the more short-term aspects of keeping existing networks operational in the light of increasing traffic loads. Because of the amount of investment involved, plus the consequences of being unable to meet the demand, these issues require very careful planning and control $^{25}$ . Thus it is realistic for the Greek managers to be apprehensive about this complex management problem, especially given the current problems with the Greek national telecommunications system. Currently, Greece has one of the oldest national networks in Europe, telecommunications services remaining a monopoly of OTE, the Greek Telecommunications Organization. Most commentators and users agree that the network is inadequate; there is a backlog of more than one million applications for telephones, it is very difficult to obtain telex and fax connections, and the general quality of the services is poor. Leased analogue telephone lines provide the current data communications network.

An important step towards modernization $^{18}$ was the introduction in 1990 of the HELLASPAC public packet-switched data network. This advanced network was aimed specifically at data traffic and offers facilities comparable to similar networks in Western Europe. Currently the network can support 2 500 subscribers (through 900 gateways) but, by the end of 1991, this should have been extended to 1 700 permanently connect users plus 4 000 users from the telephone network (over 2 050 gateways). Plans exist to introduce videotext, electronic mail and electronic data interchange services over the network. Two further targets comprise HELLASCOM, a high speed digital network of leased lines, and HELLASTEL, a value-added videotext network.

The application of artificial intelligence and expert systems techniques received a vote of no confidence by our managers, being ranked last both currently and for 1992. Only five per cent of the companies we visited mentioned that their department is involved in expert systems development. It could be argued that this was due to a lack of awareness $^{26}$ , although certain highly respected US commentators similarly believe that such systems will have little general impact on organizations, being largely confined to specialized applications $^{27}$ .

## Conclusion

The response by the Greek IS managers in our interviews was very helpful in producing a picture of their concerns. These concerns fell into the following five areas of responsibility (in order of perceived importance): strategic management, people management, systems development, end-user computing, and technology management.

Their responses (in 1989) were very similar to those in the UK (in 1987), suggesting that Greece was approximately two years behind the UK. The main differences were in certain key aspects of strategic management. The Greek managers were much more concerned about the location of the IS function within the organization, apparently still awaiting organizational restructuring to reflect the growing importance of IT. The UK companies seemed to have overcome these problems and moved on to worry much more about the contribution of senior management to IS planning - a concern that the Greek managers will probably encounter in a few years' time.

The dominant area that worries most IS executives is strategic management. They claim to be most concerned with trying to identify opportunities for the strategic use of IS in the business, and the alignment of IS strategy with the organization's business strategy. These concerns and the managers' views of the position of their companies within the strategic grid suggest a certain over-optimism and ambition that do not match the reality of the IS departments' applications portfolio, the organizational position of the IS manager nor the development methods used $^{28}$ . However, all such opinions are very relative, depending on how, and against which criteria, comparisons are made. In the context of Greek information systems, the views of the managers interviewed suggest an awareness and confidence that should prove beneficial to the future development of their organizations' systems.

However, there are a number of concerns clouding the horizon. First, the expectations that people issues will become relatively less important in 1992 seems misguided. Second, there seems to be a rather worrying gap between the views of the IS managers and their senior management. There appears to be a lack of mutual understanding between the two sides regarding the appreciation of IT problems, the involvement of senior managers in IS planning and the involvement of IS managers in strategic planning. Third, there was evidence of the traditional Greek free-wheeling approach to management, characterized by small family businesses. This was seen in the aspirations of managers running well ahead of the actual situation, the lack of systematic systems development methods, the optimism surrounding evaluation and the lack of concern with end-user computing. However, if the investment, enthusiasm and optimism regarding the technology can be translated into a committed, yet critical approach, Greek businesses should enter the twenty-first century with a much more competitive position.
