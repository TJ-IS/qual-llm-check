# The process of strategic information planning

Albert L. Lederer and Veronica Gardiner\*

School of Business Administration, Oakland University, Rochester, MI 48309-4401, USA

Strategic information planning is the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications. It is a critical undertaking for both information managers and other managers. Organizations typically carry out a multi-step process to create a strategic information plan. Method/1 is an example of such a process. A description of it illustrates the activities and challenges experienced by organizations performing strategic information planning.

Keywords: information systems management, information systems planning, strategic planning

For years, improved strategic information planning has been one of the most serious challenges facing information systems managers $^{1,2}$ . In today's world of increasing globalization, escalating competition, rapid technological change, proliferating computers, and burgeoning user demand, strategic information planning promises not only to remain such a challenge but to become even more perplexing $^{3}$ .

Although arduous, strategic information planning gives information managers the opportunity to identify broad initiatives, specific applications, and critical technologies to help their organizations carry out their current business strategy more successfully $^{4}$ . It also offers them the means to identify opportunities for using information systems to create new business strategies $^{5-9}$ . It gives them the chance to visualize the potential contribution of information technology (IT) to their organization.

Strategic information planning can help elicit top management's objectives, a critical information management need often not meet $^{10}$ . It can help convince top management of the potential strategic impact of information and thus increase the MIS department's visibility and funding $^{11}$ . It can reveal new, high impact applications $^{12}$ ; it can help better predict hardware, software, and personnel requirements and it can pinpoint ways to improve the department $^{13}$ . It can enable a firm to develop an organization-wide data architecture $^{14, 15}$ .

Finally, perhaps the most important point, the failure to prepare and effectively follow a strategic information plan can result in disjointed and redundant databases, incompatible hardware, duplicated software, squandered resources, and forfeited opportunities.

## Definition and theoretical basis

By definition, strategic information planning is 'the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and realizing its business goals' (Reference 16, p 446). The portfolio might include a wide variety of applications. Examples include accounting, human resource, manufacturing, research and development, and marketing systems as well as electronic mail systems, expert systems, desktop publishing, computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing systems, and many others.

To develop a portfolio, an organization must accomplish a panoply of related tasks. It first forms a project team of business and systems managers with a team leader. The team then carries out an extended study during which it defines new applications, specifies databases, describes a network of hardware and software, estimates resource requirements, sets priorities, and develops a migration plan.

Many methodologies are available for performing strategic information planning. Examples include business systems planning $^{17,18}$ , information engineering $^{19}$ , Method/1 $^{20,21}$ , PROplanner $^{22}$ , information quality analysis $^{23}$ , business information analysis and integration technique $^{24}$ , business information characterization study $^{25}$ , and critical success factors $^{26}$ . Although organizations occasionally develop their own methodology internally, more often they select and customize an existing one. Frequently they carry out their planning study with consultants from the vendor of the methodology.

Theoretically, strategic information planning can be seen within the framework of an input-process-output function $^{27}$ . Figure 1 shows this and extends it to include feedback loops. Input includes the organization's current business plans, IT, structure, and technology trends. Output is the strategic information plan of proposed applications. The Process is the strategic planning methodology that uses the Input to create the Output. Implicit in strategic information planning is the anticipated favourable impact of the Output on the success of the Organization. Moreover, as an ongoing process, strategic information planning evaluates the success of previous plans as Input to future planning activities, hence the feedback loops in Figure 1.

A better understanding of strategic information planning would be valuable to information managers who have not yet carried it out. One way to better understand it would be to examine it within the context of a specific methodology. This would enable organizations who had used a different methodology to contrast it to the one described here (although most strategic information planning methodologies address fairly similar activities). It would also be helpful because detailed information about a particular methodology is generally proprietary, and thus often difficult to obtain without acquiring the methodology or contracting for consulting services.

This paper describes the activities an organization would follow if it chose Method/1 information planning methodology. The material was gathered from interviews with Method/1 consultants and users, interviews with users of other strategic information planning methodologies, and two books detailing the application of Method/1 $^{20, 21}$ .

## Methodology

The objectives of Method/1 strategic information methodology are:

\- to identify the organization's information needs;

● to find new opportunities for using information to achieve a competitive advantage;

\- to define an overall IT strategy for satisfying the organization's IT objectives:

\- to define data, application, technology, and organizational requirements for supporting the overall IT strategy; and

\- to define the activities needed to meet the data, application, technology and organizational requirements, and thus implement the overall IT strategy.

According to Method/1 documentation, by treating the strategic information planning process as any other corporate strategic planning, its effectiveness is enhanced. This means it should cover the same planning horizon (typically three to five years) and management should update and revise it along with other corporate strategic plans.

To achieve its objectives, the methodology uses a layered approach. The top layer is the methodology itself. A middle layer of techniques supports the methodology and a bottom layer of tools supports the techniques. Examples of the many techniques are dataflow diagramming, matrix analysis, functional decomposition, focus groups, and Delphi studies. FOUNDATION, Andersen Consulting's CASE tool set, includes computer programs that support the planning process.

One rationale for this layered approach is that it can permit different tools and techniques to serve different organizations in their own unique situations. Also, the approach can isolate less frequently changing tasks at the methodology level. Thus, new tools and techniques may become available while the top layer, the methodology itself, evolves more slowly in response to management's critical, new questions.

The methodology consists of ten activities (referred to as work segments) that an organization completes as it develops its strategic information plan. Table 1 identifies each work segment. The first five formulate information strategy. The final five further formulate the information strategy but also develop action plans. A break between the first and final five provides a top management checkpoint and an opportunity to adjust and revise.

Each work segment is an entity with its own objectives, inputs, and outputs and each has its own set of guidelines for carrying out its tasks. These are described below. Various segments require specific skills as shown in Table 2; in fact, according to some, the skills and experience of the team members may be more important than the methodology itself. ## 1. Scope definition and organization

Top management's expectations from a strategic information planning study may be great and the opportunity to disappoint them may be even greater. To attempt to promote realistic expectations, the main activities of the first work segment are:

● to determine key planning issues;

● to define the project scope and approach;

● to organize the team; and

● to obtain commitment from top management.

Every organization is unique. Thus the team customizes the planning process to the unique needs of the organization. It does this by deriving its goals for the process from its understanding of the organization and from management's goals for the process.

The team emphasizes factors that will produce a successful planning project. These factors determine the particular work segments needing to be performed and the specific areas of the business to study .A study with an excessively wide scope might stall, while one with an excessively narrow scope might miss important opportunities. If the scope is too wide, management commitment may be diluted; thus the importance of building management commitment may be increased. In one case, a multi-location corporation, developing its first corporate-wide information strategy, encountered numerous delays due to difficulties in gaining commitment from dispersed management.

The keys to setting the project scope are to quickly determine the overall approach, the extent that each segment is complete at the beginning of the study, and the importance of each segment for the particular organization. Then based on these determinations, the team decides the remaining activities. Based on the importance of each area of the organization to the business and on the anticipated, potential impact of IT on the area, the team decides the specific areas to study.

In addition to the proper scope, skilled and capable team members are essential in order to complete the project successfully. The needs of the specific client must be defined so that the team leader can select those members with the skills and experience that fit these needs. The team leader also attempts to anticipate the key areas in the organization that will be affected, and selects members from them in order to obtain their interest and commitment. This may even result in the attendance at key meetings by a large number of part-time members whose commitment may be seen as more valuable than their skills and experience.

The team leader identifies a pool of potential team members and discusses them with the sponsor of the project. The sponsor then approaches management to see if these individuals can be made available for the planning study. (Interestingly, a test of the importance of the project is often the willingness or reluctance of management to permit their participation. Management's reluctance may indicate to the team leader that it sees the project as unimportant.) After their selection, team leaders orient them to the scope, goals, and methods of the project.

Team leaders establish project guidelines, intermediate checkpoints, and other controls for the team. The team uses work plans and organization charts to reflect these controls and to attempt to bring the planning project in on time and within budget.

The project team begins its efforts to gain the commitment of senior management in this work segment. Senior management is continually involved throughout the project, in order to prevent any possible resistance, obtain its confidence, keep it interested in the study, and thus increase its support for the study's completion and the implementation of its recommendations. This also serves to demonstrate senior management support to subordinates, so as to maintain their interest in the study. Thus the team briefs key management personnel with the project's scope, objectives, and approaches during this work segment.

Continual briefings throughout the planning process enable the team to present critical findings to management. This allows management to provide essential feedback and guidance. For example, in one case, a financial services firm sought an accelerated upgrade to its financial reporting systems. The team briefed top management weekly. As a result of the briefings, top management remained interested in the progress of the planning study and aware of its critical findings. Thus key issues were promptly resolved and the plan was developed quickly. More importantly, top management understood the plan and funded its implementation.

## 2. Business and competitive assessment

Although a strategic information plan deals extensively with technology, perhaps more importantly it should embody the organization's current and future business environments $^{28}$ . The key activities to make it embody these environments are:

● to understand the business strategy and competitive environment;

\- to identify the implications of the environment;
- to define strategic information needs.

The team begins by reviewing the organization's strategic business plan to help it understand the firm's direction. This can be especially challenging in many organizations where top management does little strategic business planning, or where it prefers to keep its business plans confidential. It is all the more challenging because strategic information planning is virtually impossible without knowledge of the strategic business plan.

If business plans exist in the minds of top executives but not in writing, then team members talk with them to draw out key points. This is not unusual. However, if business plans exist neither in writing nor in the minds of top executives, then an effort to define key business planning issues is recommended to be made in advance.

The team also examines the organization's products or services, operations, structure, and functional responsibilities. It analyses the activities that bring a product or service to the customer. It focuses on important areas within the organization and on how IT can add value, reduce costs, and create an advantage.

Simultaneously, it studies the external environment of the organization to ascertain its competitive strengths and weaknesses. The team intends that the strategic information plan will capitalize on these strengths and shore up the weaknesses. For example, a pharmaceutical company considered its sales force to be a major competitive strength in the marketplace. A principal conclusion from its planning process was the need for significant investment to improve the force's effectiveness and efficiency. As a direct result, the company provided sales representatives with personal computers linked to central corporate systems to enable them to retrieve product and customer information before, during, and after sales calls.

To understand the environment, the team evaluates the effects of these five forces on the organization $^{29}$ :

● the relative power of major buyers;

● the relative power of major suppliers;

● the threat of substitute products or services;

● the threat of new entrants into the industry; and

● the current basis of competition within the industry.

The team also examines technological, environmental, and industry trends that may affect the firm.

Based on its understanding of the organization's business strategy, strengths, and weaknesses, the team then attempts to identify opportunities for using information to help it gain an advantage over its competitors or avoid a competitive disadvantage. Because an organization can gain this advantage by altering industry structure, improving present business, or creating new business opportunities, the strategic information planning process will include a search for the means of using information to accomplish these.

Also, the team can use these identified opportunities to determine previously unrecognized strategic information needs. Thus this work segment begins the vital task of linking the information plan to the business strategy. (The team carries out this business and competitive assessment work segment concurrently with the next two, the present status assessment and the IT opportunities, to permit the sharing of their findings.)

## 3. Present status assessment

Strategic information planning not only considers the external business environment but also evaluates the organization's internal environment. This internal environment assessment is done to ascertain the organization's current status before seeking directions in which to guide it. The key activities for accomplishing this are:

● to document existing information and systems;

● to assess the effectiveness of the MIS department;

● to review functional operations;

● to assess current operations, technology, and capacity; and

● to evaluate the IT position of the competition.

The team first analyses existing and planned systems. It assesses how well these systems fit the organization's needs and how effectively the organization uses its information resources.

Next, the team focuses on the MIS department. It analyses the quality of its service, the cost of the service, its staffing level, the skills of its members, its management practices, policies, procedures, and ability to support existing and potential information systems.

The team also focuses on the functional departments' business processes and information deficiencies. It evaluates how well current information systems meet users' needs and tries to identify ways to significantly restructure and improve business processes. The team assesses users' understanding of their current capabilities not only to reveal potential applications but also to identify existing capabilities with which the users are unfamiliar.

The team investigates the organization's current hardware, software, operations, and capacity. It assesses the organization's ability to support current information systems needs. Recommended changes may affect these areas.

In the final activity in this work segment, the team examines industry trends in technological applications and contrasts them with the organization's existing IT. The team compares the organization's sophistication to that of its competitors to get an accurate view of its current condition.

As an example, the team at a utility contrasted its own MIS department to departments of its competitors, and discovered that its department lagged significantly behind. Hence, it was able to argue successfully for new systems and technology for upgrading the department.

## 4. IT opportunities

One important objective of strategic information planning is to capitalize on the rapid changes in IT that continually open new opportunities. The key activities to identify such opportunities are:

● to analyse IT trends;

● to determine information needs;

● to define major IT objectives; and

● to identify opportunities for improvement.

MIS department team members begin the search for opportunities by investigating trends in technology. They study the use of technology within the organization's own industry and in related industries. For example, an insurance company examined the use of IT not only in the insurance field but also throughout the broader financial services industry.

Simultaneously, functional area team members investigate applications trends. They also research the use of technology in those applications.

The team then combines these two trend analyses with findings from the two previous work segments to determine each functional area's information needs and those of any cross-function business processes. Having established these needs, the team summarizes overall organization needs based on overall organizational objectives, processes, and functions.

Finally, the team targets areas where the organization might best use information systems. The business and competitive assessment (work segment 2 of Table 1), present status assessment (work segment 3) and IT opportunities (work segment 4) help the team assess how critical each area is to the organization and how well it currently uses IT. Critical areas currently not using IT well are targeted most heavily. Unimportant areas are targeted least heavily.

The team prioritizes the areas and takes special note of short-term opportunities which should be pursued immediately or at the end of the next work segment. Initiatives with the greatest opportunities to affect the organization favourably receive the highest priority. Both quantifiable benefits (such as cost reductions) and non-quantifiable benefits (such as the support of business strategies) play a role. Top management assesses the relative importance of quantifiable and non-quantifiable benefits based on its customary business values.

## 5. IT strategies

Strategic information planning seeks to identify specific IT strategies to help the organization to use existing technologies more effectively and take advantage of new technological opportunities. Activities for building these strategies are:

● to develop high level information strategies;

● to define the architecture of required systems;

● to identify high priority projects; and

● to confirm direction with management.

To develop IT strategies, the team first considers crucial planning issues and options in light of the objectives raised in the previous work segment. Possible examples include executive information systems, decision support systems, potential mergers and acquisitions, distributed and cooperative processing, and security and control. Some offer great opportunities while ignoring others may portend disaster.

The team prepares broad, top management-level IT strategies dealing with these issues and options. Examples of such strategies might include the exclusive use of custom development or of application packages. Other strategies might include the efforts to be a technology leader or a quick follower or simply to lag behind other organizations. Some firms may choose a strategy of adhering to conventional industry IT standards while other firms may not.

Next, the team develops the conceptual design of an architecture of information systems, data, technology, procedures, policies, and personnel to execute the strategy. This effort represents a first stab at envisioning the organization's future information environment, and thus represents a major accomplishment.

To develop the conceptual design, the team begins with its definition of objectives, overall strategy, and particular policies. Existing well-developed policies in an organization will pre-determine many aspects of an architecture. Examples of such policies include procedures for the handling of modifications to application packages or for the granting of deviations from industry standards. Where such policies do not exist, models and guidelines are useful; for example, the seven layer Open System Interconnection (OSI) Reference Model offers recommendations about data communications interfaces. For a small, simple organization, the development of the architecture may be easy; for a large, complex multi-national with various headquarters around the world, each with its own unique needs, the process can be extremely complex.

Conceptual design uses such popular techniques as matrix analysis, Porter's five force analysis, functional decomposition, dataflow diagrams, organization design frameworks, business process flow diagrams, etc. The decision to use each depends on the work segment and the specific needs of the organization. Moreover, this initial design will be extended and elaborated upon later, probably using many of the same tools.

The team assesses the required changes in management policies, personnel, information requirements, hardware, software, and competitive position that it expects the proposed information architecture to bring about. It also assesses the architecture's economic impact so that it can rank the individual projects within the architecture.

The team presents its strategies to management for approval. This major checkpoint allows the team to learn management's impressions of the planning project so far, and thus set the direction for the next three work segments. High-priority projects can begin now, provided they are independent of other projects. For example, a bank began a project at this time because the introduction of a new product clearly required it.

## 6. Organization plan

Unfortunately, many organizational problems can thwart the adoption of new technology. An organization plan deals primarily with the assignment of personnel to tasks and is intended to avoid such problems. Key activities for creating such a plan are:

● to develop a change management approach;

● to develop a human resources plan.

In developing the organization plan, the team focuses not only on the issues that can prevent adoption but also on those that can facilitate it. It considers these issues both within the MIS department and throughout the rest of the organization. It first identifies the MIS department's and key functional areas' management practices necessary to expedite adoption. The MIS department's practices include the planning and control of information systems. Functional area practices include the roles and responsibilities of individual departments and their personnel plus the hiring and training of new personnel.

The team determines differences between the current and proposed organization because they can form the basis for resistance. It develops an approach for implementing organizational change and for avoiding or dampening potential resistance to the necessary changes. The term ‘change management’ describes these activities.

During strategic information planning, the key to change management is that the team understand the changes in the business necessary to generate the plan's benefits. The team asks managers and others specific questions to assess the organization's readiness to change current procedures, and to find potential barriers to change.

Participation, communication, and education are assumed to be the keys to a successful change management program. The team lays the groundwork for explaining expected changes to workers, how the changes will be carried out, and why workers will like them. The lack of participation, communication, and education are seen as the keys to the failure of a change management program.

As an example, with a large consumer products manufacturer, the team focused not only on technological concerns but also on changes in business processes and related organizational change. For high-priority systems, the team interviewed workers to assess their readiness for the organizational changes to be effected by the new technology. This enabled the team to identify potential resisters and explain to them why the new technology would help them.

Next, the team compares the skills needed by the change management approach to those in the present status assessment (work segment 3), in order to determine the need for new skills. The team develops a broad human resources plan, defining the new roles and responsibilities based on these skills. In one case, a financial services company found that the skills of its MIS professionals had become outdated because they had spent virtually all their time maintaining existing systems and no time on projects that would have kept them current with technological advances. Thus the team was able to recognize the need for new skills before new technologies could be implemented.

Hence, the strategic information planning study often finds that MIS professionals and others require significant education and training to upgrade their skills. Moreover, new skills may demand new compensation planning, performance appraisals, and the recruiting of new employees. (The team carries out this work segment concurrently with the next two, i.e. the data and application plan and the technology plan, to permit the sharing of their findings.)

## 7. Data and application plan

The team converts the IT strategies (work segment 5) into more specific plans for data and applications. Key activities for accomplishing this are:

● defining data and applications;

● defining development and maintenance approaches; and

● developing a data and applications plan.

The team designs initial data entities and high-level specifications for applications. It also further defines the future business processes and identifies requirements for data management, security, and training. The team specifies actions needed to implement the proposed architecture.

The team also evaluates tools to employ during project development and maintenance to improve their efficiency and effectiveness. Examples include application packages, CASE techniques, prototyping, and end-user computing. In addition, the team identifies activities required to put the tools and techniques into practice.

Finally, the team carefully assesses the costs, benefits, and risks of the various IT strategies. For evaluating capital projects, each organization typically has its own rules (such as simple payback, internal rate of return, etc.). The team uses those techniques to remain consistent with the organization's practice.

The team identifies the resources and actions needed to put the applications development and maintenance tools into practice. It prioritizes the actions. It then assembles a data and application plan and presents it to top management for approval.

## 8. Technology plan

To support the data and application plan (work segment 7), strategic information planning produces a technical architecture of specific computer and communications hardware along with supporting database and systems software, and a plan for implementing it. The activities of this work segment are:

● to develop a technical architecture; and

● to develop a technology plan.

The team first identifies the hardware and software required to support the data and application plan. It then defines an overall technical architecture to meet the requirements while being flexible enough to accommodate future requirements and changes. It may also adjust the data and application plan if the anticipated expenditures are excessive.

The team summarizes the technical architecture to help it prepare a plan for maintaining the current hardware and software and for migration to the new technical architecture. Because the architecture may necessitate new skills, the team identifies their requirements and considers them as it develops training plans. Likewise, if the new architecture demands new security features, the team develops security plans. Finally, the technology plan contains new policies and actions required for its implementation.

As an example of a successful technology plan, the team at an investment bank effectively linked business strategies, IT opportunities, and proposed technology architecture. As a result, the planning study was more plausible and the team found it much easier to convince top management to fund the technology plan than did the team at another organization that tried to justify new telecommunications and database management systems on a stand-alone basis.

## 9. Information action plan

Strategic information planning requires a means for guiding the organization toward implementing its organization plan, data and application plan, and technology plan. Activities of developing a plan to accomplish this are:

\- by developing a migration plan;

\- by preparing an information action plan; and - by approving and initiating the information action plan.

The team first develops alternative migration plans. Each migration plan includes an overall approach, key projects, and their order of implementation. The priorities set in the data and application plan (work segment 6) determine the order. The team selects one migration plan based on its costs, benefits, and risks.

As an example, one team devised a migration plan for developing a new executive information system (EIS) whose key projects are built on existing transaction processing systems despite these being deficient. Another migration plan, which was rejected, would have upgraded the transaction processing systems prior to developing the EIS. The selected plan succeeded because it facilitated the rapid development of the EIS with an implementation sequence featuring the early delivery of benefits to top management.

Next the team incorporates the results of the prior work segments into a comprehensive information action plan. This document is the final output of the formal, strategic planning activity and it includes:

\- a summary of the organization's IT strategy;

● a summary of actions in the data and application, organization, and technology plans;

● the migration plan;

● the costs and benefits in light of project relationships;

\- a description of the process for annually updating the action plan;

\- an appendix of reference materials.

The team reviews the information action plan and presents it to management for approval.

## 10. Project definition and planning

With the approval of the information action plan, the organization can start implementing new projects. This work segment is a model for beginning the implementation process with an individual project. It illustrates the connection between the overall strategic information planning activities and the implementation of specific projects. For small, simple projects, this work segment is quite brief. For large, complex projects, it is prolonged. The key activities here are:

● the initiation of the project definition;

● the definition of requirements and development of a conceptual design; and

● the obtaining of management approval.

The team defines the results that the project will produce. It prepares a plan for carrying out the project and defines a new team for doing so. It establishes controls for ensuring that the new team carries out its tasks in a timely and standard fashion.

The team then identifies more detailed project requirements and develops an initial, conceptual system design. Additionally, it may review related existing systems, available packages, and development alternatives (such as CASE techniques or prototyping). Finally, the team presents the project definition, requirements, and conceptual design to management for its approval and the authorization to complete the development and implementation of the project.

## Dealing with potential problems

Strategic information planning is not free of problems. Sometimes strategic information plans are not implemented. Also, excessive and unnecessary planning may be done in unimportant areas. Sometimes insufficient or no planning is done in important areas, and this goes unrecognized until late in the study.

Experienced strategic information planners typically take actions to prevent these problems or, after they have occurred, recover from them. For example, the team attempts to prevent the problem of failure to implement the plan (which unfortunately is evident only after the completion of the plan) by setting clear objectives at the study's outset and by obtaining top management involvement, in order to increase the chances that it will agree with the final conclusions of the planning study.

To prevent excessive planning in unimportant areas and insufficient or no planning in key areas, the team leader seeks team members with the appropriate experience and skills throughout the study. To recover from excessive planning in unimportant areas, the team first uses periodic checkpoints with top management and others in the organization to recognize that excessive planning is being done. Then, planning is halted in the unimportant areas. To recover from missing key areas, the team typically seeks a change in the scope of the planning study. However, if the change in scope increases the cost of the planning project, top management approval may be necessary.

## Conclusion

Strategic information planning is a critical issue for today's systems managers. The description above of Method/1 depicts the activities of an organization as it prepares its strategic information plan. It also illustrates many of the challenges that it faces.

The description portrays strategic information planning as an extensive and meticulous process. If the process were merely to identify high impact applications, then a simpler one would suffice. However, the complexity of the integration of business processes and IT is probably the reason that strategic information planning is much more comprehensive. It is also perhaps the reason that strategic information planning may be more appropriate in large organizations concerned about their competitors (such as banks, manufacturers, and insurance companies) or interested merely in cost controls (such as government agencies who also increasingly use these methodologies).

The description also depicts top management as playing a critical role in the process. Not only is top management an active participant in some of the work segments, it also acts almost continuously as approver of the process. This is done to increase the chances of successful implementation of the plan, and suggests that implementation may be valued more than the planning process itself $^{30}$ .

Thus, the description demonstrates how strategic information planning can help an organization identify its information needs and find new opportunities for using information to achieve a competitive advantage. It also shows how strategic information planning can help define an overall IT strategy along with data, application, technology, and organizational requirements for supporting that strategy.

Moreover, it displays how strategic information planning can help define the activities needed to meet those requirements and thus implement the strategy. As a result, organizations successful at strategic information planning, such as the pharmaceutical company, utility, insurance company, consumer products manufacturer, and investment bank described above, may make better use of their databases, hardware, and software.

Nevertheless, strategic information planners sometimes say that they have not sufficiently achieved those objectives. When doing so, they most often complain that their organization failed to implement the results of the planning study to the extent they had expected $^{16}$ . Their complaints suggest that when this has happened, they should diligently assess the reasons why. Such an assessment may help them improve the planning process in the future cycles designated by the feedback loops in Figure 1.

Even when the plan is not fully implemented, planners have still educated top and user management about the current role of IT in their organization and about its potential to do more. Communication with management may be improved and it may be easier to obtain its support for new projects. Hence, even a planning study without full implementation can have substantial benefits. Thus it is not surprising that organizations in increasing numbers are performing strategic information planning.
