# Taking technology opportunities to the business

David J. Grimshaw\* and Mervyn A. Hinton

Advanced Technology Department, Woolwich Building Society, Administration Centre, 30 Erith Road, Bexleyheath, Kent DA7 6BP, UK

This case study examines a financial institution in which a technological opportunity and its potential benefits are identified by technology planners. The particular technology in this instance is the Geographical Information System (GIS). The use of GIS in the financial services sector is growing rapidly. Publication of case studies, other than those related to public utilities and the public sector generally, has been, to date, descriptive and sketchy. This case study takes a critical look at the process of identifying the technology and the business requirement, piloting GIS and the benefits gained. The main message of this case study is that new technologies (like GIS) may be identified as an opportunity by the technologist before being recognized as such by business executives.

This case study relates to the Woolwich Building Society, and is set against the background of the UK building society sector $^{†}$ .

## What are geographical information systems?

The idea of using locational analysis, for example, based on gravity models $^{1}$ , was first used by building societies during the 1960s and 1970s for identifying new branch sites. These models, which relied upon the use of census data, produced little mapping output.

People employed in the environmental and public service sectors might have an understanding of the term 'geography' and geographical data, but to most business people, the term 'geographical information system' has little meaning $^{3}$ . Certainly, at the Woolwich Building Society, little use is made of the term 'GIS'; systems are described according to their applications and would include 'customer', 'product' and 'branch' databases.

In recent years, there has been an increasing realization that data is one of the most important resources possessed by an organization $^{4}$ . Accordingly, emphasis has been placed on gaining maximum return from this resource, especially in those areas catered for by the GIS industry. Many data agencies supply geographic data; for example, Figure 1 shows a typical customer profile using the MOSAIC customer classification system based upon the UK Census and other sources of data.

GIS has a major role to play as the competition within the financial sector intensifies, due in part to the current economic climate. Markets for many financial products have reached saturation point and organizations are seeking to consolidate their market share in an attempt to reduce non-supportable cost-income ratios.

The Building Societies Act 1986 paved the way for building societies to offer a range of financial products and services. This increased competition, at a time when the housing market suffered a downturn, and created a business environment in which customer information became a crucial factor in the sustained marketing of successful financial services. The important driving force here was the maintenance of market share.

The legislative framework and the competitive environment have, according to research by Capello et al. $^{5}$ , influenced the structure of building societies by increasing the numbers of mergers, branches and estate agencies owned by building societies. Assets have also been concentrated in the largest building societies. For example, in 1991 the Woolwich took over the assets of the Town and Country Building Society (previously the fourteenth largest society), and is currently the third largest society in terms of number of branches.

The overall findings of a recent survey $^{6}$ show that 34 per cent of all building societies in the UK either have or plan to have GIS. For the top 30 societies, this proportion rises to over 50 per cent. Some 70 per cent of these societies consider GIS to be a decision support tool. This contrasts with the popular view in the public sector that GIS is an operational tool.

## GIS at the Woolwich: planned or ad hoc?

At the Woolwich Building Society, several applications for GIS have been identified by the Advanced Technology Department. This department has as its objective the research of new technologies and the demonstration of their value to the business areas. Development of GIS has been aided by siting both the information technology (IT) and business functions at the same central location, i.e. the corporate headquarters. Hence, exchange of information is rapid and effective, which may contrast with organizations that have decentralized their IT and research activities away from planning, marketing and other head office functions.

Planning processes for business and information systems at the Woolwich Building Society, if measured against the Galliers and Sutherland $^{7}$ stages of growth model, are relatively advanced. The Information Systems Group contains a number of planning departments which are consulted by the business when reviewing long-term objectives.

Figure 3 shows the general framework for the way in which GIS could be considered in relation to the corporate information strategy $^{8}$ . This framework suggests that the Woolwich Building Society has in place the business, information and technology processes outlined. In the case of GIS, the thrust for its consideration by the business came from the technology element of the framework.

The information systems planning processes in place were developed in the 1980s. However, the economic forecasts upon which they were based have turned out to be rather optimistic. This has left the Society with spare storage and processing capacity in its IT infrastructure. The current depressed market for financial services presents the systems planners with the challenge of scaling down activities. A natural reaction to this immediate pressure is to reduce the planning activities. The question this should leave in the reader's mind is: "Would reduction in planning activity lead to more ad hoc information systems developments?"

As a result of a series of successful presentations to the business, GIS has been well-received and has consequently been given a high priority for implementation. The project manager believes that seeing GIS in action is all that is needed to sell it to the top level of managers and to gain project sponsorship $^{9}$ . These presentations to the business outlined the benefits of GIS in two areas: operational systems and strategic systems. These are now looked at in greater detail.

## GIS: A strategic or an operational system?

In line with the key aim of consolidation of market position, one of the most relevant applications of GIS lies in the area of customer marketing and sales. This can be broken down into two main sub-areas: customer development and customer recruitment.

The reasoning behind the customer development application is that significant opportunities exist for cross-selling financial products and services to existing customers. By combining commercially available demographic data with existing customer data it is possible to create unique customer profiles. An understanding of the relationship between customer characteristics and the consumption of financial products and services should allow the generation of additional revenues from existing customers. During times of economic downturn, it is prudent to divert resources to maintain and increase customer loyalty rather than to attempt to attract new customers from competitors. Such market consolidation is essentially based upon achieving a lower cost per sale.

While the existing customer base provides the source of the Society's potential for organic growth, any strategy for future development necessitates the identification of potential customers. Again, by accurately profiling and mapping existing customers, it is possible to identify suitable areas for implementing customer-recruitment campaigns.

During the next decade, building societies will be confronted with tough challenges to their newly established market share. IT will be viewed as the means to attain and increase competitive advantage $^{10}$ . Specifically, it is likely that GIS will rise to dominate as a key tool for strategic planning.

GIS can facilitate high-level decision-making, and help in ensuring the integrity and survival of the organization, particularly as competitive pressures increase. There are two main areas in which GIS technology is likely to have a major impact upon business strategies: competitor analysis and branch rationalization.

GIS also provides a means of examining, in detail, the market share a competitor enjoys. Information about a competitor's market share, product penetration, customer penetration and branch network distribution all become readily available using commercially available datasets. Branch analysis allows branch performance trends to be easily identified. Success achieved in the marketing of financial products can also be quickly quantified. Again, economic pressures stress the importance of gaining maximum value from the branch network.

GIS also provide a means of defining and profiling the catchment areas for a building society's own, or a competitor's, branches. The figure shows branch catchment areas defined by measuring the driving time from branch locations. This is one of several techniques for market definition being implemented by CCN Marketing's Macromap system.

## The value of piloting

As part of the process of recognizing problems and identifying possible solutions provided by a GIS, the Woolwich Building Society is conducting a series of pilot studies within the business. The ultimate objective is to implement a system that will meet critical business requirements.

Pilot studies are being implemented not only to evaluate the various systems currently available, but also to demonstrate the applicability of GIS to Head Office functions. Encouraging users to participate in the evaluation process has allowed the refinement of business requirements, as well as exposing comparative strengths and weaknesses of the various vendor products (and data) under evaluation as GIS prototypes. Use of prototyping ensures that, when final implementation does take place, the users will be provided with systems that will not only fulfil their requirements, but also possess the important property of familiarity.

## Issues raised

Managerial and organizational issues raised by the GIS pilot studies require consideration. For instance, should a single GIS be installed as a common resource to meet business requirements across all functional areas, or should several be implemented to provide task-specific applications for each of the Head Office functions? The final outcome will not only depend upon the business requirements, but will also be impacted by the level of resources necessary for GIS maintenance and support.

Another important issue relates to the adoption of commercially available geo-demographic datasets. Three categories of data sets have been identified: 'base' data such as the 1981 UK Census; 'lifestyle' data, an example of which is CCN's MOSAIC; and 'application' data such as the banking and insurance data supplied by FRS. Datasets selected will depend upon specific business applications, but each of the categories have a role to play in the successful implementation of GIS.

## Conclusion

By taking a small-scale approach to the development of GIS, the Woolwich Building Society has been able to demonstrate clearly the necessity for geo-demographic data in the attainment of key business objectives. The use of pilot studies within business departments enables systems to be tailored to the users' needs. The business executives may now be firmly in the driving seat, but the introduction of the GIS concept itself came from, in this instance, the technology planners. This is an important point, as it is conventional wisdom that businesses first identify their requirements and then look for technology solutions, purely as a delivery mechanism $^{11}$ . This case illustrates that technological opportunities can usefully be identified (by technology planners) before the business itself has identified a specific need.
