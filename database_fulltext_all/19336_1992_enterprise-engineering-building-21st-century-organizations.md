# Enterprise engineering: building 21st century organizations

James H. Brown\* and Julian Watts\*\*

James Martin & Co., USA

A major change is occurring in the nature of work and the way that organizations relate to a rapidly changing environment. Many enterprises (and management teams) face obsolescence because of their inability to respond and adapt to this new situation. Enterprises can adapt if top management designs an appropriate information infrastructure, one which leverages human learning. A set of change processes, known as enterprise engineering, equips organizations to move towards such an infrastructure.

Keywords: enterprise engineering, business re-engineering, strategic visioning, information engineering, learning organization, information infrastructure

Thomas Kuhn first described discontinuities in the evolution of scientific ideas, now widely referred to as paradigm shifts $^{1}$ . The accepted wisdom becomes increasingly unwieldy and unworkable in new situations. The complexity of its models impedes progress and provokes breakthrough thinking. A new paradigm is invented that matches the new experiences far more effectively than the old model and replaces it quite rapidly — practitioners of the old school rapidly lose ground and become obsolete. Kuhn postulates that this cycle has taken place continuously throughout scientific history (for example, the Newtonian to Quantum shift in physics). It is our belief that an analogous paradigm shift is currently underway in the ideas concerning work and management, both in business and government.

Increasingly, management is finding that their old models of organizations, processes, work and technology are far too complex to keep up with the dynamism of the marketplace. A new learning model of the enterprise is emerging that promises to be able to keep pace with the changing environment. The old ways of organizing and working will rapidly become obsolete because they cannot compete with the learning model's responsiveness and fluidity. This paper addresses how executives can be proactive in changing their organizations to take advantage of the new learning paradigm rather than clinging defensively to the old model as it fades.

The authors believe that a central consequence of the new model is the emergence of an intimately connected human/technology partnership or information infrastructure as a critical success factor in all organizations. Information technology already pervades most business activities. An emerging awareness that work has changed fundamentally is forcing top management to rethink the architectural assumptions of their enterprises. An integrated set of four change processes, known as enterprise engineering, are proposed as the means for management to understand the new paradigm's implications, and create as well as move their organizations toward such a new architecture. The fundamentals of enterprise engineering are described in this paper, in business terms. Detailed descriptions of the change processes and how they can be implemented are not within its scope but are available from the authors.

## The new paradigm

The new paradigm starts with how management thinks about work. It was once management's goal to make work as standard as possible — matching it with the mass production of relatively few standard products. This ‘rational-economic’ model of human behaviour popularized by Taylor $^{2}$ and implemented by Henry Ford has had a profound impact on how managers view their own role as well as their staff. As organizational psychology advanced, the rational-economic model was challenged. Mayo $^{3}$ highlighted the social and affiliative needs of people in organizations. Further work, notably by Maslow $^{4}$ , identified a hierarchy of needs with self-actualization and the maximization of human abilities and resources as the ultimate goal. For many years the concept of self-actualization was only applied to managerial and professional groups. Schein stated that 'there is clear evidence that the drive toward self-actualization is an important — perhaps crucial — aspect of managerial and professional behaviour. It is not clear, however, how relevant this motive is to the lower level employee . . .'5.

The authors believe that the new paradigm is partially based on the growing recognition by managerial/professional groups of 'self-actualization for the masses'. Increasingly managers now believe they must enrich jobs and empower workers to match up with the dynamics of rapidly changing product diversification, the fragmentation of markets and the basic aspirations of employees. There is a move away from the manufacturing/economic view of work with its focus on dividing and devaluing work with the aim of reaching the lowest cost per worker. The new service-oriented thrust emphasizes aggregating and adding value to each person's work to get the highest value produced per employee. The authors contend that the emergence of the new paradigm is accelerating because a business need for self-actualization of the masses has occurred at the same time as technology has become capable of delivering empowering information to every worker in an enterprise.

The previous management focus on standardized products and replaceable skills led naturally to a top-down flow of power within enterprises. Everyone, including the workers, believed that the only really capable people were management, who had the full picture. Only management was capable of integrating all the fragmented pieces of work together. Thus, the common metaphors for organization were ones of top-down power, i.e. command and control, hierarchy, etc. Currently, management is beginning to see that, in a very dynamic environment, there needs to be a different approach to value integration — that work is integrated most effectively by the people doing it. This view, motivated by the search for quality and cost effectiveness, is behind the new metaphors for the organization such as networked, orchestral model, and web. In this revised model, management relinquishes much of its integrating power to the workers in order to achieve greater effectiveness, speed, quality, and most importantly, creativity. The workers themselves are beginning to sense that they, as much as management, are the assets of the company. Thus, management's role must inexorably shift away from re-integrating work they used to divide to providing an enabling infrastructure to leverage and channel the new kind of work they have empowered workers to perform.

The change in management's ideas about work is only one facet of the paradigm shift. There is a discontinuity emerging in the structure of the marketplace. Organizational boundaries in the old paradigm were conceived using a planetary metaphor — companies had known, predictable 'orbits' with suppliers, government and customers. There was a high degree of inertia and reliance on the stability of the status quo. The new marketplace metaphor is better described as a high energy 'plasma' — a dynamic, fluid, energetic mix of relationships that never remains stable. Companies, suppliers, even competitors have multiple roles that affect everyone else. Management loses the predictability of the planetary structure — but gains the possibilities and freedom of the plasma model. No relationship is out of the question, or enduring, in the new paradigm. The idea of trying to control the future through long-term relationships begins to seem hopelessly outdated. The best that a company can do is position itself, through a flexible infrastructure, to flow effectively with the unpredictable energy of the plasma by continuously changing products, strategies, alliances and partnerships.

The relationship between an enterprise and its customers is also becoming exceedingly dynamic. Assumptions about long-term customer relationships, and the basic passivity of the customer who accepts mass-marketed products, will not work much longer. Needs are becoming so dynamic that customers must be included in the product life cycle to capture the essence of their rapidly changing tastes. This almost seems to signal a return to the age of craftsmen who made unique products for each customer. In the knowledge-based corporation, customers will effectively become knowledge-workers themselves by interacting with the design and production process to obtain exactly what they want. Companies that wish to be customer-driven will depend on an expansive infrastructure which allows them and their customers to learn and work together successfully within the plasma of the marketplace.

In essence, as the 21st century dawns, any organization, whether business, charity, or government, must be seen as a very fluid set of purposeful collaborations. Such groups will only persist if they adapt successfully in a rapidly changing environment of worldwide competing collaborations. Group success will depend on the ability of its human members to learn more effectively than competing organizations. That is why a learning information infrastructure — a human/technology partnership that enables global collaboration and knowledge leveraging — is at the core of every organization's future success and survival. That is also why there is an urgent need for executives to undertake enterprise engineering in the 1990s.

## What is a learning information infrastructure?

At the heart of a learning information infrastructure is what Senge calls the discipline of systems thinking. This fundamental skill is the foundation for all the other skills and tools involved in creating a learning organization $^{6}$ . Systems thinking in Senge's terms is both a set of techniques and a mindset that produces powerful learning, especially in complex situations. A crucial leadership role is creating a culture of pervasive systems thinking. We agree with this view, but also stress that executives must choose to take on a new role related to systems thinking. Our metaphor for this role is the architect — one who envisions a new building or city or organization before it exists, and designs, plans and directs builders towards its construction. We believe that a central, crucial aspect of a learning information infrastructure is that it must possess an architect — a leader who directs the infrastructure to create itself through pervasive, powerful systems thinking . . . of which, more later in the 'Strategic visioning' section of this paper.

A learning infrastructure has several salient features. It has a keen ability to recognize and solve complex problems. It learns systematically to solve problems better and better — it models and abstracts lessons from its stream of experiences. Finally, it positions itself to learn from a diverse network of individual, local experience. These characteristics hold for any human group, no matter what its size. The difficulty today is that larger and larger organizations are required to learn faster and faster in a globally dynamic environment. Such sophisticated learning depends on the integration of technology, effective processes and highly skilled humans — a human/technology partnership. In other words, in the 1990s and beyond, an effective enterprise must be designed with the assumption that human performance and technology are becoming inextricably unified (Figure 1).

A learning information infrastructure is an organized structure of networks, human skills and processes, and leverage-producing tools and practices that facilitates learning and concerted action in complex situations (Figure 2). The design (or architecture) of a ‘world class’ information infrastructure must therefore focus on its central purpose — leveraging enterprise learning. There are five primary attributes that describe an appropriate architecture for a learning information infrastructure: orderliness, responsiveness, seamlessness, extension, and empowerment.

## Orderliness

For pervasive learning to be possible in a large enterprise, shared language and knowledge are crucial. Its infrastructure, therefore, must enable interaction among diverse knowledge sources. For example, the role of enterprise models, standards and other knowledge structuring tools are important components of connecting, in an orderly fashion, the diverse local learning of a global enterprise.

## Responsiveness

Time compression is a key capability for successful learning in a dynamic environment. The infrastructure architecture must be capable of supporting continual work group re-assembly and rapid end-to-end throughput in a fluid organizational 'plasma' environment. For example, 'just-in-time' product design and delivery to meet unique customer needs depends on integrating distributed work groups, networking and diverse computer applications.

## Seamlessness

In a global enterprise, with numerous, shifting alliances, there must be a common 'look and feel' to its work processes in order to achieve flexibility. For example, easy 'any-to-any' skill transferability throughout the self-managed work group environment requires cooperative processing, common user interfaces, and computer tools like 'client-server' and 'groupware'.

## Extension

The scope of infrastructure is crucial in the global environment. The extended enterprise consists of customers, partners, government agencies and other stakeholders that need to work together effectively for common purposes. For example, electronic data interchange (EDI), shared repositories of objects, and interoperability are all attributes of the architectural attribute of extension.

## Empowerment

At its heart, information infrastructure is a human/technology partnership. Human learning is at the essence of the infrastructure's power. Empowerment involves attributes which make technology human, such as multi-media interfaces, new management paradigms and tasks that share power, and high-performance work processes for self-managed work groups.

The details of information infrastructure implementation may vary from company to company, but these five fundamental architectural attributes will not, because they track the new paradigm's demands. They support maximizing the value that each individual can contribute by linking all persons within the extended organization together — the individual worker, the entire value chain of the company or government, and their customers or clients — using very powerful and flexible computerized knowledge networking technology. Management must focus on organizational learning and guiding the energies created by such networked knowledge. In this ideal, discussed by Zuboff $^{7}$ , an organization is seen as concentric rings of powerful employees and stakeholders centred around the knowledge network, with management on the outer rim, guiding, enabling, and rewarding achievement (Figure 3). The structure of the organization is intentionally left fluid by top management — in order to yield simultaneously newness and 'virtual specialization' as well as economy of scale in production and knowledge generation. These new ideals must become top management's central images of the shape of the future information infrastructure as they go about creating it. In the 21st century, enterprises must achieve both size and agility, vibrancy and stability, empower the individual and at the same time, emphasize the larger enterprise's goals. "The manager's main challenge in the 1990s is to question their views and practices about hierarchy, control, distance, access to information — in short, their entire managerial system."⁸. The conflicts among these demands can only be resolved by a learning information infrastructure created by an architectural vision.

## How does enterprise engineering produce a learning information infrastructure?

"No matter how persuasive the advocates of change, it is very rare that an entrenched establishment will reform its ways. Establishments die or retire themselves or fall in revolutions; they only rarely transform themselves."9. This quote from Gilder highlights the challenge facing any enterprise that wishes to accelerate its own learning and rate of change — the old ways within an enterprise are the inertia that resists this acceleration, defending themselves against new possibilities. A key implication of the quote, however, is that some organizations do learn and transform themselves.

The learning process used in such an organizational change sequence must be learned themselves. This is not a classroom project — the enterprise must learn by doing. It must begin to practise these processes and methods in order to change. Once it begins, the enterprise's learning infrastructure grows out of doing these change processes and, in a sense, the information infrastructure becomes these learning processes! The power of the change processes of enterprise engineering lies in the fact that they both enable an organization to grow systematically a learning infrastructure and, at the same time, energize the enterprise to close its strategic gaps. Thus, enterprise engineering is a continually maturing set of processes for both learning and managing enterprise change for long-term success. We will describe these processes in their current form, keeping in mind that they will evolve continuously as part of a learning infrastructure.

Figure 4 shows a graphic view of these four processes as overlapping with one another yet distinct. All are necessary to achieve the goals of enterprise engineering but they can be implemented incrementally. Most organizations will begin with one of the change processes, total quality management (TQM) for instance, but eventually must engage in all four to become radically more effective. Why are all four necessary?

Without strategic visioning, leadership never says 're-build this enterprise according to the following vision and plan ...'. The resulting vacuum reflecting top management's tacit acceptance of the status quo, damps out any real change within the enterprise ('They' don't really want to change; why should I?').

Without TQM, the value of continuous learning — for example, how to 'delight' customers and produce excellent results — has no powerful way to enter an enterprise's culture. The spread of systematic learning is drastically curtailed, and instinctive management reigns.

Without re-engineering, the burden of the past continually damps out real change, clean-slate thinking, and invention. The powerful forces that can really transform an enterprise are always kept 'in the box'. There is no powerful way to close strategic gaps that stand in the way of real competitive advantage.

Without information engineering, there is no real hope for enterprise-wide sharing of information or leveraged learning. Local improvements can be achieved, but not global, enterprise-wide learning.

The creative and imaginative use of IT lies at the core of such enterprise-wide learning. Therefore, a crucial assertion of enterprise engineering is that executives, as the architects of change, must deal with IT as one of the key enablers of enterprise-wide change and leverage. The human/technology partnerships demands it! This is a very different focus than executives delegating to IS organizations the development of so-called 'strategic IT applications'. Enterprise engineering integrates IT into the very thought processes of business executives. IT becomes inextricable from management productivity, and eliminates at its source the division that exists today between 'users' and 'techies'.

It is also important to distinguish enterprise engineering from other ideas being discussed at present: enterprise integration (e.g. the US Air Force EIP and ESPIRIT CIM-OSA programs), vendor architectures (e.g. IBM's SNA/SAA and DEC's DECnet), and strategic management or planning approaches. Enterprise engineering focuses on creating the underlying learning human/technology partnership that provides the crucial, and currently missing foundation for implementing all the above ideas. That is to say, enterprise engineering positions a complex, extended organization to effectively assimilate such ideas because it provides a robust infrastructure that allows such sweeping changes to be effectively learned by the entire organization.

A top management team must understand the scope and essentials of the four change processes so that they can guide the enterprise as it learns how to do this new kind of work. They also must learn to perform some of this new work themselves. They learn to be architects in the strategic visioning process of enterprise engineering. Each organization will approach these change processes in its own unique way, probably incrementally at first, to try them out, and get the 'feel' of them. But ultimately the full set of enterprise engineering processes must be done coherently by the entire organization — and top management must be the leaders of this effort. They must be the architects of the new learning information infrastructure.

The four change processes encompassed by enterprise engineering are outlined below. The summaries highlight the role of each process in addressing organizational complexity and producing a learning information infrastructure.

## Strategic visioning

'Strategic visioning' is a top-down learning process that creates the architectural 'shape' of the organization's learning information infrastructure and guides the builders as they grow this infrastructure.

Arie de Geus of Shell stated that long-term enterprise success depends on “the process whereby management teams change their shared mental models of their company, their markets, and their competitors. For this reason we think of planning as learning, and of corporate planning as institutional learning.” $^{10}$ . This sums up the central purpose of strategic visioning quite well — positioning leaders to learn about and model their future enterprise to maximize its chances of success. It is the process that senior management uses to create an architectural vision of a powerful learning enterprise.

Architecture presupposes a knowledge of 'building' technologies in its design processes. Therefore, executives must understand IT in order to architect a powerful learning information infrastructure. Architecture also deals with aesthetics and moral values — the 'rightness' of the architect's design. Architecture 'requires seeing man as the measure' $^{11}$ . It deals with creating the sources of human energy and responsiveness that make a company successful. Entering the 21st century, management faces an extremely dynamic environment and increasingly complex technology, as well as unavoidable global moral issues. Executives must deal with the structural and systemic needs of human beings in this environment. Therefore, strategic visioning focuses on designing a learning information infrastructure that best fits the needs of empowered humans striving to create a suitable enterprise for the 21st century.

It is easy to see why only top management can be the architects of a new enterprise. Only they have the overall perspective to decide that a radically new enterprise must be designed and built. Only their insights have the scope and boldness to imagine a new unified vision of organizational life. There are many specialists in a company who see part of the future — but only top management can integrate their contributions, and breathe life into them by saying, 'That is the precise architecture we need for our company for the future'.

Every organization already has an implicit, largely unseen, architecture inherited from the past, based on the old paradigm. To change the enterprise radically means that this foundation must be changed. Management must be willing to take on the founder's role anew, while they continue to deliver results today. This is an extremely difficult challenge, one which many management teams refuse to consider. Nonetheless, the on-rushing changes in the external world demand this kind of 'clean slate' thinking about the foundations of success. Even though in reality there is no 'clean slate' for executive management, they must attempt this kind of thinking to enhance significantly their enterprise's future success. In the end, it may be that many of the original founder's ideals will be retained, but in strategic visioning these must be temporarily put aside to truly re-architect the organization.

The enterprise architect's work must be brought to fruition by pragmatic builders. Designs and blueprints are not new organizations. Enterprise 'builders' need precise 'engineering' processes to achieve their results — the 'how to' of these processes must be spelled out clearly and agreed upon by the top management architects and the builders. The following sections give a brief description of enterprise engineering's remaining three integrated building processes and outlines how they fit together. All three processes are essential to growing a learning information infrastructure.

## Business re-engineering

'Business re-engineering' is an episodic process that achieves radical learning and change in work processes and employee skills through clean slate, 'breakthrough' projects.

The key word in business re-engineering is ‘breakthrough’ (Figure 5). Top management must set very aggressive performance targets for basic business processes to drive innovation and accelerated learning. ‘There is a correlation between the magnitude of the breakthrough and the size of gap between “business as usual” results and the levels of results committed (by re-engineering teams).’ Thus, the strategic gaps identified in strategic visioning become a key source of energy for accelerated enterprise learning and change in the business re-engineering process.

Value stream optimization is at the heart of re-engineering and accelerated clean slate learning. A value stream is the collection of all work, both within a company, and in its external suppliers and distributors, that delivers value and delight to a specific segment of customers. Business re-engineering uses value streams to cut across all existing complexity, caused by inappropriate organizational and management constructs, in order to yield new ways of thinking about effectiveness. By demanding that a team think in divergent ways about work and customer value, without regard to 'sacred cows', business re-engineering forces a high degree of learning to occur within the enterprise.

There are two fundamental, mutually supporting tasks in creating a breakthrough — thoroughly analysing the root causes of customer satisfaction problems and waste in the existing work flow to establish a baseline for current work effectiveness, and imagining a new ideal value stream to deliver value with far less work. These two tasks are done to kindle ideas, and because the tension between current reality and the chosen radically changed target value stream energizes the team to bring the new process into existence.

The ultimate objective is for the team's learning and successes to be replicated throughout the organization. Champions and cross-functional re-engineering teams are selected to be ‘pathfinders’ of change and to find radically better ways of performing selected processes — to reach the very aggressive targets management sets for the team. Each team relies on its own appointed senior management champion to overcome organizational barriers to its success, as well as to promulgate its success throughout the operating unit. The role and stature of the ‘champion’ cannot be over-emphasized in this ‘building’ process. The success of the team in making radical change happen rests on this person's credibility both with top management as well as throughout the operating unit.

## Information engineering

'Information engineering' is a continual learning process that plans and implements the information infrastructure necessary to achieve knowledge order and leverage throughout the enterprise.

Information engineering provides a necessary set of engineering methods and processes to build the enterprise architect's vision of human knowledge sharing and leveraging — just as construction engineering provides the needed tools to builders so they can fulfil the vision of the classic architect. An architect understands the inherent promise in technology for achieving his vision, but engineering is also necessary to integrate the many technological, human, and economic details required to reach the architect's vision. This is why information engineering is more than just a systems development methodology which improves quality and productivity — it is a necessary process that grows information infrastructure.

At the core of information engineering is an insistence on order. The vision of a pervasive, powerful information infrastructure across a large company can only be reached by imposing order on the knowledge generation process. While the company is a dynamic entity, its information infrastructure must possess stability. Such stability is often rejected by an organization in favour of more responsive, less controlled information systems implementation. The result of this approach, ultimately, is chaos — the inability to easily share knowledge across an enterprise, or to respond to rapidly changing business needs. The only certain way to achieve such flexibility is for the architect (top management) to design orderliness and structure into the enterprise's information resources (Figure 6).

Information engineering uses an integrated set of roles, tasks, tools, and deliverables to engineer operating computer applications and collections of information, based on business vision and models. It uses automated tools iteratively to refine these models into greater and greater specificity, finally reaching the exact set of software procedures and data structures that represent the ways the business works. If used faithfully throughout a company to define its information infrastructure, information engineering facilitates a common language about the processes and knowledge of the company. Information engineering is the method top management uses to provide a stable knowledge foundation to a rapidly changing business.

## Total quality management

'Total quality management' (TQM) is a systematic learning process that achieves a culture of customer focus and quality through continuous business improvement.

TQM is, essentially, a methodology that changes an enterprise's attitudes about excellence. Its basic ideal is well summarized by Berry: "If TQM is aimed at anything, it is aimed at winning and keeping customers — keeping them delighted" $^{12}$ . TQM simplifies complexity by orienting every person in an enterprise toward learning continuously about customer satisfaction and quality — the essentials of success. TQM also brings a common, systematic language to discussions within an enterprise, facilitating measurement, experimentation and feedback. It is a crucial process in creating Senge's pervasive 'systems thinking' throughout the enterprise. Much has been written on the subject, so that little has to be said about the basics of TQM here. What needs attention, however, is the integration of TQM with the other 'building' methods just described.

The heart of the difference between TQM and re-engineering is that TQM is evolutionary, while re-engineering, with top management's considered instigation, is revolutionary. What the two have in common, however, is increasing the value produced by each individual worker. The more each worker understands their unique crucial role in quality and customer 'delight', the more successful TQM is. And the more value that re-engineering can pack into an individual's work, the more successful it is. Thus, the ability of a company to learn about its 'value streams' and to manage and optimize their effectiveness is at the heart of both TQM and re-engineering.

While information engineering also focuses on continuously improving business processes, it does so in order to capture a model of these processes as the basis for developing the advanced, flexible information infrastructure discussed previously. But information engineering adds additional value. It does not assume that the scope of a value stream necessarily defines the scope of the business systems and information that support that value stream. Information engineering seeks to optimize information use across an enterprise. It develops an ideal model of the organization's entire information infrastructure to optimize the flow of information across all value streams. It does so in order to allow information to be shared fluidly throughout an enterprise. Thus, the integration of the information engineering, TQM, and re-engineering processes must be accomplished within a larger context than simply focusing on process improvement or value stream re-engineering. This enterprise-wide context is captured and kept visible in the set of architectures first sketched by strategic visioning, and captured with precision in information engineering: information engineering's information, business systems, and technical architectures. These three architectures provide the overall framework for TQM, re-engineering, and information engineering to be done coherently across the enterprise.

## Summary

A new paradigm is emerging, characterized by fluid organization structures, dynamic global relationships and empowered knowledge workers. The key to success for organizations operating within this new paradigm is the development of a learning information infrastructure.

Many organizations (and management teams) face obsolescence because of their inability (or unwillingness) to respond and adapt to this new paradigm. The authors believe that organizations entering the 21st century can evolve successfully only if their top managers choose to exploit the new paradigm rather than resisting it. Enterprise engineering enables top management to architect and grow a learning information infrastructure which will, in turn, enable the entire enterprise successfully to exploit the new business paradigm to its advantage.
