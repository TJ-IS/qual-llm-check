# Computer-based information systems for development planning: the significance of cultural factors

Shirin Madon

Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

New technological advances in microelectronics have recently prompted a number of developing countries to introduce computer-based information systems as a strategic tool for promoting socio-economic development. Despite efforts, these initiatives have not resulted in more effective planning and monitoring systems. Apart from problems related to inadequate resources and infrastructure, experience suggests that the introduction of information technology for development planning implies cultural values which are fundamentally at variance with those of traditional societies. There is, however, a paucity of literature which exposes the interplay between these cultural factors and the process of technology adoption. In this article, we review the literature on organizational culture and information technology. We then present a case study which attempts to link the current theory to practical experiences. Finally, we discuss ways in which the analysis of cultural aspects can lead to more effective management of information technology.

Keywords: culture, development administration, information systems

In recent years, much has been said and written about information technology being a vital resource for promoting socio-economic change in developing countries $^{1-3}$ . The declining capital cost of equipment and the rapid improvements in hardware and software technology have prompted many developing countries to undertake administrative reform directed at achieving decentralized planning through the diffusion of information technology to local areas. For example, in India, district planning by elected representatives is being introduced in the states with the National Informatics Centre placing microcomputers in each of the 439 districts. In Africa, the Resource Management for Rural Development Project of Kenya is currently engaged in the introduction of microcomputers in the districts. In South-east Asia, Malaysia is extending its Integrated Development Project Information System (SETIA) to the districts $^{4}$ .

However, despite the alleged potential for developing countries to 'leap-frog' the stages of development through the application of information technology, most of these initiatives have not met with success. Literature invariably focuses on problems relating to lack of resources and infrastructure $^{5,6}$ . While these are undoubtedly important obstacles for effective technology diffusion, some writers have argued that poor performance is due to the fact that systems developers assume that development planning is a rational process and therefore ignore informal aspects of the administration which embody deep-rooted cultural traditions $^{7,8}$ . Considerable fear has been expressed that the introduction of new technologies drawn from modern industrialized society implies cultural values that are fundamentally at variance with those of traditional society $^{9,10}$ .

A small number of recent studies have acknowledged the need to identify cultural values within the informal context surrounding an information system. Their main contribution has been to develop conceptual models to cope with the plurality of goals, perspectives and interests of the planners $^{11, 12}$ . While these models offer useful tools to expose the plurality of perspectives and vested interests inherent in development administration, they are nonetheless rather static in nature and do not improve understanding of the interplay between organizational culture and technological change. As a result, the dynamics of cultural change in development administration due to recent computerization efforts remains poorly understood.

In order to improve understanding in this area, we first review the general literature on information technology and organizational culture. We then present the findings of a case study of computer-based information systems for development planning in India in an attempt to apply theory to practice.

## A REVIEW OF LITERATURE ON INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY AND ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE

The topic of organizational culture has advanced significantly in terms of conception, measurement and theoretical status. Earlier work on corporate culture tended to be prescriptive in nature. Many writers focused on identifying desirable attributes of organizational culture which ought to be possessed by successful companies $^{13, 14}$ . However, such prescriptions were found to be rather simplistic when applied in reality. Organizations which appeared similar in function, structure and environment revealed themselves to have very different cultural characteristics. This was revealed in Barley's case study $^{15}$ of the introduction of information technology in two similar hospital departments had totally different consequences because of their cultural context.

The complexity of understanding organizational culture has resulted in a move away from prescriptions towards describing how culture is formed in organizations and the relationship between different subcultures within one organization. For example, in semiotics, consideration is given to the assumptions, expectations and beliefs of the agents involved and they are assessed in relation to the social environment in which they exist $^{16}$ . In a similar vein, some interesting work has been done on identifying various cultures through the use of language in organizations $^{17, 18}$ .

The majority of the literature has tended to focus on information technology as a vehicle for cultural persistence in organizations. For example, it is advocated that the stable forces operating within an organization may be so firmly embedded into a social system that they become perceived as an institutional property and become difficult to change $^{19,20}$ . The use of information technology has also been linked to the persistence of power bases and domination within organizations. This comes across in Kraemer's study in which it is argued that information may be appropriated and used to serve the interests of various parties in conflict $^{21}$ . Another way in which information systems have been considered as instruments of domination concerns their potentiality for increased surveillance through the visibility of human work as discussed by Zuboff $^{22}$ .

Cultural approaches which depict information technology as a vehicle for cultural persistence in organizations have been criticized for being too monolithic and static and for not accommodating the reality of subsystems in permanent conflict $^{23, 24}$ . A wide range of emergent uses of information technology, quite apart from the apparent intentions of the original designers have been quoted $^{25, 26}$ . This has resulted in new cultural practices being formed as actors depart from accepted ways in order to rectify structural contradictions. These contradictions urge participants to engage in social conflict directed at changing the status quo thereby relieving perceived inequities $^{27}$ .

The idea of structural contradiction with respect to information technology is illustrated in Markus's research $^{28}$ on the development of a new information system in a decentralized corporation. The proposed system did not match the organization's structure because it required upwards reporting of detailed performance data by autonomous divisions. The prospect of a centralized system in a decentralized organization threatened local autonomy and led to conflicts directed towards changing the configuration of the system.

While acknowledging its potential for cultural transformation, information technology is not an autonomous agent capable of forcing social change: it is itself a product of human action and its relation to cultural change is very complex. Some writers argue that like all artefacts, information technology is subject to social interpretation which in turn implies that the same technology can acquire different meanings in different cultures $^{9,29}$ . This important insight was revealed in Barley's case study $^{15}$ quoted earlier in which the application of technology symbolized deskilling and control of clerical work in one setting, and upskilling and liberation of work in the other.

In summary, it appears that in order to address both persistence and change, cultural theory needs to provide a dual account that explains on the one hand how patterns of behaviour become crystallized and how, on the other hand, those patterns change. This duality of persistence and transformation occurs at the same time as a separate, but related, process as described below:

...institutionalisation will never remove all structural contradiction, so the conditions for cultural change are always present. However, the processes leading to potential change are themselves constrained by the institutional order. $^{30}$

In the context of developing countries, there has been little effort to apply these theories to empirical evidence of information technology applications. Hence, our understanding of the cultural implications of information technology for development planning remains speculative.

The next section presents a case study of computer-based information systems for rural development in India. The findings are based on field work carried out in the state of Gujarat in Western India during the period 1988-91. Data to support the findings comes from primary and secondary sources. Primary data sources included a variety of administrators at central, state, district, block and village levels. The main means of data collections was via semi-structured interviews in order to enable respondents to introduce variations on their choice of answer. Secondary data sources consisting of government publications, working papers, reports and memoranda were provided by government departments and other agencies. A longitudinal research design was adopted in order to explore patterns in the co-evolution of information systems and social settings. The author returned to the research site at intervals of about nine months in order to see how the situation had altered.

## CASE STUDY

In the mid-1980s, the Government of India realized that a massive amount of data had been collected for managing the Integrated Rural Development Programme (IRDP) but that not much of it had been used effectively. Simply the very large dimensions of the programme meant that data compilation was often delayed because of shortages in skilled manpower.

Moreover, the vast amount of data maintained in the records of local level offices was not easily retrievable and could not therefore be used for further analysis for micro-level planning. Consequently, there was little interface between progress as reported upwards and progress as witnessed in reality $^{31}$ . It was recognized that computer-based information systems offered the possibility of providing a local level of decision support for IRDP management. This led to the genesis of the Computerized Rural Information Systems Project (CRISP).

In October 1987, the Government of India gave sanction to all 439 District Rural Development Agencies (DRDAs) in the country to purchase a PC/AT microcomputer and the requisite peripherals $^{32}$ . A menu-driven package called CRISP designed by the National Informatics Centre at central government level was provided together with database, spreadsheet and word processing packages.

## Information technology as a vehicle for cultural persistence in development administration

It was envisaged that the CRISP initiative would increase the efficiency of report generation and facilitate the availability and analysis of micro-level data for planning at the district level. However, the findings reveal that in the majority of districts in Gujarat, the manual system still predominates and that CRISP is largely redundant. We identify below six major factors which have resulted in information technology being used as a vehicle for cultural persistence in development administration.

## 1. The culture of bureaucratic inefficiency

Public administration in developing countries follows the style of colonial bureaucracies which tended to be very elitist, inflexible, authoritarian and paternalistic $^{33, 34}$ . Remnants of these traits have inevitably been carried over to successor bureaucracies in the new states, especially in the public services resulting in the inefficient running of projects. For example, in the late 1970s, a multitude of rural development projects emerged in India which lacked strategic direction and therefore failed to address the complexity of the task of rural development $^{35}$ . Moreover, the performance of the programmes was evaluated merely in terms of whether physical and financial targets had been achieved rather than in terms of poverty alleviation.

During the mid-1980s, several attempts were made by the central government to develop computer applications for planning in order to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of rural development programmes. However, as the design of these systems was invariably based on the existing manual systems, they only served to perpetuate the existing inefficiencies inherent in the administration. The thrust to develop computer applications for development planning was neither matched with an adequate information infrastructure, nor with the requisite human, financial and technological resources to support the venture $^{32, 36}$ .

The deliberate design decision taken by the central government to develop CRISP as an overlay of the existing IRDP manual monitoring system meant that the CRISP data sets were not derived from a model which reflected the reality of the local environment. As a result, CRISP merely served to perpetuate inherent inefficiencies in the administration rather than serving as a vehicle for cultural change within the administration. The method of evaluating the performance of CRISP was similar in nature to that of other computer projects — i.e. they were evaluated in terms of whether the hardware and software had been installed, rather than in terms of how the equipment and personnel were functioning.

## 2. The culture of highly politicized decision-making

One of the most important cultural characteristics of development administration in India is the highly politicized style of decision-making, especially in the public sector $^{34}$ . Rational judgement is often sacrificed for political expediency. For example, politically motivated decision-making has often resulted in poor project planning and control as was witnessed in the rush to extend development planning to all blocks in India in the late 1970s without understanding the functioning and dynamics of the local socio-economic milieu $^{37}$ .

The highly politicized style of decision-making in development planning has also had the effect of undermining the planning role of the state government and district authorities $^{38}$ . Over time, the centre has begun to do much of the state's work and the state has subsumed much of the district's work. This upward shifting of functions has made planning out of touch with reality and has reduced the task of local administrators to the ritualistic observance of targets set by higher authorities. The hidden cost of imposing targets by higher levels has been to shape the values of administrators at the local level. The task of rural development management is seen by them as an impersonal and mechanical exercise of little value since they are not given any opportunity to interact with superiors or become involved in planning functions. This has led to feelings of apathy amongst local administrators towards their duties. There is a general reluctance to undertake any activity which is conceived in the wider interests of the programme, but which does not make the business of reaching targets any easier.

The application of information technology to development planning in the mid-1980s evolved as a by-product of conflict between the centre and the state $^{39}$ . Many state governments reacted negatively because they felt that the centre was imposing its authority on them without their consent. In the light of these experiences, the cornerstone of the CRISP project was supposed to be decentralization of the technology diffusion process. However, in practice the system was designed and developed without any consultation with state governments concerning their requirements. The state governments consequently felt that the CRISP initiative was once again an unconstitutional move made by the centre to bypass its authority and to establish a direct channel of communication with the districts. The initiative therefore served to rekindle the context of hostility between the centre and state administrations which caused the state government to act as an opposition group to the CRISP project soon after it was launched.

The rush to diffuse technology to district level was similar to that of extending block-level planning on a nationwide level as mentioned above. Hasty design decisions were taken without any consultation with district administrators who play a leading role in the decentralization process. Lack of user involvement in systems development served to perpetuate feelings of neglect and apathy amongst local administrators towards the task of rural development and led to the development of a system which did not match the local reporting requirements. Moreover, the lack of any channel of communication between systems developers and users prevented users from being able to communicate their concerns about the system. Many situations were recorded in which users felt that a feature of the CRISP system was dysfunctional, but there had been no channel available for them to communicate that knowledge thereby perpetuating the impersonality and anonymity inherent in the administration. The only recourse has been for users to signal the problem through resistance to change.

## 3. The culture of leadership

The concept of the ‘leader’ or ‘father-figure’ is an inherent characteristic of Indian administrative culture $^{40}$ . According to Gupta and others $^{41,42}$ the belief system of Indian administrators is based on an image of an authoritative, yet nurturing father figure which individuals learn to value and look for in working life. In return, junior officers develop respect and reverence for superiors and a willingness to accept authority.

Many cases have been recorded where enlightened senior administrators at district level have succeeded in inspiring and motivating their subordinates to modify outmoded practices and structures $^{43, 44}$ . In the context of CRISP, districts where leadership messages were strong, positive and consistent accepted the computer system more easily. However, in the majority of cases in Gujarat, the computer represented an alien object to the directors who were themselves unaware of the advantages to be gained from CRISP. Moreover, the transfer structure inherent in development administration gave DRDA directors little incentive either to receive computer training themselves or to send their staff for training since all staff including themselves were on deputation and could be transferred at short notice.

Leadership messages have therefore tended to be inconsistent and nebulous. In many cases, junior staff have taken this as a signal to disregard the CRISP initiative resulting in non-utilization of the technology. The influence of strong leadership, coupled with the institutional rigidities of the transfer structure have resulted in lack of continuity in efforts at district-level computerization. For example, in one district in Gujarat, the director in 1987 was personally interested in computerization and motivated his staff to acquire training. However, the same enthusiasm was not matched by the subsequent director. This has resulted in a stagnation of the initial progress made to computerize operations at the DRDA.

## 4. The culture of roles, status and hierarchy

An important component of Indian culture is the caste system which is a social and political phenomenon of such great importance in India that no analysis of administrative culture can afford to ignore it $^{45}$ . Within the administration, casteism results in superior-subordinate relationships having a clear allocation of rights and duties across the boundaries which determine the social hierarchy. As Mathur $^{46}$ observes, “it is almost as if a new caste system has emerged within the civil service with senior civil servants being the Brahmins and junior officers the Sudras”.

The value attached to status and hierarchy by administrators is an important factor in local work practices. Singhi's study $^{47}$ concerning the value orientation of Indian administrators found that some 70% of administrators agreed with the view that caste and status consciousness influenced the relationship between the higher and lower officer ranks. Officers who have power, either in terms of their seniority or their influence, have tended to remain aloof from their subordinates. With the introduction of CRISP, many DRDA directors viewed the computer as a status symbol rather than as a strategic tool for promoting socio-economic development. This became apparent in their choice of physical location of the machine. According to central government guidelines, the computer was to be located in a cabin, adjacent to the director's cabin. In the majority of cases, however, the popular choice of location was the director's cabin even though the director himself was often too busy to use the machine. As a result, the computer was grossly underutilized because users felt intimidated to use it in the presence of the director.

## 5. The culture of secrecy built around information

The closed and non-participative style of bureaucracy which was part of the administrative culture has resulted in a reluctance to divulge information to other officers through fear of eroding one's power base or status within the administration. With the manual system, it had been possible for directors to manipulate figures on paper in order to ensure that targets were met. This often resulted in the promotion of vested interests in order to please rural rich politicians. However, with the computerized system, it was feared that information would become more visible to many and that the elasticity of data would be reduced. Faced with the prospect of a possible realignment of power, it was reported by junior officers that some directors actively discouraged efforts to computerize by preventing staff from attending training courses, or by insisting that the computer be kept in their cabin.

In addition to the fear of losing power through greater visibility of data, another kind of power struggle was also at stake. With the advent of the computer system, power was seen to derive from special knowledge of how to manipulate information relating to development planning. In most DRDAs, it was the junior staff who obtained this 'special knowledge' since directors and senior staff were usually too busy to attend training courses. It was reported by senior officers that in some cases, directors felt at risk of losing power to their subordinates and of thereby undermining their own status and prestige within the DRDA.

## 6. The culture of preference for informality

Many writers have argued that the imposition of imported bureaucratic institutions which are totally alien to the traditional culture of administrators has resulted in a dualistic environment $^{34, 46}$ . This dualism exists because while the process of decision-making and the acceptance of responsibility appear extremely formal, this formalistic environment is only a cover for a substantially different reality of informal behaviour based on deep-rooted values from a more traditional past $^{48}$ .

The coexistence of traditional and modern sectors has meant that individuals are expected to function by rationality, impersonality and anonymity which is totally alien to their traditional culture. This has resulted in bureaucrats becoming estranged from their own needs and values and has given way to negative behaviour in the form of corruption, nepotism and the promotion of vested interests. For example, even though the forms of a merit system are outwardly observed in the Indian bureaucracy, non-merit considerations greatly influence promotions, assignments, and dismissals within the administration $^{34}$ .

Dominance of the informal subsystem provides insights into how decisions are made at the local level which ultimately affects the validity and utilization of data in the CRISP system. The bias towards informality results in the non-use of information for decision-making within the administration. Problem-solvers are unwittingly programmed by their environment to live in a world where external data stores are not critically valued for problem-solving. This results in a passive or negative attitude towards the value and use of data from external sources. The formal process of management as laid down by the government is thereby subverted by an informal system of negotiations between functionaries and politicians at the local level. This situation has lent itself to opportunities for the promotion of vested interests of which numerous examples were recorded during the fieldwork.

## Information technology as a vehicle for cultural transformation in development administration

The problems experienced during the first three years of the CRISP initiative meant that by the end of 1989, there was a distinct possibility of the CRISP project stagnating. In most of the district offices, the computer was not being used and administrators had reverted to the manual system of functioning. During the latter half of the year, the tensions between the centre and state gradually resulted in a change in the balance of power with the state level training institute having a more practical and pivotal role to play in steering the way towards end-user computing. By directing its training towards the use of high-level languages and by encouraging local capacity-building, the training institute was not only in a position to alter the course of DRDA computerization in Gujarat but also to take a new stance of devolving power to sub-state levels.

The action of the state government has opened up a major debate in Gujarat about the future of computerization at DRDAs. On the one hand, the central government working through the National Informatics Centre believes that it is better to have a menu-driven system designed by the centre for district level computerization. On the other hand, the state administration believes that the future of computerization lies in local initiative-building with appropriate technical support. This debate appears to reflect the historic reluctance of the centre to devolve power to the states. Despite attempts made by the centre to introduce counter strategies to resist end-user computing, the training institute has been able to proceed with granting more flexibility to sub-state levels by virtue of its status as the nodal agency for computerization.

State level support towards end-user applications has triggered off a wave of enthusiasm amongst local administrators. The sheer existence of the microcomputer at the DRDA has prompted some degree of curiosity and self-learning. DRDA directors have also begun to provide local technical and human resources to support the system. For example, since 1990 most DRDAs have started to employ persons on a daily wage basis for data processing at the DRDA. In addition, some DRDAs have recently taken the initiative to engage local consultants to conduct training to its staff on general computer awareness and on the use of high level languages. Some districts are training as many as eight members of staff. In these districts, additional computers have been purchased by the director out of the DRDA's annual interest income.

There is also growing realization among DRDA directors that the current use of computers does not afford any real decision-support for IRDP planning at the district level. The administrative culture built up over the years has reinforced a particular rationality of using numerical analyses and quantitative data relating to physical and financial targets. By doing so, social and political biases have tended to persist behind a screen of 'rational planning'. Over the past two years, many district officers are beginning to identify useful categories of information in order to expose the causes of differences in people's well-being. This suggests that the promotion of end-user computing has helped to raise the propensity of planners in local offices to use data and information for problem-solving thereby providing an opportunity for cultural change within the administration.

The use of the computer has also brought about a change in working group relationships within the DRDA. Errors are immediately detected within the processing system and responsibilities for errors easily traced. Each individual is therefore made more aware of his and other peoples' contribution and unsuccessful workers appear to be put more in the spotlight than ever. Relationships between senior and junior staff have also undergone a change in style. Up until now, a formal relationship existed between juniors and seniors with seldom any contact between the two. As junior officers have begun to perceive the value of computers for their daily functioning they are brought into direct contact with senior staff in order to discuss potential computer applications for the DRDA.

These findings support the theoretical notion of information technology as a vehicle for cultural persistence in organizations. Over the years, the work procedures and style of management operating within the administration have become so firmly engrained that they are perceived as 'part of the system' and hence difficult to change. In the findings, this related to the difficulties encountered in changing the style of bureaucratic functioning and the culture of informal decision-making practices. Theory also links the use of information technology to the persistence of power bases and domination within the organization. In the findings, there was evidence of information technology being used as a tool for each level of administration maintaining power over lower levels of the administration. The highly politicized style of decision-making coupled with the importance attached to status, power and leadership have resulted in junior administrators being totally cut off from the management of rural development. As a result, previous attempts at administrative reform through decentralization have not been successful.

After 1990, the data reveals patterns of cultural change in development administration. Structural contradictions were revealed which led to emergent uses of information technology at the district level quite apart from the intentions of the original designers. Despite inertia during its first few years of implementation, information technology has acted as a probe to reveal inefficiencies in basic administrative tasks and procedures and has begun to be used as a vehicle for promoting cultural change within the administration on issues such as centralization and decentralization, work procedures are relations between workers. New trends in thinking concerning the role of information technology in organizational culture have moved towards the concept of organizations as existing in a state of permanent conflict. In the context of the case study, these conflicts and contradictions have urged administrators at the sub-national level to engage in social conflict directed at changing the status quo towards a new and more equitable social order. The realignment of power and control has led local administrators to recognize how information can be harnessed in a more useful way as an instrument of development.

## DISCUSSION

The scope of this section is to suggest ways in which an improved understanding of the cultural context of development administration can lead to more effective management of information technology. While there is no universal ‘culture of developing countries’, local information technology practitioners need to devise appropriate ways of coping with the characteristics of local culture. Below we raise some key issues which need to be addressed in order to achieve this. Although the discussion addresses the specific context of development administration in India, it may be generalizable to other public sector organizations undertaking administrative reform through decentralization.

## 1. Strategic management — the role of the central government

At present, the usage of technology for decentralized development planning is as a result of isolated initiatives without any preconceived strategy. Equipment is being acquired in an ad hoc way by different user organizations with little coordination and planning. Strategic direction is therefore needed to promote long term socio-economic development, continuity and the sharing of resources. In the absence of such a policy, there appears to be a real danger of 'losing control' in terms of national development priorities and goals. This danger is all the more acute in the light of recent trends towards district initiatives.

## 2. Tactical management — the role of the state government

Policy towards long-term development goals needs to be supplemented by a strong state level cell which would provide an overall framework for managing the process of change. Issues of education/training and support are of key importance at this level. As the introduction of information technology involves significant cultural change in development administration, the role of top management would be to provide leadership in this transformation process. DRDA directors and senior officials cannot be expected to motivate staff to use computers without themselves having some general knowledge of the equipment and its potential. Moreover, given the importance attached to status, they will shy away from discussing crucial questions if they think that junior staff will discover them to be ignorant. A special kind of training is therefore required to sensitize senior staff at the district level towards the application of computers for improved decision-making at the DRDA and towards all aspects of managing the technological resource including the development of human resource capability and the logistics of data collection and processing.

Senior officers must also be made aware that the diffusion of technology needs to be accompanied with changes to local work and decision-making processes. Training sessions themselves could be used as a forum for staff to exchange experiences about the impact of any new administrative procedure or structure that has been introduced at individual DRDAs. Senior DRDA officers could be introduced to problem-solving skills and to the use of information for decision-making. Decision-support tools or simple spreadsheets can be used to generate debate about a variety of 'what-if' type situations relating to the causes of differences in people's well-being. Teamwork in carrying out projects needs to be given prime importance to ensure that all participants are involved in the process of systems development and to encourage personalized style of working thereby eliminating the psychological hiatus between senior and junior officers.

The state government ought to provide adequate technical support to individual districts and monitor the performance of computerization efforts in terms of how the equipment and personnel are functioning. Each DRDA needs to be provided with a permanent computer-literate officer in order to overcome the frequent turnover of staff due to the transfer structure.

## 3. Information management — the role of the district

District officers need to actively encourage the inclusion of extension staff as information sources for rural development planning. These workers have very good knowledge about the socio-economic status of villages. However, these insights have tended to remain simply at the level of informal awareness without becoming inputs into a systematic feedback system from the field.

Accountability can be taken down to the community itself. For many rural poor families, success in rural development projects is vital for survival. Yet these groups are often unaware of the benefits designed for them and are unable to direct these advantages towards themselves because of the highly politicized culture of decision-making in the administration and because they lack systematic and relevant information to hold development administration responsible for their actions. District officials could involve local informants in the process of data collection thereby encouraging the community itself to take responsibility for collecting and using data. The main contribution of this paper has been to apply current theory about the role of information technology in organizational culture to empirical evidence. Between 1987 and 1990, the findings support the notion of information technology as a vehicle for cultural persistence in the organization. Over the years, the cultural context of development administration had become so firmly embedded that any attempts to modify structures, attitudes and patterns of work soon had to be abandoned. The theoretical perspective of information as an instrument of domination is also supported by the findings. First, the CRISP data sets focused on information related to physical and financial targets and excluded other information which may have provided the opportunity for local decision-support. Second, at a lower level of administration, senior officers at the district level were interested in maintaining their power bases and feared losing status and credibility in the administration due to a realignment of power.

According to the theory, information technology is also a vehicle for cultural transformation in organizations. This notion is supported in the findings which described that cultural transformation in development administration is occurring in modifying practices, attitudes and patterns of behaviour. Theory predicts that the process leading to cultural transformation in organizations is shaped by the existing institutional order. According to the findings, this transformation is occurring as a result of prevailing social conflict between various levels of administration.

Despite the encouraging trends towards cultural transformation in the administration, technology itself cannot force administrators to abandon long-established ways of relating to their environment. This implies that the process of cultural transformation needs sensitive management. A second contribution of this paper was to propose some tentative guidelines as to how this could be achieved.

While the current research afforded the opportunity to study the process of cultural change brought about by computerization in one situation, further research is needed to demonstrate how variability in cultural context would affect the shape, pace and direction of systems implementation. A second limitation of this paper is its simplistic treatment of the concept of culture. Within development administration, many different sub-cultures exist relating to caste, class and administrative rank which the author did not attempt to analyse. Finally, a third concern with the current research is the question of proof. Organizational culture is not something that can be absolutely verified by collecting data. Cultural factors are often hard to locate because they are implicit. It is this ‘taken-for-granted’ quality of culture that presents important research and practical problems in organizations.

## CONCLUDING REMARKS

The article supports the hypothesis that information technology cannot be understood in isolation from its cultural context. A variety of cultural factors related to the acceptance and usage of information technology in development administration were put forward based on fieldwork carried out in selected districts of Gujarat in India.

It is hoped that the insights gained from the findings will improve our limited understanding of the role of information technology in organizational culture. The practical benefits of increasing such understanding in the context of computer-based information systems for development planning is that practitioners can regard information technology as a flexible medium through which cultural characteristics are created, conveyed and changed. Some guidelines were put forward as to how this process could be managed.
