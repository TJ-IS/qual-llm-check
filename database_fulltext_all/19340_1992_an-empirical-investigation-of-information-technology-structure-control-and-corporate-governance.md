# An empirical investigation of information technology structure, control and corporate governance

Zeinab A. Karake

Associate Professor of Management, Catholic University of America, 309 MCM, Washington, DC 20064, USA

In a study involving 72 large publicly-held organizations, the relationships between information technology (IT) structure, control, and corporate governance were examined. Despite the fact that some have called centralized systems electronic dinosaurs, many companies are turning to this traditional approach because it does offer one thing that decentralized systems generally lack, namely, control. The results indicate that information technology structure (centralized vs. decentralized) is strongly related to management equity ownership and to concentration of stock holding. For example, an organization where top executives and managers own a relatively high percentage of the company's stock possesses a more centralized information technology structure than an organization with a relatively lower percentage of management ownership. This means that user departments in the latter have more control over their IT functions than users in the former. Conversely, companies with a high level of stock concentration have a more decentralized IT structure. In addition, the research revealed a statistically significant relationship between a company's equity to debt ratio and the level of IT centralization.

Keywords: IT, control, governance

Different organizations subscribe to various overall organizational structures as well as various information technology (IT) structures, ranging from highly centralized to highly decentralized. Proponents of the 'organizational fit' concept maintain that to avoid causing organizational friction, an organization should structure its IT resources to conform with its organizational context variables, such as decision-making structure, organizational size, managerial philosophy, etc. $^{1-6}$ .

The relationship between the general organization structure and information technology structure was studied by Ein-Dor and Segev $^{2}$ and Wheelock $^{6}$ . Ein-Dor and Segev, who conducted their study using field interviews of 53 organizations, found a positive correlation between the centralization of the overall organization and centralization of the information technology function. Further, their study did reveal a correlation between the size of the organization and the degree of centralization of the IT function. These findings however, were challenged by other empirical studies undertaken by Olson and Chervany $^{7}$ , and Olson and Davis $^{8}$ whose research failed to find any statistically significant relationship between overall organizational structure and IT structure. It appears that these conflicting results are caused by either the qualitative operational variables used to categorize structure, or by the exclusion of other determinants of IT structure. [For a detailed discussion of this issue, refer to Karake $^{9}$ .]

In this paper, it is argued that the information technology structure is correlated with management ownership structure, equity to debt structure, and concentration of stock holding. Managers who own a relatively high proportion of total equity have greater incentives to exercise tighter control over company's operations; consequently, they would tend to prefer a centralized IT function. On the other hand, in a firm where the shareholders' equity to debt ratio is relatively high, the ownership of such a firm would be generally more diversified than the ownership of a firm with lower equity to debt ratio. This diversity tends to cause more conflicts among managers and shareholders in the firm. In this situation, the owners will push the managers to delegate authority down the hierarchy; this would lead to decentralized overall organizational structure and decisions and, consequently, decentralized IT structure. Managers are willing to decentralize decision-making in such organizations in order to decrease agency costs. [For an excellent review of agency theory, refer to Eizenhardt $^{10}$ .] According to Fama $^{11}$ , although self-interest motivations exist in a firm, the welfare of the individual (management and stockholders) depends on the success of the firm. Therefore, by decentralizing the firm's operations, conflict among members of the firm is reduced, and consequently, the perceived cost of agency is, also, reduced.

## Centralization vs. decentralization of IT

No doubt that major changes in information technology are profoundly affecting the people, processes, structures and strategies of an organization. Improved communications capabilities and data accessibility would likely lead to systems centralization of business processes across traditional functional, product, or geographic lines. One of the important forces driving the trend toward centralization is the growing reliance on integrated systems in recent years. No matter what the business, getting the market faster requires closer links among design, production and service activities.

In 1958, as rapidly growing companies hired droves of middle managers and decentralized decision making, Leavitt and Whisler $^{12}$ wrote a controversial article predicting a reversal of that trend in the 1980s. Their prediction, which met with strong criticism at that time, held that the combination of management science and information technology would prompt a shrinkage of middle management ranks and greater centralization during the 1980s.

The longstanding debate over whether to centralize or decentralize information technology resources was examined by King in 1983 in terms of fundamental organizational and economic factors $^{13}$ . King concludes that at the heart of the debate has been the objective of IT professionals to determine an appropriate arrangement for the deployment of IT resources in organizations, given user needs and the desire of management to control both costs and uses [Ref. 13, p. 319]. He examined three separate aspects of the centralization/decentralization issue: (1) centralization vs. decentralization of control — this concerns the locus of decision-making activity in the organization; (2) centralization vs. decentralization of physical location — this concerns the siting of IT facilities; and, (3) centralization vs. decentralization of function — this refers to the position of an activity or responsibility within the structure of the organization.

Until the late 1970s, the proponents of IT centralization were nearly unanimous in the conclusion that, regardless of its impacts, centralization saves money. Their argument focused on economies and cost-cutting, which is referred to as efficiency. On the other hand, the proponents of decentralization have emphasized improvement in computing services and user satisfaction as their main objective. This is what is referred to as effectiveness. This debate among academicians and practitioners, over whether IT should be centralized or decentralized has intensified recently. Many researchers argue that after a period in which many companies experimented with decentralizing their information technology organization, the pendulum is swinging back once again $^{14}$ . Companies are consolidating data centres, beefing-up authority of their central staffs, and establishing companywide technical standards and work procedures.

In summary, the centralization/decentralization debate of IT has tended toward trade-offs, in which the organizational advantages of centralized control, uniform operations, and economies of scale have been pitted against user department needs for ready access to computing and opportunity for fitting computing capabilities to department requirements.

King's article is descriptive in nature. Empirical studies were based on the concept of 'organizational fit'. Proponents of this concept $^{1,3-6,13}$ argue that the variance in IT structures is attributable to the difference in overall organizational structures and context variables in corporations. They maintain that to avoid causing organizational friction, an organization should structure its IT systems to conform with its overall organizational context variables such as organizational decision-making structure, managerial philosophy, and organizational form. More recently, in a study involving 52 large organizations, Tavakolian $^{5}$ examined the relationship between IT structure and organizational competitive strategy. He concluded that IT structure is strongly related to competitive strategy. For example, an organization with a conservative competitive strategy was found to possess a more centralized IT function than an organization with an aggressive competitive strategy. This means that user departments in a conservative organization have less control over their information technology function than do user departments in an aggressive organization. Tavakolian's findings provide additional support for the 'organizational fit' concept.

During the last part of the 1980s, a number of companies adopted sophisticated telecommunications networks and centralized databases which integrated all aspects of highly centralized businesses. Senior managers started using these centralized databases and networks to monitor and control their organizations and to signal to employees the kinds of things they should focus on. With centralization comes the concentration of decision making at a single point in the organization. Corporations which have been structuring themselves along the lines of small, tribe-like entrepreneurial units, in order to create new efficiencies and mobility, have realized that they cannot operate simply as a collection of independent tribes. The tribal-sized groups require a corporate infrastructure to integrate and align team efforts with the overall direction of the corporation. Centralized information technology makes this integration possible [Ref. 15, p. 51].

In hard economic times, the potential cost savings from centralization are causing even those companies deeply committed to business unit independence to consolidate data centres. Some authors argue that as the cost of communicating with a mainframe at a remote site drops, it becomes less necessary to have multiple data centres near local business offices. As a consequence, it becomes economically viable to centralize information technology $^{14, 15}$ .

Another major reason for the recent move towards centralization of information technology is the involvement of senior executives and line managers in using the technology. No longer willing to delegate the strategic or tactical uses of this technology to the information systems department, these managers are taking the lead in applying information technology to the most important areas of their businesses. Many are using the technology as a core element in aggressive new approaches to the marketplace and to enhance control of internal operations $^{16}$ . In other words, volatility in the business environment, coupled with technology's ability to provide management with efficient communication and information is radically changing the way managers look at IT. The major issue is how best to use IT for coordination and control of the firm's activities in order to reduce costs.

Examples that depict the move toward centralization abound. [For a thorough discussion of this issue, refer to Karake $^{9}$ .] The investment firm Merrill Lynch (New York) and the nation's sixth-largest bank (Los Angeles) have reverted to centralized management of their information technology departments. This move was part of the management-by-results (MBR) program both companies initiated in 1987. Under such a program, the IT department and end-users jointly conceive, build and own strategic goals. This written contract predetermines productivity measures and reward shares $^{17}$ .

In 1987, Hewlett Packard Company's (HP) corporate structure changed from one of divisional autonomy and entrepreneurship to a type of centralized management. During the past four years, this change has brought improvement in HP's ability to bring products to market.

In 1988, Texas Instruments, Inc. (TI) completed the centralization of its world-wide electronic data interchange (EDI) operations in order to make it easier for its trading partners to deal with its many business units and to encourage new partners to participate in its EDI network. The major internal applications that are EDI-ready are purchasing, traffic, marketing, shipping, accounts receivables, and accounts payable. This centralization of EDI has cut operations cost, reduced data entry errors, and improved management control over the different facets covered. The EDI network contains 38 000 terminals and desktop computers, 1250 minicomputers and 40 mainframes in 17 countries $^{18}$ .

General Signal Corporation, a diversified manufacturing firm with 28 autonomous units, had never centralized much of anything until March 1987, when it decided to create a central IT services. This has been far from the norm for such a decentralized firm. While each subsidiary is still free to choose its own IT destiny, the General Signal Services utility has convinced most of them to sign on because of its economies of scale, additional CPU capacity and the elimination of data centre operation worries $^{19}$ .

Trailer Train Company, which supplies 65% of the flat-cars used in North America, is in the process of centralizing its information systems, by hooking the company's 60 remote sites to the headquarter's host computer in Chicago. This would effectively take the organization from a decentralized to a centralized computer environment. The move toward centralized computing environment falls in step with Trailer Train's overall corporate philosophy of a centralized structure $^{19}$ : (1) it is more economical, (2) the company will be able to provide more effective support, (3) it increases efficiency in decision making, and, (4) it provides flexibility in making changes.

Recently, corporate data centres have been coming under closer scrutiny as part of a general trend toward cost reduction and central control in US business. Data centre operations represent 50% to 70% of the information technology budget and are growing. The people at the top of corporations start to consider how to cut back on expenses and at the same time have access to information when needed. Among the more publicized cost-cutting efforts was the discourse in January of 1990 that AT&T was moving to consolidate its data centres operations, and the 1989 decision of Eastman Kodak Co. to consolidate its data centre operations from four to one $^{20}$ . Information technology executives at both companies say that consolidation will accomplish far more than just cost reduction. Both strategies, in fact, are as much techniques for improving the quality of information services and instituting greater management control as they are for reducing expenses.

It is concluded, then, that the strategy to centralize information technology in some of the larger corporations in the USA is driven by a number of factors, the most important of which is greater management control.

## Development of hypotheses

## Organizational size

Information systems integration and centralization are thought of as organizational innovations. As such, and based on Schumpeter's thesis, size is frequently thought to be the most important factor in innovation because larger firms are seemingly better endowed with resources than smaller firms. In addition, organizational size is said by some researchers to directly or indirectly influence information technology structure. The supposition is that since size is an important determinant of other organizational context variables, especially organizational structure, it should be related to IT structure $^{5}$ . Ein-Dor and Segev $^{2}$ show, empirically, that size as measured by total revenue, is positively related to the degree of centralization of IT function. As such the first testable hypothesis is formulated.

There exists a positive relationship between the move toward centralizing the IT function and organizational size. [H01]

## Equity ownership

According to agency theory, the challenge of the agency relationship arises whenever the principals (stockholders) cannot perfectly and costlessly monitor the agents' (managers) actions and information, and this, in turn, gives rise to agency cost. If equity is high relative to debt, this implies that the ownership of the firm is more diversified; therefore, more conflict would be expected between managers and stockholders $^{21}$ . In addition, management of the firm has control over the expenditure of the resources of the firm. Management with centralized authority might be tempted to use this authority to divert the resources of the firm for its benefits. Two ways management may accomplish this are by paying itself inflated salaries and/or by consuming an excessive amount of perks $^{21}$ . Stockholders will want to curtail this diversion of resources by pushing toward decentralized structure which leads to the sharing of power, authority, and responsibility among a larger number of managers. In other words, owners will push toward decentralization to ensure that their interests are served best in a sense that strategies would be based on detailed knowledge and increased business-level 'ownership' of decision-making $^{21}$ . Alternatively, if decision management and control are concentrated in a small group of people, it would be fairly easy to engage in some type of non-optimal behaviour. The decentralization of decision-making puts a set of checks and balances in the decision mechanisms of the firm that makes it more difficult for the firm's managers to expropriate the wealth of the shareholders. As such the second testable hypothesis is formulated

There exists a negative relationship between the move toward centralization of IT and the equity to debt ratio. [H02]

## Management ownership

When managers own a large percentage of the company's stock, there is a tendency that they would require more control over the company. Managers who own a substantial percentage of shares in their firms have an incentive to limit their wealth reducing actions. They are aware that maintaining profitability of the firm and securing competitive advantage over the long term requires their full attention if they want to maximize their earnings; in other words, those managers must be able to determine whether the firm is on track with its strategies. In order to be able to accomplish that, information technology has to be centralized at the top level. As a consequence, managers would know when to intervene to prevent and correct any non-value maximizing decision at lower levels in the organization. Hence, the third testable hypothesis is formulated

There exists a positive relationship between the centralization of IT structure and the level of management stock ownership. [H03]

## Board of directors

The stockholders elect the board of directors, which in turn decides on the major business policies of the corporation. Among the duties of the board are authorizing contracts, deciding on executive salaries, and arguing major loans with banks. The make-up of the board of directors is different from company to company. In most cases, though, it contains several officers of the corporation and several outsiders. Traditionally, then, directors have functioned as guardians of the financial interests of stockholders, with a specific concern for earnings and dividends. With time, responsibilities of the board of directors have increased. Recognition of the growing responsibility of directors is not entirely voluntary. The courts and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) are actively involved in expanding areas of board responsibilities. In holding board members responsible, the SEC has taken the position that anyone in a position to know what is going on and to do something about it will be held liable. Board members are perceived as agents of control and the more timely and accurate information they have the better it is for them to make sound decisions.

The appointment of outside directors is important if the board is to meet its responsibility in overseeing the performance of the chief executive officer (CEO) and salaried management teams. As the legal representatives of corporate stockholders, outside directors are expected to help promote strategic orientations that benefit stockholders' wealth $^{22}$ . Outside directors, then, better represent the traditional interests of stockholders by monitoring the overall effectiveness of management rather than giving routine approval of management programs, and as such they would oppose the concentration of power, authority and decision making in a few salaried managers. Consequently, they would press managers to decentralize company's operations. Based on the foregoing, the fourth testable hypothesis is formulated

There exists a negative relationship between the centralization of IT structure and the number of outside directors on the board. [H04]

## Concentration of stock holding\*

Although the precise functional relationship between concentration of ownership and control is unknown, research spanning more than 50 years suggests that the proportion of stock required to exercise significant control in large firms may be quite small [Ref. 23, p. 56]. Concentrated stock ownership by outside owners is included in this research, then, because previous research has suggested that monitoring of management by outsiders is more likely once some significant level of ownership concentration is reached $^{23}$ . Where equity is concentrated, owners would tend to have more influence and control potential over the internal structure of the corporation. Control potential is defined as ‘the wealth gain achievable through more effective monitoring of managerial performance by a firm’s owners’ [Ref. 24, p. 1158]. Under such circumstances, those influential owners will push toward decentralized structure. At the same time, managers have an incentive to decentralize in order to reduce any conflict with the owners, consequently reducing agency costs. Hence, the fifth testable hypothesis is formulated

There exists a negative relationship between a centralized IT structure and the level of concentration of stock ownership. [H05]

Although a specific level of concentrated outside ownership is difficult to justify on theoretical grounds, Gomez-Mejia and colleagues $^{23}$ have found significant differences using a 5% threshold. Thus, given no reason to expect other threshold levels to be appropriate, and following prior research, outside stock ownership will be measured as a dichotomous variable set to 1 if 5% of a firm's stock was owned by a single individual or organization that was not a manager of the corporation and to 0 otherwise $^{25}$ .

## Methodology and data collection

An empirical examination is conducted to determine the significant relationships between information technology structure, control and ownership structure. Specifically, an experiment is designed to test the five hypotheses formulated in the previous section. A cross-sectional matched-pair design is used to compare corporations with centralized information technology structure to similar corporations with decentralized information technology structure. The function of a matched-pair design is to control for factors in the samples that are being matched and to limit the effect of such controlled factors.

## The sample

A comprehensive list of corporations with centralized and decentralized IT structures is not readily available. As a result, the firms in the experimental group were identified through an extensive search of the literature during the period 1988-89. The firms in the control group were identified through: (1) a questionnaire which was administered by the author during the period 1989-90,\* and, (2) personal contacts, where possible. The main criteria for selecting the experimental group were that the company is publicly held and has a centralized IT function. The search resulted in 36 companies. ## Operational measurements

The primary dependent variable, in this research, is the structure of the IT function (centralized vs. decentralized). The independent variables are, as postulated in the statement of testable hypotheses, size (S), management ownership (M), stockholders' equity to debt ratio (E), number of outside directors (O), and concentration of stock holding (C). All of these variables are indicators of the degree of control exercised in the company. Table 2 describes the measurements of the variables identified.

## Data collection

Publicly available data were collected on the operational variables described above for the 36 experimental and 36 control sample points. Data on revenues, management stock ownership, stockholders' equity and company debt, board of directors, and concentration of stock holdings were collected from the companies' proxies and annual reports. Where possible, the data were cross-validated from the proxies against information provided by The Standard and Poor's Industrial Guide and Moody's Industrial Manual.

## Statistical analysis

The logistic regression (logit) model was selected because the dependent variable is discrete (binomial), and because logit analysis avoids some of the strong assumptions of other multivariate analysis techniques like discriminant analysis (e.g. the independent variables are normally distributed and equality of the variance-covariance matrix).

The functional equation was constructed as follows

$\mathbf{Y} = \mathrm{f(E,O,M,S,C)}$

where Y, E, O, M, S, and C are the variables identified in the section headed Operational measurements.

Interpretation of multiple logistic regression results depends implicitly on the assumption that the explanatory variables are not strongly correlated. When this ideal assumption of independent explanatory variables is violated the data are said to be multicollinear. The issue here is not the presence of multicollinearity but its severity. The process of detecting multicollinearity and measuring its severity consists of several guidelines; unfortunately, none of these can be formulated into formal statistical tests of significance.\* Table 4 presents the product moment correlation for the observed variables.

The five variables were analysed using a maximum likelihood estimation procedure in a logit analysis. This model evaluates the relative importance of the five control variables in predicting the likelihood that a company will have a centralized IT structure. The Chi-squared value for the overall model was 37.98 with 3 degrees of freedom (significant at the 0.0001 level). Table 5 presents the results of running a stepwise logit regression analysis on the dependent variable. This procedure selected three variables for inclusion in the model. The model as a whole is statistically significant as indicated by the P-value (0.0001). The maximum likelihood estimates of the logistic regression indicate that all the signs are in the predicted direction. These results indicate that hypotheses 2, 3 and 5 are supported, with the variable management ownership relating to a centralized information technology structure, in a positive way; and stockholders' equity to debt ratio and stock concentration relating to IT centralization negatively. The board composition and size of the corporation had no significant direct effect on IT centralization. In other words, hypotheses 1 and 4 were not supported by the analysis.

There is at least one feasible explanation for the finding that there is no relationship between the number of outside directors and IT structure: management may succeed in getting outside directors elected to the board who are either incapable or unwilling to properly control management and the organization. In this eventuality, outside directors would not be providing the monitoring services contracted for by the shareholders.

The study also failed to support the hypothesis of agency cost as exemplified by the size of the corporation. A possible explanation for this unexpected result is the choice of the experimental and control groups. As mentioned in the methodology section, the choice of the corporations was based on a matched-pair design, and the matching variable was size as measured by revenues. In other words, the impact, if any, of the size variable was neutralized through the sample selection process. Another possible reason is that the structure of IT may be related to the design and complexity of the organization more than to its size.\*

Before drawing this paper to its conclusion, it is important to place the above analysis in context by identifying the limitation of the study. First, the results should be viewed as descriptive in view of the author's inability to control for various changes in the context variables of the experimental and control groups during the period of this study. Second, some variables which are thought to affect organizational structure and, consequently, IT structure, like R&D spending and the organization's information intensity, were omitted from the analysis because of unavailability of data on these variables. $^{†}$ Third, the data in this study were collected through a variety of means and from different sources, including a mail questionnaire. The data, therefore, are subject to the well-known limitations of such surveys. Although efforts were made to reduce these problems in the design and pretest of the survey instrument, the risk of introducing some bias in the results cannot be eliminated entirely.

## Conclusion

This study identifies a number of characteristics that are significantly related to the structure of IT. These characteristics were empirically identified as management equity ownership, stockholders' equity, and the level of concentration of stock holding. The finding that companies with relatively higher stockholders' equity to debt ratio tend to adopt a more decentralized structure supports the premise of agency theory that large stockholders' equity means a larger number of shareholders, and consequently a higher likelihood of conflict between management and stockholders. To decrease this conflict stockholders will push managers to decentralization in an attempt to delegate power and authority to a larger number of managers thus decreasing the likelihood of the management to shirk, consume an excess amount of perks, and/or engage in non-value maximizing investments. Each of these actions may affect the revenues and/or expenses (and therefore the value) of the firm. Firms with decentralized authority are less likely to experience these distortions in their value.

Our analysis supports the third hypothesis that companies with higher management ownership tend to prefer a centralized structure which allows them to exercise tighter control over their wealth. The more shares managers own, the higher the likelihood of a centralized IT structure. The study also supports the fifth hypothesis that companies with high concentration level of stock holding tend to have a decentralized IT structure.

The objective of this paper was to identify those company-specific characteristics that affect the structure of information technology in the organization. Different organizations subscribe to various organizational structures as well as various information technology (IT) structures, ranging from highly centralized to highly decentralized. Previous research concerning IT structure centred around the 'organizational fit' concept which maintains that to avoid causing organizational friction, an organization should structure its IT resources to conform with its organizational context variables, such as decision-making structure, organizational size, managerial philosophy, etc.

This research has shown that the structure of information technology is correlated with management ownership structure and company governance. Managers who own a relatively high proportion of total equity have greater incentives to exercise tighter control over company's operations; consequently, they would tend to prefer a centralized IT function. On the other hand, in a firm where the shareholders' equity to debt ratio is relatively high, the ownership of such firm would be generally more diversified than the ownership of a firm with lower equity to debt ratio. This diversity tends to cause more conflicts among managers and shareholders in the firm. In this situation, the owners will push the managers to delegate authority down the hierarchy and which would lead to decentralized decisions and, consequently, decentralized IT structure. Managers are willing to decentralize decision-making in such organizations in order to decrease agency costs. Although self-interest motivations exist in a firm, the welfare of the individual (management and stockholders) depends on the success of the firm. Therefore, by decentralizing the firm's operations, conflict among members of the firm is reduced, and consequently, the perceived cost of agency is, also, reduced.
