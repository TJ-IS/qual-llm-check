# Process and product success in information systems development

Timo Saarinen\* and Markku Sääksjärvi\*\*

Helsinki School of Economics and Business Administration, Runeberginkatu 14-16, SF-00100 Helsinki, Finland

Several guidelines and methods for ensuring success of development projects have been introduced in the information systems (IS) literature. In this paper, their contribution to explaining variation in different success criteria are empirically investigated with the data gathered from recent IS development projects in major Finnish companies. Competent system analysts and users, appropriate development methods and management support were found important for the success of the development process. These factors did not, however, explain the success of an IS as a product in terms of its quality and its impact on the organization. Instead, product success was heavily dependent on the characteristics of the investment, especially the novelty of the system and the specificity of the design. Advanced IS organization was positively related with both process and product success, but two widely promoted mechanisms for initiating development projects — steering committee and strategic IS planning — were not positively associated with any of the success criteria. The results of this study imply that managers should emphasize different factors in IS development depending on the type of investment and relevant success criteria in each situation and that there is a clear need for research on finding and justifying new IS investments providing an appropriate balance between expected payoff and risk.

Keywords: information systems, implementation, effectiveness, success, user satisfaction

Implementation of computer-based information systems (IS) has been found to be problematic in many companies. This has motivated researchers to actively investigate the reasons for success and failure $^{1-4}$ . However, many of the guidelines and methods developed to overcome the implementation problems seem to fall short. This is evidenced by continuing reports of large numbers of failures $^{5,6}$ . Even though the reports may be exaggerated, it appears that the results of many IS investments are more or less disappointing, both to the users and managers.

In the past, applications utility has been extended from transaction processing to support for decision making and control of the organization. Also, many systems supporting operational activities have recently been developed, which support changes in organizational structures and inter-organizational communication and cooperation. The focus in success evaluation has changed accordingly. Besides assessment of the success of the development and use processes, the quality of the IS product and its impact on the organization must be evaluated more carefully than was done hitherto $^{7,8}$ .

In this paper we examine contribution of various factors, such as the state of the IS function, management involvement in project initiation, the investment type, the quality of development resources and the use of different development methods, on success of IS development projects. We seek to explain success and develop management guidelines for IS development projects. We especially focus on analysing possible differences in factors explaining success and failure when using different evaluation criteria for IS investments.

The empirical analyses of 48 recent IS development projects in major Finnish companies show that factors affecting process and product success are, indeed, different. This finding has implications for both researchers and practitioners. Researchers should choose success criteria carefully so that they can be influenced by the independent variables being studied. Managers should emphasize different factors, depending on the relevant success criteria in each situation. We were also able to identify some interesting differences in factors that explain success and those that explain failure. For example, advanced IS function is related to high success but complexity of the system and development project is a good predictor of failure. High levels of system analyst ability are typical for successful projects while low levels of user ability are often encountered in failed projects. Finally, it was discovered that management involvement in project initiation is associated with process success, but does not explain emergence of the most promising IS investments. There is still a clear need for future research on where and how proposals for the best IS investments are derived.

This paper starts with a review of factors affecting IS success and continues with a discussion about different criteria for measuring success of an IS development project. Methodology for the empirical survey and findings from the data analyses are described and discussed next. Finally, some conclusions are drawn at the end of the paper.

## Factors affecting project success

Investigation of the reasons for success and failure of IS investments has fascinated researchers for a long time. Kwon and Zmud $^{4}$ summarized results from such investigations in five different research traditions: factor studies, mutual understanding research, process studies, political research and prescriptive research. According to Kwon and Zmud $^{4}$ , factor studies have identified management support, high quality design, designer-user interaction, and motivated and capable users to be regularly related to success $^{1}$ . Mutual understanding research has concentrated on the quality of the designer-user interaction and reached an agreement that more, rather than less interaction is better (see a review in Ives and Olson $^{9}$ ). Process studies focus on the stages of organizational change process that is associated with the introduction of a new system. These studies have found commitment to the change and implementation effort, and extensive project definition and planning to be the most important determinants of the success of the change process $^{10, 11}$ . The political research stream has been able to show that many seemingly irrational or inconsistent behaviours can be understood by looking at the different goals of and consequences for all stakeholders $^{12}$ . Prescriptive studies try to identify implementation risk factors and give advice on what strategies should be used to overcome these risks (for example, Alter $^{13}$ ).

The above explanations for success are mostly based on the quality of the development resources and methods that are used to ensure success of the development process. But, effective utilization of computers often requires a long learning period. As a result, qualified development resources and proper methods are not enough; organizations should have also advanced infrastructure and applications portfolio, as a basis for the development of the most promising systems $^{14-16}$ . Furthermore, an attitude of risk taking is often needed. If developers are only imitating other organizations using standard applications, the resulting benefits may be marginal or even negative (for example, Dos Santos, Peffers and Mauer $^{17}$ ). The type of the system is often an important prerequisite for success and therefore also initiation of the investments should be taken into account in studying success $^{18, 19}$ .

In order to study the contribution of these various factors on success, we combined them into five categories: state of the IS function, management involvement in project initiation, type of the investment, quality of development resources and use of different development methods.

## State of the IS function

State of the IS function represents the cumulated investments in IS and describes the ability of the IS function to mediate the IS investments to realize benefits (for example Nolan $^{14, 15}$ ). Taking full advantage of IS often requires a long development and learning period. When information systems are diffused throughout the organization, the ability to effectively integrate organizational activities within and between companies emerges. Infrastructure of the IS and system analysts', users' and managers' capabilities have developed sufficiently to permit the initiation and implementation of development projects for which the probabilities of success are high. Therefore, both the state of the IS in the corporate level and also in the business unit in which the project is carried out should be taken into account when studying the success of IS development projects.

Cheney and Dickson $^{20}$ found that well-managed IS functions tended to produce higher levels of user satisfaction. Mahmood and Becker $^{16}$ found a correlation between maturity of the IS function and user information satisfaction. These findings support the belief that more mature IS organizations are indeed capable of producing better applications and services to their users, and lead to expectations of a positive association between state of the IS function and success of IS development projects.

## Management involvement in project initiation

Management involvement, especially in the selection, goal setting and project initiation phases early in the development life-cycle, has been found important in earlier studies $^{1, 18, 21}$ . IS steering committees and strategic IS planning projects, in which managers can initiate development projects and adequate management support is assured, are commonly used mechanisms to achieve management involvement $^{19}$ . If managers are involved in the initiation of the projects it is more likely that the systems support company strategies and help increase the organization's performance. It should also be easier to get adequate resources for implementing such applications.

## Investment type

Characteristics of the investment have received only limited attention as an explanation for success as indicated in an extensive review of IS success studies by Hirschheim and Lyytinen $^{2}$ . This is surprising, since from the management point of view, selection of the investment may determine if the project can ever be a success or not. If managers merely replace old systems just with new versions of technology, only efficiency is improved. It may be necessary to develop common, widely used systems, but it is not a way to gain competitive advantage. High pay-off systems have to be new and company specific. Unfortunately, the results of development in such cases are also highly uncertain. In addition, large and complex systems are more difficult to implement than small and simple ones. Based on these observations characteristics of the system in which the investment is made seem likely to affect the success that is achieved.

## Quality of development resources

Many studies have found quality of the development resources important for success. Researchers have frequently stressed the importance of capable and motivated users for success $^{1,4,9,22,23}$ . Also, system analysts' capabilities and commitment to the project have been found essential $^{1,23,24}$ . In some studies it is argued that a balanced combination of system developers and users is, in fact, what is needed $^{23}$ . Based on these results there are high expectations that quality of the development resources is an important determinant of success.

## Use of different development methods

Implementation methods have been widely studied in the IS literature. In these studies it is commonly argued that no approach, method, tool or technique is superior in every situation, but different development strategies should be adopted in different contexts. Many risk factors have been identified and managers have consequently been advised on what methods should be used to manage these risks $^{13,25-28}$ . Burns and Dennis $^{27}$ compressed the implementation risk factors into two basic concepts; complexity and uncertainty. Mathiassen and Stage $^{28}$ accordingly summarized the available implementation strategies into two broad categories: rational and experimental approaches. Approaches having a rational ideal correspond to high design quality and extensive project definition and planning. Rational approaches apply such analytical techniques as abstraction, decomposition and structured methods. Experimental approaches correspond to intensive designer-user interaction applying active user participation and experimentation using such means as prototyping. In practice, both approaches are needed: when more complexity is involved in the situation the rational approach is needed more, and when more uncertainty is involved the experimental approach is needed more. When both complexity and uncertainty are high a mixed methodology is called for. Saarinen and Vepsäläinen $^{29}$ developed these ideas further by formulating a model for proper allocation of development effort between the two approaches. Based on the basic principle of the model not only the use of different implementation approaches but an appropriate mix of them is important for success.

The choice of development methods should also take the make-or-buy decision into account. As an alternative to internal development, ready-made products or development resources can be bought or contracted. Use of internal and market resources should be balanced by a procurement strategy suitable for each situation. Common systems having low uncertainty can effectively utilize extensive market procurement $^{30-32}$ . The more company specific and uncertain systems are, the more one should, however, rely on internal development strategies to avoid unnecessary risks and ensure availability of specific resources and knowledge for the project $^{30-32}$ .

## Criteria for the success of an IS development project

Success of an IS development project is difficult to measure. Managers would often prefer to use cost-benefit analyses and traditional investment analyses, applying such criteria as the return on investment, net present value or pay-back period. Although IS investments often share features with investments to improve production capabilities, in many cases the benefits are difficult, if not impossible, to measure. In addition they may also be easy to manipulate. This is because many of the benefits are either intangible or are realized only after a long time. Furthermore, IS development projects can also be seen as organizational development investments. Economic evaluation and quantitative measures alone are not usually adequate to capture the benefits of such investments; the use of subjective evaluation and multiple and diversified criteria is often necessary $^{33-35}$ .

One of the best known of the subjective measurement instruments for IS success is user information satisfaction (UIS); UIS allows comparisons of measured performance across organizations, systems and measurement points $^{36-39}$ . UIS has been widely accepted even though criticized, especially for neglecting essential issues related to success $^{40-44}$ . For that reason, we extended it in our recent study so as to better support comprehensive assessment of IS development projects $^{8,45}$ . While the UIS questionnaire measures success indirectly by IS services and quality of the IS product, our approach also covers success of the development process (investment costs and efficient use of resources) and impact of the IS on the organization (benefits of the investment). The main structure of our measurement instrument is illustrated in Figure 1.

We developed measurement scales for each of the four constructs — success of the development process, success of the use process, quality of the IS product and impact of the IS on the organization — and empirically tested them. The detailed items of the questionnaires and structure of the scales are described in Appendix II.

Our survey data indicated that project managers and line managers have different perceptions of success and goal achievement. Project managers' perceptions of success are heavily dependent on the success of the development process; line managers seem to have broader goal structure, taking not only development process, but also its results (quality and impact of the IS product) into consideration when assessing IS investments. These findings inspired us to study whether not only the relevant criteria for success are different for project and line managers, but also if factors influencing them are different.

## Survey methodology

## Data collection procedure

To investigate factors affecting success criteria, we carried out a survey in major Finnish companies. The data were collected from the 200 largest companies and 25 largest banks and insurance companies in Finland $^{45}$ . Altogether, 272 IS managers from these organizations were contacted by mail and asked to contribute to our study (some companies have an IS manager for each business unit). A short questionnaire sent to the IS managers asked for a list of all projects finished in the past two years with a brief evaluation of their success. We also asked the IS managers to name the two most recently implemented information systems for our detailed review, and asked them to name the project manager and the line manager responsible for the system so that we could send more detailed questionnaires to them.

![](/api/attachments/2GA5F586/fulltext/images/1bc5e737c7a0b6775c4b72021f668649bb58163612520f788ef648d88b464175.jpg)  
Figure 2. Framework of factors affecting different success criteria in an IS development project

Within one month 102 IS managers returned the first questionnaire. However, 47 of them were not able or willing to participate in the study and returned an empty questionnaire. The main reasons were that they had no new information systems implemented in the past two years, the company was just reorganized, the IS manager was recently nominated or they were too busy to participate. The remaining 55 companies form a representative sample of the population in terms of the industry and size of the company. The IS managers in these companies gave their brief evaluation of success from a total of 247 information systems. They also indicated 101 most recently implemented systems for our detailed analyses (some organizations had not implemented two systems in the past two years). Furthermore they gave us the names of project and line managers of these systems and later on we received responses from 70 project managers and 62 line managers to the detailed questionnaires.

These response rates were achieved by reminder letters and several telephone calls. Altogether we have 48 responses containing evaluations from both project and line managers forming basis for our analyses in this paper. Detailed tests of these projects assured us that we have a representative sample of 247 projects completed in large Finnish companies with regard to the type of the system, application area, and overall level of success assessed by IS managers $^{45}$ .

Profile of the participating companies and the information systems

The average net sales of the companies studied was FIM 1906 million per year (in 1989 FIM 1 = US \$0.25), and the average employment was 2317. Around two-thirds of the companies were manufacturing; one-fifth were retail and wholesale companies. Less than 10 per cent of the companies came from banking or insurance, and the rest came from the service sector.

One-third of the systems under study were designed to support accounting and one-third marketing. One-fifth of the systems supported manufacturing activities, and the rest of the systems business administration or purchasing. The average budget of the information systems studied was FIM 1.2 million, the average duration of the development project was 17 months, and the average of the total effort was 41 man months. Three-quarters of the system had been especially made for the utilizing organization; one-quarter of the projects had been based on tailoring an existing software package. However, in all projects a thorough requirement analysis had been carried out, so the sample did not include pure off-the-shelf package installations. However, quite often development resources had been contacted from outside suppliers. On average as much as 35 per cent of total development effort came from outside sources. Overall, the projects were quite regular business information system projects in large companies.

## Key variables

Next we describe what measures for the key concepts we use and how these measures are constructed. The sources, scales and descriptive statistics are given in Appendix I for the independent variables and in Appendix II for the dependent variables.

State of the IS function. This is measured by the coverage of applications portfolio and maturity of the IS function, compared to that of competitors. They both are measured in the corporate level evaluated by the IS managers and in the business unit level evaluated by the line managers.

Management involvement in project initiation. This is measured by the initiative, whether top-down from management or bottom-up from users or IS department, and by the procedure for project initiation, as a part of a strategic IS planning project or as a one-shot activity. Also, existence of an IS steering committee is recorded. Finally, management support for the project is measured by a single question asked of the project managers.

Investment type. Specificity of the system is defined as an average of two attributes: uniqueness of the supported object system and extent of existing system/knowledge is used as a basis for requirements. Uncertainty is measured by the level of characteristics of the information system that are expected to cause problems in the identification of user needs. The detailed questions posed to system analysts measure the clarity and stability of requirements. The composite uncertainty measure is an average. Complexity is measured as a composite of the number of users, development effort, development cost and duration of the project. These measures were transformed into seven-point scales and the composite complexity measure was computed as an average of them. Novelty of the system is a measure of how much of the system is new, instead of replacing an existing system.

Quality of development resources. Measurement of users' and system analysts' abilities is based on two similar multi-item scales. The detailed questions measure the level of DP skills, knowledge about supported business, ability to communicate and commitment to the project. Both scales are constructed as an average of the responses to questions using retrospective evaluation before the project started. In order to improve reliability of the measurement both users' and system analysts' DP skills are evaluated by the project managers: knowledge about the supported business is evaluated by the line managers. Other items are assessed by third parties: users' abilities are evaluated by the project managers and system analysts' abilities are evaluated by the line managers.

Use of different development methods. In this study use of rational implementation approach is measured by the extent that object system modelling is used in the requirement specification phase and life-cycle strategy, tight management control and formal development methods throughout the project. Use of experimental implementation approach is measured by the level of user participation, the use of prototyping in the requirement specification phase and the use of evolutionary methods in the design and construction phases. User participation is measured as an average of three items: the proportion of project members who are users, the share of all users represented in the project and the users' share of the total development effort. Implementation approach is finally analysed using experimentation rate, a ratio of the use of experimental implementation approach and the sum of experimental and rational approaches.

Procurement strategy is measured in two ways: the level of using outside resources in the project and use of a software package as a basis for the system.

Success. Measurement of success is based on project and line managers' perceptions on multiple attributes expected to be important for success $^{8,45}$ . Success is measured in four dimensions: success of the development process, success of the use process, quality of the IS product and impact of the IS on the utilizing organization. Success of the development process is measured by the evaluation, after implementation, of the adequacy of IS staff and user abilities, success of the development phases, and controllability of the development process. Success of the use process is evaluated, after implementation, as the adequacy of the capabilities of IS staff and users involved in the use process, effective communication between them and consequent responsive IS services. Quality of the IS product is measured by the qualified and flexible user interface and format, reliability and relevancy of the information provided to the users. Impact of the IS on the organization is measured by the changes due to use of the system with positive impact on the organization's efficiency and profitability, decision making and control as well as communication processes and reorganization of the organization. The detailed questions and structure for each of the four scales are described in Appendix II. For more details see Saarinen $^{8}$ .

## Findings

In this section we present results from the analyses of the factors affecting the success of the development projects. Using univariate statistics we first analyse relationships between single independent variables and success. We then use multivariate statistical methods to study the effects of all the independent variables, taken together, on each of the success criteria.

Relationships between single independent variables and project success

State of the IS function. Table 1 shows correlations between independent variables and success criteria. Coverage of the applications portfolio and maturity of the IS function, as perceived by both IS manager and line manager, are positively correlated with success. However, while coverage of the applications portfolio in the business unit is significantly correlated with the impact of the IS on the organization; the corresponding corporate level measure is not correlated with organizational impact. In general, correlations between the state of the IS in the business unit and success are even higher than those between the state of the IS in the corporate level and success. These findings clearly support the importance of the state of the IS for both process and product success. It also seems that advanced IS function in the corporate level has strong associations especially with process success and quality of the IS product. Advanced IS function in the business unit also has high correlations with product success variables, including impact of the IS on the organization.

Management involvement in project initiation. Steering committees and strategic IS planning have no association with any of the success criteria. However, management initiation has positive correlations with the quality of the IS product. In addition, the level of management support is highly correlated with the success of the development and use process, but not with the quality of the IS product and its impact on the organization. Most of the correlations between management involvement in project initiation and success are surprisingly weak.

Investment type. Results in Table 1 indicate that systems with low complexity often have high quality and systems with low requirements uncertainty have succeeded best in the use process. The most important finding is, however, that specific and new systems have high impact on the organization. It seems rather clear that the type of system is an important explanation for the product success.

Quality of development resources. As expected users' and system analysts' abilities have high correlations with project success. However, it was interesting to find out that they correlate strongly with the process success variables but only system analysts' abilities correlate weakly also with product success variables. It seems that not even best users and developers can guarantee product success which, in many cases, may be uncontrollable for them.

Use of different development methods. As Table 1 shows, experimentation rate is negatively correlated with process success. This means that conventionally developed systems employing rational implementation approaches succeed better than projects relying heavily on experimentation, in terms of the success of the development process. Otherwise implementation approaches and use of outside resources do not have any association with the success criteria. However, use of software packages has a strong negative correlation with all other success variables except the success of the use process. This indicates that the use of software packages has not been successful for systems included in our study. However, this does not mean that they could not be appropriate for smaller organizations and for systems with more standard requirements or smaller size than the systems in our sample.

## Joint effects of the independent variables on project success

Because of many possible interrelationships between the independent variables we analyse also their joint impact on success by using multivariate statistical analyses. In order to carry out the analysis we first divided the 48 projects into three groups of as equal size as possible according to the level of process success.

The first group contains the cases that failed, the second group contains the cases that succeeded at an average level, and the third group contains the most successful projects according to process success. The same procedure is repeated for the other three success criteria. These classified success variables are used as dependent variables when applying a discriminant analyses technique to find out which factors predict high or low values for each success criteria. For each of the four analyses we use two discriminant functions and a stepwise procedure minimizing Wilks Lambda in order to identify variables that are most important for discrimination. At each step the variable leading to the most significant change in Wilks Lambda is entered in or removed from the analysis. Determination of the significance is based on an F statistic. The results are combined into Table 2. The columns show the standardized canonical discriminant function coefficients for all four analyses. The rows at the bottom show the significance of the discriminant functions, means of the discriminant scores evaluated at group means and reclassification rates. Even though the reclassification rate was lower in the analysis for the quality of the IS product than in the other analyses the discrimination can be regarded as successful for all four criteria. As the results show, however, quite different factors were important for explaining success and failure in each of the four analyses.

Success of the development process. The initial columns of Table 2 show results from the discriminant analysis for the success of the development process. Failures have negative values for both discriminant functions. Averages have negative values for the first function and positive values for the second function while successes have positive values for the first function and negative for the second one. ● high coverage of applications in the corporate level

● high level of management support

These results provide us with quite a bit of additional information about the predictors for success and failure of the development process. First, coverage of applications portfolio both in the corporate and business unit level is important. However, results from the discriminant analyses show that high coverage in the corporate level is related with success while low coverage in the business unit is more a predictor of failure. Management support for the project is typical for the projects that succeed in development process. But an interesting observation is that systems initiated in strategic IS planning projects tend to fail in the development phase. The reason might be that they are more complex and requirements are more uncertain than in the other projects, making them more difficult to develop. It may also be that the estimation of the budget and development schedule is difficult because of the lack of experience with these kinds of systems. High abilities of both system analysts and users are needed but while high system analysts' abilities is an important characteristic explaining success, poor user abilities is a powerful predictor of failure. Use of software packages is clearly related with failure in development process, as already shown by the univariate analyses. It should be remembered, however, that the systems in this sample were often tailored quite extensively, making them more difficult to implement than off-the-shelf installations.

Success of the use process. Second columns in Table 2 show results from the discriminant analysis for the success of the use process. Failure projects have negative values and averages have positive values for both discriminant functions. Success cases have positive values for the first discriminant function and negative values for the second one. Based on the analysis projects succeeding well in the use process can be described as having the following characteristics:

\- high coverage of applications both in the corporate level and in the business unit and high maturity of the IS in the corporate level

● project initiated by the management and high level of management support

● high experimentation rate

● high level of using outside resources.

The typical features of the failures in use process are:

● project initiated in a strategic IS planning project

● system with high specificity

● system with high requirements uncertainty

\- use of software packages.

Coverage and maturity of the IS function seem to be very important for getting the use process to succeed. Projects initiated by management and having high management support have succeeded in use process while projects initiated in strategic IS planning projects have failed. It may be that they change the conventional organizational procedures requiring also more extensive adaptation of the IS function than the other projects do.

Company specific systems with high uncertainty are often related with failure in the use process. The reason may be that these types of systems are under constant pressure of change, at least in the early stages of their life-cycles, the period covered in this study. Use of software packages is related with failure, but outside developers in combination with high experimentation, i.e. prototyping and user participation, are good predictors for the success of the use process. It is possible that users have to take active roles and learn about the system already in the early stages of the lifecycle and at the same time good relationships with the IS people are established helping to get the service during the use phase, too.

Quality of the IS product. As the third columns at the foot of Table 2 show, failures have positive values and average cases have negative values for both discriminant functions. Successful projects have negative values for the first discriminant function and positive values for the second function. The following characteristics are typical for the projects having produced high quality IS products:

\- high coverage of applications in the business unit
- project initiated by the management.

Typical project producing a system of low quality can be described as follows:

● project initiated by a steering committee - high complexity and high requirements uncertainty
- use of software packages.

It seems that advanced IS function, especially covering applications portfolio in the business unit, is very important for producing systems with high quality. In these environments there is likely to be an integrated set of systems forming a basis for developing new systems providing management with high quality information. If the system is initiated by management it will have good possibilities to succeed, but if it is initiated by a steering committee it is likely to be of lower quality. In systems with high complexity and requirements uncertainty is often seen to be the failure. Also, systems based on software packages are related with low quality of the IS product. Overall advanced IS function, active management role and specially made systems seem to be the most important factors explaining the quality of the IS as a product.

Impact of the IS on the organization. Results from the discriminant analysis for the impact of the IS on the organization are shown in the final columns of Table 2. Failure projects have negative values and averages have positive values for both discriminant functions. Success cases have positive values for the first discriminant function and negative values for the second one. The standardized discriminant function coefficients indicate that projects with high impact on the organizations have the following features:

● high coverage of applications both in the corporate level and in the business unit

● high specificity and novelty

● high level of using outside resources.

Systems with low impact on the organization can be described as follows:

\- high complexity

● high requirements uncertainty

● use of software packages.

Advanced IS function is important for achieving high impact of the system on the utilizing organization. In such an environment it is likely that new systems with high asset specificity, but relatively simple and certain requirements, are developed. This type of system cannot be based on software packages. Successful systems have been especially made, often using extensively outside resources. The characteristics of the system seem to explain high impact on the organization quite well. However, the analyses do not explain where, how and by whom the best IS development projects are initiated.

## Limitations of the study

There are some limitations for generalizing the above results. Firstly, the framework used in the study was sufficient for demonstrating that the factors explaining process and product success are different. However, it is not a comprehensive model containing all possible factors explaining project success. Therefore, the differences/similarities of other potential factors affecting process and product success remain open. Secondly, use of perceived measures may affect on the results. However, many significant relationships between variables based on the answers of project and line managers indicate that the results are not seriously distorted by the halo-effect. Thirdly, the sample used in the study may be biased. All systems in the sample are based on thorough requirements analysis, making them more suitable for internal development than for market procurement. Furthermore, the projects are rather large, leaving out of the study, for example, DSS projects and expert systems in which user capabilities and participation may have an effect also on product success. Fourthly, due to the small sample size reliability of the statistical analyses was decreased. Finally, the study measures success at a point of time, rather than over a long period following systems implementation. As a result the observed impacts may seriously underestimate actual impacts (see Dos Santos and Peffers $^{46}$ ).

## Conclusions

Our primary research question in this paper was whether the factors affecting the success of the development and use processes are different from the factors affecting the quality of the IS product and its impact on the organization. The findings give substantial evidence that they are indeed different. This observation has implications for both researchers and practitioners. The researchers have to choose carefully the success measures for each study so that they can be influenced by the independent variables being the object of interest. The managers should emphasize different factors depending on the success criteria relevant in each situation.

The results showed that management support, capable system analysts and users as well as proper development methods are related to process success. Characteristics of the investment are associated with the quality of the IS product and its impact on the organization and the state of the IS function is related with both process and product success. Rather surprisingly, steering committees and strategic IS planning do not have any positive effects on success.

Even though not conclusive, our findings indicate some practical ways to improve success of development projects. Firstly, in spite of the fact that management involvement does not affect product success, line management should be tightly involved in initiating IS investments so that their support is achieved and adequate resources are allocated to the implementation of the systems. Secondly, that improving capabilities of both system analysts and users may increase success cannot be overly emphasized.

In this study we did not find any predictors for the emergence of the most promising IS investments. Neither capabilities of participating people nor steering committees and strategic IS planning ensured product success. The use of economic theories could help find investments with proper balance between expected payoff and risk. An inspiring environment and appropriate incentives may also explain product success. However, further studies are clearly needed to explore the best opportunities for IS investments and development of organizational assets.
