# The implementation of an electronic market for pig trading in Singapore

Boon Siong Neo

Information Management Research Centre (IMARC), School of Accountancy and Business, Nanyang Technological University, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 2263

Electronic markets have been described as strategic means for altering the structure of industry forces for competitive gains. However, there has been little description of the realities of implementing electronic markets. Further, it has been generally assumed that strategic information technology (IT) initiatives are aimed at competitive business gains within the industry. How IT may be used by government agencies to regulate the structure of industries for social and regulatory purposes has not been explored. Using a case study of Singapore's Hog Auction Market (HAM), this paper describes the realities of implementing an electronic market to change the pig trading industry for market efficiency and social objectives. The impacts of HAM on the Singapore pig trading industry are described and lessons relevant for implementing electronic markets in other contexts discussed.

Keywords: implementation, electronic markets, inter-organizational systems

Over the past few years authors have advocated the use of electronic markets for strategic gains $^{1-6}$ . These writings primarily illustrate how electronic markets are increasingly used as a means for market coordination as the costs of electronic communications fall and as the ability to convey complex information through networks increases. Most published cases of electronic markets described their strategic positive impacts without revealing the problems that were encountered in their implementation $^{1,7-9}$ .

This paper describes a case whereby an electronic market was used by a government-linked company expressly to influence and change the forces of the pig trading industry in Singapore. It describes how market participants initially reacted negatively to the implementation of the Hog Auction Market (HAM) system, and how the flare-up was eventually resolved through regulatory means.

The next section summarizes the main arguments of the electronic market literature and recent calls to be more wary of the strategic IT ‘promises’. This is followed by a brief discussion, using the implementation literature, why it is that one should expect resistance to electronic market implementation. In describing the HAM case itself, we first discuss the pig trading industry in Singapore prior to HAM’s implementation, and show how prevailing market conditions led to the development of HAM. We then describe the HAM system and how it was used, and show how HAM was designed to change the pig trading process. The implementation of HAM, the market reactions, and how the resistance was resolved are then described. Finally we evaluate the implementation of HAM and discuss lessons that should be noted for implementing electronic markets in general.

## Literature background

Bakos $^{1}$ defined an electronic market as “an inter-organizational information system that allows the participating buyers and sellers to exchange information about prices and product offerings”. The interorganizational systems and electronic markets literature have essentially used Porter’s $^{10}$ industry structure model and generic strategies to illustrate how such systems have the potential to secure strategic gains for the initiating firm by lowering costs, differentiating products and services from substitutes, erecting barriers to entry, raising customers’ switching costs, improving bargaining power with suppliers, and changing the basis of competition with rivals $^{1-3, 5, 6, 11-14}$ .

Recently, some researchers have raised cautions about the difficulties of realizing competitive advantage through the use of IT per se. Laudon and Turner $^{15}$ cautioned that “the ability of organizations to use IT for sustained competitive advantage was far more difficult than many thought. Implementation of strategic systems is much harder than appreciated.” Similarly, Emery $^{16}$ warned that it would be a delusion to think that strategic advantage could be met through an independent strategic system. Scott Morton $^{17}$ indicated that MIT's Management in the 1990s research program found little evidence to support the contention that sustainable advantage can be achieved from IT apart from other organizational distinctive competencies. Vitale $^{18}$ warned that there are significant risks in implementing a strategic IT application, even when a system may appear to have been successful. Kim and Michelman $^{19}$ found power struggles between doctors and administrators to be a barrier to using IT strategically in hospitals. Similarly, Lederer and Nath $^{20}$ warned that the implementation of strategic systems can be expected to be difficult because of the extent of organizational change required.

The case to be discussed involved the use of an electronic market for pig trading in Singapore to complement the traditional arrangements between buyers and sellers. It is an example of the growing literature of the use of IT by entities other than traditional profit-making business firms. For examples, Lindsey et al. $^{21}$ described the strategic use of the TELCOT system by a cotton growers' cooperative in Texas, and Venkatraman and Kambil $^{22}$ described the electronic tax return filing implemented by the US Internal Revenue Service. As we begin to examine how IT is being used internationally, we can expect government-related institutions to play a much bigger role in IT diffusion and use than those described in the existing literature $^{23}$ . HAM represents the use of IT by a government-linked organization to achieve business, social and regulatory objectives.

Research in IT implementation has highlighted the need to incorporate the political and organizational change perspectives when designing and planning for IT applications in an organization $^{24-28}$ . Resistance to IT implementation is to be expected because of various reasons, including human personalities, system design weaknesses, or social and political causes $^{29}$ . Using an electronic market in an inter-organizational context means that the impacts of IT would be felt beyond a single organization to other external, independent entities. If changing an organization's internal structure and processes through an IT application is difficult, changing industry structure (consisting of many external organizations with varying levels of access to resources) can be expected to be even more challenging. It would be naive not to expect independent profit-minded firms to resist the implementation of electronic markets, to play 'implementation games' $^{27}$ , and to retaliate against the initiating organization $^{10}$ . The HAM case shows the strong reactions of affected players despite the fact that they were dealing with a government-linked organization with access to the regulatory powers of related government agencies. It describes how issues of risks, power struggles and retaliation by affected parties were managed by the initiating organization.

## Pork consumption in Singapore

Singapore is a small island nation situated at the southern end of the Malayan peninsula in South-east Asia, and just north of the equator. It has limited land and no natural resources, and depends on trade as its lifeline. Almost everything that Singapore needs for its survival and progress are imported, including most of the food and water it consumes. It has a market economy with a democratically elected government that had been returned to power in free elections held every few years since 1959.

The constraints on land caused Singapore to phase out most of its farms in the 1980s, and it now imports almost all its food requirements from neighbouring countries. The Primary Production Department (PPD), a government agency, ensures that all imported food items are fit for consumption through a variety of control and regulatory mechanisms, like certifying suppliers' farms, licensing importers, issuing import permits, and inspecting food items for compliance to health and hygiene standards. The government has a wholly-owned private company, Primary Industries (PI), to process and distribute meats, produce and seafood, and to provide a range of services to the food-related industries.

Singaporeans have traditionally preferred fresh food items to frozen ones, which are perceived to be inferior in quality, nutrition and taste. Most families shop for fresh food daily in conveniently located open market stalls, although the shopping patterns are slowly changing with both spouses working in many younger families. The stall holders typically start their day in the early hours of the morning when they buy their produce from wholesalers, and bring it to the open markets for display and sale beginning at about 7 a.m. Meat sellers buy their poultry/animals from the importers, arrange for the slaughtering of the poultry/animals in abattoirs, and collect the meats for display and sale in their respective stalls when the market begins. Most market stalls wind down their business and close for the day by about 11 a.m. Fresh produce and meats are also sold in supermarkets, which have business hours from about 9 a.m. to 10 p.m., catering to the needs of working couples and higher income families.

Pork is the main meat item consumed by the Chinese, which make up 75% of Singapore's population of 3 million in this multi-racial and multi-lingual society. Pork is not easily substitutable in Chinese diets because of its extensive use in daily cooking, and other customary and religious festivals. During 1989, 1.1 million pigs were consumed by Singaporeans. With pig farming already phased out in land-scarce Singapore, all pigs are now imported from neighbouring countries. Malaysia is the main supplier, trucking between 2000 to 3000 pigs to Singapore daily, accounting for 85% of all pig imports. Indonesia supplies the remaining pigs, which are shipped in daily.

Pigs are classified into four main types. The largest group, about 90% of all pigs sold, consist of porkers. They weigh about 80 kg each, and are usually used for home and restaurant consumption because of the high quality of the meat. Sucklings are pigs that are just two weeks old, and are typically sold to restaurants to be roasted for wedding parties and other celebration feasts. Runts weigh about 30 kg each, and are bought for home wedding parties and certain religious festivals. Sows are the balance stock, sold after about six reproductive cycles, and are the lowest quality of pigs on the market. They are sold to food processors, and are used in the popular barbequed meats and other cooked foods.

## Singapore's traditional pig trading process

Pig farms in Malaysia and Indonesia must be certified for minimum hygiene standards set by PPD before they are allowed to export pigs to Singapore. Once a farm is approved by PPD, a farm number is assigned to identify consignments of pigs from the farm. Each farm is allocated an ‘export quota’ based on the number of sows that it has. About 120 foreign farms have been certified by PPD.

There were no licensing requirements for pig importers before May 1990. Since Malaysia is the dominant source of pigs for Singapore, the following description of the pig trading process uses Malaysia as the main reference point. Before HAM, all pig imports were handled by 23 importers, who would contact farmers directly for the supply of pigs. They would apply to PPD for an import permit for each consignment (truckload) of pigs, paying a fee of S\$150 per permit. The import permit would then be sent to the farmers to accompany each consignment, which normally consists of about 90-100 pigs transported in a truck. PPD officers stationed at the border would inspect import documents before allowing each truckload of pigs into Singapore. Once allowed into Singapore, regulations do not allow pigs to be returned to the farms or re-exported elsewhere.

The trucks were driven to holding areas at one of two PI-operated abattoirs at Jurong and Kim Chuan. A PPD officer would inspect the pigs as they were unloaded and disinfected (at a fee of 75 cents per pig). The importers would then take possession of the pigs and assemble them into lots for sale to pork merchants and buying agents. After the pigs were sold, the buyers arranged to have them slaughtered at the abattoirs. The pig carcasses were then collected early the next morning for sale at the open markets or for delivery to customers. The useful yield of pork per pig is between 75% and 80% of its gross weight, depending on the weight of the pigs — smaller pigs tend to have lower yields.

During 1990, the four biggest importers were strongly linked together through their ties to a trade association and controlled about 35% of the market. They would meet each Monday to set the price for pigs for that week. Through the trade association, which was dominated by the 'big four', the importers acted like a cartel by controlling the supply of pigs, fixing prices and preventing new entrants from the industry. Although there were no legal constraints for market entry of pig importers, new players found it very difficult to break into the closely knit network of pig farmers, importers and merchants that were based on personal contacts, relationships and negotiation.

Importers would bring pigs from farms on a consignment basis — farmers were paid only for pigs sold. Although most buyers paid the importers cash upon purchase, farmers did not receive the net proceeds of the sale until about a month later, after a commission of 7–10% had been charged. Information regarding the pig industry resided within the group of pig importers. Since the importers determined the quantity to import, quality of lots (by mixing pigs of different quality into one single lot), the weight of pigs sold and selling prices, they were able to manipulate the market by keeping prices high and making substantial profits, ultimately at the expense of local consumers.

## Pig trading under the HAM system

PI was aware of the importers' trade-restricting practices, and being a government-linked company servicing participants in the food industry, had been looking for ways to change existing pig trading practices toward a more open and fair process. Their attention was invariably drawn to Taiwan, where open auction market systems had been used for pig trading for several years. In 1988, PI decided that it should follow the Taiwan model and develop a similar system for Singapore. It set up a wholly-owned subsidiary, Hog Auction Market (HAM) Private Limited, to oversee the project's development and implementation. A tender was called in late 1988, and was awarded in early 1989 to Singapore Electronic Engineering Limited (SEEL), then a government-owned company providing industrial and security systems to defence-related businesses. SEEL, with about 700 employees, went public in August 1991, and is now listed on the Singapore Stock Exchange. The HAM system was completed in about a year and began its parallel run on 1 March 1990. The total cost of the project, including construction of an amphitheatre, was about S\$5 million.

The HAM system consists of two major subsystems, namely the front-end Auction Process Subsystem (APS) and the back-end Management Information Subsystem (MIS). The APS consists of two 3Com file servers, two intelligent outstations (IOS), an electronic weighing scale, an electronic display panel, cashier stations, seat allocation stations and auctioneer stations. The MIS is run on a HP3000 minicomputer that maintains the database of pig trading and produces various summary reports. The system configuration of HAM is shown in Figure 1. A four-person SEEL project team modified an existing proprietary system to be used as the intelligent outstations, selected other hardware from third-party vendors, configured the entire system, and wrote the software to control the auctioning process and generate required management reports.

HAM is housed next to the Jurong abattoir in an amphitheatre consisting of two halls, which have a combined seating capacity of 374 bidders. Currently, only one bidding hall is used, and about 40 bidders would be in the amphitheatre at any one time. A model of the amphitheatre is shown in Figure 2.

After obtaining the necessary import permits, sellers inform HAM about when they would like their consignments of pigs to be auctioned. This data would be entered into the system, and the consignments to be auctioned each day are sequenced by ballot before HAM begins its business at 9 a.m. The auction sequence for the day is shown on a sheet distributed to bidders as they enter the amphitheatre. The sequence sheet shows the order of consignments to be auctioned, the identification numbers of pigs in each consignment, the farm from which the consignment was shipped and the name of the seller.

Before bidders are allowed to participate in the auction, they have to pay a deposit at the cashier's office located at the amphitheatre's entrance. Regular bidders can establish an account with HAM and can also apply for a credit line. Each bidder selects an available seat in the amphitheatre and is given a specific identification number. They bid according to the pig requirements of their principals, and they charge between \$3 and \$5 per pig. This group of 'professional bidders' has increased the efficiency of the auction process to the extent that each pig is now shown for 5 seconds or less before it is sold (reduced from 15 seconds as a result of bidder suggestions).

Once sold, a pig is moved out of the semi-circular track into a holding area, where the winning bidder's ID (identification number) is stamped onto the pig. The pigs are subsequently sorted by bidder IDs, and slaughtered at the nearby abattoir the same evening. The pork is then collected by the bidders and transported out of the abattoir area.

After each bid is accepted, HAM updates the information onto a file in the file server, which is updated into the back-end MIS at the end of the day. After deducting the 3% commission, sellers are paid for the pigs sold within three days. The back-end MIS provides summary reports for internal HAM management and market participants on a weekly basis.

## How the HAM system changed the pig trading process

HAM has brought a number of important changes in the hog market of Singapore. First, HAM changed the fundamental nature of pig trading in Singapore from one based on private negotiations between importers and buyers to an open market bidding system. Sellers can offer their pigs to many buyers at the same time, and buyers can buy from any seller without personal contact. HAM induces competitive market prices for pigs according to their quality, supply and demand. Under the traditional method, the importers had control of access to market information because of the links and contacts they had with both sellers and buyers. These linkages, coupled with the private trading arrangements, made new entry extremely difficult. HAM changed the structure by introducing open and public trading in pigs. Price information on pigs auctioned is available to all without discrimination.

Second, prices under the HAM system are determined by supply and demand factors, not independently by the importers. Under the traditional system, importers set the prices weekly and buyers decided how much they would buy at the set price. The prices set were relatively stable and varied little from week to week. Under the HAM system, buyers judge the quality of each pig in an open process and bid for it accordingly. As expected, prices dropped, and varied more with the HAM system. When HAM was introduced, prices dropped an average of 15% in the first two months of operation, and varied by as much as 40% in the same period.

Third, HAM provides market information back to farmers more quickly and more precisely, and indirectly forced suppliers to offer better quality pigs. When importers fixed prices weekly, and mixed pigs of different quality into a single lot, pig prices provided little information about actual market conditions and preferences. Further, because of the delay in payments, farmers got feedback on price information four weeks after the sale had been effected. In contrast, the HAM price for each pig provides an instantaneous market assessment of demand, supply and quality. HAM provides price information on each pig sold, and communicated the information to the farmers with the payments three days after the auction. The price paid for each pig gives farmers the information they need to decide on their market niche.

Fourth, HAM has reduced the need for the middle-men, the importers, and changed their role drastically. Before HAM, farmers had to sell their pigs through importers that were stationed in Singapore. Farmers can now bypass the importers to sell their pigs directly to HAM. HAM charges a lower rate of commission (3%), so it became relatively attractive for farmers to switch. In mid-1992, about two years after HAM became operational, 50% of pigs were sold directly by farmers to bidders without going through importers. If importers are used, their roles have become limited to that of providing logistics for handling and transportation, since they no longer have exclusive market links and information. During the parallel run, pork merchants largely avoided the use of HAM, with pigs bought through HAM accounting for only 12% of Jurong abattoir's capacity. In April, before the cut-over to HAM, the Malaysian pig farmers petitioned the Singapore Cabinet Minister in-charge of PPD for review and deferment of implementation plans for HAM.

At the day of direct cut-over to HAM, the Malaysian pig farmers stopped pig shipments to Singapore and boycotted the local market for three days. Since 85% of Singapore's pork requirements came from Malaysia, and the Chinese diet is dependent on pork as its main source of proteins, the boycott caused havoc in the market and attracted much public concern. The problems resulting from the shortage of fresh pork, and the reasons for the boycott, featured prominently in the local news media for several days. Prices jumped 20% and many stalls were closed during the period of the boycott. Press conferences and releases by several government departments were held to calm public reactions, and to encourage the consumption of frozen meats or other types of fresh meats.

At the same time, the pig importers and pork merchants association applied to the Singapore courts and obtained an injunction to stop PI from implementing the plan to allow only HAM-purchased pigs to be slaughtered at the Jurong abattoir. Pig importers and pork merchants also publicly accused PI and HAM for the confusion in the meat market, the market instability because of the price fluctuations that occurred since HAM was introduced, the inconvenient operating hours and the under-utilization of the Jurong abattoir. The prices of pigs have varied up to S\$100 per pig during a week, which is 40% of the average price of S\$250 per pig. The average price per pig has also dropped by between S\$20 to S\$40 since the introduction of HAM, affecting the incomes of both farmers and importers.

PI countered these accusations in press statements and conferences, explaining that the price fluctuations were the direct result of the merchants' ability to assess pig quality under the HAM system. It also sought to explain the benefits of HAM, especially to consumers, and cited the availability of the smaller abattoir at Kim Chuan for merchants wishing to continue trading in pigs in the traditional way.

In the ensuing legal tussle, PI successfully applied to the court to suspend the injunction against HAM. However, the court restored the interim injunction a few days later after hearing arguments from both parties. With the injunction against HAM in place, pork merchants continued to trade in pigs the traditional way without restrictions in the choice of abattoirs. Thus the pig importers and pork merchants seemed to have stopped HAM in its tracks and derailed it from changing the forces that shape pig trading in Singapore.

About two weeks later, the Ministry of Trade and Industry announced a new ruling, requiring all pig importers to be licensed before they are allowed to import pigs for sale in Singapore. Pork would become the second food to be regulated under the Price Control Act. Rice, the oriental staple food, was the only item to be regulated from the early 1970s because of profiteering by rice merchants. A key licensing requirement stipulated that all licensed pig importers must sell at least 20% of their pigs through an approved auction market. This proportion would be progressively increased as importers and merchants became used to the auction system. PPD was named to be the government agency that would implement the new licensing requirement. Although the new regulation did not stipulate the use of the HAM system directly, it was the only approved auction market at the time the ruling came into force.

The Consumers Association of Singapore, perceiving the new ruling to be in the interests of the consumers, welcomed the licensing requirement and further urged the government to restrict the granting of licenses only to those importers who sell all their pigs through an auction market system. The pork merchants association, apparently shocked by the new regulation, remained silent throughout the entire episode.

Although importers and merchants were encouraged to develop their own computerized auction systems to compete with HAM, such development was unlikely to occur because of the substantial costs, infrastructure and expertise that would be required. Further, any new auction market system would have still required government approval before it could have been used to satisfy the licensing requirement. Even if approval were to have been granted, it would have been unlikely to restore the lost market control of the importers.

About three weeks after the new licensing rule came into force, the pig importers and pork merchants associations withdrew their court injunction against HAM and agreed to use the Jurong abattoir exclusively for pigs bought through HAM. By the end of 1990, about seven months after HAM's implementation, pig trading through HAM accounted for $36\%$ of the market. By mid-1992, HAM-traded pigs accounted for about half the market. ## Implementing HAM: evaluation and lessons

The HAM case shows that fundamental changes in business practices can occur when electronic markets are implemented, resulting in some winners and some losers, with both potential winners and losers likely to resist its implementation. In this section, the HAM case is evaluated and major issues are highlighted that should be considered when implementing electronic markets. These lessons provide guidelines for managers wishing to better manage the potential reactions from parties adversely affected by the implementation of electronic markets.

## 1. IT alone cannot effect the desired change in the structure of industry forces

PI had hoped to use HAM to facilitate a transition to open market trading in pigs that would ultimately make the market more efficient and benefit local consumers. Without HAM, PI would not have the requisite mechanism to alter market trading patterns for the social good. However, the HAM system alone could not effect the desired changes apart from the regulatory powers of PPD under the Price Control Act.

Although PI recognized that it could not expect traders to use HAM voluntarily, and tried to provide disincentives for non-use by tying the use of the Jurong abattoir to HAM-traded pigs, it did not work according to plan. The government had to resort to regulatory powers to overcome the court injunction obtained by the pork merchants that would have killed the HAM system. The strategic potential of HAM was uniquely linked to its ultimate owner (the government), which has a regulatory charter to ensure fair trading in primary products, in a larger socio-economic context where government agencies tend to play a proactive role in regulating business practices to achieve social policy objectives. In countries where the government's role is not as interventionist, other mechanisms may be needed to ensure the success of socially-oriented electronic market implementations.

This lesson is consistent with current thinking that IT will not gain significant advantages apart from other core organizational resources and competencies. Summarizing the results of MIT's Management in the 1990s research program, Scott Morton $^{17}$ states:

... there is no technical or economic reason that can allow one organization to capture excess economic rents from the use of IT for any sustained period of time. Competitive and technological forces simply do not permit any single organization to enjoy a sustainable competitive advantage merely from its use of information technology ... the benefits do not flow from the mere use of IT but arise from the human, organizational and system innovations that are added on to the original business benefit. [italics added for emphasis]

## 2. A critical level of utilization is a necessary condition for the impacts of electronic markets to be felt

Viewed from a system standpoint, the 20% HAM utilization as a requirement for licensing served primarily to increase the system usage to a critical level where the desired system impacts could begin to take effect. We can speculate whether other strategies besides legislation might have been used to increase the rate of adoption by pig traders. However, the results from the mandatory system usage are less speculative: by the end of 1990, (1) pig prices have dropped by 20%; (2) 47 out of about 100 exporters supply their pigs directly to HAM without going through the importers; (3) HAM's share of the pig trading market from 12% in March (voluntary usage) to 36% in December 1990 (voluntary and required usage), and 48% in mid-1992; and (4) as a sign of the times, the business of the largest pig importer shrank by 26%. The growth in HAM utilization made it unnecessary for the government to increase the mandatory 20% level as announced in May 1990.

Electronic markets like HAM fall within the category that Markus $^{30}$ classified as ‘interactive media’. Markus identified two characteristics of interactive media that made it extremely vulnerable to start-up problems and discontinuance: (1) the universal access or public good feature — users cannot be prevented from enjoying it even if they have not contributed to it; and (2) reciprocal interdependence among users. In the HAM system, the publicly available price information is useful to sellers who may not use HAM, and HAM prices have served as the benchmark for the entire market. Markus argues that without a critical mass of users, not only is use unlikely to spread, it is likely to be extinguished altogether. The key issue for successful implementation of electronic markets like HAM is not necessarily the enforcement of mandatory use, but the planning of strategies to obtain a critical mass of early adoptors so that the system will be given a fair chance of having the intended effects $^{12}$ .

## 3. A strong champion is a necessary but not sufficient condition for IT success

Strong championship of IT use has been found to be associated with IT success $^{21,31,32}$ . The HAM case showed that a strong sponsor-champion, in this instance a government-linked company that is a major player in the food processing industry, by itself did not guarantee success. Although the pig traders knew that the HAM system had the backing of government, it did not deter them from taking retaliatory actions aimed at sabotaging the system. There may be a need to distinguish the role of a system champion for the development of a new system from the ultimate success of the system. Although a system champion may be able to energize others to support and sponsor the development of a new system, its role in the utilization and success of a new system may be more limited unless it also has authority to enforce usage.

## 4. Expect retaliation from affected parties

Firms affected adversely by an electronic market can be expected to resist and fight against the system. In the HAM case, the retaliatory actions can be classified into four categories: appeal, avoidance, projection and aggression $^{25,29}$ . In April 1990, the pig farmers association sought to stop HAM by appealing directly to the Minister of National Development. When HAM was first introduced in March, the pig merchants simply avoided the use of the system. When the use of the HAM system was made mandatory for the use of Jurong abattoir in mid-May, the pig merchants publicly blamed HAM for causing market confusion, price instability, and abattoir under-utilization. Finally, their aggressive behaviour is demonstrated by the three-day boycott and the use of legal injunction to stop HAM.

An objective analysis of the impacts of an electronic market on various industry participants, both positive and negative, is essential to enable the initiating firm to plan for specific actions to ensure its successful implementation. In seeking to use the HAM system to change the pig trading process, PI was acting in the public's interests. Although consumers would benefit in terms of lower prices and better quality pork, the impacts on other industry participants were less clear and could be, and indeed were, interpreted negatively. But this need not be the case.

The farmers could benefit by getting prices commensurate with the quality of their pigs, pay less commission for the sale of their pigs, be paid within a shorter period of time, obtain better market information and be less dependent on local importers. However, there would be pressures for them to produce better quality pigs, and those who are unable to do so may suffer a reduction in demand or lower prices. Those who choose to use importers and are unable to bargain for a lower rate of commission may have to pay more commission overall because of the additional 3% payable to HAM.

The merchants could benefit by being able to buy the kind of pigs their customers need, be less dependent on the importers, and pay lower prices than previously. However, social relationships with the importers were part of the traditional system, and such relationships have given buyers some stability in their supplies. Besides introducing new uncertainties about the availability of pigs, the new system also requires less social contact and relationships between importers and merchants, causing some unhappiness to the merchants.

Even with the importers, the impacts were not uniform. The system affected the big four importers worst of all, eroding their market power and reducing their market role to that of providing logistics for the import of pigs. Their earnings dropped as farmers bypassed them altogether. However, some of the smaller importers who were already constrained by the power of the bigger importers could see the introduction of HAM as an opportunity to break out of their control and gain a greater share of the market.

After determining the impacts on various groups, the initiating firm should consider reaching out in cooperation to those firms that may stand to gain from the introduction of electronic markets. In the HAM case, three major groups — the pig farmers, the importers and the pork merchants — all retaliated against it, although the farmers, merchants and smaller importers could gain from HAM's introduction. The initiating firm cannot take for granted that groups that stand to gain will perceive the potential impacts accurately and respond appropriately. The initiating firm should therefore engage in a dialogue with such groups, communicating clearly its intentions, plans and analysis of the expected impacts. The firm may then offer strategic alliances with these firms to ensure the success of the planned IT initiatives. Such efforts to reach out to potential beneficiaries of the new system should result in greater support from these groups.

There are obvious risks from such attempts: potential beneficiaries may not perceive the benefits, or may be unwilling to commit to any coordinated efforts with the firm. The premature disclosure of information may result in leaks to the marketplace and lessen any intended effects from the element of surprise. However, managing such risks are not different from managing other strategic moves or new product introductions made by a firm $^{33}$ . One also needs to recognize that managing potential beneficiaries is the easier task compared to the need to counter expected retaliations from adversely affected parties.

## 5. Develop a plan to counter expected retaliations

The use of HAM undermined the power and market control of the importers and reduced their earnings. The importers resisted through aggression, appeal, projection and avoidance. With the court injunction against HAM, the situation would have remained an impasse, derailing the plans for HAM, had regulatory powers not been invoked. In other situations, initiating firms are unlikely to have similar regulatory powers to enforce the use of electronic markets.

Porter $^{10}$ warned that actions taken by a firm to improve its position relative to its competitors would elicit strong retaliatory reactions that may not only negate the intended effects, but also hurt the initiating firm in other areas as well. There is thus a need to understand and deal with the resistance that can be expected from those adversely affected firms. A key for implementing electronic markets is the ability to predict and influence retaliation. Porter recommended that firms taking threatening moves consider at least five key issues regarding retaliation: (1) How likely is retaliation?; (2) How soon will retaliation come?; (3)

How effective will retaliation potentially be?; (4) How tough will retaliation be, where toughness refers to the willingness of the affected parties to retaliate strongly even at their own expense?; (5) Can retaliation be influenced?

Retaliation is more likely when there are many firms whose power is relatively equal or when affected parties are able to unite against the initiating firm, when the products are standardized, when there are high fixed costs, and when industry growth is slowing $^{10}$ . An understanding of these industry characteristics and the profiles of industry participants would enable a firm to forecast the likelihood, timing, strength and effectiveness of potential retaliation. A good defence is erected when affected firms, after evaluating the signals and attempting to retaliate, conclude that retaliation is unwise.

An important concept in influencing retaliation is commitment. Commitment is communicating unequivocally a firm's intentions about a strategic move and the resources it can and will commit to ensure the success of that move. Retaliation is more likely when competitors perceive that an initiating firm may back off when faced with retaliation. When competitors see that a firm is totally committed to the success of an electronic market, and are convinced that the firm would counter any retaliatory moves, which would lead to protracted and costly battles, they may be dissuaded from retaliating.

In the HAM case, PI could have sought to influence potential retaliatory actions by communicating unambiguously that public interests require that the HAM system be used by all concerned. PI could also have enlisted PPD to make clear its commitment to correct price manipulation and profiteering, and to offer the use of HAM as a means to effect the necessary change without excessive public exposure. In such a demonstration of PI and PPD's commitment, the importers might have been dissuaded from retaliating, being convinced that they would have been in a no-win situation. The HAM system might have been viewed by the importers as a means of escaping public censure.

Thus, a clearly developed strategy to deal with potential retaliations ought to be part of any plan to implement electronic markets. Without such a strategy, the initiating firm may be caught without an appropriate response, suffer embarrassment and jeopardize the investments made.

## Summary

The current literature obscures the difficulties and realities of implementing electronic markets. This paper described the implementation of HAM and highlighted the issues and realities of using electronic markets strategically. The initiating firm can and should expect resistance to its moves. When an electronic market is used to change the power balance among industry participants, to transform their roles and to reduce their market share and profits, affected firms may retaliate and undermine the attempts of the initiating firm. The initiating firm should expect such retaliations and develop a plan to counter such moves. There is also a need to identify potential beneficiaries and turn them into strategic allies.

PI has since commissioned SEEL to develop a similar auction market for the Kim Chuan abattoir. Since mid-1990, HAM's market share has grown steadily, and the buyers have become accustomed to electronic auctioning. In fact, the bidders have become so proficient that the initial 15 seconds of auctioning per pig has become 'too slow', and with bidder feedback, HAM has progressively reduced the auction time per pig to five seconds by mid-1992. As PI looks forward to the Kim Chuan HAM implementation, it is quietly confident that it will be a 'non-event'. Besides some transitional technical issues, PI does not expect to have any 'hiccups'. They are confident that they have learned from HAM's earlier implementation, and that similar problems would not recur this time.
