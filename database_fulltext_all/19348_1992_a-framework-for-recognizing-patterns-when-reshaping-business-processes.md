# A framework for recognizing patterns when reshaping business processes

Mats Lundeberg

Institute for Development of Business Processes (Institute V) and Department of Information Management, Stockholm School of Economics, 113 83 Stockholm, Sweden

Reshaping business processes is an extensive field of current interest. This article is based on the idea that there are a number of frameworks or frames we can use as a basis for recognizing patterns that will improve our ability to reshape business processes. A combined, conceptual framework based on a framework for perceiving situations, a framework for perceiving processes, and relationships between these frameworks is presented. Examples of patterns that can be recognized using this framework are given. Finally, the article discusses briefly how recognizing such patterns can help you in your work with reshaping business processes.

Keywords: conceptual framework, patterns, business processes, levels of abstraction, perspectives, phases

In trying to help persons achieve sustainable outcomes in business, I have found that there are a number of frameworks or frames we can use as a basis for recognizing patterns that will improve our ability to reshape business processes. My purpose with this article is to present a conceptual framework for recognizing such patterns.

Reshaping business processes is an area of current interest $^{1}$ . The realities we deal with in working with business processes and their reshaping or redesign are rich and comprehensive. In this article, I offer a combined, conceptual framework based on a framework for perceiving situations, a framework for perceiving processes, and relationships between these frameworks (Figure 1). The situation framework includes three situation frames: levels of abstraction, perspectives, and points in time. The process framework contains three process phases: process levels, process focuses, and process phases. I then combine the two frameworks addressing relationships between situations and processes. I also give examples of some patterns one can recognize by using these frameworks as a basis. At the end of the article I discuss how recognizing such patterns can help in the work of reshaping business processes.

During the 1980s my research focus shifted from information systems development to business process development. The combined framework presented in this article is one of the outcomes of this shift, addressing issues such as persons' involvement in business processes, different perspectives in reshaping business processes, as well as the need for handling parallel work processes. Parts of the framework have been applied in more than 50 business process redesign projects under my guidance. Although the focus of the applications so far has been on reshaping business processes, the framework has also been used when handling other types of change processes, and in connection with leadership training.

## Framework for perceiving situations in reality

As stated above, the situation framework contains three situation frames related to levels of abstraction, perspectives, and points in time. I start by describing these three frames one by one.

## First situation frame: different levels of abstraction

Typically, when persons of different categories meet and discuss problems and opportunities regarding their business, they address issues at different levels of abstraction. The top leaders may be concerned about the results at the bottom line. The production people are perhaps interested in increasing the uptime of the production process. The information systems people are maybe thinking about how to build more flexible information systems. Your challenge, in perceiving situations in reality using the first situation frame, is to recognize to which level of abstraction each issue is referring. One problem is that there are no universally true levels. Different levels will be relevant in different situations. In Figure 2 we find seven typical levels of abstraction in business.

The logic of Figure 2 goes like this. There are a number of persons (stakeholders or interest group members) with certain characteristics. These persons behave in different ways in order to influence the achievement of certain results. In order to achieve results in businesses, activities are carried out. These activities need information. The information is provided by information systems, which in turn interact with the environment in order to exchange information.

The basic relationship between the different levels of Figure 2 is influence. A person's beliefs, values, and capabilities influence his behaviour, which in turn influences the results he tries to achieve. The intended results influence the activities needed in order to achieve these results. The activities influence the information needed to carry out the activities. The information influences the information systems needed. The person's behaviour, as well as the activities, and the information systems, influence the environment.

Vice versa, the environment influences the information systems, the activities, and persons' behaviour.

The information systems influence the information provided, the information influences the activities, the activities influence the results, and the results influence a person's behaviour, which in turn influences his capabilities, beliefs and values.

If we redraw Figure 2 and include arrows to illustrate the ‘influence’ relationships, we get Figure 3, which illustrates that in influencing the environment, one has three basic choices:

1. To influence the environment through your own behaviour.

2. To influence the environment through the activities of a business that you can influence.

3. To influence the environment through the information systems of a business that you can influence.

In the same way that there are no universally true levels of abstraction, there is no universally true way of illustrating these levels. I have used rectangles placed above and below each other so far. But there are other possibilities.

When we talk about influencing and being influenced by the environment, some people think it would be natural to illustrate the environment as being 'all around'. In a sense it is, it depends on how one interprets the relationships between the different levels. For the sake of simplicity, let us focus on the three levels persons, behaviour, and environment, from Figure 3. In Figure 4 I have surrounded these three levels by ellipses. This figure is one way of illustrating that the environment surrounds or influences persons and their behaviour.

## Second situation frame: different types of perspectives

Typically, when persons meet and discuss problems and opportunities regarding their situation, they will not only use different levels of abstraction, they will also perceive issues from different perspectives. I see things from my position and with my view, and others will naturally see things from their positions and with their views. It is also possible for me to try and see things from other persons' positions and with their views and vice versa. When we see issues together with both my own and your views at the same time, we can have combined views of our problems and opportunities. We can thus try and put ourselves in meta-positions in relation to our own positions or 'positions beyond' and get combined perspectives of the situation. Using these distinctions, we can recognize three types of perspectives:

1. Own perspective Perceiving from own position with own view

2. Other perspectives Perceiving from different positions or with other views

3. Combined perspectives Perceiving from meta-positions with combined views

In Figure 5 we find these three types of perspectives illustrated in the form of a staircase with three achievement levels. The logic behind this figure goes like this. The first achievement level is to perceive things from my own perspective, that is from my own position with my own view. The second achievement level is to be able to see things also from other perspectives, that is from different positions or with other views, and to switch between my own perspective and other perspectives. In this way, the second achievement level presupposes the first achievement level. On this type of staircase, you cannot jump steps. You have to climb step by step. The third achievement level, finally, is to be able to perceive things from combined perspectives, that is from metapositions or 'positions beyond' and with combined views, and to be able to switch between the three different types of perspectives.

## Third situation frame: different points in time

Situations change over time. When a situation changes, this means that a list of problems and opportunities describing the situation on different levels of abstraction may include some new and some changed issues, and maybe some of the old problems have disappeared.

Perspectives also change over time as people learn. When your perspective changes, this means that you can perceive a situation from your own perspective today differently from how you perceived the same situation yesterday. The same goes for other persons from their perspectives.

To be more precise, we can add 'time stamps' (see Figure 6) to our perceptions in recognizing different perspectives and levels over time. One time stamp is related to the time of perception ('My observation today') and one time stamp is related to the situation perceived ('of our activities yesterday').

## Combining all three situation frames: a framework for perceiving situations in reality

From your own perspective today, you can perceive past and present situations (see Figure 7). You can also have perceptions about future situations. If you focus on a given situation, you can compare the perceptions you have about that situation today with the perceptions you had yesterday. You might also imagine what kind of perceptions you will have about that specific situation tomorrow. In a similar manner, other persons can perceive past, present and future situations from other or even combined perspectives $^{8}$ .

I have observed the same pattern also in business. Quite a lot of projects dealing with implementing information systems have been proposed and carried out without focusing on what improvement of the business processes these systems should lead to.

The pattern of singular perspectives. The pattern of singular perspectives comes in two major versions in connection with business process redesign using IT. Relying on such descriptions without specific time stamps may lead to undesirable outcomes. For instance, building an information system to solve a problem which existed in the past, but which does not exist anymore. Another example is building an information system for an existing situation that you already know will not exist in the future.

## Framework for perceiving processes in reality

As stated above, the process framework includes three process frames related to process levels, process focus, and process phases. I now describe these three frames one by one.

## First process frame: different process levels

Just as there are different levels of abstraction in describing situations, there are different process levels. The specific subprocesses of a work process can be seen as either on a task-oriented level, or on a relationship-oriented level, or on both levels. Typically, when people act, they differ in emphasis on task and relationship. Some people will be task-oriented and concentrated on the subject matter. Others may be relationship oriented and emphasize the persons involved. Some people will be wholeness oriented and try to achieve a balance between task and relationship. Figure 8 is one way of looking at a work process with the levels task and relationship.

The logic of Figure 8 goes like this. A process (the circle in the middle) has relationship aspects (behaviour) and task aspects (task processes). Behaviour and task processes thus represent two different levels of a process. The output of a process is relationship output (person outcomes) and task output (task outcomes). The input of a process is relationship input (person preconditions) and task input (task preconditions). The task outcomes (as well as person outcomes) are influenced by person preconditions, task preconditions, behaviour, and task processes. In understanding why the outcomes turn out as they do, the reasons may be found in either of these four preceding factors. The person and task preconditions are factors external to the process, whereas behaviour and task processes are internal factors.

## Second process frame: different process focuses

Now that we have recognized different process levels, it is time to look at different process focuses. By a specific process focus I refer to a specific type of subject matter contents that people can concentrate on in order to facilitate going from one situation to another. Typically, when people act, they will do a number of things without explicitly thinking about what types of focus they are using in the ongoing process. Some people tend to be action-oriented and focus on initiatives in order to achieve outcomes quickly. Some people tend to be inventive and focus on a number of alternatives before acting. Some people tend to be to the point and want to understand the core of matters before acting. Some people tend to be vision- or purpose-oriented and want to focus on clarifying visions and goals before acting. Some people, finally, are here and now oriented and want to perceive or understand where they are before doing anything else. Five different types of process focus are:

The point is that each of these five different types of focus can influence going from one situation to another. However, strictly speaking, only the focus ‘achieved outcomes’ is necessary to cover consciously. The four other types of focus all add to the quality of the achieved outcomes. Hence, the point is further that it is the balance between the different types of focus that leads to intended outcomes. All these types of focus can contribute, although the balance between them may vary from one specific process to another.

In Figure 9 we find the above five types of process focus and the relationships between these focuses. The logic of this figure goes like this. The focuses ‘current situation’ and ‘intended future situation’ influence ‘need for changes’. The focus ‘need for changes’ influences ‘change alternatives’, which in turn influences the ‘outcomes’ (or the resulting situation). Please note that ‘influence’ does not necessarily mean that the work processes with these focuses are carried out in strict sequence. There may be a number of iterations and overlaps. The point is that each of the five different types of focus can add to the quality of the outcomes in going from one situation to another. The five types are closely related to five important preconditions for persons working with business process redesign: receptivity, ability to create visions, insight, inventiveness and initiative.

## Third process frame: different process phases

Intuitively, we know that there is a difference between thinking about something and doing something. This is embedded in statements such as: 'It is what you do that counts, not what you say you are going to do'. In addition to this, we can think in advance about what we are going to do, and we can think afterwards about what we have done. Using these distinctions, we can recognize three different types of phases of human processes:

\- Choosing goals

\- Operating

● Evaluating evidence

Choosing what we are going to achieve Doing Comparing what we have achieved with what we wanted to achieve

For each of these three different types of process phases, we can apply the different types of process focus from the second process frame ( 2∞ ). In introducing the frame of different process focuses ( 3∞ ) I talked about acting in order to achieve outcomes. 'Acting' can be 'deciding', 'operating', or 'testing', depending on the type of phase of the work process at hand.

In Figure 10 we find these three types of process phases and their relationships. The logic of this figure goes like this. The type of phase ‘choose goal’ influences ‘evaluating evidence’, which is represented by the arrow from left to right. In order to achieve the goal it is necessary to do something, to operate. In parallel with the type of phase ‘operate’, the evidence is evaluated from time to time in order to be able to end the process. The results from this evaluating can either lead to further operating or to choosing other goals. We thus have a number of iterations between the types of phases ‘choose goal’ and ‘evaluate evidence’ on one hand, and the type of phase ‘operate’ on the other hand. The double arrow represents these iterations.

Examples of patterns related to the process framework

Three typical examples of patterns related to the process framework are as follows.

The pattern of not paying attention to persons and their behaviour. There are a number of Swedish businesses in which there are persons that state that 'in our company we stick to the subject matter instead of paying attention to personal matters'. Such statements seem to be especially common in engineering-oriented companies. In such environments, you find a number of persons who are frustrated with the outcomes of their work. 'We played by the rules, we stuck to subject matter, and yet we do not achieve what we want'. The message is that there are always persons behind the subject matter you are dealing with, like it or not. These persons and their behaviour influence the outcomes, person outcomes as well as task outcomes.

The pattern of acting without knowing about the need for changes. In business situations, a number of action-oriented persons maintain that it is better to do something than not to do anything at all. This corresponds to a focus on the last box of the second process frame ( ). Sometimes such persons also pay attention to where they are and where they want to go before they start acting (corresponding to the first two boxes of the second process frame( ). But if they have not understood what needs to be changed (what the critical success factors are) in going from the current situation to the intended future situation, there will be problems. Chances are that their actions will lead to other outcomes than they intended. Focusing on the understanding of the need for changes is one possibility for increasing the odds of achieving the intended outcome.

The pattern of planning and doing without evaluating. In implementing information systems in Swedish business, the tendency seems to be to spend much more time planning and doing than evaluating. The consequences of such practice are that you do not know whether you achieved what you wanted or not. Another consequence is that you miss important opportunities to learn about what happened in order to improve similar work in the future.

Examples of patterns related to the combined framework

An interesting possibility with the combined framework is to study how patterns in specific situations influence patterns in specific processes and vice versa.

Let us assume that we observe a pattern of singular perspectives, for instance a situation with persons that have a strong information systems perspective. Starting from such a situation, this might lead to a process with the pattern of not knowing about the whole need for changes. Specifically the need for changes that is related to other perspectives, such as a user perspective, may be lacking. That might in turn lead to a situation with focus on the information systems rather than the results in the business the information systems are supposed to support.

## Recognizing patterns when reshaping business processes

How can you use the presented conceptual framework specifically when reshaping business processes? In order to examine this, I first introduce an example of phases in reshaping business processes based on the framework. I then discuss briefly how recognizing patterns can be an important part of reshaping business processes.

An example of phases in reshaping business processes

Figure 17 shows the V model for reshaping business processes in relation to the seven typical levels in business (from Figure 2). The V model illustrates the following nine phases of reshaping business processes (some of which are based on information systems development $^{9}$ ):

![](/api/attachments/ZNC6FHA8/fulltext/images/3256d8378715cdb0d0b9d32bc10675b4ceb07abe3dc19fdc5c4dbc5c5b22e17d.jpg)  
Figure 17. The V model for reshaping business processes

Business process review. Reviewing the current situation, the intended future situation and need for changes on an overview level for the focused business processes as a whole. The outcome of a business process review is a prioritized list of selected business processes to develop and a tentative list of need for changes within these selected business processes.

Change studies. Studying the current situation, the intended future situation, need for changes, different change alternatives, and making an action plan for the selected business processes. The outcome of change studies is the chosen action plan for the development of the selected business processes.

Activity studies. Analysing and designing the activities of the selected business processes. The outcome of activity studies is a number of blueprints for the intended future activities of the selected business processes as well as a number of specifications of necessary preconditions, such as organizational values, individual capabilities, and/or information systems.

Information studies. Analysing and designing the information contents of future information systems. The outcome of information studies is a number of blueprints for the intended future information contents, which serve as specifications for the subsequent system design.

System design. Designing information system solutions to the specified information contents. The outcome of system design is a number of blueprints for the realization of the information systems.

Realization. 'Building' or 'producing' the information systems. The outcome of realization is a number of information systems to be used in supporting the activities of the selected business processes.

Implementation. Implementing the intended future activities utilizing developed preconditions. This phase often has considerable overlap with other phases.

Follow-up studies. Comparing the achieved new situation for the selected business processes with the intended future situation described in the change studies. The outcome of follow-up studies is an evaluation of achieved results as well as a list of further needs for changes for the selected business processes.

Business process evaluation. Comparing the achieved new situation for the business processes as a whole with the intended future situation described in the business process review. The outcome of business process evaluation is an evaluation of achieved results as well as a list of further needs for changes for the focused business processes as a whole.

## Frameworks, patterns and reshaping business processes

Using the combined framework for recognizing patterns presented here together with the V model for reshaping business processes, represents an opportunity for basing business process redesign more on recognizing patterns than on following predefined steps. Each of the phases in the V model can be described using the process framework with multiple levels and focuses. The outcomes of these phases can be described using the situation framework with multiple levels and perspectives. Working in such a manner is being specific about trying to achieve wholeness oriented patterns. If you are able to avoid patterns similar to those mentioned above (for instance: focus on information systems rather than results; not paying attention to persons and their behaviour; acting without knowing about the need for changes) you have the opportunity to save valuable time and effort in your work with reshaping business processes.

The fact that more than 50 business process redesign projects have used parts of the framework presented here shows that it is possible to use the framework and to recognize patterns when doing so. Examples of experiences reported in this connection are:

However, many questions remain to be answered, such as: What are the ‘costs’ of using such a framework? What are the effects? Is it worth the effort?

In this article, I have presented a conceptual framework with a number of frames you can use as a basis for recognizing patterns. I have summarized the idea behind recognizing patterns in reshaping business processes in Figure 18. This figure illustrates levels in reshaping business processes. It distinguishes between four different process and activity levels (from bottom to top):

● perceiving reality with the help of frameworks;

● recognizing patterns in such perceptions;

● reshaping business processes using patterns;

The level behaviour is a reminder that this is a challenge for the persons working in this way, in that it has consequences for their identity, beliefs, values, and capabilities. Personally, I find it much more exciting to work with recognizing patterns rather than following predefined work steps — but also much more demanding.
