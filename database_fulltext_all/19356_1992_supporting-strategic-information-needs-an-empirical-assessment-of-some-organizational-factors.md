# Supporting strategic information needs: an empirical assessment of some organizational factors

Sridhar N. Ramaswami, Sree Nilakanta and E. James Flynn

College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, Iowa, USA

This study examines the direct and indirect impact of organizational context factors (strategy and environment) on the emphasis placed by firms on strategic information support. Data from a questionnaire survey of 167 banking institutions in mid-Western USA are used; analysis of the results shows that firms adopting marketing and service differentiation strategies place greater emphasis on strategic information. Results also indicate that firms facing diverse and volatile environments perceive a greater need for strategic information than firms facing less dynamic ones. Additionally, an analysis of indirect effects suggests that a firm's environment influences its choice of competitive strategy, which, in turn, affects the emphasis placed on strategic information support.

Keywords: strategic information, competitive strategy, environment

For many years, researchers and managers have stressed the importance of ‘strategic information’ for developing and implementing effective competitive strategies $^{1-3}$ . Organizations need to monitor both the external and task environments so that strategic factors likely to have a strong impact on competitive success or failure can be detected early $^{4}$ . Waterman $^{5}$ , for example, argues that one reason excellent companies are able to constantly ‘renew’ themselves is because they ‘know more’ and ‘treat information as their main competitive advantage’.

Firms need both internal and external information for strategy formulation and implementation. This need is often fulfilled by using a broad range of information systems such as internal accounting, marketing intelligence, and marketing research systems $^{6}$ . An internal accounting system provides data (e.g. sales, inventory, cost, and other recurrent data) that may become useful information for marketing managers, particularly in evaluating implementation of strategies. The marketing intelligence system is a scanning system that provides managers with information about developments in different marketing sub-environments such as customers, competitors, suppliers, intermediaries, regulation, and the general economy. The marketing research system emphasizes the gathering of new data for the investigation of specific problems $^{7}$ . The latter two systems or tools form the core of the ‘strategic information support’ (SIS) function of firms.

A primary objective of this study is to estimate empirically the relative importance of two organizational context factors, strategy and environment, in influencing the information support provided for strategic decision-making. Firms adopt different competitive strategies to gain unique positions in the market $^{8,9}$ . A firm adopting a differentiation strategy may place more emphasis on strategic information compared to a firm that uses an undifferentiated strategy. Additionally, a firm in a volatile, competitive environment may rely on strategic information much more heavily than a firm in a stable, less dynamic one. The environment can also have an indirect impact on strategic information emphasis through its influence on choice of strategy. Firms operating in a dynamic environment may emphasize strategic information more if they choose a differentiated strategy than an undifferentiated one.

Knowledge of the relevance of strategy and environment factors as antecedents to information support has important implications for the information technology (IT) function in organizations. For instance, firms gather diverse and unrelated pieces of data from the environment. Not only do these data have to be combined to yield relevant information, but also systems have to be established within the organization for sharing the information with organizational members in different functional areas. Researchers in recent years have focused on the criticality of interfunctional sharing of marketing information for making firms more market-oriented $^{10}$ . If market orientation implies more effective organizational performance, the IT function could play a facilitatory role by which such firms can attempt to predict their organization's information needs from contextual factors such as strategy and environment.

## Background review and hypotheses

Organizations make decisions at three levels, namely, strategic, tactical and operational $^{11}$ . The information needed to make these decisions differs in terms of scope, time orientation, and original source. Firms do not usually face much difficulty in gathering information for supporting operational decision-making since this comes from their internal operational information systems. This is not true of information needed for supporting strategic decisions. Due to the complex nature of these decisions, the labour and financial resources needed for gathering and processing strategic information is likely to be high both in terms of quality and quantity $^{12}$ . But not all firms can allocate resources to strategic information collection and processing. An important implication of this is that firms that meet their strategic information needs may be in a better position for deriving competitive advantage in the marketplace.

## Definition of strategic information support (SIS)

Strategic information support is a broad term that describes the activities of a firm in the collecting, processing and analysing of information needed to make strategic marketing decisions. Two of the most frequently used mechanisms for collecting such information include environmental scanning and market research. Environmental scanning refers to the acquisition of information about events and trends in an organization's environment, the knowledge of which would be of assistance to top executives in identifying and understanding strategic threats and opportunities $^{13}$ . Market research is that part of market intelligence that involves specific inquiries about clearly defined problems. It is particularly useful for assessing market needs and characteristics, and identifying positions adopted by competitors in different segments of the market.

Both environmental scanning and market research place a high emphasis on activities related to the storage and efficient retrieval of information, and therefore on the availability of information processing systems and equipment. As more external information is collected, information processing becomes more complex $^{14}$ . This necessitates the selection of personnel with analytical skills to work with such complex information. The need for smart people to manage the smarter systems of the present has received considerable attention from many researchers and practitioners $^{15, 16}$ .

## Firm strategy and SIS

Competitive strategy may be defined as the positioning tactics employed by a firm $^{9}$ . Miles and Snow $^{8}$ , Porter $^{9}$ , and other researchers have examined the construct of competitive strategy through strategic typologies. Such typologies are useful for conveying parsimoniously fundamental differences in the strategic approaches used by organizations $^{17}$ . Two of the three generic strategies suggested by Porter are cost leadership and differentiation. A third generic strategy suggested by Porter, focus strategy, is built around serving a particular target market either through meeting their needs better than competitors or through achieving cost efficiencies (Reference 9, p. 38). Since the focus strategy is separable from the first two generic strategies only through the breadth of the target market, it is not discussed as a separate strategy in this study.

A cost leadership strategy emphasizes low cost relative to competitors. This low cost is usually achieved, in manufacturing units, through concentrated effort by management to improve production and delivery technologies, product improvements aimed at simplification and substitution with cheaper inputs, and scale economics. Cost containment tactics that are appropriate for a service industry (such as banking), however, would include elements such as routinization of tasks, and automation of information processing. These tactics focus primarily on the internal operations (rather than external operations) of the firm to increase efficiency of service delivery.

Routinization and automation help executives learn from experience and, accordingly, carry out their functions more efficiently. An IT function that focuses on automating routine tasks, developing standard operating reports, and controlling deviations through exceptions reporting could play a supporting role in this cost containment process. These tasks depend less upon strategic information collected from the external environment than they do upon operating information. Thus:

H1(a) Firms that adopt a low cost strategy will tend to place less emphasis on strategic information support than firms not adopting this strategy.

A differentiation strategy requires the creation of a product or service that is perceived to be unique by the consumer and that permits a firm to command higher than average prices. Specific components of this strategy could include:

(i) marketing differentiation through advertising, sales promotion, and emphasizing personal selling to customers;

(ii) product differentiation through new product development and product modifications; and (iii) service differentiation through emphasis on service delivery quality, and overall image development.

These strategy elements describe the firm's position in its target product market. A firm may choose to have a superior position vis-à-vis its competitors on one or more of these elements.

For effective formulation of a differentiation strategy, a firm would need organizational processes by which it can identify, evaluate and disseminate information about the market and competitors to members inside and outside the firm $^{18}$ . This information would help the firm cope with the uncertainties associated with its long-term product-market positions. Therefore, this information is likely to be non-routine, and not be governed by short-term efficiency considerations.

Mechanisms frequently used to collect this external information are market research, competitive monitoring, and environmental scanning $^{2}$ . Thus:

H1(b) Firms that adopt a differentiation strategy will tend to place a greater emphasis on strategic information support than firms not adopting this strategy.

## Environment and SIS

Besides a firm's competitive strategy, the nature of its external environment may also influence the extent of emphasis provided for the SIS function. For instance, the dissimilarity in needs and preferences of a firm's customers (market diversity) and the instability in their tastes and expectations (market volatility) will most likely lead to greater emphasis on information, particularly if the firm is operating in several product markets $^{19,20}$ .

Diversity and volatility are but two of the several components of the external environment of a firm. Achrol and Stern $^{21}$ have suggested that researchers need to develop a comprehensive specification of the environment construct. Instead of itemizing specific variables that affect particular firms in different industries, Achrol and Stern conceptualize the environment in terms of its abstract qualities or dimensions (e.g. homogeneity–heterogeneity, stability–instability, concentration–dispersion, turbulence, etc.). We now present a discussion of the linkage between such dimensions and the strategic information needs of firms.

Diversity: Environmental diversity measures the dissimilarity in needs and preferences of a firm's customers $^{21}$ . Dwyer and Welsh $^{22}$ found that a diverse environment leads to decision uncertainty in two ways. First, in a diverse environment, decision-makers face greater difficulty in collecting and processing information about each customer. Second, diversity requires the adoption of multiple strategies to address the several needs and preferences of a firm's customers. Further, when the degree of customer heterogeneity increases, the probability of incompatibility among a firm's strategies also rises. Resource dependence theory states that organizations that compete in industries requiring many different inputs (resources), or that produce many different outputs (for heterogeneous customers), should find environmental relationships to be more complex than organizations competing industries with fewer inputs and outputs. The link between diversity and information support was established by Leblebici and Salancik $^{23}$ , who found that environmental diversity may be anticipated and handled through information processing. We suggest, therefore, that an increase in environmental diversity would increase the emphasis on SIS.

Dynamism: Environmental dynamism refers to the frequency with which a firm's strategies change in the market environment $^{21}$ . The changes result from the volatility of the preferences of customers in each of the firm's product-markets. These changes increase the uncertainty faced by the decision-maker, thus creating difficulties in strategic planning, coordination, and marketing-mix decisions. Similar to diversity, an increase in environmental dynamism is likely to lead to increased emphasis on SIS.

Capacity: Environmental capacity refers to the richness or leanness of opportunities in the market environment $^{21}$ . The opportunities are defined with respect to the future potential of the market and include factors such as general economic potential, demand for specific services offered by the firm, and incentives provided by local governments for improving the business climate in the target market. Target markets that have high future capacity offer incentives for firms to pursue the market actively. We posit that this would increase the emphasis on market intelligence, in order to monitor competitor policies and programs within the served market. On the other hand, Achrol and Stern suggest the opposite may be true in markets with high capacity. Their premise is that high market capacity may result in a decreased need for market intelligence because of the low levels of uncertainty associated with decision-making in these markets.

Conflict and interdependence: Environmental interdependence refers to the extent of competitive reactivity and sensitivity to one another's programs of action $^{21}$ . The greater a firm's sensitivity to competition, the lower its ability to predict outcomes associated with its marketing actions, and the greater the need for an intelligence system. Pfeffer and Salancik $^{24}$ suggest that conflict is present whenever there is interdependence among competitors. They argue further that conflict is directly related to perceived uncertainty. This suggests that high levels of conflict and interdependence will result in increased emphasis on SIS as a way of coping with uncertainty.

The impact of these various dimensions of the environment on the emphasis provided for the SIS function is summarized by the following hypotheses:

A firm will place greater emphasis on SIS when the firm perceives its market to have

H2(a) high rather than low diversity;

H2(b) high rather than low dynamism;

H2(c) high rather than low capacity;

H2(d) high rather than low interdependence; and H2(e) high rather than low conflict.

## Environment, strategy, and SIS

H1 and H2 focused on the direct associations between strategy and SIS and environment and SIS, respectively. The environment may also be indirectly associated with SIS because of the influence it has on choice of competitive strategy $^{25,26}$ . Because firms may prefer a differentiation strategy to any other strategy in environments characterized by high diversity, dynamism, and capacity, the emphasis on SIS may be stronger in these environments than in less diverse, less dynamic, and lower capacity environments. Conversely, a firm is less likely to choose a low cost strategy in diverse and dynamic environments and hence may show a weaker emphasis on SIS. The indirect effect of the environment would be to emphasize SIS more when choosing a differentiation strategy, and less when choosing a low cost strategy. Because of the exploratory nature of the indirect relationships, the following proposition is tested in this study.

Proposition: In environments characterized by high diversity, high dynamism, high capacity, high interdependence, and high conflict, the emphasis of SIS will be greater for a firm choosing a differentiation strategy than a low cost strategy.

## Research method

## Sample

To overcome the confounding problems that may arise when firms from different industries are included in a sample, the elements for this study were chosen from a single industry, namely, commercial banking. A random sample\* was drawn from this population in a mid-Western state in the USA. Because the study focused on strategic information related to the product/service market, the Marketing Director/Manager/V.P. was identified as the key informant. Of the 450 banks solicited for this study, responses were obtained from 186 yielding a response rate of 41 per cent. Nineteen responses could not be used because of missing values and therefore the effective sample size was 167.

## Instrument development

## A questionnaire was developed in three phases:

1. The preliminary instrument was prepared based on measures proposed by previous researchers, and from the conceptual definition of constructs. For instance, the strategy and environment measures were constructed from previous studies $^{21, 27-30}$ , while the SIS measure was based on the conceptual definition and domain specifications proposed by Herring $^{31}$ .

2. This instrument was discussed with scholars to assess content validity.

3. The revised instrument was then pre-tested with senior marketing managers in 25 banking institutions not included in the sample.

## Data collection

The state banking association initially announced the study through its newsletter. The questionnaire was then mailed to the Marketing Directors/Managers of 450 banks with a cover letter from the President of the Banking Association soliciting their participation in the study. To ensure that key respondents actually filled out the questionnaire, the respondents were requested to identify themselves. They were, however, assured confidentiality of their responses. The identification was necessary to link their responses with other firm characteristics collected as part of a larger study. In return, the respondents received an executive summary of the findings on the strategic orientation and performance of comparable banks.

## Operationalization of variables

Strategic Information Support (SIS): Because prior research has not empirically examined this idea, a measurement scale was developed based on the construct's definition. SIS pertains to activities associated with the 'collection, processing, and analysis of strategic information' (a definition adapted from Herring's $^{31}$ definition of business intelligence systems). The extent to which a firm emphasizes SIS is evaluated by examining the firm's extent of reliance on mechanisms for the collection, processing, and analysis of strategic information. Strategic information is usually collected through marketing research, competitor monitoring, and environmental scanning. Items relating to the degree of emphasis provided by a firm for each mechanism are included as part of the scale. Investment in information processing equipment is used as a surrogate indicator for emphasis on the processing function. This item is used because interviews with pre-test respondents suggested that banks contracting the information processing function to external agencies used information primarily for making tactical decisions, whereas banks that invested in information processing equipment were able to use research and scanning information for making strategic decisions. Finally, an item relating to the extent of use of qualified personnel for analysing strategic information measured the analysis component of the construct. All items were measured using a five-point Likert scale. These items reflect managerial perceptions of the extent of SIS emphasis and do not include any objective, quantitative indicators. Because reliability analysis showed a poor correlation between competitor monitoring and the other items, this item was excluded from the final scale and a summed scale of the remaining items was used for measuring emphasis on SIS. (The reliability of this composite scale was 0.70.) The actual items included in the questionnaire are reported in the Appendix to this paper.

Firm strategy: Researchers have used two methods for measuring a firm's strategy. One method uses a predefined typology of strategy orientation, where firms are classified based on self-ratings. The second method obtains information on a set of strategic elements, which is then reduced using factor analysis to yield a few dimensions of strategy. The second approach is used in this study because the disadvantage due to the self-report bias from the first approach outweigh the disadvantage that might result from a non-conforming factor structure for the strategies.

The strategy elements were identified from previous literature in the strategic management area $^{9,27}$ . Examples of the strategic elements used include product innovation and modification, pricing, advertising, promotion, service, quality control, image in the community, personal selling, etc. Previous strategy researchers $^{28-30}$ have shown that these items have acceptable content validity. A five-point Likert scale was used to measure the items corresponding to the strategy elements.

Factor analysis of the strategy items yielded four factors, namely, product differentiation (PDCTD), marketing differentiation (MKTD), service differentiation (SERVD) and cost focus (COSTF) (see Table 1). These factors corresponded to the strategies of differentiation and cost leadership proposed by Porter $^{9}$ . The internal consistency of the items measuring each of the four dimensions showed reliability coefficients (Cronbach alpha) of 0.69, 0.77, 0.79 and 0.45 $^{\dagger}$ respectively. While the measurement properties of the cost focus construct is less than desirable, it is retained in the model because of its centrality. The findings with respect to this construct will, however, need to be interpreted with caution. The items corresponding to each dimension show high convergence and discrimination properties by loading strongly on the factors they represent and weakly on other factors $^{33, 34}$ .

External environment: Table 2 provides operational definitions of the environmental dimensions discussed in the previous section. These were adapted from Achrol and Stern $^{21}$ . The Appendix to this paper gives a description of the measures and the scales employed. Diversity (DIVER) is measured using two items that capture the extent to which a bank's customers show variability with respect to their preferences for services. The scale for dynamism is composed of four items that measure the frequency with which a bank's competitors change product mix, personal selling, sales promotion, and advertising strategies.

Dynamism is further classified into two categories, one dealing with marketing practices (DYNMP) and the other relating to competitive practices (DYNC). One item (change in advertising strategy) was dropped from the DYNMP scale due to poor reliability. Market capacity (MKTC) refers to the opportunities and resources provided by the market environment in which the bank operates. This is measured by items such as economic growth potential, market demand for products and services, commercial growth potential, incentives from the local governments, and increase in population in the market areas served by the bank.

Because environmental conflict (CONFL) reflects a competitor's use of unfair and aggressive tactics, it is measured by items that focus on the severity of competitive tactics such as misleading advertising, high pressure salesmanship, and misleading customers. Finally, environmental interdependence (INTER) is measured in terms of speed of competitor response to marketing tactics such as price reductions, sales promotion campaigns, advertising and publicity campaigns, and new product introductions.

All six environmental dimensions showed excellent internal consistency, with alpha values of 0.67, 0.79, 0.86, 0.81, 0.90, and 0.78 respectively.

Besides understanding the current needs of its customers, researchers have suggested that a proactive organization would like to know how these needs will change in the future. For example, Kohli and Jaworski $^{10}$ report on a case where, to anticipate customer needs accurately, a blue chip company assigned certain individuals exclusively to the task of studying trends and forces. The important conclusion that may be drawn is that managers may need to monitor the environmental forces that can shape and change the current needs of customers.

The second element of marketing differentiation is positioning. Positioning decisions that are based on customer evaluations of competitive offerings are likely to have a better chance of success. Competitive monitoring and customer feedback are crucial elements for product positioning.

The above discussion illustrates why firms that adopt a marketing differentiation strategy may emphasize the SIS function. Similarly, use of a service differentiation strategy requires a bank to focus on identifying customer needs, addressing customer dissatisfactions, maintaining strict control over quality of service delivery, communicating a high service image to consumers and competitors, educating consumers, and offering a wide range of products $^{35}$ . Most of these elements are externally focused and need to be tracked and integrated with the firm's database system.

Two other strategy factors, product differentiation and cost focus, did not show any relationship with the emphasis provided for SIS. The first result is not surprising because the product offerings of the banking industry do not show a great deal of differentiation. The banking community continues to view its business as 'banking' rather than 'financial services delivery'. Grubbs and Reidenbach $^{36}$ call this the 'intractability' problem, the effect of which has been a somewhat low degree of product innovation effort, with banks typically imitating only what other banks do. Also, compared to other industries, the new product development process in the banking industry is relatively underdeveloped $^{37}$ .

The lack of a significant relationship for the cost focus variable possibly reflects the dilemma faced by managers concerning the method used to achieve a low cost market position. Firms could either scale up the operations and take advantage of economies-of-scale, or scale down and eliminate costly market expansion activities. The former would require greater emphasis on SIS, whereas the latter would imply a lower emphasis. While we do not have specific information on how the sample banks achieve their low cost position, the above result leads to the speculation that they may be equally divided in terms of the method used. Future research should attempt to identify how firms operationalize the low cost strategy and the resulting implication on SIS.

Environment and information support: Results support the hypothesis (H2a) that there is a positive relationship between market diversity and the emphasis on SIS. Differences in the needs, preferences and expectations of customer segments raise two kinds of uncertainties. First, banks have to choose a subset of these markets to serve. Second, the banks then have to understand and determine the viability of different marketing-mix programmes in these markets. The types of information needed to make the market choice, as well as the programme choice, pertains to customer needs and competitor postures in the different sub-markets. Such information is usually gathered through specific market research studies and regular monitoring of competitors. This result supports Leblebici and Salancik's $^{23}$ observation that diversity is an enduring characteristic of the market, and that organizations operating within a diverse market will create permanent information systems.

The hypotheses (H2b) that there will be a positive relationship between market dynamics and SIS emphasis is partially supported. Changes in marketing practices of banks are positively associated with emphasis on SIS, whereas strategy changes adopted by competitors are unrelated to emphasis on SIS. The former result is plausible since dynamic marketing practices require organizations to collect large amounts of information, varying from one situation to another. While the latter result is surprising, the implication that banks do not need information to respond to changes in competitive strategies is supported by Leblebici and Salancik's $^{23}$ finding that market volatility is not associated with institutional forms of information-gathering in the banking industry.

The hypothesized (H2c) positive association between market capacity and SIS was not supported. Analysis of indirect effects shows that the relationship may be less direct than supposed. The hypothesized impacts of environmental interdependence and conflict (H2d & e) among competing banks are only partially supported. Interdependence among banks does not affect the emphasis on SIS, while conflict generates additional emphasis. Interdependence measures the speed of competitor responses to a firm's actions, while conflict measures the strength of these responses. Perhaps interdependence becomes important only if banks are also concerned with changes in strategies adopted by its competitors. Results show that banks are more likely to identify the types of competitive responses rather than their immediacy.

## Indirect effects

A path analysis was performed to assess the indirect effect of environment on the relationship between strategy and emphasis on SIS. These effects offer some interesting results. First, the indirect effects are associated only with two types of differentiation strategies, namely, marketing and service differentiation. Second, while four of the six environmental dimensions show indirect as well as direct effects, the two remaining environmental dimensions (market capacity and environmental interdependence) reveal only indirect effects. These two results provide support for the conceptual framework used in this study. Without the indirect effects, we could have concluded that market capacity and interdependence are not relevant for SIS emphasis. Finally, the magnitude of the total effects suggests that dynamism in marketing practices and market diversity are the most important environmental dimensions in explaining variability in SIS.

## Conclusion

Researchers have proscribed several organizational context variables that could affect the establishment of marketing or competitive intelligence capabilities. This paper has examined the effect of two of these context variables, namely, strategy and environment, on the emphasis provided for the SIS function by a sample of firms in the banking industry. The findings of our study have several important implications for managers and researchers. First, top management must realize that certain strategies require a greater emphasis on strategic information support. For example, service or marketing differentiation calls for an enlarged intelligence capability. Second, turbulent environments often create a need for market information. This study has shown that firms facing customer diversity and competitive intensity place a greater emphasis on SIS $^{\ddagger}$ . With the increasing sophistication of the bank consumer, and greater levels of competition from non-bank financial institutions, the emphasis on strategic information is likely to increase in the future for firms that employ differentiated strategies in volatile and uncertain environments. Third, the environment in which a firm operates can influence its choice of a competitive strategy, and this confluence of strategy and environment has a pronounced effect on the emphasis of strategic information support.

Useful future research would be to extend this study to other industries, to examine whether these results could be replicated, thus permitting further generalization. There is also a need to develop better measures of the constructs used in this study. Questions concerning the contingent nature of environment on the choice of strategy, and the resulting influence on organizational responses, need to be addressed more rigorously.

Practitioners and researchers have postulated that to play a more strategic role in organizations, the IT department has to expand its scope of functions. Traditionally, the IT department has handled an organization's internal information needs. This study shows the possibility of expanding the IT department's scope to include collection, evaluation, analysis, and dissemination of strategic information. Our results agree with Lee and Treacy $^{38}$ concerning the influence of IT on information support. The function of strategic information support is currently performed either by the marketing department or through external consultants. Trained people are needed, not only at the functional level but also at the IT level, to ensure that information from different sources is appropriately used. The general implication is that if the IT department were to undertake a strategic role, it would need personnel with functional expertise, and it would also require greater levels of coordination with the different functional units of the organization.
