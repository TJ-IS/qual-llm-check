# Outsourcing and the trailing edge of technology

John Leighfield

Executive Chairman, AT&T ISTEL Ltd

In the early days of the use of information technology (IT), management of companies wanting to take advantage of the technology had no choice but to recruit specialists to conceive, develop, implement, operate and maintain these facilities. There was no support industry established to provide any of the ingredients needed for planning or implementation; the only assistance came from the suppliers of the raw computer hardware, who had little information systems knowledge.

Not surprisingly computers got a bad name! Companies with no knowledge of this very specialized area of technology had to establish, often from scratch — and with a senior management that was usually ill-equipped to choose good computer people — strategies for the use of computers, departments to develop and operate computer systems and all the paraphernalia that goes with it. Many mistakes were made.

Since then, in parallel with internal MIS departments, a sophisticated service industry has grown up staffed by managers whose sole purpose in life is the strategic and tactical management of technology. This, increasingly over the past few years, has provided an alternative and effective means of realizing opportunities for the use of information technology in business.

## Why outsource?

At the same time, a number of pressures in business have built up, making it even more sensible — perhaps even essential — to look at the use of such external services.

Firstly, the world economic problems of the past few years, have brought dramatically increased pressure on margins — particularly latterly in some of the biggest users of IT — the banks and other service organizations. Many of them have decided that it is as much as they can do to run their core business effectively without being embroiled in the technicalities and problems of the specialist area of IT.

At the same time, the technology itself has been developing even more dramatically; 'open systems', 'downsizing', 'client/server systems' and many other new approaches to the technology have become accepted as a better way of doing things in the 1990s than the approaches of the 1960s, 1970s and 1980s. Many companies are in a quandary as to how to manage the enormous change to take advantage of the new approaches to technology, without writing off very large investments in software and without risking discontinuity in their business as they migrate to the newer approach.

Handing over the problem to a specialist organization geared up to running the old technology, effectively exploiting the ‘trailing edge of technology’, but who is also well equipped to help with the transition to the new leading edge of technology is a solution that many organizations are looking at.

Cost containment has been a key motivator for the use of the growing ‘facilities management’ industry. Such motivation will continue to be important as margins continue to be under pressure, but we increasingly see other strategic reasons for the use of what is now better called ‘outsourcing’.

The recession has forced many companies to face up to such issues with an open mind. Having done this, many of them can see the advantages and opportunities in working together with a strategic partner who looks after the technology aspects of information systems, while they themselves look after their core business — including the strategic aspects of its information systems.

All of this is compounded by ever-increasing complexity as companies develop systems and use the technology to communicate with their business partners — both customers, suppliers and within the group. This makes it even more difficult for a single organization to handle the development and operation of all of its information systems processes. Inter-organizational systems, almost by definition, have to be offered by a third party. And, of course, with the effects of globalization, that issue is compounded as systems have to be not only inter-company, but also inter-country.

All of this leads to ever more complex projects and ones of a scale which demand the involvement of experienced broadly-based members of the service industry.

As this trend develops, further benefits ensue. In the past, those companies who have tried to operate inter-country services have tended to build communications networks of their own. As increasing numbers of companies 'outsource' their global information services, the members of the service industry are investing in major networks whose infrastructure can be shared between many customers. As this trend leads to the reduction in the duplication of infrastructure to provide information systems, the clear benefits can be shared between the users of those services and the service providers.

## An Olympian feat

In order to illustrate some of these points, let us consider a real world example of outsourcing the systems developed to support the highly successful Olympic Games in Barcelona.

By any standards, this was an ambitious project. It took six years to plan the Games. Computers were required to run all aspects of the events — ticketing, transport, accommodation, administration, and security, as well as a full results and information service.

The system had to work first time, on time.

The software — offered in English, French, Spanish and Catalan — registered every member of the 140 000 'Olympic family'. This included 40 000 competitors and representatives, 30 000 volunteer workers, 11 000 journalists and 6 million ticket sales. It controlled the movement of 2500 vehicles at 8000 pick-up points and ran the 24 000 capacity accommodation systems for the Games. It also gave biographies of all the competitors, their past successes and failures, to the many communications media staff covering the event.

This was offered through a network of 4400 PCs and 1100 laser printers based in 45 locations, linked via a 3600 node local area network to a central mainframe.

The value of the software contract alone amounted to £9 million. All for a system that could only be used for the duration of the Games — 15 days from opening to closing ceremonies.

## The issues

The project amply illustrates, in exaggerated form, the issues to be faced by managers using IT:

\- The system was critical to the smooth running of the Games. For elements of the system to be unavailable for even a short period would have been a very high profile disaster.

\- It had a fixed budget. Income for the Games was limited by the level of sponsorship available.

\- The systems were complex with many having to interact with each other.

\- It was not global in geographic coverage, but the system needed to be global in scope. For instance, it had to cope with the vagaries of the keyboard layouts for the various languages — QWERTY for English, AZERTY for French etc.

All of this had to be delivered by a committee brought together to organize the games, which almost by definition, had no track record of delivering such systems.

## The approach

The route that was chosen is therefore an interesting one. Most of the project was outsourced. External suppliers were brought in on fixed contracts to develop, deliver and run the system. Of course they had a greenfield site both at the start of the project and at the conclusion — a luxury that few IT Directors have!

In the six years that the Games took to organize, the committee developed an exceptionally good knowledge of the requirements and the structure of the system that they needed. However, they did not have, and by choosing the outsourcing route, did not need to have detailed understanding of the implementation of the system. This freed them from the considerable overhead of this task. They concentrated — par excellence — on their core business!

## Impacts on business

Increased pressure on margins will mean that managers in most industries are forced to evaluate and then re-evaluate core business and the manner in which it runs. This is likely to result in a period of tremendous change in the way business is structured and operated. Organizations are going to have to come up with a means of coping with the accumulated 'baggage' of past, outmoded systems. The outsourcing route offers that opportunity. To suppliers of 'trailing edge of technology' services which will help them cope.

In the past outsourcing providers have often used cost containment as one of the key selling points. However, the issues noted above offer a completely new rationale for companies to use it.

Obviously, cost control and containment have not gone away as management issues — the recession has ensured that! The continued need to reduce costs and standardize operations, will conflict with the need to invest in globalization and integration.

## Going global

The increasing need to support large projects is also putting IT services organizations themselves under increasing pressure. In order to support even pan-European (let alone global) projects, suppliers must have pan-European support infrastructures in place. Building such support networks consisting of both hardware and people, requires a great deal of funding. This is difficult for all but the strongest of companies, especially with margins under threat.

The opportunities — and the risks — are tremendous for both customer and supplier. In order to realize the opportunities and minimize the risks both parties must come together to operate in a partnership. Unless this is done, the fundamental survival of businesses could be threatened.

Unfortunately, in business the Olympic ideals do not apply. Although it is nice to take part, for long term survival you have to win. The effective use of IT outsourcing is helping many organizations to do just that.
