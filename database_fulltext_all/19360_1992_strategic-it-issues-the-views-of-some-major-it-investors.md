# Strategic IT issues: the views of some major IT investors

Tim Brady, Ross Cameron, David Targett and Chris Beaumont\*

School of Management, University of Bath, Claverton Down, Bath BA2 7AY, UK

Despite substantial evidence that IT can transform organizations and the industries in which they operate, management behaviour towards IT can differ markedly. Differences exist within an organization, between organizations, and in comparison to the academic literature. This article describes research to find what external, strategic issues managers believe to be important with regard to their organization's use of IT and their attitude and behaviour towards those issues. The investigation was undertaken in six leading-edge UK organizations through in-depth personal interviews with managers from different parts of the businesses. Three broad issues are discussed. The first is the notion of IT being a source of competitive advantage. The second relates to collaboration in IT investment and development. The third concerns organizations' IT strategies.

Keywords: IS, strategy, advantage

'Information technology as an “information engine” can do for business what the steam engine did in the days of the industrial revolution', proclaims the introduction to the MIT 90s research programme, investigating the impact of IT on organizations $^{1}$ .

Such claims are not unusual in the literature on IT. Managers have been deluged by a host of articles arguing the merits of IT, and how organizations must seize the opportunities offered by IT either to gain the ascendency over competitors, or, as a minimum, to stay competitive. They have read or heard the stories about organizations that have ‘changed the rules of the game’ through the innovative use of IT—names such as Baxter/American Hospital Supply, McKesson and American Airlines, for example. They have been offered a multiplicity of frameworks for analysing their own organizations. The paper $^{2}$ “What is strategic about strategic information systems?” summarizes this literature.

However, while few managers may doubt the enormous potential of IT in transforming their organizations and the markets in which they operate, not many seem to have been able to realize such potential. This article contains the findings of research to identify managers' attitudes and behaviour on the major strategic issues relating to IT in six leading-edge organizations in three business sectors in the UK.

The research found differences within and between organizations, but particularly in comparison to the academic literature. Managers were asked what issues they believed to be important with regard to their organization's use of IT, and what their attitude and behaviour towards those issues were. The investigation was undertaken through in-depth personal interviews with senior managers from different functions.

The article focuses on three broad issues which are concerned with the external, strategic dimension. The first is the notion of IT being a source of competitive advantage. While some of the literature argues that IT can be a source of sustainable competitive advantage, the research suggests that in practice this is rarely the case. However, it also indicates that the contestable competitive advantage offered by IT should not be overlooked in the organization's competitive context.

The second issue relates to collaboration in IT investment and development. Although some inter-organizational IT collaboration has occurred, organizations have on the whole pursued IT initiatives independently, subsequently driving up the industry-wide costs of competition.

The third issue concerns organizations' IT strategies. Organizations differ in the context, scope and means of development of their IT strategies.

## Research methodology

A series of face-to-face interviews was conducted with executives of six organizations in three industry sectors in the UK. The three industry sectors were selected because they have seen significant investments in IT: finance, retail and manufacturing. The six companies were not chosen to be representative of their sectors. Instead they were selected because they are regarded as being ‘leading-edge’ in their use of IT. The companies are all household names, with turnovers well in excess of £1000 million.

The research is aimed at quality and depth of evidence rather than coverage. The companies were also chosen because they are of significant size within their sectors and because access to senior managers across a range of functions could be obtained.

The purpose of the interviews was to explore perspectives on IT in some depth. They were designed to find out about attitudes, decision-making modes and nuances rather than factual information, actual situations rather than wants and ideals for the future. The questions covered attitudes to IT, decision-making processes, organizational structure and the evaluation of investments.

Face-to-face interviews were appropriate given the relatively unstructured nature of the dialogue as well as the breadth of topics addressed. These face-to-face interviews were undertaken with senior general managers, senior functional managers (end-users of IT—usually from the marketing function) and senior IT executives.

Evidence from the literature is integrated throughout the article and compared with the research findings.

## IT and sustainable competitive advantage

Porter is a major authority on competitive strategy (CS) and competitive advantage (CA). However, this research suggests that Porter's frameworks are insufficient (or perhaps just misunderstood) in considering IT in the organization's competitive context. They overlook the most frequently occurring type of CA that arises from IT—contestable competitive advantage. In this research the advantages accruing to organizations through their use of IT were significant, despite the fact that the duration and vulnerability of the advantage rendered them unsustainable.

## The old model

Until the late 1980s, many leading academics argued that IT can offer organizations a sustainable competitive advantage (SCA). Porter's contention is that the long-run profit potential of an industry is a function of five basic forces: the threat of new entrants, the threat of introduction of substitute products or services, the relative bargaining power of suppliers and buyers, and the intensity of rivalry among existing firms $^{3}$ . Porter argues that to generate a CA over its competition, a firm must pursue one of the generic strategies—either be the low-cost producer, or differentiate its products on factors other than price. The firm can adopt such strategies to the market as a whole, or to a narrow segment.

Porter claims that actions will not lead to above average performance unless ‘sustainable vis-à-vis competitors . . . The strategic value of a [cost or differentiation] advantage hinges on its sustainability’ $^{4}$ .

King also places great emphasis on the sustainability of a CA $^{5}$ . King et al. argue that the strategic use of IT is one in which IT assists the organization to achieve a key objective and when the IT enables the achievement of a SCA. Many others present similar arguments $^{6-9}$ .

The empirical evidence offered by others, and the findings of this research reveal that IT has enabled many organizations to achieve key objectives. However, few claim to have generated a SCA from their IT.

The new model

In contrast to Porter and King, others now argue that IT cannot lead to a SCA. For example, Cecil and Goldstein state that 'It is virtually impossible for major IT users (including vendors) to gain advantage based on IT alone . . . CEO's of most companies are hard put to cite even a single example of having achieved more than a short-lived advantage from their competitors in IT' $^{10}$ . Cecil and Goldstein argue that the SCA success stories of IT were generated in days long gone.

Perhaps the most telling comment however comes from Max Hopper, the architect of American Airlines' SABRE system, who states that 'while it is more dangerous than ever to ignore the power of information technology, it is more dangerous still to believe that on its own, an information system can provide an enduring business advantage. The old models no longer apply' $^{11}$ .

All executives interviewed in the research stated that they have not generated a SCA from IT. Almost all have generated some form of CA from IT, but they have found this advantage to be unsustainable, as the following comments indicate:

You can have a CA for an amount of time, but everybody catches up ... we had a lead, but it is one which the others have caught up on so we will go onto something new.

(Director of Data Processing, Food Retailer)

In the early '70s, we had the best on line and overnight branch system. That advantage was around for 5 years. It was only when a new generation of systems came out that the others caught up.

(Senior General Manager, Bank)

Products can create no SCA: they have to be continually refurbished, endorsed, reinvested in. The SCAs have bugger all to do with products and technology.

## (Senior General Manager, Bank)

The concern with the ‘old model’ arguments is not that SCAs are unimportant but rather that they undervalue the importance of contestable CAs. Applying Porter's criteria, none of the companies interviewed has generated a CA through IT. This is due to the fact that the benefits are short- to medium-term only, and can be emulated by competitors. The companies had to build up their systems through continuing incremental innovations in order to maintain competitive edge.

As Ghemawat noted, 'Managers cannot afford to ignore contestable advantages. For one thing, even moves that offer ephemeral advantages may be worth making, if only to avoid competitive disadvantage. For another, some contestable advantage may survive uncontested' $^{12}$ .

Interviewees from a food retailer argued the merits of contestable CA through IT. An example is EFTPOS (Electronic Funds Transfer at Point of Sale) which was introduced in 1988. In 1991 over 26% of customer purchases were paid by debit card, providing a windfall of approximately £1.5 million in direct interest savings, not to mention the very significant savings from reduced store security, and administrative costs. EFTPOS also offers customers numerous benefits.

There were also many mutual benefits such as those offered by the cashback facility.

While executives argue that most CA from IT is unsustainable, it is important to examine whether the benefits derived from IT are significant enough to consider in the overall competitive context of the organization. This was demonstrated by a case study of a manufacturer of alcoholic beverages.

Case study: drinks manufacturer. Some of the IT initiatives recently taken by the organization are:

\- A container tracking system. The company has 2 million returnable metal containers, with a total replacement value of £70 million. Bar-coding all containers will minimize container loss and increase sales control.

\- An EPOS system into associated public houses. This will increase their profitability by 3–4%, through improved stock control.

\- Direct computerized ordering from the retail outlets. While reducing paperwork, this will also form technological ties which will increase barriers to entry of competitors.

\- A system enabling representatives to record quickly and easily information such as competitors' prices and shelf space at retail outlets. This information will be used in marketing activities.

\- Computerized route planning. This will reduce distribution costs.

\- A system to enable company wide access to information generated by each production plant's Process Control Equipment.

The duration of advantage of such initiatives varies from short term to possibly long term. None of them offers a truly sustainable CA—all the advantage will be contestable. However, this is not to say that the moves should be dismissed as trivial since the company estimates the quantifiable benefits to be measured in tens of million pounds.

The business benefits stemming from the initiatives are:

\- Competitive action: competitive information, 'locking in' retail outlets.

\- Improved customer service: computerized ordering.

\- Reduced costs: almost all initiatives, but specifically bar-coding containers, and EPOS in public houses.

\- Improved efficiency: computerized route planning.

\- Improved marketing effectiveness: better monitoring of competitor's initiatives.

For companies investigated in this research, business is fought on short- to middle-term advantages. None has transformed its business or the industry in which it operates through the creation of a SCA arising from IT, and most do not expect to be able to.

It doesn't sound like very much, but it's all about cutting costs at the margin and that's competitive edge. It's not the major earth-shattering thing where you're going to take over the British drinks industry ...

(IS Manager, Drinks Manufacturer)

Porter may argue that the organization is pursuing a cost-based generic strategy. The interviewees strongly deny this, claiming they are trying to differentiate their products from the competition, while trying to reduce costs simultaneously.

As Wiseman notes, 'The distinction between contestable and sustainable advantage . . . opens a universe of possibilities that Porter has closed by, in effect, equating Competitive Advantage with Sustainable Competitive Advantage' $^{13}$ .

## Lack of practical frameworks for strategic IT

There are of course many frameworks for dealing with strategic IT. However, organizations lack prescriptive and ‘actionable’ frameworks which can be applied to identify the areas for maximum IT leverage.

To assist organizations identify the areas for maximum IT leverage, Porter proposed 'value chain analysis', which disaggregates an organization into strategically relevant activities $^{3}$ . While Porter's value chain, or variations of it, are of tremendous appeal, executives' comments indicate that they do not use such tools effectively.

An action-oriented framework which may be more relevant to the interviewees (but which was not tested on them) may be that proposed by Cecil and Goldstein, who identify four different IT strategies: reduce, extend, first mover, focused hassle $^{2, 10}$ .

An example of the ‘first mover’ strategy is a food retailer’s introduction of scanning devices, which offered it a short-term CA. Scanning devices enabled the company to reduce prices, and realize other benefits such as improved freshness and quality of produce through greater stock control. As a result of these factors, the retailer claimed an increased market share. Despite the fact that competitors have now also introduced scanning devices, the retailer has had the time to exploit its first mover advantage to achieve other advantages—brand loyalty for example.

## Interfirm collaboration and competition

While the last section focused on the sustainability or otherwise of any CA resulting from IT, it is of fundamental importance to identify what is the actual source of CA through IT. The drinks manufacturer case-study illustrates that there can be a number of sources. In some situations, the IT itself provides a CA, as in the case of computerized ordering. In other instances, CA arises from the use of the information generated by IT, such as in the use of information from process control devices.

Interviews revealed widespread confusion regarding the source of CA in IT. This confusion is illustrated by organizations' different perspectives on what many believe will be a key to management in the 1990s—interfirm collaboration.

## Interfirm domestic collaboration

The literature suggests that the 1990s will be a decade of interfirm collaboration. Rosabeth Moss Kanter, for example, argues that organizations need to become 'PALs'—to pool resources with other organizations, ally to exploit opportunities, or link systems in a partnership $^{14}$ . It is through such alliances, Kanter argues, that organizations are able to compete better without adding internal capacity.

Ohmae argues that alliances are not simply desirable, but absolutely necessary, given the globalization of markets and industries, converging consumer tastes and rapidly spreading technologies $^{15}$ .

With specific reference to IT, MIT 90s researchers argue that IT has the capacity either to exacerbate or to soften existing competition, turning rivalries into cooperative ventures $^{16}$ .

The development of free trade blocs, in particular the single European market, will further such collaboration. Indeed, PA reports that cross-border IT alliances within the EC increased to 46 in 1986, from an initial level of only six in 1983. It reports that EC-US alliances have also increased (from 32 to 49), but at a slower rate $^{17}$ . Supporting literature to the MIT 90s research confirms such trends $^{18}$ .

Against this background, it is ironic that the companies interviewed revealed a lack of willingness to collaborate with their traditional competitors. To collaborate with a competitor in an area where one organization has, or could have a CA, would by definition threaten the sustainability of such an advantage.

While collaborative ventures and strategic alliances 'should be viewed as highly unstable social systems' $^{18}$ , this research suggests that the reason for the lack of interest and progress in such collaborative ventures is not instability, but a lack of skill in identifying the source of organizational competitive advantage.

Ford provides a useful distinction between three categories of technologies. Basic technologies are those on which the company depends and without which it would not be able to operate. Distinctive technologies are those through which the company excels on a technical offering. External technologies are those which a company buys in so as not to divert the company's operations from those in which it excels $^{19}$ .

This research reveals that companies too often compete on ‘basic technologies’, which given the size and resources of the companies in competition, only serves to drive up industry-wide costs of competition. That is, by failing to understand what could lead to a CA in the first place, companies have individually invested heavily in IT when a collective investment may have sufficed.

When auto-teller machines (ATMs) were first developed, the major banks hastily installed them around the UK, believing that their presence would generate a CA. The industry soon realized, however, that the presence of an ATM would not, in itself, generate a CA. Indeed it was proving to be an industry-wide cost burden.

For this reason, two clearing banks collaborated in ATM access, by enabling one's customers access through the other's ATM, and vice versa. This saved both banks approximately £100 million. This is an example of 'competitive advantage giving way to cooperative advantage'. As was stated by one executive:

You must cooperate on those areas where you would just dissipate resources.... It's foolish to slug out a battle with scarce resources when neither of you can win, and indeed when there can be no winner as the business solution is generic.

(Senior General Manager, Bank)

The bank realized that an ATM was not a distinctive technology, and in itself it would not provide it with a competitive advantage—its source of competitive advantage would be the range and type of services that are offered to customers when using a card, whether they use it at a ‘home’ or ‘away’ ATM.

One may argue that the banks rushed to install ATMs, not just to achieve staff reductions, but also in the belief that the first to do so would own the network. Given the cost of establishing a network, the argument ran, the competition would settle to use it for a fee. This argument can be rejected on account of the size and resources of the organizations in question and the general level of competitiveness of the industry.

Other areas of collaboration exist in the banking industry in situations in which it is in all ‘competitors’ interests. For example, banks have agreed common standards in clearing cheques (8L), which ensures that all banks prepare magnetic tape in a format usable by all. Other areas of agreement include message standards for EDI, and SWIFT.

However, different organizations have different perspectives even on this:

This is a means of moving data which is necessary to maintain the money flows in the country as a whole. If we couldn't do that we'd all be stuffed ... we're actually providing services in parallel. We'll always do our own thing. I think it's because IT is the vehicle through which business expands these days.

(Senior General Manager, Bank)

Such an attitude prevails elsewhere in that bank:

We did get involved with EFTPOS UK and we are involved in BAPS ... I think that ... IT is seen as a competitive advantage, and we wouldn't want to share it.

(Senior General Manager, Bank)

These executives are arguing that they have an understanding as to how IT is a source of CA, claiming that they only collaborated on agreed standards because it was in their interests to do so. However, they clearly expressed the opinion that they would continue to offer 'services in parallel' with competition, believing that IT itself is the source of CA. This lack of understanding as to the source of CA may be an inhibitor to further collaborative arrangements in the banking industry since executives of the other bank argue quite the opposite, stating that the added value services offered on the IT network are the source of competitive advantage, not the network itself:

The competition (in EDI) was in the quality of the service and the ability to integrate that service with the customer, not in the size and profile of the wire ... the trick is to not use your resources on a non competitive area.... We don't care about the technology per se. We want to compete on the service quality: what can you get out of our ATMs.

(Senior General Manager, Bank)

The retail companies interviewed have cooperated in few IT ventures. Although all interviewees in these organizations stated that they believed that IT offers no SCA, they continued to pursue IT opportunities independently in order to generate a middle-term CA, which may be used for purposes that are more sustainable.

One area in which retailers have collaborated is through the Article Numbering Association (ANA), in insisting that manufacturers improve the quality of bar coding on product packaging. This has benefited all retailers, and thus it was in their collective interests to pursue.

The drinks manufacturer illustrates that the notion of CA should be inextricably linked with the ‘core business’ of the organization. The organization has, over recent years, reconsidered what its core business is and acted accordingly.

## Interfirm global collaboration

With the increasing globalization of markets, organizations must broaden their outlook, both in terms of potential opportunities, and potential threats. The retail organizations, for example, concede that over the coming years they will be subject to greater competition from retailers from continental Europe, in particular those from Germany, which will establish operations in the UK where margins are considerably higher.

Although companies interviewed perceive their market in global rather than domestic terms, few are actively using collaborative initiatives to seize such opportunities. This is despite the fact that all companies interviewed concede that they do not currently have the resource base to penetrate and eventually dominate the world markets in which they operate. One exception is the drinks manufacturer which has a high degree of integration of its operations with those of its parent company.

Only one of the retailers had worked with retailers from overseas, primarily in the USA, with whom it had ‘compared notes’. While this can hardly be referred to as collaboration, the executives stated that such contact does provide it with ideas for IT development and process improvements.

In the case of the banking industry, the globalization of financial markets has been more rapid than most other industries, and thus many of the collaborative standards-based initiatives discussed above apply internationally.

## Interindustry collaboration

One of the major opportunities presented by IT is its ability to enable companies in different industries to integrate. The banks have been eager to seize opportunities to integrate their services with the value chains of other industries. Developments in EFTPOS and EDI for example could not have been achieved without cooperation between the two industries—retail and banking. Even in such cases however, executives from both retail and finance institutions indicated that they have been too slow and reluctant to work with other industries to come to agreements that have benefited all concerned.

Two of the retailers argue that they have been able to generate a (contestable) CA through linking their operations with manufacturers of products to be sold in their stores. This ‘business network redesign’ is an indication of a high degree of IT sophistication and development, which few organizations have attained.

In summary, therefore, though leaders in their respective markets in the UK, the organizations surveyed concede that they have been slow to collaborate with other organizations, both within the same industry domestically and internationally, and with different industries. Alarmingly, some do not perceive the need for this to occur.

## IT strategies

The term ‘IT strategy’ is used by organizations to describe the document containing plans, intentions and policies for the organization’s current and future use of IT, and ‘softer’ IT-related issues, such as the integration of the IT department with end-users, for example.

Earl distinguishes between three levels of information strategy—the IT, IS and IM strategies $^{20}$ .

1. IT strategy focuses on the technological architecture which drives, shapes and controls the IT infrastructure. Earl refers to this as 'the how'.

2. IS strategy is concerned with aligning IS development with business needs and with seeking strategic advantage from IT. Being business led and demand oriented, it is referred to as 'the what'.

3. Information management (IM) strategy contains the management framework which guides how the organization will run its IS/IT activities. Earl refers to it as 'the wherefore'.

While it is useful to distinguish between these different types of information strategy, the research reveals that delineating between the three is infrequently done by organizations. In this section, the term IT strategy is used to describe the organization's IT, IS and IM strategies, as defined by Earl.

## IT strategies: planned or emergent?

Similar to the long-running debate regarding strategy formulation at the corporate and strategic business unit level, there are two opposing schools of thought on the topic of how IT strategies are developed.

First, the traditional school views corporate strategy development as occurring through a rational, fundamental analysis of the business, its competition, the market, and any other stakeholders. Such a 'hard-headed' view is espoused by Ansoff, Andrews and Porter, for example.

The opposing school believes that corporate strategies emerge, arguing that the processes used to arrive at the strategy are fragmented, evolutionary and intuitive. This has been described as 'logical incrementalism'. Miller is of this opposing school. He argues that 'virtually everything that is written about the information technology strategy process depicts it as a deliberate process. In reality, a strategy can emerge in response to an evolving situation just as easily as it can be developed through a process of deliberate formulation' $^{21}$ .

Miller proposes that IT strategies can be developed by compiling an inventory of past IT decisions, and an inventory of the current IT assets, and expanding these to provide the most appropriate combination to achieve the organization's goals.

From this research, it appears as though the IT strategies of organizations have been developed through a combination of rational analysis and alignment with business objectives, and a continuation of previous policies, emerging into a strategy.

## Contents of IT strategy document

Although organizations do not structure their information strategy in Earl's terms, it is useful to use Earl's framework to consider whether adequate attention is given to each element.

The how. The area that receives most attention in the IT strategy is what Earl classifies as 'the how': the IT framework and technology-related policies such as architecture, risk attitudes, vendor policies and technical standards. Given that there is considerable input from personnel with IT backgrounds in the construction of the IT strategy, it is hardly surprising that on the whole the organizations addressed these issues thoroughly.

The what. The what is concerned with aligning IS development with business needs and seeking advantage from IT. Contrary to the opinion expressed by Earl, this research suggests that IT strategies are closely linked with organizations' business strategies and long-range plans. The executives interviewed clearly perceive that in fact the IT strategy starts with the business objectives.

Both IT and non-IT executives argued that alignment of the IT strategy and business objectives is fundamental if IT is to play more than merely a passive support role in the organization. As Angell argues, 'Companies without a coherent policy linking technological development to corporate goals, tend to surrender responsibility for IT to technocrats, who may indulge their fascination for irrelevant technology, without considering the wider needs of the organisation' $^{22}$ .

An example of the integration between IT strategy and business strategy is presented by the drinks manufacturer. Prior to the Director of Management Services constructing the organization's first IT strategy, an extensive interviewing process was undertaken with executive directors to identify the organization's business objectives. The ramifications of these objectives on the IT strategy were profound:

... we know we had to be flexible to change in the market place, ... and we also knew we wanted to grow by acquisition. So what we had to do was develop an [IT] structure that was going to be very flexible to change.

(Director of Management Services, Drinks Manufacturer)

The most recent Price Waterhouse IT survey reveals that integrating IT with corporate strategy is the major issue facing IT managers, as it has been for the past two years $^{23}$ . However, the same survey reports that no less than 36% of respondents state that their business operates on a planning horizon of one year or less. Clearly this presents IT managers with the difficulty of having to adapt longer term IT strategies and investments, often which have very considerable development time, to short-term business plans.

The wherefore. The IM strategy is concerned with the relationship between IT specialists and users, and between the centre and business units. Organizations differ considerably not only in their levels of skill in addressing such factors in their strategy, but also in the extent to which they insist that such factors be specifically addressed at all.

For example, the IT strategy of one of the banks covers such topics as the integration of the IT functions with user departments in tremendous detail, leaving little area for confusion. In contrast, one of the food retailers does not cover such issues explicitly, but they are addressed and discussed through the IT strategy development process and the IT investment decision-making process, both of which call for high levels of interaction and discussion between IT executives and end-users. Such sessions provide a forum for these 'soft' issues to be addressed. At the other extreme another food retailer appears to pay little, if any, attention to such 'soft' factors.

Some organizations opt for more of a ‘simultaneous loose-tight’ approach $^{24}$ , allowing other departments to make up their own minds on certain types of IT decisions, so long as they are made within the framework of the organization’s IT strategy. Hence they are offered some autonomy, but not enough to result in ‘computer anarchy’, as Eason describes it $^{25}$ . Such a ‘loose-tight’ approach is used by the drinks manufacturer which enables it to avoid the development of ‘chewing gum and string solutions’. As one executive stated:

What I've tried to do is to give people as long a rein as I possibly can and said do whatever you like, so long as you obey the rules which have been laid down.

(Director Management Services, Drinks Manufacturer)

## Contributors to IT strategy

Organizations also differ as to who contributes to their IT strategy. One food retailer's IT strategy is developed by one person: the IT Strategy and Planning Manager. There is no end-user, or other IT expert assistance in this IT strategy formulation. In most other organizations, the IT strategy is developed by a number of persons.

Galliers' study into IT strategies of UK companies reveals that the number of personnel involved in the IT strategy process varies from just one or two to over 10, with the norm being three to five members $^{26}$ . Furthermore, it is interesting to note that Galliers also states that the great majority of IS planners believe that the process should be headed by a senior non-IT business executive $^{27}$ . In contrast to the one-person strategy described above, some organizations opt to have major stakeholders as parties to the IT strategy formulation. This is said to be done to ensure a 'market-led' IT strategy and so that user departments feel a sense of ownership of the IT strategy. Not only does this assist in implementation, but importantly it reinforces in the mind of end users the role and importance of IT in the company.

One of the food retailers insists that the IT strategy be developed and reviewed by IT executives and end-users alike. The review is used to generate ideas for future IT investment possibilities.

## Conclusions

The depth of the interviews and the seniority of the interviewees gives weight to the research findings which were, in summary:

1. IT and sustainable competitive advantage. The research supports the view that IT is not perceived as being a source of sustainable competitive advantage. The fact that the advantage arising is not sustainable however, does not nullify its importance in the organization's competitive context.

2. Interfirm collaboration and competition. It has been argued that the 1990s will be a decade of interfirm collaboration. The behaviour and comments of organizations in general do not support this. By failing to identify the sources of competitive advantage, the costs of competition are being driven up by firms competing when they should be collaborating.

3. IT strategies. The organizations differed in the detail and coverage of their IT strategies. Overall it is the 'softer' issues that are most poorly addressed in IT strategies. In contrast to claims made in the literature, both IT and non-IT executives are very aware of the need to align the IT strategy with the business objectives.
