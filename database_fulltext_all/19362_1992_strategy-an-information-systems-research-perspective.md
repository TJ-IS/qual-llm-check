# Strategy: an information systems research perspective

Yolande E. Chan and Sid L. Huff\*

School of Business, Queen's University, Kingston, Ontario, Canada K7L 3N6

The past decade has witnessed the development of a new stream of research, one focused on information systems (IS) and their relation to company strategy and the attainment of competitive advantage. A growing number of IS researchers are turning their attention to investigating questions in the IS/business strategy area. This article is intended to provide an overview of business strategy research findings and recommendations that are especially relevant to IS researchers, focusing on key conceptual and measurement issues in three areas: competitive strategy; strategic fit; and business performance. It may be used as a ‘primer’ for IS researchers considering conducting research with business strategy as a reference discipline. It also should be of interest to IS academics generally, inasmuch as the relationship between IS and business strategy has become a key issue in this field.

Keywords: information systems research, business strategy research

The past decade has witnessed the development of a new stream of research, one focused on information systems and their relation to company strategy and the attainment of competitive advantage. The term strategic information system has become widely used. Interest in this area continues to develop, as evidenced by the growing number of academic and practitioner articles appearing in books and journals (see, for example, Refs 1–4).

Keen $^{5}$ drew the attention of information systems (IS) researchers to the importance of grounding their research in relevant reference disciplines. IS research frequently is multidisciplinary, addressing managerial, behavioural, and technical concerns. It can usefully employ theories and methods from related disciplines. Business strategy has become an increasingly relevant discipline for IS research. However, IS researchers are at a significant disadvantage when attempting to investigate problems in this area, since many possess only a scant knowledge of the literature, models and paradigms in the business strategy field.

This article is not intended to provide a comprehensive review of the existing strategic IS literature; such reviews may be found elsewhere $^{2,6-8}$ . It is instead intended to provide an overview of business strategy research findings and recommendations that are especially relevant to IS researchers. As such, it may be used as a ‘primer’ for researchers considering carrying out studies in the strategic IS area, utilizing business strategy as a reference discipline. It also should be of interest to IS academics generally, inasmuch as the relation of IS with business strategy has become a critical issue in this field. The article includes a carefully selected bibliography from the strategy literature designed to enable IS researchers to explore more fully the concepts discussed. It also includes appendices summarizing competitive strategy typologies, major empirical strategy research programs, and performance measurement approaches. Interspersed throughout are key recommendations of strategy researchers. These are summarized and highlighted in the form of short normative statements of good research practice.

As the literature reviewed below indicates, two key factors directly impacting company performance are business strategy and IS strategic fit (the alignment between business strategy and IS strategy). Accordingly, the article is organized as follows. First, the strategic IS literature is summarized briefly. Then relevant business strategy literature is reviewed. The concept of business strategy, with an emphasis on competitive, as opposed to corporate strategy, is explored. The concept of strategic fit is introduced and discussed. Finally, business performance is discussed. Theoretical as well as measurement issues are addressed throughout the article in order to assist IS researchers to develop as well as test their own research models.

## Strategic information systems literature

'Strategic information systems' have been described as information systems used to support or shape an organization's competitive strategy, its plan for gaining and maintaining competitive advantage $^{4,9}$ . They can significantly change business performance as measured by one or more key indicators, contribute to attaining a strategic goal, and fundamentally change the way the company does business or the way it competes in its industry $^{10}$ .

The IS research on strategic systems embodies conceptual discussions as well as empirical studies. The conceptual articles examine issues such as the general question of using IT to gain competitive advantage (e.g. Refs 2, 11-14), strategic IS planning methodologies (e.g. Refs 15-18), and frameworks for identifying opportunities for strategic IS (e.g. Refs 7, 9, 19-26).

The empirical studies often have employed case studies and historical analyses (e.g. Refs 27-29) to describe the evolution of strategic IS applications within the across organizations and industries. There also have been a number of case studies and cross-sectional studies examining factors and processes leading to successful strategic IS planning, systems development, implementation, and use (e.g. Refs 6, 30-33). Other studies have created measurement instruments to assess IS strategy, competitive advantages resulting from strategic IS, and business performance impacts of strategic IS (e.g. Refs 34-38).

There continues to be much theory building and testing in the strategic IS area today. In the section which follows, business strategy literature which can be used as a guide for strategic IS theory development is examined in detail.

## Aspects of business strategy

What is strategy? Hofer and Schendel $^{39}$ state that it is ‘the match an organization makes between its internal resources and skills (sometimes collectively called competences) and the opportunities and risks created by its external environment’ [p 12]. Business strategy aims to establish a match or fit between the organization and its environment. Strategy-making can be viewed as rational and comprehensive or it can be viewed as sequential and incremental. The former view is characteristic of the ‘strategic planning’ school; the latter view is more adaptive $^{40}$ .

Mintzberg $^{41}$ points out that formulated or intended strategies may not be realized; unplanned patterns of behaviour may emerge. Researchers may elect to study intended and/or realized business strategies.

Two identifiable streams in business strategy research exist: strategy as content, and strategy as process. Robinson and Pearce $^{42}$ write:

Strategic management research has evolved from a narrow, process focus to a similarly narrow, content focus as researchers have attempted to understand the strategic management-organizational performance relationship. The strategic management concerns of top managers have evolved incrementally from process considerations to a concern with both the content of strategies and the processes for deriving and implementing effective strategies ... While researchers have suggested the need to simultaneously study strategy content and process as determinants of organizational performance, research of this type has not been undertaken. [pp 44, 47].

IS investigators conducting research in the strategy area should consider both strategy content and process issues. Practical constraints may make it difficult to do so, however. A tradeoff exists between reduced error variance and increased complexity and costs.

Both strategy content and strategy process issues may need to be considered when conducting strategy-related research.

Learned et al. $^{43}$ write, ‘Strategy has two equally important aspects . . . The first of these is formulation; the second is implementation’ [p 20]. Researchers may focus on either strategy formulation or implementation, bearing in mind that these processes are closely intertwined. Information systems can be used to gather data to assist with strategy formulation. IS researchers can examine the impacts of information access, flow, and integration on the strategic decision-making process. Information systems also may be employed to exploit company strengths and function as competitive weapons. Once planned, these systems, like other components of business strategy, must be implemented. Only a few IS studies to date (e.g. Refs 44, 45) have focused on strategic system implementation issues.

Researchers may focus on strategy formulation and/or implementation issues.

A somewhat related dichotomy in the strategic management literature concerns strategy as encompassing means and ends versus strategy as encompassing means alone. Porter $^{46}$ subscribes to the former, broader view. He writes:

Essentially, developing a competitive strategy is developing a broad formula for how a business is going to compete, what its goals should be, and what policies will be needed to carry out those goals. [p xvi]

Hofer and Schendel $^{39}$ subscribe to a more narrow view of strategy, writing:

We adopt the narrow concept of strategy and shall consider goal setting and strategy formulation to be . . . distinct, although interrelated. [p 20]

Most researchers subscribe to the Hofer and Schendel point of view. IS researchers seeking to operationalize the strategy construct must decide whether they wish to assess company goals as well as means.

Researchers must decide whether to focus on company goals as well as means.

## Competitive strategy

The literature distinguishes between corporate strategy and business unit\* strategy. Porter $^{47}$ argues:

A diversified company has two levels of strategy: business unit (or competitive) strategy and corporate (or company-wide) strategy. Competitive strategy concerns how to create competitive advantage in each of the businesses\* in which a company competes. Corporate strategy concerns two different questions: what businesses the corporation should be in and how the corporate office should manage the array of business units. [p 43]

Most IS research to date has been concerned primarily with business unit level strategy, and the use of information technology for competitive advantage in a given industry. Many research opportunities currently exist, however, with regard to ways in which information systems can be used to support and/or drive corporate strategy.

## It is important for researchers to distinguish between business unit strategy and corporate strategy.

Hofer and Schendel $^{39}$ suggest that business unit strategy has four separate components: competitive advantages $^{†}$ , distinctive competences, synergy or internal fit, and scope or product-market combinations. Information systems can impact each of these strategy components. IS can be used to obtain competitive advantages when it represents, or is used to exploit, a firm's distinctive competence. It can be used to improve inter- and intra-firm synergies and to expand company product and market options.

Morrison $^{48}$ asserts that the four Hofer and Schendel strategy components can in fact be reduced to two: competitive methods (the means by which companies obtain competitive advantages) and scope. He argues that distinctive competences are organizational capabilities that may, but also may not, be used in strategy formulation. They impact primarily the nature and sustainability of competitive advantages. Also, synergy may be viewed as a strategy objective, rather than as a strategy component. In many studies, attention can be focused solely on competitive methods because scope is more important to corporate strategy than it is to business unit strategy $^{39}$ . In addition, once strategic business units (SBUs) have been selected for any given study, firm scope has in effect been determined.

Beard and Dess $^{49}$ point out that researchers seeking to study competitive strategy are well advised to use single-industry firms, and not single-industry subunits of multi-industry corporations, as their primary units of analysis. Miller $^{50}$ uses undiversified, autonomous companies in his study. He avoids including divisions in his sample, as they often must conform to head office policies. In addition, corporate policies (e.g. transfer pricing) can make divisional tactics and performance difficult to assess. Several researchers, however (for example, Dess and Davis $^{51}$ ), choose not to work with single-industry corporations, but instead use standard industrial classification (SIC) codes to help them identify firms concentrated in one line of business or industry only.

When studying competitive strategy, researchers should consider working with firms operating in or concentrated in single industries.

Snow and Hrebiniak $^{52}$ and Hambrick $^{53}$ suggest that managerial (non-CEO) awareness of competitive strategy and/or firm distinctive competences — the skills and assets a business possesses that are superior to those of its competition — frequently is limited. Conclusions reached on the basis of interviews with non-CEOs may have diminished validity. Hambrick $^{53}$ also points out that non-awareness of business strategy varies with the industry involved. Executives in very competitive industries appear to have a greater awareness of business strategy.

Researchers cannot assume that strategic awareness exists. Whenever possible, they should direct their questions to CEOs and their immediate assistants. When other executives are consulted, the more senior their rank, the more likely they are to have valid perceptions of company strategy.

## Researchers should utilize CEOs and top ranking officials as information sources when assessing competitive strategy.

As well as competitive substrategies, business units have investment substrategies, and also integration substrategies $^{48}$ . Researchers studying business unit strategies in global industries may need to examine all four substrategies. However investigators working with domestic industries may be able to focus only on competitive substrategies. This is because firm investment and political environments may be relatively homogeneous, and firm integration issues (within a country versus across countries) tend to be less critical.

## Measuring competitive strategy

IS investigators conducting research in the strategy area frequently need to characterize, or measure, company strategy. There is little point, for example, in trying to uncover links between information systems and strategy, if strategy cannot be formally assessed to begin with. Hambrick $^{53}$ discusses four approaches which have been used in the past to operationalize competitive strategy: textual descriptions of strategy (e.g. case studies); measurement of parts of strategies, focusing on one or a few key variables; multivariate measurement of strategy, often resulting in the creation of taxonomies; and strategy typologies $^{\ddagger}$ .

Recent empirical studies of strategy have focused on the identification of strategic taxonomies $^{55}$ , an approach considered state-of-the-art $^{42}$ . Although there is much to commend this approach, Roth and Pearce $^{56}$ point out the need for investigators to go beyond proposing new taxonomies; existing taxonomies need to be confirmed (or disconfirmed). Verifying previously derived classifications is difficult, however, because of the industry-specific nature of competitive strategy. Except for very broad taxonomies, most are not likely to generalize well to research settings different from the ones in which they were originally derived.

## Textual descriptions

Harrigan $^{57}$ examines the case study approach, concluding that firms' past strategies can be inferred successfully from studies of their histories, and comparisons of how various firms in the same industry have responded to strategic change. Multiple interviews within each firm can be combined with archival data gathering (e.g. journal articles, annual reports) to provide a richness in understanding strategy that cannot be obtained via a survey approach. She also discusses the usefulness of the Delphi study, a variation of the case study approach, wherein company interviewees are shown the study's interim findings, and given repeated opportunities to provide feedback and furnish new insights.

Mintzberg $^{41}$ and Mintzberg and Waters $^{58}$ provide examples of historical research focusing on strategy formation and strategic change. They track the strategies of a retail chain, Steinberg Inc., over several decades $^{58}$ , and review the strategies of Volkswagenwerk and the US government in Vietnam $^{41}$ . They examine decision streams in organizations over long time periods, identifying periods of consistency and change in patterns of organizational decision-making.

To obtain a rich and detailed understanding of strategy from multiple viewpoints, consider case studies or historical approaches.

## Deductive typologies

Much of the original strategy measurement work involved the use of typologies. Porter $^{46}$ is responsible for perhaps the best known strategy typology — a classification scheme encompassing cost leadership, differentiation, and focus strategies. Several researchers (see Refs 51, 59, 60 for example) provide empirical support, albeit somewhat mixed, for the Porter classifications. Chrisman, Hofer and Boulton $^{61}$ develop a classification system which combines those of the Porter $^{46}$ and Abell $^{62,*}$ classification schemes. They position this combined typology as a foundation for future strategy research.

Several well known competitive strategy typologies are outlined in Appendix I. The advantage to IS researchers of measuring strategy in terms of typologies is the relative ease of operationalization of this approach. Each company studied simply has to be assigned to one of the available strategy categories (e.g. low cost producer). However, the typology approach suffers from crucial limitations: a serious lack of precision in measurement, and a reliance on single-item, categorical scales.

If simplicity of measurement is important, and categorical data are acceptable, consider using a typology to assess strategy.

## Multivariate empirical studies

Many of the empirical studies reviewed here test strategy typologies. Others generate taxonomies, grouping strategy-related variables together, thus permitting an understanding of patterns existing among the variables. Still other studies, such as Venkatraman's $^{65}$ , take a comparative $^{\dagger}$ as opposed to a classificatory approach, operationalizing strategy traits and employing multi-item scales whose measurement properties can be assessed. Unfortunately, except for a few studies (e.g. Refs 50, 65–69), scale reliabilities generally have not been reported.

If precision is important, and many firms are to be studied, employ either a taxonomy or comparative measurement approach.

IS researchers seeking to operationalize competitive strategy may choose to employ a narrative (i.e. case study), typology, taxonomy, or comparative measurement approach, depending on their research objectives. Investigators choosing to employ an inductive, grounded theory approach $^{70}$ , may be better off relying on case studies and taxonomy development. Those preferring more theory driven, deductive methods may find that typologies and comparative measurement approaches are better suited to their purposes. Several well known empirical strategy studies are summarized in Appendix II.

## Highlighting strategic fit

Miles and Snow $^{71}$ define strategy in terms of ‘maintaining an effective alignment with the environment while efficiently managing internal interdependencies’ [p 3]. The alignment or fit between an organization and its environment is fundamental to the notion of strategy. Venkatraman and Camillus $^{72}$ state that strategic fit should be considered a central concept of strategic management.

IS researchers can usefully view company information systems strategy in terms of its fit with, and support for, business strategy. The better the observed IS strategic fit, or congruence between business strategy and IS strategy, the better the expected company performance $^{73}$ .

Consider, for example, two companies with different business strategies. (A typology approach will be used for simplicity's sake.) If one company is a low cost producer while the other attempts to differentiate itself through an emphasis on customer service, the use of technology primarily as a tool to maximize the efficiency of company operations is congruent with the first company's strategy, but not necessarily with the second's. A maximally efficient organization may be so automated that customer contacts, and hence customer service levels, are reduced. IS improperly targeted in the second organization could, instead of supporting company strategy, potentially work against its realization. In summary, to the extent that a company's use of information technology, or its IS strategy, is congruent with its business strategy, there exists IS strategic fit, from which may flow improved company performance.

Several different perspectives on strategic fit exist. The focus can be on factors external to the company, internal to the company, or both. IS researchers can examine, for instance, the fit between strategy and information systems that are interorganizational (i.e. external) or internal. Depending on the investigation, the content of fit or the process of arriving at fit may be the subject of study $^{72}$ .

Fit has been used repeatedly as a normative concept to highlight the importance of synchronizing complex organizational elements (e.g. information systems) and strategy. It is noteworthy that the concept of fit is also dominant in the parent disciplines of business strategy, e.g. organization theory and industrial organization economics $^{72}$ .

Alignment — or fit — is fundamental to the notion of strategy.

## Measuring strategic fit

## Drazin and Van de Ven $^{74}$ write:

The key concept in a contingent proposition is fit, and the definition of fit that is adopted is central to the development of the theory, to the collection of 9ta, and to the statistical analysis of the proposition. (Emphasis added) [p 515]

## White $^{73}$ adds:

The fit between the internal organization of an enterprise and its strategy is central to strategic management . . . At a general level the logic relating strategy, internal organization and performance is compelling. Different strategies pose different administrative requirements, which should be solved by different forms of internal organization. Refinement of this logic is a more difficult theoretical problem. It requires defining a precise concept of strategy and linking it to the set of organizational attributes that can be adjusted to facilitate the effective, low-cost implementation of the strategy. Add to this the problems of measurement, difficulties in obtaining data and the dynamic nature of the phenomenon, and it is understandable that broad-based empirical demonstrations of the strategy-organization-performance relationship, especially at the business level, are rare. [pp 217, 218]

To date, researchers have tended to discuss IS strategic fit in qualitative terms only, and have made few attempts to quantify fit\*. Given its business performance implications, it is critical that researchers improve their ability to measure this concept.

Drazin and Van de Ven $^{74}$ review three approaches to examining fit, which they term natural selection, bivariate interaction, and the systems approach. In the context of strategic IS research, the natural selection approach hypothesizes that there should be congruence between strategy and information systems, but does not address performance implications. The bivariate interaction approach attempts to explain variations in performance by examining the interaction of specific strategy and IS factors. It assumes that strategy and IS each can be decomposed into elements that can be examined independently. The systems approach is holistic. It relies on multivariate analyses to examine patterns of consistency among multiple strategy and IS characteristics, and their impacts on performance.

Drazin and Van de Ven recommend examining more than one approach to measuring fit in research studies. Complementary information allows measurement triangulation and leads to a more complete understanding of the strategy-IS relationship.

Within the natural selection, bivariate interaction, and systems approaches, a number of competing specific theoretical and mathematical models can be used to assess fit. Venkatraman $^{75}$ discusses several, depicting fit as moderation (calculated using regression or ANOVA interaction terms), mediation (modelled using indirect or intermediate variables), matching (operationalized using difference scores or regression residuals), gestalts (arrived at via cluster analyses), profile deviation (operationalized using pattern analyses), and covariation (computed using factor analyses). Each of these alternatives is supported by a different mathematical model, and has different theoretical implications $^{75,76}$ . The models differ primarily on three dimensions: the number of variables they employ (i.e. their complexity); the extent to which the detailed nature of fit can be specified (typically, the more variables in the equation, the less specific the researcher can be about the form of the fit-based relationship); and the degree to which fit calculations require the specification of a criterion variable (e.g. financial performance) $^{75}$ .

Researchers interested in measuring the strategic fit of information systems plans or portfolios, are well advised to familiarize themselves more fully with various models, and choose the approaches best suited to their purposes.

## Linking strategy to business performance

Good congruence, or fit, among strategy-related variables should be reflected in good business performance $^{77}$ . Ginsberg and Venkatraman $^{78}$ argue therefore that any comprehensive strategy must include performance. They suggest either that strategy and performance be viewed as endogenous variables, dependent on organizational context; or that strategy be viewed as an exogenous variable directly influencing organizational context and performance.

Researchers should include business performance as a key variable in strategy-IT studies.

Miller and Friesen $^{79}$ point out the limitations of studies which examine only simple bivariate relationships between strategy and performance. Several relevant factors are likely to be excluded from these studies. Ginsberg and Venkatraman $^{78}$ report a number of pertinent contingency factors which also should be considered. These include technology, organizational structure, market structure, market share, product life cycle, environmental uncertainty, and managerial characteristics. These and other contingency factors impact the strategy-performance relationship.

Clearly, the relationship between strategy and performance is complex. Hofer $^{80}$ writes:

One of the major reasons for this lack of progress [in the development of strategy theories] has been the assumption that such strategies were situational, i.e., that they depended on so many factors unique to a given situation that no general propositions could be developed. [p 785]

IS researchers are most interested in the strategy-technology-performance relationship, but still need to gather as much data as possible on other (non-technology) company and environmental contingency factors. Differences in these factors are likely to account for large portions of otherwise unexplained variance.

In IS-strategy studies, it is important also to gather data on key company and environmental contingency factors.

Lenz $^{81}$ provides evidence to suggest that when researchers conduct studies across several industries, environmental factors may have a much greater influence on firm profitability than variables that top management can control directly. However, within a single industry, firm-specific strategies can account for the majority of variance in firm performance. In cases where managerial discretion is restricted, for example in heavily regulated industries, then even at the industry level the environment may influence firm performance to a greater extent than does strategy.

Hambrick and Lei $^{53}$ and Douglas and Rhee $^{82}$ point out key differences between the industrial and consumer sectors, and suggest that business strategy research samples be drawn from either sector but not both. Numerous authors (e.g. Weill $^{38}$ , Chakravarthy $^{83}$ and Miner $^{84}$ ) go further, suggesting that the relationship between firm strategy and performance be studied within and not across industries, even industries within the same sector. Dess and Robinson $^{85}$ caution that comparisons made without controlling for industry profitability, confound intraindustry and interindustry variation, and business-level and corporate-level strategy effects. IS researchers who nonetheless choose to do cross-industry research are advised to measure performance relative to the average performance for the industry in which each firm operates $^{42}$ .

The relationships between strategy and performance should be studied within and not across industries whenever possible.

Strategy-IS-performance relationships may hold only within (i.e. not across) strategic groups within an industry $^{86}$ . Strategic groups are subgroups of firms that are highly similar with respect to key strategic dimensions such as cost structure, degree of vertical integration, product diversification, product differentiation, and formal organization. Fiegenbaum et al. $^{86}$ argue that the explanation of firm performance improves when strategic group factors are considered.\*

Strategic group members tend to have similar competences and follow similar strategies over time. Research instruments can be designed to identify key strategic dimensions. Strategic group maps can be developed for the industry or industries in question. The existence of strategic groups within industries points out that, depending on the research question, even one industry may represent an overly broad research domain.

It is important to identify major strategic groups within industries studied.

## Measuring business performance

Financial performance measurement has been the dominant approach employed to measure business performance in strategy research to date. Non-financial performance measures, such as the role of new product introduction, or product quality, have not enjoyed the same popularity. However, financial performance is but one aspect of business performance $^{88}$ ; and even this aspect is multidimensional $^{38}$ . Weill $^{38}$ , pointing out the complexity and multifaceted nature of financial performance measures, suggests that researchers ask executives in a particular industry what the important performance measures are, and use these measures.

IS investigators may also wish to consider IS-specific organizational performance measures such as aggregate assessments of information systems usage and effectiveness (see for example Refs 89-96). However, care should be exercised when aggregate and/or composite performance measures are being formed. Bourgeois $^{40}$ discusses several complexities associated with the weighting of dimensions (e.g. financial returns and information systems effectiveness) when an overall performance score is desired.

\*The theory of mobility barriers (somewhat similar to entry barriers at the industry level) explains why firms in the same industry with different asset configurations find it difficult to move between strategic groups. McGee and Thomas $^{87}$ point out that technological change and other factors may affect the mobility barriers surrounding one group to a lesser or greater extent than the barriers surrounding another group. The differential impacts may help to explain performance.

Venkatraman and Ramanujam $^{97}$ point out that constructs such as business performance are organizational level constructs. In order to assess them, ideally we require multiple informants. Using single CEO/manager responses may lead to reduced construct validity $^{98}$ . Researchers who must rely primarily on single informant responses are well advised to conduct inter-rater reliability checks. They can do so by working with multiple respondents in a subset of companies studied, and reporting the degree of respondent agreement.

Researchers should use multiple informants to assess business performance and/or report the level of respondent agreement.

Fredrickson $^{66}$ and Fredrickson and Mitchell $^{67}$ point out difficulties associated with combining individual responses. They suggest that aggregating individual responses to an organizational level is not appropriate unless there is general agreement within the firm on the issues being studied.\* When there is sufficient disagreement, some investigators (see Herbert and Deresky $^{64}$ , for example) may elect to discard the data in question.

Phillips $^{99}$ recommends that researchers ask questions in such a way as to decrease the complexity of the social judgement required of informants. Investigators should ask for specific, factual (rather than opinion-based, ‘guesstimate’) organizational data whenever possible, in order to improve the validity of their conclusions. A question such as ‘How does your company use its information systems to improve business performance?’ is almost guaranteed to elicit widely varying responses from individuals within the same organization. In contrast, questions such as ‘How has your new online system improved customer service?’ or ‘What business activities are carried out through your Customer Service Online System?’ are likely to result in more uniform responses.

Venkatraman and Ramanujam $^{97}$ discuss the pros and cons of subjective and objective modes of performance assessment and the use of primary data (e.g. interviews with company executives) and secondary data (e.g. public records and stock market indicators). They argue that no approach is intrinsically superior. Their study suggests that managers tend to be less biased in their performance assessments than researchers have given them credit for. They argue that perceptual data from senior managers tend to correlate strongly with objective data, and can be employed as acceptable operationalizations of business economic performance. Dess and Robinson $^{85}$ also lend support to the argument that subjective measures of performance correlate strongly with objective measures. They recommend the use of subjective measures especially when obtaining non-financial data.

Whenever possible, utilize subjective and objective performance data from both primary and secondary sources.

Harrigan $^{57}$ also reminds researchers of the need to employ multiple data sources (e.g. published materials, field interviews, and archival data) in order to improve the robustness and validity of their research findings. However, this must be done with care. Dubofsky and Varadarajan $^{100}$ point out, for example, that accounting and market-based measures of performance may give apparently conflicting results, even though both purport to measure firm performance. Market measures reflect market perceptions of future earnings; accounting measures reflect the previous year's earnings and current balance sheet. There can be discrepancies between these measures, especially when firm strategy exhibits lagged effects. Ford $^{101}$ warns similarly of differences between institutional records and questionnaire data; questionnaire measures tend to tap more emergent phenomena.

## Concluding remarks

Business strategy is a discipline especially relevant to strategic IS researchers. The review of the literature points to its strengths and weaknesses. In the discussion above, the focus has been on its strengths — the lessons learned, measures and methods developed, by which IS researchers may be guided. There are however weaknesses which should be mentioned. In particular, it should be noted there is little agreement on how, for example, to go about assessing business strategy and business performance; many different approaches are employed. There are also few, if any, well-established theories linking the strategy and performance constructs.

Although much has been learned in the business strategy area, much more remains to be learned. An argument may be made that the business strategy discipline is not yet well established. IS researchers, trying to develop measures and theories based on the strategy literature, are faced with non-trivial challenges. However, valuable work remains to be, and must be, carried out in the area where the information systems and business strategy disciplines intersect.

Lyles $^{102}$ , discussing a research agenda for business strategy in the 1990s, writes:

[An] item that seems to appear consistently on the research agenda is technology and innovation ... Its importance to the future competitiveness of organizations seems to be making it a priority in strategic management. [p 373]

Information technology and company strategy are very much intertwined. IS researchers have a contribution to make in this area, and must bring their particular expertise to bear. In order to do this, they must ground their research in relevant reference disciplines, despite the difficulties faced. This article has sought to provide an introduction to business strategy as a reference discipline. Key conceptual and measurement issues have been addressed. Competitive strategy, strategic fit, and business performance have been highlighted. The intent has been to better equip IS researchers to make their contributions in the area of information systems and business strategy.
