# The strategic positioning of information systems in post-acquisition management

Yasmin Merali\* and Peter McKiernan\*\*

\* Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

\*\*Department of Management, University of St Andrews, Kennedy Gardens, Kinnessburn, St Andrews, Fife KY16 9DJ, UK

Although corporate growth through acquisition continues to be a popular strategy for the 1990s, the failure rate of acquired companies remains high at 50 per cent. This paper focuses on post-acquisition integration of information systems (IS) and information technology (IT) and its impact on post-acquisitive performance. Based on the results of detailed case studies and a preliminary survey, it is found that managers involved in pre- and post-acquisition decision-making often fail to adequately consider the strategic importance of IS/IT in contributing to the acquisition outcome. IS integration is often driven by the immediate requirements of operational consolidation, and organizations fail to invest in the establishment of IS infrastructures to support longer-term corporate requirements. It is suggested that the reactive status accorded to IS coupled with an observed lack of organizational learning prevents organizations from exploiting IS effectively for the achievement of acquisition success.

Keywords: post-acquisition management, information systems integration, information technology integration

Merger and acquisition activity is a prominent feature of the corporate marketplace in Europe and the USA. For example, in the USA, acquisition transactions exceeding \$35 million in value increased from \$186.4 billion in 1985 to \$234.1 billion in 1989. Acquisitions by European firms accounted for 12.3 per cent of these transactions (Haspeslagh and Jemison, 1991). Within Europe in 1990, the value of cross-border takeovers rose by 22 per cent to \$61 billion (Economist, 1991). Although ‘Europe 1992’ was the central catalyst for this activity, it is suggested that the current recession, competition, squeezed sector margins and supply-side overcapacity will contribute to its perpetuation through the 1990s.

One reason for the escalation of merger and acquisition activity is that it represents a ‘fast track’ for contestants in an increasingly competitive environment. Mergers and acquisitions constitute part of organizational strategies to achieve growth and diversification objectives that cannot be met within the desired time frame by internal growth alone. Typically, acquisition objectives include diversification, market share maximization, profit maximization, acquisition of supply/distribution channels, acquisition of skills, expertise or technology, and increasingly in the past decade the acquisition of information resources.

## Acquisition performance

Ironically, although growth by acquisition continues to be a popular component of corporate strategies for the 1990s, research in this area consistently indicates that post-merger or post-acquisition performance does not live up to the ex ante expectations of either the predators or the target organizations. The expected improvements in profitability and in the wealth of shareholders often do not materialize (Bohl, 1989; Linder, 1989; Violano, 1990; Kubilus, 1991), and 50–60 per cent of acquisitions are later divested (Porter, 1987). Recent evidence from an American Management Association (AMA) survey (Bohl, 1989) indicates that ex ante expectations of increases in profitability, pay-back and market share are the primary reasons underpinning any merger or acquisition. However, lower productivity, losses in market share, reduced profitability and increased employee turnover are frequently the result.

Many reasons have been advanced by economists and corporate strategists for this shortfall in performance (Howe, 1986; Haspeslagh and Jemison, 1987). It has been suggested that:

\- because of opportunism the emphasis has been on an acquisition's prospect and price and not on the evaluation of its contribution to corporate strategy;

\- there has been a failure to establish objectives for acquired companies that fit into overall corporate strategy;

\- the divorce of ownership from control among major decision takers within the organization (managers, employees) from those of shareholders (major institutions) may lead to an incongruence in objectives during the acquisition process;

\- the short-term outlook of many US and UK acquisitions, driven by city financial institutions and stock exchanges, may result in a quick fix of capturing value, through asset stripping or tax benefits, rather than creating value over time in the newly acquired organization.

The speed, ambiguity of purpose and diversity of people involved in the acquisition process, along with the severe time and secrecy pressures, mean that legal and financial perspectives dominate negotiations. This tends to pre-empt strategic analysis and organizational integration considerations.

## Post-acquisition integration and acquisition performance

It is now recognized that the post-acquisition integration process constitutes a vital link between the acquisition motive and subsequent performance. Even where the acquisition choice and acquisition objectives are compatible with corporate strategy, the acquiring organization often fails to realize its acquisition objectives because of problems in the management of post-acquisition integration (Salter and Weinhold, 1979; Haspeslagh and Jemison, 1987).

## Issues of strategic, organizational and cultural fit

Researchers seeking to establish causal relationships between company characteristics, post-acquisition integration management and acquisition outcomes have focused extensively on questions of strategic, cultural and organizational fit between the predator and target companies.

The diversity of findings from researchers studying the performance of related and unrelated acquisitions (Salter and Weinhold, 1979; Chatterjee, 1986; Lubatkin, 1987; Singh and Montgomery, 1987; Seth, 1990) supports the view that strategic fit, while important, is not the sole determinant of acquisition performance. In the case of related acquisitions, although synergies may be present, they will result in superior performance only if they can be eventually realized through effective post-acquisition integration. This is, in turn, contingent upon organizational fit (Datta, 1991).

There has been much debate concerning organizational restructuring with the decentralization of decision-making and consequent divisional autonomy. In this way, many successful acquirers have been able to manage the heterogeneous nature of the organizational cultures with which they have come into contact through acquisition. Clearly the integration, motivation and retention of personnel from both the predator and target organizations is an important determinant of consequent performance. A lack of clear strategy concerning these softer, organizational issues may be among the main causes of post-acquisitive failure.

## Issues of IS and IT integration

Whilst the literature has focused on issues of strategic, organizational and cultural fit, the anecdotal evidence from executives and management consultants suggests that there have been other, perhaps, more significant causes of poor post-acquisitive performance. This is particularly true in the recent merger wave in the UK (1987–89). It is conjectured that one of the most difficult components to deal with in the post-acquisitive integrative phase has been the definition of the new corporate IS infrastructure requirements and the incumbent integration and development of the inherited IS/IT structures. In many cases there has been a huge capital cost of integrating IT systems, and in many others, a huge void in defining the types of information systems that are necessary to facilitate business and organizational integration.

Both the anecdotal evidence and the academic literature in this field are sparse. In view of the continued popularity of the strategy of business growth by acquisition and the increasing influence of IT and information availability on restructuring the global business environment of the 1990s (Scott Morton, 1991), this is an area which deserves more attention than it has so far enjoyed.

The purpose of this paper is to review experiences of management of IS/IT issues in acquisition management and to determine how these relate to management perceptions of the role of IS/IT in the post-acquisitive business entity. An acquisition integration life cycle model (Merali and McKiernan, 1993) is used for analysing the positioning of IS/IT considerations in the context of the pre- and post-acquisition concerns on the management agenda.

Existing literature in this area is reviewed in the next section. Subsequent sections present our research methodology, research results to date and emerging conclusions.

## MERGERS AND ACQUISITIONS — THE INFORMATION DIMENSION

## The role of IS and IT in post-acquisition management

In mergers and acquisitions IS/IT can play either a reactive or a proactive role. The reactive role is evident when IS/IT needs to change to accommodate other operational considerations. The role is proactive when IS/IT is the facilitator for other organizational change or when the acquisition of an organization's IS/IT is the prime motivator behind the deal.

## Reactive role of IS/IT

A key determinant of the integration task is the nature of the interdependence between the two organizations and how that interdependence is to be managed (Haspeslagh and Jemison, 1991). Scholars have concentrated on either inter-organizational interdependence (Levine and White, 1961) or intra-organizational interdependence (Thompson, 1967). Acquisitions involve elements of both, because the acquired organization may have disappeared only as a legal entity, continuing to operate as a discrete entity within the acquiring corporate structure, or it may be absorbed into the acquirer's operational infrastructure.

The degree of integration and differentiation required between merging organizations, and between functions within the merged organizations, is contingent upon the particular environmental circumstances and strategic requirements of the merger or acquisition (Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967). Haspeslagh and Jemison (1991) provide a more useful framework for characterizing acquisition types and their contingent integration requirements. They identify four acquisition types (absorption; preservation; holding; symbiosis) characterized by the level of organizational interdependence contingent upon the acquisition strategy, and define the level of business and IS/IT integration necessary for achieving the objectives for each of the acquisition types, as shown in Figure 1.

Preservation and holding acquisitions are characterized by the retention of autonomous and independent systems after acquisition. Research on management of post-acquisition integration of IS/IT, has therefore focused primarily on absorption and symbiotic acquisitions which often require a significant level of systems integration for operational consolidation.

In absorption acquisitions the strategic task requires a high degree of interdependence to create the value expected, but has a low need for organizational autonomy to achieve it. This implies full consolidation, over time, of the operations, organization and culture. This is likely to be the case when the two organizations share the same business areas, thus making IS/IT integration essential (Linder, 1989). Clearly, IT is pervasive in most organizations in the modern era. It is the means through which control, planning, financial, operating and management information systems are achieved. When the acquiring company wishes to merge the two operations, then consolidation of the two IS/IT architectures is usually a top priority (Carlyle, 1986; I/S Analyzer, 1989; Stone, 1989). Ball (1988) estimates that 70 per cent of merged companies combine IS operations immediately after the merger transaction takes place, whilst up to 90 per cent eventually combine IS operations into a single data centre, usually within a year. By consolidating the operations of the two organizations and thereby reducing excess capacities and lowering per unit costs through the achievement of scale economies, direct and indirect costs of operation can be reduced, so increasing margins. IS/IT has a reactive role, in that it must be integrated to consolidate other operations.

Symbiotic acquisitions represent a combination of both high autonomy and interdependence between organizations. This presents the most complex challenge to IS/IT response. It may be possible that certain systems will be centralized or, where this unduly interferes with either organization's autonomy, bridges need to be implemented across unchanged systems.

## Proactive role of IS/IT

IS/IT can play a proactive role in the merger and acquisition process, by creating opportunities for gaining competitive advantage (Loverde, 1990), or by driving the organizational change process (Linder, 1989). For instance, IS/IT can be used as a focus and common direction for the overall integration process, especially for organizations that are heavily dependent on information. A common IS/IT infrastructure can be used as the basis for a single, new corporate style by providing a common language, common sets of performance measures and operating methods and a common vertical and horizontal distribution of power (Linder, 1989). Whilst IS/IT may not be singularly responsible for creating these, it ratifies and implements them in visible ways throughout the combined organization.

Clearly the role of IS/IT should not be overstated in this regard, yet in information intensive businesses, organizational policies, practices, status and influences can be embedded in the IS/IT infrastructures. Changing functionality thus becomes a fulcrum for implementing comprehensive organizational change.

## Stage 1: Acquisition target selection

IS is the least studied of all corporate activities in pre-acquisition negotiations. In an AMA survey Bohl (1989) reported that, in general, information was adequate in the ex ante stage for major decision-making regarding human resources, benefits and pay-roll, finance, general management and operations, sales and marketing and manufacturing. However, fewer than half of the AMA respondents had full information on computer hardware systems and fewer than a third had full information on software or voice and data communication systems. There are, however, a number of cited cases from the financial sector where IS considerations were critical in pre-acquisition decision making [see for example the accounts of savings and loan associations mergers given in Cossey (1991) and Harvey (1990)].

Cases do exist where the necessary detailed information regarding the IS/IT infrastructure is not made freely available to the acquirer (Bohl, 1989; Fiderio, 1989). However, there is evidence to indicate that even when the information is freely available from target organizations, it is frequently not collected (Bohl, 1989). The lack of time (Harvey, 1990) and the low priority accorded by management to the IS function (Bohl, 1989) are cited as reasons for this omission.

It is also reported that, generally, the IS function is not represented in the decision-making group at the pre-acquisition stage, and consequently the IS perspective is not recognized at this level (I/S Analyzer, 1989; Harvey, 1990; Loverde, 1990).

## Stage 2: Acquisition target evaluation

## Evaluation of integration requirements and IS/IT integration costs

Given that the required degree of interdependence between the two merging organizations dictates the level of IS/IT integration that is necessary in post-acquisition management, a definition of the acquisition objectives and the post-acquisitive needs would appear to be an important part of any pre-acquisition evaluation exercise. Whilst the acquiring organizations do define areas of potential operational synergy during the pre-acquisition justification exercise, most of them fail to evaluate IS/IT integration requirements and costs at this stage.

The focus of the target evaluation exercise tends to be on the cost savings attributable to operational consolidation. The level of the contingent IS/IT integration is not explicitly defined at this stage (Bohl, 1989). As acquisition motives are usually of a financial or strategic nature, the pre-event evaluation focuses on financial performance, products and the markets rather than on post-acquisition management issues, of which IS integration is perceived to be a part (Rosenberg, 1987; Fiderio, 1989).

The failure to consider the cost of IS/IT integration at this stage may restrict the realization of potential operational synergies at the implementation stage. Also, the integration of IS/IT occurs at a cost which will ultimately affect the value generated by the transaction.

## Due diligence and the evaluation of IS/IT

The due diligence exercise is the investigative study carried out by the acquiring organization when evaluating the acquisition target. The failure to conduct an evaluation of the acquired organization's IS/IT infrastructure during the pre-acquisition due diligence stage is often cited as a major reason why IS/IT systems contribute to post-acquisition problems (Carlyle, 1986; Fiderio, 1989; I/S Analyzer, 1989; Johnson, 1989; Hoffman, 1990). Whilst recommendations are frequently made that a technical due diligence exercise be carried out prior to any acquisition (Bohl, 1989; Johnson, 1989; Cossey, 1991), it would appear that, in the majority of cases, the pre-acquisition due-diligence process does not include a thorough evaluation of the IS/IT infrastructure (Bohl, 1989). A key reason for this may be the difficulty of evaluating IS (Willcocks, 1992). Most companies do not know the total value of their investment in IS/IT and often overlook the value of software and data (Ryder, 1988). Investments in IS often underpins the realization of intangible objectives such as quality of service or contribution to competitive advantage. Evaluation of such features tends to be limited to qualitative studies based on user surveys or the indirect impact on performance of some function or other (see for example Lincoln and Shorrock, 1990).

In the cases where due diligence does include an evaluation of IS/IT, this often comprises little more than an inventory of the existing hardware and software systems (Bohl, 1989). It would appear that acquisition analysts do not attempt to estimate the worth of the target's IS in terms of effectiveness and quality. The need to evaluate the IS development culture, the positioning of the IS function and the IS skills and training infrastructure of the acquired organization is most often overlooked.

## Stage 3: Planning for post-acquisition integration

Planning for post-acquisition of IS/IT tends to be deferred until the acquisition transaction has been completed. Then, the challenge for the integration planners is to achieve consolidation and the realization of the economies that justify the transaction without disrupting the work of employees or inconveniencing customers (Horton, 1989; Linder, 1989). The evidence, however, suggests that the focus is on scheduling and timing rather than on planning (Linder, 1989).

One key planning problem is the decoupling of personnel responsible for pre-acquisition decision-making from line managers responsible for integration planning and implementation. Rumelt (1974) suggests that it is possible for unique capabilities of the acquired firm, which may be needed by the acquiring firm, to be scrapped by ‘culturally arrogant’ acquiring employees who were not involved in the initial negotiations. Further, where there is overlapping functionality of systems in the two organizations, there is a tendency to impose the acquiring organization's systems without fully considering the merits of the acquired systems (Violano, 1990). Linder (1989) contends that the resolution of the cultural clash (Sales and Mirvis, 1984; Quinn and Kimberley, 1984; Buono, Bowditch and Lewis, 1985; Graves, 1986) and 'balance of power' issues at this stage is a prerequisite for the success of the implementation process.

Additional problems are presented when organizations confine the scope of the exercise to the integration of systems in areas which necessarily need to be consolidated for the immediate realization of the acquisition objectives. This tends to be done very swiftly indeed (Bohl, 1989; Linder, 1989; Violano, 1990) as there is a great deal of urgency for operational consolidation. Any existing longer-term systems plans in the acquiring organization are pushed aside to accommodate this short-term consolidation of activities (McWilliams, 1988; Linder, 1989; Stone, 1989), and product innovation may grind to a halt (Linder, 1989). Such myopia is clearly detrimental to an organization's competitive position. Moreover, this 'short termism' can gloss over inherent human resource issues of the integration, retention and motivation of IS staff. The acquisition process itself can be extremely unsettling and may cause very able personnel to depart (Carlyle, 1986; Moad and Carlyle, 1988; Horton, 1989; Desmond, 1990). The difficulty in integrating personnel may stem from a clash of personalities and cultures, lack of communications or a reluctance to change (Desmond, 1990). It is important for the acquiring organization to position its staff to be receptive to acquired systems if these are to be adopted, as the 'not invented here' syndrome can be detrimental to successful consolidation (Loverde, 1990).

## Stage 4: Implementation of integration plans

The lack of communication between the planners and implementors can lead to short-sighted, short-term objective setting by those responsible for implementing the IS/IT integration plans. Decisions made for reasons of expediency of implementation may impact negatively on the quality and effectiveness of systems (Carlyle, 1986; Stone, 1989; Harvey, 1990). Such decisions can be costly to reverse. Often organizations live with this or maintain inefficient or redundant and incompatible systems in order to maintain the required functionality.

Divergent IS/IT technologies (Atkinson, 1990), incompatible data structures and ageing or badly documented software (Carlyle, 1986), plus the tendency for planners to hand responsibility to operational staff without adequate briefing (Linder, 1989), account for much disruption. Carlyle (1986) has suggested that the heaviest cost during implementation is often associated with software conversion; hardware and communications consolidation is relatively easy and provides for large economies of scale.

The importance of cultural fit in determining successful outcomes in post-acquisition integration has been discussed extensively (Hall and Norburn, 1989). Also, researchers have explored the relationship between the organizational stages of growth and the nature and extent of post-acquisitive integration (Shrivastava, 1986; Harvey, 1990).

With respect to IS and IT, Linder (1989) suggests that political and power structure issues are key determinants of integration success. However, there is a contrast between these findings in the banking sector and reports from other, less IS/IT-dependent sectors (Carlyle, 1986; Atkinson, 1990) where technical integration difficulties featured most prominently. This suggests that organizational and management ‘IS maturity’ may determine the success of the post-acquisition integration of IS/IT. In Linder’s (1989) study, post-acquisition integration of IS/IT appeared to be adequately resourced, and management awareness of the business and strategic relevance of IS/IT was generally high. It is possible that the technical problems which were perceived as being most important in the other studies stem from the low importance accorded to IS/IT and are actually manifestations of poor resource allocation, inadequate planning or weak project management in the implementation stage. Again, the literature does not provide details of the management issues for cases where technical problems are perceived as being paramount.

## Stage 5: Post-integration evaluation

Executives and academics tend to evaluate acquisition performance and acquisition strategies on economic criteria in the short term following an acquisition (Haspeslagh and Jemison, 1987). The acquisition process itself is usually not reviewed. Moreover, the majority of organizations do not formally review the IS/IT integration process (Bohl, 1989; Main and Short, 1989).

## Stage 6: IS, IT and acquisition strategy

Much has been written about the exploitation of IS/IT for competitive advantage (e.g. Benjamin et al., 1984; McFarlan, 1984; Wiseman, 1985; Porter, 1987). Organizations need to formulate and implement strategies for IS, IT and information management (IM) in order to establish an IS/IT infrastructure which is capable of responding effectively to long-term and changing business information needs (Earl, 1989). Moreover, it is emphasized that the business strategy planning and the IS strategy planning exercises ought to be integrated in recognition of the potential of IT to shape and enable business strategies (McFarlan, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Henderson, Rockart and Sifonis, 1987; Galliers, 1991). In the acquisition literature these issues have received little attention. Senior management often tends to view IT as a cost of doing business, rather than as a contributor to business effectiveness (Moad and Carlyle, 1988), and it appears that in their eagerness to reap the benefits of consolidation as rapidly as possible, organizations fail to consider the strategic need for longer-term IS planning (Kennedy and Worth, 1990).

## METHODOLOGY

The lack of available empirical evidence on the role of IS/IT in the acquisition process and, in particular in post-acquisition management, suggests an investigative methodology containing three stages:

\- review of the related literature to identify important issues and relationships;

\- exploratory case studies to analyse these issues and relationships further;

\- mail survey to investigate and test the relationships.

## Literature review

The initial objective was to uncover the salient empirical trends in the above nexus. However, as stated in the preceding section, the review covering both the academic and practitioner literatures established the paucity of relevant evidence.

## Case-based evidence

The majority of the existing evidence in the literature was US-based, and it was considered useful to complement this with UK data whilst preserving the US link for comparative purposes. Hence, a decision was made to choose case evidence that spanned both sides of the Atlantic. Further research would investigate cases elsewhere in the world.

The inherently sensitive nature of acquisitions placed an emphasis on access as the key to sample choice. After several rejections, the research was allowed to progress in a large British-based, multinational corporation. Over 80 per cent of its growth in the previous five years had been due to global acquisitions, many of these in the UK and US. Access to the firm was conditional upon complete anonymity and so size is not disclosed and the parent and subsidiaries that were the focus of the research are given pseudonyms.

Wind PLC is a multinational corporation with major interests in the publishing and information management industry. It has grown substantially over the past five years through international acquisition. Two in-depth case studies were carried out in two separate companies in one of its divisions, Flute (UK) and Oboe (US) both of which are information providers. These companies were acquired by Wind in 1985 and 1989, respectively. Both acquisitions were considered to be successful in meeting Wind's objectives in gaining market leadership in this sector.

Semi-structured, personal interviews were conducted among senior and middle management executives in Wind, Flute and Oboe during the summer of 1991. It was necessary to interview several individuals in each organization to provide a continuity and consistency of their perceptions of the pre- and post-acquisition phase events and processes. Two interviewers attended each session with one in an active, and the other in a passive, interviewer mode. The former maintained eye-to-eye contact and asked questions according to a pre-established interview guide, while the latter acted both as a check on the process and as a cross validator of the interview summaries that were completed immediately after each session. These summaries were then returned to the executives for verification of their accuracy and for permission to process them further without infringement of confidentiality.

The interview data was reinforced by the accumulation of a substantial amount of secondary data available from within the parent organization. This information consisted of detailed accounting and control data along with public relations data that had been provided for public consumption around the time of the acquisitions. In addition, a database of the parent company's strategic intent, corporate and business unit strategies was obtained through a series of top executive interviews at the parent company.

This accumulated evidence (McKiernan and Merali, 1992), together with the existing knowledge provided in the literature, became the basis for the third survey phase in the methodology which is described below.

## Survey-based evidence

Cross-sectional surveys were designed to focus on the key variables identified from the cases in relation to the acquisition–IS/IT linkage.

## Criteria for selection of firms

Firms surveyed were acquired to satisfy three criteria:

## 1. The firm had to be in information/IT intensive sectors.

Hence, their reliance on computers in their daily business activities was deemed to high. The Mechanical Engineering and Financial sectors satisfied this criterion and ensured a contrast with each other and with the case sample above. See Table 1 for the Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes sampled.

## 2. The acquisition must have occurred between 1987 and 1990.

The choice of this period was based upon the assumption that most firms would, by 1992, have had sufficient time to integrate their IS/IT function and be able to make a critical assessment of this process. This period also coincided with the end of a strong period of economic growth in the UK and other Western economies.

3. The acquisition must have had a minimum value of £1 million at 1987 prices. This was based upon the assumption that both firms would therefore be of a sufficiently large size to possess a well established IT function.

## Selection process

Acquisitions Monthly was the main sample frame. The summary data for the number and SIC codes identified is contained in Table 1. As many firms had made more than one acquisition during this period and some had ceased trading, changed their names or become fully incorporated into the predator's business, it was necessary to carry out supplementary searches to filter the sample down to identifiable targets for research. This filtering was performed using the FAME database, Microview, Extel's Change of Name (1965–1992) publication, Who owns Who (1992) and Microextel cards. This process realized a population of 200 firms, of which 110 were in the mechanical engineering sector and 90 in the financial services sector.

## Instrument design

Based on the issues identified in the case study analysis, the survey was divided into four parts, three of which represent the chronological stages of the acquisition process. These parts were:

1. Organizational details of the acquiring and acquired firms.

2. The pre-acquisition due diligence process.

3. The post-acquisition integration process.

4. The post-integration review.

Dillman's ‘Total Design Package’ (1978) was utilized with a view to improving the response rate, speed and quality. The package features included clear objectives specified in a concise cover letter, appealing layout for easy completion, avoidance of dichotomous choices, unambiguous language, logical sequence of questions, tests on survey length, high quality paper, finish and careful choice of colours, completion reward, pre-paid response envelopes, numerous drafts and pre-testing of the whole package.

Survey results. The response speed and response quality were both of a high level and presented no problems. Only 16 companies responded to the questionnaire, however — a response rate of only 8 per cent. This was disappointing. A subsequent analysis of non-respondents established that the sensitivity of the project topic and the confidentiality of the due-diligence process prevented many individuals from participating. This was so despite assurances of confidentiality. Hence, given the small sample size, the results that follow are indicative only and require further testing.

## RESULTS

## Firm and acquisition characteristics

The main business objectives for both the acquired and the acquiring companies in the survey were profit maximization, gaining market share or establishing a position in a new market. The acquisition case studies and the survey acquisition were all of the absorption or symbiotic type, and achievement of their acquisition objectives was contingent upon some degree of post-acquisitive integration.

Two-thirds of the acquiring firms had an annual turnover of over £200 million and had 2000 or more employees. Approximately 90 per cent of the acquired companies employed less than 200 staff and had an annual turnover of less than £50 million. In terms of investment in IT, 50 per cent of the acquiring companies and over 90 per cent of the acquired companies had less than 20 staff in the IT function. The IT budgets as a percentage of annual turnover were similar for acquired companies, with a fifth of the companies committing over 5 per cent of annual turnover to IT, but the majority committing less than 1 per cent.

The survey results are presented below in the context of the integration life cycle model (Figure 2) used for the literature review.

## Stage 1: Acquisition target selection

Acquisition target selection was based primarily on the goodness of strategic fit. Issues of organizational and cultural fit were not considered, and finance personnel were the key actors in the process for both the manufacturing and finance sectors.

In contrast to the AMA survey, a quarter of the respondents did include members from the IT function in the pre-acquisition decision-making activity (Figure 3). These companies were all from the finance sector and used IS/IT at all levels of the organization. This finding suggests differences between sectors in the level of consideration accorded to IT, and is consistent with other documented cases (Cossey, 1991; Harvey, 1990) in this regard.

## Stage 2: Acquisition target evaluation

Evaluation of integration requirements and IS/IT integration costs

Three-quarters of the respondents considered operational integration costs in their pre-acquisition assessment procedure. However, although over 90 per cent of the companies stated that IT had some role to play in supporting the merged company's business strategy, less than 50 per cent considered IS/IT issues in their pre-acquisition evaluation and less than 25 per cent considered integration requirements for IS/IT. This suggests a mis-match between the intellectual level (where organizations may subscribe to the concept of exploiting IS/IT for achievement of business objectives) and organizational reality.

## Due diligence and the evaluation of IS/IT

The due diligence evaluation of target IS/IT was carried out in less than 50 per cent of the cases. This represented little more than an inventory of the IT staff numbers, hardware, software and communications capabilities of the target organization. No attention was paid to the quality or effectiveness of systems. Moreover, neither the information infrastructure nor the IS development environment and skills-base were evaluated. These results are consistent with those from earlier case studies (McKiernan and Merali, 1992) where, even in the information industry, target evaluation did not extend to quality issues and integration planning was contingent upon post-acquisition product consolidation efficiency.

## Stage 3: Planning for post-acquisition integration

As noted above, in the majority of cases, IS integration was generally not considered in the pre-acquisition phase. However, in the post-acquisition phase, 70 per cent of the respondents formulated formal/semi-formal general integration plans (Figure 4). Timescales for planning were relatively short, with one-third of organizations taking less than one month to formulate the plans. Planning for IS integration was contingent upon decisions relating to operational integration. During the post-acquisition phase, almost 30 per cent of the respondents found that they had to change their pre-acquisition assumptions regarding IT because of the changed business environment, or because of changed goals, or because new information had become available or there was inadequate pre-acquisition assessment.

Figure 4. IS issues considered in post-acquisition integration planning

In over a third of the cases, a conscious decision was made not to integrate the IT/IS function. The decision for IT/IS integration was taken immediately in less than 50 per cent of the remaining cases. The rest took the decision to integrate 6–12 months after the acquisition had been completed.

In the pre-implementation planning stage, 75 per cent of the cases paid attention to hardware, communications and software compatibility issues, but only 25 per cent considered databases in this context. Only half of the respondents included the organizational aspects of the integrated IT/IS function, IT/IS integration budget, integration timescales and IT personnel requirements in their pre-implementation planning. Responsibility for the integrated IS function was considered in just one-quarter of the cases.

In all cases the planning was confined to meeting the short-term objectives for operational consolidation. Systems were considered piecemeal, and no strategic planning was done for the longer term provision of an organizational information infrastructure. These results are consistent with the findings of Moad and Carlyle (1988) and Kennedy and Worth (1990).

## Stage 4: Implementation of integration plans

The integration of the IS/IT functions was expected to be completed within six months in half of the cases, and within six to twelve months in all cases. Over 60 per cent of the cases in the survey finally achieved full integration.

Linder's (1989) study, which focused on organizations where the 'IS culture' was mature, shows that the technical difficulty of IS integration does not have a negative impact on the integration outcome. The AMA survey, anecdotal evidence and previous case studies (McKiernan and Merali, 1992) indicate that technical difficulties do have a negative impact on post-acquisitive IS integration. In the latter cases, where systems were technically difficult to integrate, the plan for full integration was abandoned and redundant systems were maintained. In these cases, although the organizational usage of IS/IT was quite sophisticated, the 'IS maturity' (in terms of awareness of the business and strategic relevance of IS/IT, and in the assumption of active, responsible managerial roles for facilitating the integration of IS/IT into the organizational context) amongst management was low. This suggests that management 'IS maturity' is a more fundamental determinant of integration effectiveness for IS/IT, than organizational maturity in the usage of IS.

## Stage 5: Post-integration evaluation

Generally, the acquisition process was not well documented and organizations had difficulty in providing information about past acquisitions. Only two of the organizations conducted post-implementation reviews of IS integration. The reviews were conducted by the IT function three to six months after implementation of the integrated IS, and had a narrow focus. No data was collected for longer-term assessment and the alignment of the IS integration with the achievement of corporate and acquisition objectives was not evaluated. The impact of the pre-acquisition decision making and planning process on IS integration was not examined. These findings are consistent with the previous research, which indicates that few organizations conduct reviews on the acquisition process as a whole, and on the IS integration process in particular (Bohl, 1989; Main and Short, 1989). Exceptions reported included those with mature 'IS cultures' such as the cases described by Linder (1989).

## Stage 6: IS, IT and acquisition strategy

Over 50 per cent of the acquiring organizations believed that IS/IT was essential for their company. The rest stated that IS/IT had at least a ‘supporting’ role to play. They perceived IS/IT in the target organizations as having a similar, if less critical, role.

The majority (85 per cent) of acquiring organizations had an IS/IT strategy in place. Of these, 83 per cent believed that their IS/IT strategy was well aligned with their business strategy. The rest stated that their IS/IT only partially supported their business needs. It is notable that in two-thirds of the cases, the IS/IT strategy was formulated by the 'Head of IT', and that manufacturing businesses were frequently without formal strategy in this area. Both findings are consistent with Galliers' (1987) prior research.

In the pre-acquisition stage, IS/IT appeared to be absent from the strategic frame for decision-making. In over 50 per cent of the cases the acquiring organization did not have information about its target's IS/IT strategy and its alignment with the business strategy. In the majority of cases, no attempt was made to consider the merged business entity and its requirements for an IS/IT strategy. As noted earlier, in only 25 per cent of the cases was the IS/IT function represented at the decision-making level, and in 50 per cent of the cases IS/IT issues were not even considered at the pre-acquisition stage. The reason given for this omission was that 'IS/IT is not important to business strategy'.

## DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

These results suggest that in acquisitions, organizations are failing to align their IS and business strategies, and that management is not evaluating the implications of this omission. This is consistent with results from case studies in the information industry (McKiernan and Merali, 1992), where the decision to centralize production operations in order to exploit synergies resulted in impoverishing the quality of one of the acquired company's products.

Organizations in the financial sector appear to have a little more consciousness of IS issues than those in the manufacturing sector. This finding is in keeping with results from research on information systems planning practice (Galliers, 1987). However in this study, the marginally superior consciousness of financial organizations did not translate into greater attention being paid to IS strategy/business strategy alignment for the merged business.

The results also suggest these organizations are focusing on IT integration from a perspective of glueing businesses together and perhaps failing to look at IS integration for provision of quality information. Together with the apparently low importance accorded to IS/IT by executives in the acquisition decision-making process, this phenomenon has several disturbing implications for the effective exploitation of IS/IT investments for business advantage.

The IS function is not accorded the high intellectual level of importance claimed for it by the IS management literature in operational reality. For companies that are adopting ‘growth by acquisition’ as a business strategy this can be particularly harmful. Rapid business growth may take place without the development of an underlying IS/IT infrastructure to support it. The fact that organizations believe that they have IS strategies but are not aligning them with business strategy suggests that this may be the case. In a decade when European acquisition activity will be driven by economic motives concerned with fundamentals like synergy, economies of scale and overcapacity (Economist, 1991), post-acquisition integration and effective management and efficient exploitation of the IS/IT resource would appear to be a critical issue.

Key areas for the success of acquisition objectives (with respect to value creation through the synergies) are investigated in isolation from their supporting IS, and data/information quality issues are ignored. It has been argued in the literature that IS is no different in the acquisition context from other functional areas (Hoffman, 1990). However, it may no longer be realistic to take this view, as IS/IT are increasingly becoming part of the way that organizations do business, and shaping the way that people work. Where information is being used, there is a need to examine the effectiveness of the underlying IS. A failure to consider IS/IT at the strategic level will preclude organizations from exploiting IS/IT in a proactive role for positioning the organization favourably in its competitive environment.

The cost of ineffective integration is not appreciated. In the absence of post-integration reviews, the longer term cost of maintaining inefficient or redundant systems is not appreciated. More importantly, the cost to the business of relying on systems of indeterminate effectiveness may be considerable, but this too remains an unknown. Measures of effectiveness are difficult to define. The process of formulating IS/IM/IT strategies (c.f., Earl, 1989; Galliers, 1991) and that of aligning the IS strategy with the business strategy is important in generating meaningful effectiveness criteria against which information infrastructures can be evaluated. In the case of post-acquisition management, no IS strategy is formulated, and the effectiveness issue is not addressed.

Expertise is lost. The lack of IS and IM strategies and the absence of longer-term plans, coupled with a failure to make explicit the potential role of current IS staff in future development, may result in the loss of able staff. This is consistent with observations in the literature (Carlyle, 1986; Moad and Carlyle, 1988; Horton, 1989; Desmond, 1990) and with the case study findings (McKiernan and Merali, 1992).

The failure to conduct post-integration evaluation implies that organizations fail to exploit each new acquisition as a learning opportunity, because they never complete the learning cycle (see Figure 5).

Organizations that have adopted acquisition as part of their corporate strategy for growth are likely to participate in multiple acquisitions in the long term. Organizational learning is therefore a crucial issue if future acquisitions are to benefit from the lessons to be learnt from the preceding ones. Moreover, much of the information relating to the acquisition process (particularly in the pre-acquisition stage) becomes personal, sometimes tacit knowledge. Given the high rate of staff turn-over, it would appear to be essential that such organizations do conduct formally documented reviews for every acquisition made. In the absence of such a practice individuals leaving the organization take their personal knowledge with them.

Outside the merger/acquisition context, organizational learning in relation to IS/IT in general is emerging as a key issue for information management: in a recent UK-based survey on key issues in information management (Galliers, Merali and Spearing, 1993) it rated as one of the top 10 issues that managers perceived as being critical (in terms of being both important and problematic) both now and in five years time.

## CONCLUSION AND FUTURE RESEARCH

The focus of this research was on defining the strategic positioning of IS/IT in absorption and symbiotic acquisitions where integration of the two organizations' IS/IT is required for the realization of acquisition objectives. It was found that IS and IT do not feature significantly in the strategic frames of acquiring organizations. The potential of IS/IT to contribute positively to longer-term acquisition outcomes was not considered in acquisition decision making and planning, and no attempt was made to formulate even skeletal IS, IT or IM strategies to guide planners and implementors engaged in post-acquisition integration. IS/IT was viewed in a purely reactive role with systems integration being driven by the expediency of operational consolidation. Post-acquisition reviews were not generally conducted, and the quality and effectiveness of the integrated IS was not evaluated in the long term.

Figure 5. Learning cycle (modified from Honey and Mumford, 1986)

It is suggested that the absence of a post-acquisition evaluation process precludes organizational learning, and that the failure to pay attention to the strategic role of IS/IT in the post-acquisitive entity precludes some organizations from exploiting IS/IT in its proactive capacity to reshape and reposition the organization in its competitive environment.

It is predicted that organizations will become increasingly information intensive over the next decade (Scott Morton, 1991). Post-acquisition management in the 1990s will need to recognize the importance of IS/IT in the strategic frame, in order to ensure that investments in IS/IT are exploited effectively to position the post-acquisitive business entity favourably in its competitive environment. The business case for the exploitation of IS/IT in a proactive role, and the need to achieve flexible information infrastructures in the longer term, will become important issues on the management agenda for effecting long-term acquisition success. The findings in this research, while tentative, suggest that management ‘IS maturity’ is a more fundamental determinant of integration effectiveness for IS/IT than organizational maturity in the usage of IS. Organizational learning through evaluation of experience may be the vehicle for effecting more strategically aware post-acquisition management of IS and IT.

Further detailed case studies are planned in order to establish the status of IS and business strategy alignment in predator organizations and to test whether the suggested relationship between management ‘IS maturity’ and effective post-acquisition IS integration is demonstrable.
