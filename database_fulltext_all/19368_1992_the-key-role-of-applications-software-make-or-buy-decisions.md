# The key role of applications software make-or-buy decisions

Tony Rands

Fellow in Management Science and Information Systems, Templeton College, Oxford, UK

Past limitations in the software sector have often forced firms to use internal sources to satisfy much of their software needs. However internal sources also have limitations which affect their ability to supply adequate software. With software important in influencing the effectiveness of IT, limitations in both external and internal sources mean software acquisition decisions should be integral to formulating IT strategies. Meanwhile, specialization of the software sector into vertical markets since the late 1980s is possibly reducing its limitations, and exposing weaknesses in the tools available for making software acquisition decisions, particularly make/buy decisions. Lack of adequate tools for taking software make/buy decisions could affect firms' ability to use IT competitively where specialized software markets exist.

Keywords: software acquisition decisions, make/buy decisions, information technology management

The discussion in this paper stems from empirical work by the author into the potential impact of developments in the software industry on the management of information technology. This has involved collecting and analysing primary data, for instance, using software product databases, interviews with executives of software firms in four continents, and surveys of IT managers. It has also included literature research and further analysis to secondary data, for example UK companies' IT spending figures, kindly provided by Price Waterhouse.

One conclusion of the study is that, in the past, firms have generally been forced to produce their own software, namely integrate upstream into software development, by major inadequacies in the software market. However, data collected by the author suggests developments are taking place in the software industry which may eliminate some of these inadequacies. These developments, therefore, raise questions about the extent firms now need to integrate into producing their own software. Meanwhile, literature surveys suggest frameworks for managing software acquisition may be inadequate in the emerging climate. With software performance important in influencing the effectiveness of IT, this could adversely affect firms' ability to use IT competitively.

To explain why these conclusions have been drawn, this paper describes principles behind integration and explores its potential for obtaining competitive advantage. Using literature surveys and secondary data, the paper goes on to explain how past inadequacies in the software industry have led firms to depend on internal sources for software, and to describe changes occurring in the software sector revealed by primary data. This leads to a description of the gap these expose in frameworks for analysing software make/buy decisions. The paper concludes with some practical suggestions for managers and possible directions for future research.

## VERTICAL INTEGRATION BY FIRMS

## Vertical integration and make/buy decisions

Vertical integration is the combination of technically distinct economic processes inside a firm. It may mean firms undertaking activities downstream (forwards integration) as far as distribution and retailing, as well as upstream (backwards integration), including supplies of components and raw materials. One of the best known examples of an integrated industry is steel, where blast furnaces, converters and primary reduction mills are often included in a firm and located in close proximity.

Decisions about the appropriate degree of integration for a firm are central to its business planning, and can significantly affect performance $^{1}$ . This suggests one of the major long term objectives of management should be to balance and mix between in-house and purchased goods and services to make the most effective and efficient use of their firm's resources.

Basic principles behind integration decisions are shown in Figure 1. The single product/service provided by the firm is made up of six components/services, A to F. Producing these requires several process stages, for instance, five, P1 to P5, to produce A, and seven, P1 to P7, for C. Meanwhile the completed product/service passes through a number of downstream stages, which may include distribution.

In Figure 1, the position of the dotted line indicates the firm buys A with process stage P1 completed, and itself undertakes the remaining stages P2 to P5. This means the firm has integrated backwards to embrace those processes for A. Meanwhile the firm buys C completed as far as stage P5, and then only undertakes P6 and P7. Thus the path followed by the dotted line represents the boundary of the firm, with process stages outside being purchased.

As far as component F is concerned, at present the firm makes P2 to P4, buying F with P1 completed. A decision to buy, rather than make, stage P2, i.e. switch to purchasing F with P2 completed, would reduce the degree of integration of the firm and thereby, as shown in Figure 1, change its boundary. Thus vertical integration is influenced by make or buy decisions $^{2}$ ; the results of these decisions, whether consciously or unconsciously taken, determine the extent to which firms themselves provide components and services, or purchase these.

## Make or buy practices in evolving industries

Although the degree of integration of a firm may be perceived as the outcome of individual make or buy decisions for each process stage, overall integration patterns may often be observed. Research suggests these are largely shaped by external pressures, which lead firms to undertake, or refrain from undertaking, certain upstream or downstream activities. As industries around firms develop therefore, these pressures can vary, meaning firms should respond by modifying where they integrate, and the extent.

For instance, during the embryonic and early development phases of an industry, there may be insufficient external suppliers of certain processes $^{3}$ , and firms may have to integrate into providing them, to guarantee essential supplies. Also, even when sufficient suppliers emerge, during the early phase of an industry, markets may be poorly organized $^{4}$ , leading to high transaction costs. These are caused by difficulties in searching for components and services of the required specification, as well as low supplier reliability. The result could be that, even though sufficient external suppliers exist, firms in immature industries have little choice but integrate.

Meanwhile, as industries become mature with established suppliers and reliable channels for trading, firms may no longer be forced to integrate out of necessity, and may safely purchase many of the goods/services they require $^{3,4}$ . As just mentioned, this suggests that, as an industry grows from an embryonic to a mature stage, firms may need to adjust make or buy practices to reflect contemporary conditions.

## Benefits and penalties of vertical integration

Various studies $^{3-5}$ suggest that, in mature industries, firms can profitably undertake certain processes they could easily purchase because it is beneficial so to do. Thus, in the case of steel, firms often integrate to achieve cost reductions by eliminating reheating between process stages. Benefits of integration often stem from the ability to exercise direct control over the integrated processes, through, say, the specification of services or allocation of resources $^{4}$ .

However vertical integration also incurs penalties. Reasons for these include problems in balancing capacities across different areas of the firm $^{4}$ , as well as managing a range of diverse and often unfamiliar technologies. There may also be insufficient activity levels to achieve scale economies. Where industry structures change rapidly, Harrigan $^{5}$ argues ‘a highly integrated posture . . . could reduce a firm’s manoeuvrability and damage its profitability’. When external conditions leave firms with little choice but provide essential components and services themselves, integration may have to be undertaken in adverse circumstances. This may mean, although essential supplies are guaranteed, firms are unable to integrate efficiently or effectively.

This suggests that, as integration patterns are influenced by make or buy decisions for individual processes, these decisions should allow for both the potential penalties and benefits of vertical integration. In consequence, Dale and Cunningham $^{6}$ have argued that the make or buy decision is ‘a strategic area with one of the greatest long range effects’; this implies it should be treated as a policy issue, with management responsibility at the highest level.

## Using vertical integration for competitive advantage

Anderson and Weitz $^{2}$ believe ‘the primary reason for vertical integration is the absence of competitive markets for products and services the firm needs’. Using transaction cost analysis, first proposed by Williamson $^{7}$ , they argue that because of the inherent efficiency of markets and the unavoidable inefficiencies of bureaucracies created to manage vertically integrated operations, greater efficiency can generally be achieved if vertical integration is not undertaken. They conclude integration is a deviation undertaken when these conditions do not exist. Without becoming involved in semantics, Anderson and Weitz imply firms should integrate where markets possess 'inefficiencies' and/or those firms can create 'efficiencies' by producing internally.

Harrigan $^{5}$ suggested that ‘the key in using vertical integration is recognising which activities to perform; how to relate these activities to each other, how much of its needs the firm should satisfy in-house; how much ownership equity should be risked in doing so, and when these dimensions should be adjusted to accommodate new competitive conditions’. Different ways of using integration have been studied by various writers, among them Teece $^{8}$ , who explored how make or buy decisions could be influenced by technical innovation. Meanwhile Aoki $^{9}$ considered the information efficiency of vertically integrated and horizontally structured firms.

Sutton $^{4}$ identified two generic strategies for firms using integration for competitive advantage, namely to seek cost reductions and improvements in general competitive position. Harrigan $^{5}$ combined these into one overall policy, suggesting firms should adjust their make/buy mix so as to free internal resources to undertake a more profitable mix of activities.

Harrigan $^{5}$ also suggested how firms might achieve this through backwards integration. These include exploiting upstream supply conditions, such as shorter delivery times, and using particular product traits to drive upstream integration. For instance, if trade secrets protect some aspects of a firm's products, backwards integration may guard that secrecy; Schlumberger developed software in-house with the aim of protecting secrets of its well-logging services. Harrigan also suggested that when firms have special competencies, for example knowledge of certain processes, they may exploit these to supply related components, rather than purchase. Special competencies may endow these components with higher performance than those from outside suppliers, such as higher quality; a firm may then use this additional performance to obtain competitive advantage.

The importance of skills and competencies in shaping make/buy policies has also been recognized by other researchers. Dale and Cunningham $^{6}$ and Ford and Farmer $^{10}$ found the location of specialisms was often a factor leading managers to make or purchase different goods and services. Walker and Weber $^{11}$ identified the extent to which outside suppliers have proprietary technology, and the extent to which buyers have strong expertise as issues impacting the make or buy debate. Meanwhile Walker $^{12}$ used the transaction cost approach $^{7}$ to suggest that make or buy decisions should depend on the relative qualifications of internal and external suppliers, as well as the strategic importance of the goods and services being considered.

## INTEGRATION INTO SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT

## Modifying make/buy practices to match changing computer industry conditions

The computer industry is an upstream supplier of industrial goods and services. This means demand for its goods and services depends on them being perceived to provide firms with net gains. Porter and Millar $^{13}$ have argued that utilizing computers effectively with other IT is core to firms' viability, because IT can change products and industry structures, spawn new businesses and create competitive advantage. For firms to implement IT, they must acquire, combine and eventually update or replace a range of separate but inter-related IT components. These include equipment, but also non-physical elements such as software, education and training.

Prior to 1960, the computer industry was almost non-existent. Today's structure is the result of evolution from an embryonic stage over the past 30 years. It would appear that, as the industry has developed, so its ability to supply different components has proceeded at an unequal pace. This suggests that, as in other developing industries, firms should have modified backwards integration patterns for individual IT components, so they were relevant to contemporary supply conditions, and IT make or buy practices to match.

For instance, in the case of hardware, prior to 1960, few producers existed and supplies were generally inadequate, leading firms to integrate backwards into building their own equipment, e.g. LEO at Lyons. However, equipment supplies developed relatively quickly and, since the early 1960s, firms have generally purchased these rather than attempt to produce equipment themselves.

## Embryonic phase of the software sector

In the case of software, supplies from the computer industry have developed more slowly. Until as recently as the late 1970s, the portion of the computer industry supplying software appeared to be in an embryonic phase, with few producers and a limited number of products. Until 1969 software was often provided by computer manufacturers, bundled within hardware prices.

Possibly the slow development of software supplies, and the lack of software products was due to insufficient scale economies, caused by a proliferation of proprietary operating systems $^{14}$ and inadequate sales of each system. In this embryonic phase, software suppliers, other than computer manufacturers, were mostly software houses selling tailor-made software individually commissioned by firms.

As a result, at least until the late 1970s, external software suppliers and supplies were often inadequate. Stigler's argument $^{3}$ appeared to apply, as firms were forced to integrate backwards into software development to provide computer applications they required and support the necessary resources. In many instances these resources were concentrated within a single internal source, the DP department, staffed by specialist systems analysts and programmers. They were usually required to provide software across the spectrum of a firm's activities, often undertaking the entire software development cycle from design to installation and subsequent maintenance.

Unfortunately, the author's empirical work has unearthed little data which reveals the structure of the software sector during this embryonic phase, for instance the number of producers, product availability, or its channels of distribution. However analyses of Datamation DP budgets for that period provide an insight into the extent firms relied on internally produced software. For instance, the forecast for 1976 $^{15}$ predicted that, whereas 1.2 per cent of DP budgets would be spent on packaged software, personnel would consume 50 per cent, with a large proportion of analysts and programmers producing and maintaining internal software.

## Growth of software supplies during the 1980s

Since the late 1970s, the software sector has grown, often at compound rates of over 15 per cent per annum $^{16}$ , with the emergence of increasing numbers of software producers and products. This may have been stimulated by a broadening range of computers, a rise in computer sales, an increasing market share taken by standard operating systems $^{14}$ , and backlogs in DP departments $^{17}$ . In many ways the software sector of the computer industry may now be viewed as a separate international industry in its own right. One forecast $^{18}$ estimated sales in Europe alone to be \$26 billion in 1991, of which \$16 billion was packaged software.

This growth has provided firms with potential access to a wide range of external sources able to supply software products to satisfy their needs. Figure 2 contains the result of an analysis of product databases, and shows both the industry general and specific applications software products on the UK market during 1991. The 7200 products from over 1600 suppliers in Figure 2 compares with about 6000 products from 1200 suppliers during 1988, and provides some indication of growth rates. The products contained in Figure 2 refer to all types of machines, from micros to mainframes, and cover varied management activities, from marketing and sales to personnel. For example, Figure 2 shows there were 182 distribution and 161 transport management packages on offer.

## Continued integration into software development

Despite the potential this range of software products offers firms to buy rather than make software, survey data from DP departments shows many firms still channel the majority of software resources to internal rather than external software.

For example, analysis of firms' IT spending pattens, kindly made available by Price Waterhouse, shows that in 1990 average spending on external applications software by DP departments was 5.3 per cent of total expenditure, with personnel consuming 45 per cent (48 per cent of which was staff assigned to software development and maintenance). From these figures, it appears that on average about 81 per cent of DP resources allocated to software were assigned to internal, rather than external, software.

However, there is some evidence that, during the 1980s, there has been a small movement of DP departments' software resources towards external sources.

## Poorly organized software markets

Despite the small movement of software resources away from internal sources suggested by Table 1, it appears that the overall allocations of DP software resources internally are high when compared with the availability of software products suggested by Figure 2. Whilst internal allocation may be partly accounted for by maintenance of applications developed internally prior to the 1980s, there are indications that, despite the wide range of producers and products on offer, the software market during the 1980s has been poorly organized. As a result, firms may have been deterred from using it; instead, as Table 1 shows, software resources tend to be allocated internally.

Several writers have pointed to this lack of organization in software markets during the 1980s. For instance, Gross and Ginzberg $^{20}$ linked problems of using software packages to a lack of vendor financial stability and support to customers, firms' dependence on vendors' assessment of packages' capabilities, few standards for evaluating vendors, vendors not understanding customers' industries, few legal definitions of vendor responsibility, and suitability of available products. Meanwhile Couretas $^{21}$ claimed that 'anarchy' reigned in the micro software market, with start-ups and failures occurring 'at a dizzy pace'. As late as 1988, a DTI report $^{22}$ highlighted the poor quality of purchased software in the UK, and estimated this added as much as 50 per cent to the cost of purchasing software.

## Penalties of internal software development

Several penalties of vertical integration have already been described, and there are indications that firms encounter many of these when integrating into software development. Firstly, for many firms, integration means embracing a technology more complex than their normal activities. This may involve them acquiring additional skills which cannot easily be controlled $^{4}$ within a single management structure. Secondly, as in many firms where there is a limited internal market for individual applications, scale economies may be difficult to achieve. Limited internal markets may also mean that DP departments are expected to be 'jacks of all trade' $^{23}$ producing software across a wide range of applications. As a result, much internal production is developmental, taking place early on the learning curve. This may partially explain why much software fails to meet design specifications $^{24-26}$ . Despite rapid prototyping provided by 4GLs, the developmental nature of much internal software production creates problems.

Whilst basing applications around customizing purchased software packages has overcome some difficulties, it can create complications $^{27}$ . Firms have found modification costs high and the time required difficult to estimate $^{20}$ . Problems have also arisen with end-users developing applications, e.g. introducing costly errors into spreadsheets, raising questions about the need to adhere to development procedures $^{28}$ .

Consequently there are grounds for asserting that internal software production tends to be inefficient $^{23}$ and contains inherent weaknesses. This implies that, whilst past limitations in the software sector may have forced firms to supply essential software themselves, gaining additional advantages such as control over design, inherent deficiencies in using internal software sources mean this may have been difficult to undertake effectively.

## RECENT TRENDS IN THE SOFTWARE SECTOR

## Move towards specialized software markets

During the late 1980s commentators noted increasing specialization was moving the software sector towards vertical markets. They account for this by economic pressures $^{23}$ , the marketing patterns of software vendors $^{16}$ and firms' demands for industry-specific products and services $^{29}$ . Of the software products identified in Figure 2, 3543, or about 49 per cent are industry specific, leaving about 51 per cent generic.

One example of vertical structuring is manufacturing, where hardware producers have supplied specialized software, such as MRP packages, since the mid 1960s. Later independent software producers became major suppliers $^{30}$ . Taking a sample of 41 manufacturing software products revealed 67 per cent had been launched since 1982, with most covering a range of applications such as finance, production and distribution to field service, etc. There are also regular market surveys (e.g. Marsh $^{31}$ ), which focus exclusively on the manufacturing sector, providing data on brand leaders, market penetration and acquisition trends.

It would appear that specialization is more pronounced in some markets, e.g. manufacturing and retailing, than others. For instance, of the industry specific software products shown in Figure 2, over 1400 (about 40 per cent) were directed towards these two sectors, of which over 1000 were for manufacturing alone. On the other hand about 1 per cent (under 100) were aimed at the recreation and leisure industry.

Despite this uneven pattern of specialization, there appear to be a number of forces driving it throughout the software sector during the 1990s. These forces are as follows.

## 1. Switching caused by market saturation

Evidence suggests that, when specialized software markets become saturated, producers switch to parallel markets where specialized software products are not so widely available. For instance, interviews recently conducted with software producers for the UK manufacturing sector, where there is a 70 per cent market penetration $^{31}$ with signs of saturation, showed they are now concentrating on the UK food and drinks industry, using product derivatives.

## 2. Hardware manufacturers' strategies

Specialization in the software sector has been mirrored by several hardware manufacturers, who have reorganized around vertical industry markets $^{32}$ . A number have formed links with, or taken over, software suppliers, often strengthening specialization. For instance DEC has a co-operative marketing programme linking it with software producers who, according to its publicity material, ‘supply critical parts of solutions that customers require’. DEC states that ‘selecting the right software is vital for a successful and secure computer solution’, and claims its co-operative marketing programme ‘brings you a choice of top applications from market leaders in your specialist area’.

## 3. Differentiation strategies of software producers

Doyle and Saunders $^{33}$ have argued that specialized industrial markets tend to be orientated towards customer needs and, because of this, producers find that ‘success is rarely achieved by undifferentiated strategies’. Interviews with software producers in specialized markets confirm this view, and suggest producers have adopted several strategies aimed at differentiation. These are:

Niche marketing. Producers are launching increasingly focused products designed for niche demands in specialized software markets $^{33}$ . For instance, retailing is further segmented, as Figure 2 shows.

Added value services. Many producers are providing upstream and downstream services in addition to software products creating added value. These include needs specification, installation, maintenance, upgrading, education and training, as well as consulting.

Specialized knowledge. Many software producers are developing skills directly relevant to their market segment, for instance, in field service or bond trading; many recruit specialists directly from those fields, but a number retrain systems analysts and programmers. Many have over 40 per cent of their workforce staffed by specialists directly relevant to their market sector, often from a non-computing background.

The immediate impact of these forces is likely to be a widening of software make or buy choices beyond purchasing or not purchasing packages. Instead choices will include the types of internal and external sources to use, the upstream and downstream stages of the software development cycle to acquire as added value services, and whether software should be standard, modified in some way, or bespoke. As a result, determining a suitable software acquisition strategy by searching for the right mix between making and buying may open large permutations for evaluation, possibly making planning a time consuming activity.

## Potential new conditions in the software sector in the 1990s

Forces driving segmentation in the software sector during the 1990s may lead to a transition in its development, which, as has happened in other industries, represents a further stage towards maturity $^{34}$ . One possible effect of this maturity will be the creation of new supply conditions, different from the 1980s.

There are several reasons why new conditions may emerge. These lie principally with:

## 1. Product performance and cost

Changes in the skills of software producers will lead many to possess more industry/functional knowledge than consumer firms in their market $^{23, 35}$ . Incorporating this in software products will lead to higher IT application performance than firms could generally supply from internal software $^{23}$ . Moreover scale economies would ensure this to be achieved at a much lower price. Internal versus external cost comparisons suggest software packages have a 10-fold advantage.

## 2. Better organized software markets

The emergence of niche products combined with the specialized knowledge of producers is likely to lead to increased credibility with consumer firms and more sophistication in marketing $^{33}$ . This, in turn, could emphasize benefits of products and services, producer reliability, the need to formalize contractual procedures, etc. The increasing use of market research techniques by software producers, as well as market surveys and product evaluations, will lead software markets to be better organized in the 1990s than during the 1980s.

Should these new conditions emerge during the 1990s, they will almost certainly demand a reappraisal of firms' integration into software development and maintenance, and hence the role for DP departments $^{23}$ . For, in these new conditions, many of the market limitations of the 1980s, discussed above, will have vanished. In this case, the necessity for firms to allocate internally the levels of software resources shown in Table 1 will have diminished.

## INADEQUATE SOFTWARE MAKE OR BUY FRAMEWORKS FOR EMERGING CONDITIONS

## Prominent position of software

As mentioned above, IT is regarded as core to firms' strategic planning. Several writers have explored ways in which IT may contribute to firms, and considered bases for devising and implementing an IT strategy. One route has been to build on the value chain $^{13}$ , using systems methodologies and other approaches $^{36}$ . These provide frameworks which seek to link firms' IT strategies to their business needs. Despite this, there are still debates $^{37}$ about the relationship between business and IT strategies, as well as the factors which help firms use IT successfully $^{38}$ .

Whichever basis is adopted for planning IT, implementation involves allocating resources to acquiring various IT components, and eventually replacing or modifying them. Among these, continued developments and pressures for open systems mean that, in terms of cost, performance, architecture, etc., hardware and other physical components are placing decreasing constraints $^{24}$ on obtaining satisfactory IT performance.

In contrast, in an increasing number of situations, the ability of firms to acquire adequate software at an economic cost has become critical $^{24, 26, 39}$ in influencing IT implementation. Table 1 shows that applications software consumes a high, and increasing, proportion of the DP department resources of UK firms, making it essential that these firms obtain the highest benefit from those resources. The importance of software performance was recognized by Martin and McClure $^{40}$ who argued that ‘although computers promise to greatly improve business efficiency, that promise cannot be fulfilled without adequate software’.

## Strategic importance of software make or buy

This suggests that, with software becoming increasingly important in determining overall IT cost and performance, effectively implementing any IT strategy will be tied to possessing adequate economic software and hence acquiring that software. In turn, software acquisition involves not only determining its specification and budget; it also involves deciding whether the software should be new or existing updated, made or bought.

As discussed above, it cannot be assumed that acquiring essential software from either will automatically follow once an IT strategy has been formulated. This means determining how and from where software should be acquired needs to be integral to formulating an IT strategy, and not separated from it. This, in turn, leads to the conclusion that software is no different from other components and services for which make or buy options should be a strategic matter $^{6}$ . Given the range of make or buy options likely to be open for software during the 1990s, analyses of strategies should ideally indicate, not only which internal and external sources to use, but whether software should be bespoke design, unmodified or enhanced packages and which added value services should each source supply.

## Research into software make or buy

Despite this growing importance of software make or buy to managing IT, the author's empirical work has revealed relatively little research into the subject. As a result, there appears to be a number of gaps in our knowledge. For instance, there is little published data about the software make or buy policies and procedures actually adopted by managers. There are also few models either for deciding whether to acquire software for an application internally or externally, or for determining how many or what type of internal and external software sources are required. Less than a dozen articles on software make or buy have appeared in major journals during the past five years, whilst IT textbooks rarely devote more than a few paragraphs to the subject.

In the limited number of studies into software make or buy, single IT projects are usually considered $^{41}$ , whilst strategic aspects are almost ignored. Gremillion and Pyburn $^{42}$ proposed concepts which view software make or buy from a strategic perspective. They identified features of IT applications which could lead firms generally to buy software, rather than make. These include the commonality of applications, their impact and degree of structure. Durbin $^{41}$ also explored a number of software make or buy policy issues. These include the leveraging effects of purchasing software, which allows talented systems designers to be released to produce software which cannot be purchased.

In contrast, the use of packaged software has been comparatively widely explored in texts and papers. Aspects covered include purchasing procedures $^{40}$ , procurement problems $^{39}$ and difficulties in implementation $^{20,27}$ . Ward et al. $^{43}$ used the applications portfolio grid $^{44}$ to propose unmodified software packages be used for support applications, enhanced packages for factory, with packages unlikely to be suitable for strategic and turnaround applications. However, although works on software packages provide some insight into make or buy situations, this insight is not sufficient to analyse the complex permutations of options likely to be open to firms in the emerging software conditions.

There seem to be several reasons for the lack of interest in software make or buy. One is a general failure by managers to recognize the strategic nature of make or buy decisions for many goods and services. Surveys by Dale and Cunningham $^{6}$ and Ford and Farmer $^{10}$ have shown that many managers regard make or buy as an operational matter, taking decisions on an ad hoc basis. Another reason could be that, whilst attention in IT has been focused on reducing software costs, this attention has apparently led to a drive to raise DP department productivity through 4GLs, CASE tools, and improved development procedures $^{45}$ , rather than re-assessing software sourcing. In other words, productivity of internal software development has been perceived as the strategic issue $^{46}$ , rather than the extent to which firms should use internal and external software sources. However, as Durbin $^{41}$ argues, amending packaged software could itself lead to higher productivity than using development tools.

## Potential impact on firms during the 1990s

Lack of tools specifically designed to address software make or buy situations could put firms at some disadvantage in the software markets likely to evolve during the 1990s. At the level of planning overall IT resources, it may mean firms will be uncertain as to the correct level of internal software sources, such as staff numbers in DP departments. Dearden $^{23}$ believes firms will need to divest from DP departments but, without adequate frameworks and tools, it may be difficult to determine precisely the required level of internal software sources, or how much should be in DP departments. Firms will also need to decide what resources to devote to creating and maintaining links with external software sources, and develop procedures to manage these links. Without adequate frameworks, these decisions could expose firms to hazards $^{39}$ through inappropriate use.

As far as undertaking individual IT projects are concerned, lack of adequate software make or buy frameworks could mean firms will be uncertain that these applications will eventually deliver sufficient benefits. For a firm seeking competitive advantage through say, its IT based distribution network, could suffer if it made incorrect software make or buy choices on related projects. For, if these applications were based on internally developed software, eventual performances may be inferior than those provided by cheaper software products bought by competitors from specialized external sources. Alternatively the converse may be the case.

## FUTURE DIRECTIONS

This discussion has been concerned primarily with creating an awareness of the future importance of software make or buy decisions, and highlighting knowledge gaps in the area. However, in so doing, possible directions for research may be readily identified. In addition, the discussions lead to a number of suggestions which practitioners may implement quickly.

## Research into software make or buy

Discussions in earlier parts of the paper would indicate a number of routes for future research. These are as follows.

## Surveys of management practices and procedures

It would appear beneficial to conduct surveys inside firms which throw light on how managers actually undertake software make or buy decisions, and estimate the likely impact of how those decisions are taken. These surveys could replicate the work of Ford and Farmer $^{10}$ . The insights gained, such as identifying good and bad make or buy practice, could be shared with managers so they were stimulated to adopt better practices.

## Apply vertical integration research to software

Research into vertical integration appears to offer several possibilities for application to software. For instance, Harrigan's $^{5}$ proposed integration strategy of freeing internal resources to undertake a more profitable mix of activities is consistent with Durbin's $^{41}$ suggestion that software make or buy should be managed so that talented systems designers are released to produce software which cannot be purchased. Developing software make or buy models which recognized the location of skills and competencies, internally or externally, would build on the work of Walker $^{12}$ , and may assist IT managers plan numbers of analysts and programmers, as well as the functional skills (e.g. distribution) they should possess. Rands $^{47}$ (1990, Part E) has shown how a number of vertical integration principles may be applied to software, and further research could build on the ideas generated there.

## Build on past IT research

Past research into IT offers several openings for incorporating software make or buy analysis. For instance, much of the early analyses of IT strategies based on the applications development portfolio $^{44}$ embodied a de facto premise that software came from internal sources. Yet Ward et al. $^{43}$ have developed the approach further to incorporate software packages, and it seems likely that further evolution to include more complex make or buy options is feasible.

## Suggestions for practitioners

The discussions in this paper would indicate that certain courses of action were immediately open to practitioners, even though in-depth research into software make or buy has not yet commenced. These courses of action are as follows.

## Limit the range of internal software development undertaken

In firms in sectors where a wide range of software packages are marketed, it would appear advisable for practitioners to limit internal software development to those areas where packages cannot be purchased. This may mean purchasing and installing packages which do not provide everything users require. Practitioners should also ensure DP departments are not 'jacks of all trade' undertaking applications for a wide range of clients. This may mean focusing internal development to areas where company-specific skills may be developed and exploited.

## Vet vendors when purchasing external software

Although the software sector is maturing, in many sectors it may demonstrate symptoms of a poorly organized market for several years. The entry and exit barriers for software vendors are relatively low, whilst the costs of consumers switching vendors are typically high. This means consumers require long term relationships with vendors, for which continued vendor viability is essential. This suggests vetting vendors should be as important as assessing the merits of their products and services.

## CONCLUSIONS

I have argued that specialization in the software sector in the 1990s is likely to challenge assumptions about where firms should obtain software. It thereby raises questions about the basis on which firms' need for software is determined, as well as the production role for DP departments. For firms able to answer this challenge and to use software make/buy as a strategic weapon, new opportunities may be opened through employing IT. In taking a retrospective look at pressures which have shaped firms' integration into software production and maintenance, I hope to have demonstrated that software production is little different from other economic processes, which firms have at one time had to undertake themselves, and then been able to hand over to external sources.
