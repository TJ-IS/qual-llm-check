# Key information technology issues in Estonia

Albert S. Dexter $^{1}$ , Marius A. Janson $^{2}$ , Enn Kiudorf $^{3}$ and Jüri Laast-Laas $^{4}$

$^{1}$ Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia, 2053 Main Mall, Vancouver, BC V6T 1Z2, Canada; $^{2}$ Centre for International Studies, University of Missouri-St Louis, USA; $^{3}$ Westech Information Systems, Inc., Vancouver, Canada; $^{4}$ Institute of Cybernetics, Tallinn Technical University, Estonia

The considerable differences between countries in the Eastern European region make studying information technology (IT) issues and generalizing results rather challenging. However, careful analysis shows the Estonian IT environment shares many similarities with other Eastern European economies and may serve as an exemplar for identifying IT concerns for the region. This article discusses a Delphi-based investigation into the pressing issues encountered in the Estonian IT environment. The results are compared and contrasted with previously conducted Delphi studies in Western countries. The major concerns of Estonian IT managers focus on technological rather than managerial issues, whereas IT managers in the West report the reverse ordering. Furthermore, even though these IT managers shared some issues with their Western counterparts, new issues unique to Estonia emerged. Several conclusions about the IT environment are drawn and suggestions are made for replicating the study in other Eastern European countries.

Keywords: information systems management, key issues, management priorities, public policy issues, Estonia, Delphi

Dramatic political liberalization has occurred in the countries of Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union. This in turn stimulated economic liberalization and a concomitant movement from centrally planned and controlled economies to market oriented ones. Many information systems (IS) academics and practitioners inside and outside the region agree that effective use of information technology (IT) is essential to these societies for their successful economic transformation.

Little knowledge exists, however, regarding the problems that confront IT managers and practitioners in these countries, and how to solve them. Studying issues surrounding IT is motivated by rapidly changing technology, increased organizational dependence on IT for competitive advantage and survival, and the large investments required for its implementation. Keeping the understanding of IT issues current is necessary to ensure academics research important topics and practitioners learn from peers and industry leaders.

The primary purpose of this research is to determine key IT issues facing managers in the Eastern European and emerging Commonwealth of Independent State (CIS) economies. A secondary objective is to compare and contrast these findings with Western industrialized nation studies. It is especially important to study the Eastern European and CIS arena today. As these economies shift focus from a centrally planned command and control to more decentralized, market orientation, issues of technology transfer and uptake are important. Moreover, these nations differ in their legal, economic, social and cultural environments from Western industrialized nations.

The paper proceeds as follows. First it briefly provides the rationale for studying a former Soviet republic in Eastern Europe, and for using the Delphi method. Next follows the instrument development for the empirical research, emphasizing the role of language translation to ensure comparability with other studies. It then compares and contrasts the findings with prior Delphi studies, and concludes with future research directions.

## Rationale for a study of Estonia using the Delphi method

## Estonia study

The historic political and economic events sweeping Eastern Europe and the CIS make the study of IT issues particularly relevant today. This region comprises many distinct nations; hence it is necessary to understand the local conditions of each emerging country. The study was concluded during the last phase of the old Soviet order and provides a unique opportunity to understand a representative republic during transition.

McHenry, Snyder and Lynch (1990) called for cross-cultural research into IT in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union, addressing four difficulties of this type of research: obtaining reliable literature, getting access to appropriate personnel, communications across languages, and dealing with biases. These authors and Van Bochsel (1991), and Bahr (1990) provide important background information on IT in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, respectively. These articles provide a useful context for investigators interested in conducting research projects in the CIS economies, because the basic issues persist after the demise of the former Soviet Union.

The evolution and current state of IT in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe have been also well documented by numerous researchers including Goodman (1984), Davis and Goodman (1987), Judy (1987) and Barker (1990). Because Estonia is well integrated into the Soviet and Eastern European sphere, the IT experience in Estonia is relatively typical of the region. Estonia is an exemplar of the European regions of the CIS. Estonia is small, with a population of 1.5 million, bounded by Russia and Latvia (Rahva Hälal, 1989). Estonians are culturally similar to the Finns, sharing a common folk tradition and history. Before World War II the country was an independent Western style democracy. It was annexed by the Soviet Union in 1940. During Soviet rule the political and economic institutions were radically transformed, private ownership and enterprise were banned, and dissent was not tolerated. Estonia is similar to the countries studied by Geipel, Jarmoszko and Goodman (1991) who concluded that the relatively weak IT infrastructure of these nations arose from the lack of economic development caused by communist subjugation.

While attitudes of entrepreneurship, individualism and understanding of market mechanisms waned during the Soviet period, Estonia's political and socio-cultural orientations remain quite Western. Estonia has undergone significant economic, political and social change since the emergence of Gorbachev, culminating in the re-establishment of independence in September 1991. In many ways the conditions in Estonia during recent years are more similar to those in Hungary and Czechoslovakia than to neighbouring Russia, and certainly more European than the former southern and Asian republics. Estonia's economy promises to progress relatively rapidly; high levels of skills and education of the general population, a Western orientation, geographic location and an ability to quickly adopt reforms are all elements in its favour (Hofheinz, 1991).

Estonia lacks some technological conveniences taken for granted in the West. For example, the telephone system often functions poorly. There are other instances however, where the technology lag with the West is non-existent. A researcher working in Estonia encounters satellite television, VCRs, Western-built computers, photocopiers, fax machines, printers, international electronic mail links and other technological conveniences. The challenge, however, is to ensure access to these technologies as needed.

Important requirements for successful research in the region are awareness and preparation for local conditions and establishing and maintaining good local contacts. This study benefited from a local Estonian co-researcher who participated fully in the research project. This streamlined the research process, helped identify cultural variables that might interfere with the research, and eliminated much of the uncertainty associated with functioning in a non-familiar environment.

## The Delphi method

Identifying and prioritizing issues critical to US-based IS managers was the focus of a study by Dickson et al. (1984), who used a revised Delphi method (Dalkey, 1969; Delbecq, Van de Ven and Gustafson, 1975; Kendall, 1977; Taylor and Meinhardt, 1985). This procedure was used in subsequent studies — by Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987) in the USA; Watson (1989) in Australia; Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe (1991) in the USA; and Palvia and Palvia (1992) in India — to investigate critical IS issues.

The Delphi method is particularly attractive for this study. First, questionnaires are used to solicit expert opinion and thus the participants can be geographically remote. This ensures anonymity and prevents dominant individuals from unduly influencing the results which may arise in face-to-face group meetings. Second, the process is inexpensive and can be completed in a short time. Third, subsequent rounds of questionnaire completion enable information feedback thereby stimulating reflection, movement toward consensus, and greater depth of insight. Finally, the Delphi's findings allow statistical summarization and graphical display to demonstrate group consensus while paying special attention to outlying or dissenting opinions.

Using the Delphi method to investigate IT issues in Estonia enables comparing and contrasting the findings with those of earlier studies, and contributes to a cumulative MIS discipline. The investigative procedure allows the researcher to control for methodology, thus strengthening claims that any differences observed between Estonia and other studies arise solely from the environment.

## Instrument development

The discussion above provided background information on the Estonian IT environment and previous Delphi issues studies. The primary research objectives were to determine the important IT issues as seen by Estonian IS professionals, and to compare and contrast these issues with previous Delphi studies in Western economies.

Although this research project closely followed the Delphi methodology established in previous studies, several modifications were necessary. These included: (1) a bilingual questionnaire format; (2) an issue list based upon prior North American issues, a literature search on the Soviet Union, discussions with Estonian IT professionals, and an open-ended first round; and (3) a convenience sample.

An important difference between this study and previous MIS issues studies was the requirement for bilingual instruments (Brislin, 1980). Most Estonian IT managers understand English, and in fact 87 per cent of our group reported knowledge of English in addition to several other languages. Nevertheless, to assure the success of the study, the two languages allowed participants to respond to the questions in either Estonian or English using a single questionnaire.

The questionnaire comprised 30 issues that originated from several sources. First, a study of Eastern European computing literature contributed eight issues unique to this environment:

\- planning and implementing a telecommunications system;

\- specifying, developing and recruiting human resources;

• regulatory constraints on IS activities and ambitions;

● availability of appropriate hardware and software;

\- affordability of hardware and software;

\- satisfying the information needs of users/organizations;

\- building and maintaining reliable information systems;

\- establishing alliances with Western organizations.

Second, a consensus of previously determined issues of importance from industrialized nations yielded 12 additional items. These items are identified with the letter O (for old) on Table 1 (see e.g., Alpar and Ein-Dor, 1991). Two emerging issues that have been rated highly in recent North American research were also included:

\- IS for competitive advantage (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987);

\- developing an information architecture (Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1991).

Third, informal discussions with Estonian representatives from industry, government, and academia provided two more issues:

• ensuring the physical security of computer systems;

\- legislating copyright protection for software.

Finally, the results from an open-ended questionnaire provided the remaining eight issues:

• educational weaknesses of the workforce;

\- implementing and improving computer networks;

\- promoting hardware, software and data standards;

\- unsettled political and regulatory environment;

\- access to Western IT knowledge and advice;

\- improving information security and control;

\- changing the attitudes of IS employees;

\- loss of skilled IS workers to foreign bidders.\*

## Results and analysis

After the open-ended questionnaires were collected and analysed from 10 of the participants, 28 questionnaires were distributed, with 24 useful questionnaires returned for both Rounds 2 and 3, an 85 per cent response rate. The respondents were employed by government agencies (58 per cent), educational institutions (17 per cent), privately owned companies (13 per cent), and organizations involved in joint-ventures with the West (12 per cent). The respondents were active IT professionals and practitioners (63 per cent), and educators, consultants and vendors (37 per cent). Organizational size ranged from one employee to 1600 employees, with a median company size of 60 employees.

The open-ended portion of the questionnaire asked participants to describe briefly the IT products used in their organizations. While the unstructured nature of the responses makes a statistical synopsis impossible, it is possible to make a number of observations:

\- all organizations have DOS-based machines, typically imported from Finland or other Western countries. The most common processors are 8086 and 286, although many 386 machines are also in use.

\- some IBM PS/2, Apple Macintosh and UNIX-based workstations are also in use, although these are fairly rare.

\- large established organizations have a variety of mainframe, mini- and microcomputers manufactured by Council of Mutual Economic Assistance (CMEA) countries; new market-driven companies typically have no CMEA machines.

\- all organizations had a large variety of Western software products, the most common being WordPerfect, Lotus and Dbase IV. This software is often in the form of unauthorized copies.

\- networks are rare, although a few firms reported Ethernet environments, and one organization used a Novell LAN.

## 2. Promoting standards for hardware, software and data

Eastern European and Soviet built hardware are often ‘enhanced and improved’ copies of Western computers. This leads to hardware and software incompatibilities that complicate constructing information systems integrating locally built and Western built computers. The lack of generally accepted programming standards, data formats and database definitions further complicate data sharing and information transfer. Estonian IT managers are therefore keenly interested in promoting hardware, software and data standards.

## 3. Implementing and improving computer networks

With the increasing proliferation in organizations of stand-alone microcomputers for day-to-day information processing, a need for sharing and managing data between individual machines arises. This mandates improving existing and implementing new electronic networks.

## 4=. Satisfying user and organizational needs

Designers in the West are frequently criticized for developing information systems that fail to satisfy adequately the needs of users. System designers in Estonia differ little in this regard from their Western counterparts; however, the problem is made worse by central planning and top-down decision-making mechanisms that bypass the user as a source of developmental ideas. Furthermore, systems in state bureaucracies have heretofore supported central planning mechanisms and, thus, developers are inexperienced with developing information systems for organizational competitiveness.

## 4=. Improving information security and control

Information security and control implies protecting data from unwanted destruction, alteration and disclosure. As state controls are relaxed, the potential for compromising information security also increases. Private agencies, companies and state bureaucracies are increasing their use of computerized databases. Hence, a need for information security and control is gaining in urgency.

## 6. Building and maintaining reliable information systems

Reliability, that is, systems free from frequent breakdowns and down-time, is an important concern in the Estonian IS environment. First, components for Eastern European and Soviet computers are scarce and a lack of hard currencies make replacement parts for Western machines exceedingly expensive. Second, the necessary expertise to service and repair computer systems locally is in short supply. Third, maintaining and repairing computing equipment is made difficult by a lack of local vendor support and qualified product representatives.

## 7. Establishing alliances and linkages with Western organizations

Alliances and joint ventures with Western organizations provide much necessary managerial expertise to increase the competitiveness of Estonian companies. Alliances with Western organizations ensure access to computer hardware and other IT products that otherwise are too expensive for Estonian firms. While we have little hard data on the extent of these linkages with the West, we observe an attitude among Estonian professionals that ‘more linkages are better’. We expect in the foreseeable future, more joint ventures, more academic/professional cooperation such as that experienced in this study, as well as a variety of outright aid, both from private and governmental organizations. We have observed little cooperation between Estonian professionals and other Eastern European professionals, and speculate that this latter type of link is less important. Some professional IS societies within Estonia are now emerging, and these groups are trying to establish links with Western organizations.

## 8. Developing an information architecture

An information architecture specifies how separate classes of information support major organizational functions. The interest that Estonian IT managers show for telecommunications, computer networks and industry standards directly reflects an active concern for information architecture.

## 9. Ensuring the physical security of computer systems

In North America, replacing a desktop computer costs approximately \$1000 — less than one month's salary of a typical worker. In contrast, in Estonia, during the summer of 1991, the same computer cost 40 000 rubles — roughly 7 years' salary (Chereshkin et al., 1992). Thus, preventing damage caused wilfully, by negligence, or by misuse, is particularly important because repairing or replacing equipment is exceedingly expensive.

## 10=. Making effective use of the data resource

The data resource issue concerns how organizations can use information captured in corporate databases effectively. Managers are frequently unaware of data captured by corporate databases that could improve decision-making or, alternately, they lack the ability to access this resource. Estonian managers realize the need to achieve levels of effectiveness required by a competitive market economy.

## 10=. Legislating copyright protection for software

The lack of intellectual property protection in many Eastern European areas leads to unauthorized distribution of proprietary systems. Much software in use in Estonia is unlicensed, and IT professionals maintain large personal libraries of unlicensed copies of Western software products. The prevailing practice of software pirating discourages software vendors from entering the market.

While not shown explicitly on the graph, a distance of 1.5 times the IQR measured from either side of the box indicates location of the innerfences, in this case at 6.5 and 10.5. The most extreme observations between the box and innerfences are marked by the horizontal line at the end of the ‘whisker’ (i.e. the line emanating from either side of the box) here 7.0 and 10.0. Likewise, a distance of three times the IQR on either side of the box indicates location of the outerfences, here 5.0 and 12.0. Observations between the lower and upper innerfences are considered representative of the majority of data. Observations between the innerfences and outerfences are ‘unusual’ observations, and marked by an asterisk, here values 5.0 and 6.0. Those outside the outerfences are ‘outliers’, marked by a circle, here value 3.0.

An interpretation of the final round of the issue ‘Alliances and linkages with Western organizations’ is as follows. The median issue rating is 8.5, tightly packed, as the IQR is very small at size 1. Noting that the median is centred in the box and the whiskers have identical lengths, one concludes that the responses form a symmetrical distribution. The opinions of three respondents are at variance with other participants in the study. The opinions of two participants are unusual, and one opinion represents an outlier.

Uncovering the reasons for unusual and outlying data is an integral part of careful data analysis. In this case, the same individual scored the issue with a rating of 3 in Rounds 2 and 3. His opinion changed from unusual to outlying, because the group rated the issue higher in Round 3 than in Round 2 (i.e. the median score increased from 8 to 8.5) and it reached greater consensus (i.e. the IQR decreased from 2 to 1).

The advantage of viewing boxplots over numerical summaries is further illustrated by the left two columns in Figure 1, which refer to Rounds 2 and 3 of 'Planning and implementing a telecommunications system', respectively. The plot shows the median and IQR were unchanged between Rounds 2 and 3. However, the asterisk shown in Round 3 indicates that one individual lowered his score, which is now an unusual response in relation to the group norm. Furthermore, the whisker length became shorter in Round 3, indicating a tightening of consensus. Information similar to that described above appears for the remaining issues.

The Delphi technique, by virtue of its information feedback mechanism, encourages group consensus on the importance of issues. Measuring the change in the IQR for the ratings between subsequent rounds is an appropriate method for showing movement towards consensus. For the 11 top issues the change in spread between quartiles from Round 2 to Round 3 is also illustrated in Figure 1. One readily notes the general reduction in size of each IQR, signifying a movement towards consensus. Nine of the 11 issues have an IQR of 2 points or less by Round 3.

A further indicator of improved consensus on the importance of the issues is an increasing differentiation between the most important and least important issues (Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1991). The mean rating for the 10 most important issues increased from 8.16 to 8.35 while the mean rating of the 10 least important issues decreased from 6.03 to 5.83 between Rounds 2 and 3, respectively. Finally the standard deviations of the issues generally decrease between Rounds 2 and 3.

Even though additional rounds might improve consensus, it is unlikely that perfect agreement will ever be attained. In fact, we observed that some study participants continued to maintain certain independent views. A detailed review showed that two participants out of 28 were responsible for most of the unusual and outlying responses. While these subjects were internally consistent in their own rankings, they simply marked all issues as lower in importance than their counterparts did. Estonian IT managers can be further differentiated among dimensions such as position, education, language capabilities, location, industry and other organizational aspects; therefore, each of these factors influences a manager's view of the importance of individual issues.

## Discussion

Direct comparisons with any of the previous Delphi studies from the Western industrialized nations and the current Estonian study are difficult at best (Table 1). First, there are 15 new issues that are special to countries such as Estonia which are moving from a centrally planned economy to a market oriented one. Second, for the 15 other issues that are more comparable with the previous studies, the rankings differ dramatically.

Following Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987) and others, the 30 issues were classified into managerial and technical categories. This analysis helps clarify the meta issues that dominate the IT agenda in Estonia specifically, and more generally in Eastern Europe and the European segment of the CIS. Managerial issues include enterprise-wide problems such as policy, strategy, structure, accountability and human resources. Technology issues relate to questions of technologies and applications such as specification, acquisition, development, use, security and protection.

The analysis uncovered a domination of technology issues over managerial issues (Table 1). The first six issues are all of a technological nature, as are eight of the first 10, and 11 of the top 15 issues. The most important managerial issue is 'Establishing alliances with Western organizations', which is ranked seventh. Most other top issues concern the procurement and effective application of technology. Recall, however, that many managerial concerns, e.g. competitiveness, were simply not a feature of communist-directed organizations. This result, therefore, differs dramatically from studies in English-speaking countries where the emphasis is on managerial issues. In the USA, Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987) found managerial issues were nine of the top 10, and 11 of the top 15, while Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe (1991) classified as managerial issues, seven of the top 10 and eight of the top 15 respectively. Parker and Idundun (1988) found managerial issues were nine out of the top 10 in their UK research.

The emphasis of Estonian IT managers on technology suggests a different stage in the development of the organizational IT function. In fact, the trend from technological to managerial issues has been well documented in the literature, Dickson et al. (1984); Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987); Parker and Idundun (1988); Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe (1991) $^{*}$ , and Estonian managers might simply lag behind their Western counterparts with respect to the problem types they face. As Estonian organizations continue to modernize and Westernize their operations and organizational cultures, managerial issues will likely attain greater importance. It should also be noted that while technological issues dominate current Estonian IT concerns, the specific technology issues of importance bear little resemblance to those issues that topped the IT agenda in North America and other Western countries during the 1980s.

Estonian IT managers clearly place more emphasis on the telecommunications issue than their Western counterparts. Managers in the United States, Australia and Europe have relatively modern and functional telecommunications systems, and thus their priorities lie elsewhere. The information security and control issue was more important to Estonians than to their Western counterparts. However, Estonian IT managers are in relatively close agreement with Western managers on the importance of developing an information architecture; this issue has been rated in the top 10 in many recent international studies (Watson, 1989; Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1991).

Issues concerned with IS strategic planning, using IS for competitive advantage, and alignment of organizational and IS goals have consistently ranked highly in Western studies. In the Estonian study, these issues were ranked relatively lower. Perhaps a trend towards managerial issues will occur also in Estonian companies as IT managers increasingly adopt Western standards and modernize their operations and organizations. Certainly there appears a role for Western professionals to help incorporate ideas about IT training into management education in Eastern Europe.

While efforts were made to preserve the rigour of the research, there are several limitations to the generalizability of the study. First, the term ‘MIS manager’ is unfamiliar to Estonian organizations and individuals surveyed in this study are best described as ‘established figures in the Estonian IT community’ while the participants in the Western studies are ‘MIS managers’. Second, the study was conducted during the summer of 1991, a period of extensive political and economic reform in Estonia. It is likely that attitudes and opinions on IT issues were also in a state of rapid evolution. Third, the data for the Estonian study are slightly more recent than the data for the other studies which are used for comparative purposes.

The results of this study provide an indication of the priorities and concerns of Estonian IT managers; however, the findings are also relevant in other parts of Eastern Europe. As discussed previously, several factors suggest that the Estonian IT environment is typical of the region, but some caution should be exercised in drawing specific conclusions about the priorities of other IT managers in Eastern Europe and the European region of the CIS. In several aspects, this area is highly diverse in terms of language, national culture, degree of Westernization, pre-1939 history and even recent developments. Alternatively, these countries all shared a common economic and political system for the past 50 years which resulted in the development of comparable centrally-planned economies, organizational cultures, educational systems and industrial activities. These countries also cooperated in the development and use of information technologies. While no country in Eastern Europe can be seen as fully representative of the entire region, Estonia is relatively typical. This suggests that the concerns of Estonian IT managers may parallel those of their counterparts in other areas of the region.

In determining whether the most important IT issues in Estonia may be relevant elsewhere in the region, it is useful to consider the issues themselves. Of the 30 issues, none appear to be strictly linked to the Estonian IT environment. Most issues are universal in nature and can be seen as concerns for IT management in all parts of the world. Some of the issues do raise concerns about specific circumstances in Estonia, such as the poor state of the telecommunications system, the need to establish alliances with Western companies, the lack of software copyright protection, and unsettled political and economic environments, but these circumstances appear to apply equally well to many other countries in Eastern Europe.

## Concluding remarks and future directions

This study is a first attempt at uncovering the primary concerns of IT managers in an Eastern European country. However, the findings are expected to be relevant to countries other than Estonia, but verifying this proposition requires additional research.

First, replicating the Estonia study at a future time would be useful because continuing political, economic and technological changes will impact considerably the entire organizational climate in Estonia and IT managerial concerns. Such efforts were undertaken previously in North America and elsewhere with the series of Delphi studies and have proven useful in maintaining a current understanding of important issues and trends.

Second, it should be useful to replicate the study in Eastern European settings other than Estonia to help clarify if managers in the region face a common set of concerns, or whether the findings of this study are unique to Estonia. Western investments and interests in Eastern Europe have been largely directed toward Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Poland and Russia, and thus these countries would be particularly appropriate for supplementary studies.

Third, it would also be useful to augment the Delphi findings with in-depth case studies of Eastern European IT organizations. Estonian IT operations differ from those in other countries, but additional research is required to uncover and clarify the causes of these distinctions. Investigating the evolution of an IT department in a CIS country, to determine whether lessons learned from the Western experience apply here as well would be particularly interesting.

Finally, the Delphi method for researching IT issues is well established, forms a cumulative paradigm, and has been used extensively in North American and other international studies. This should motivate researchers to use the Delphi approach in future studies to enable comparing and contrasting findings with previous studies.
