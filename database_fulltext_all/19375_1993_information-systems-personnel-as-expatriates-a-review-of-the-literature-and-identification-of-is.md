# Information systems personnel as expatriates: a review of the literature and identification of issues

Fred Niederman

Assistant Professor, University of Baltimore, 1420 N. Charles, Baltimore, MD 21201, USA

The growing importance of information technology (IT) in facilitating international commerce creates opportunities for multinational firms. Realizing advantages from IT in the global area requires intelligent utilization of skilled personnel. However, prior research on general managers working outside their home country indicates potentially expensive problems for both individual employees and firms. This paper applies a research model previously developed in the expatriate literature to anticipate problems facing expatriate information systems (IS) personnel. From a research perspective, studying the reactions of IS personnel as expatriates can test the robustness of the proposed model. Insights from observing the adjustment of IS professionals in multinational settings may aid in better understanding domestic IS personnel issues in a time of growing employee ethnic, cultural and gender diversity.

Keywords: information technology, globalization, information systems personnel

Over the past 20 years, the business environment has been moved by two powerful trends: increasingly powerful information technology (IT) (Laudon and Laudon, 1991) and globalization of business (Porter, 1990). Computer hardware and software now available on desktops manifest the equivalent power of 1960s' systems that handled all computing functions for entire firms. Telecommunications linkages now span the globe (and are perched high above) allowing virtually instantaneous communication across continents. At the same time, there has been an increasing globalization of investment, markets, raw materials, production and labour. Products and services which have traditionally been produced within a domestic environment now face growing competition from firms around the globe. According to Reich (1991), 'The threads of the new global web are computers, facsimile machines, satellites, high-resolution monitors, and modems — linking up ideas and money from each of the company's worldwide locations with every other'.

With the trends towards globalization and increasing use of IT, firms operating globally find themselves processing information locally in more than one country and, in some cases, across borders. While anticipated legal problems involved with data crossing borders have not been as severe as once expected (Kane and Ricks, 1988), data processing in multiple countries creates new technical issues and managerial questions regarding the optimal way of utilizing data (Heywood, 1991).

As a result of global operations, firms require some combination of local systems and international connections. Software created by the company for transaction processing or decision support will, to some degree, be utilized in both field and central offices. This presents a range of issues for the multinational firm including: what level to control information processing (King, 1983); what level to locate information processing equipment (King, 1983); matching global information systems (IS) strategy to global business strategy (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991); establishing the technical platform for global IS applications (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991); sharing of data across borders (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991); and issues of IS projects spanning cultures including how to staff project teams and the on-going information processing function (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991). Firms are taking different approaches to these issues. For example, Union Texas Petroleum Corporation maintains a centralized IS department but sends staff on remote (including overseas) assignments for up to nine months at a time (Computerworld, 1991).

A major decision confronting organizations is the mix of expatriate and local nationals to staff the IS function. Expatriate and local national employees may have different strengths and weaknesses. Tung (1984) points out that while 'local nationals can effectively manage their fellow countrymen and relate well to domestic clients, they may have problems communicating with corporate headquarters' (p. 141). In the IS area, firms need to evaluate the relative merits of transferring local personnel, who may have more specialized skills, with providing higher levels of training for local nationals. Establishing the right mix of skills in a subsidiary and setting up appropriate tasks when foreign assignments are made may aid in creating successful utilization of expatriate staff. Moreover, as pointed out by many researchers of expatriate personnel issues, the cost of sending an employee overseas can be high. This has been estimated at two-and-a-half to three times the base salary (Lanier, 1979).

The issue of staffing multinational enterprises with IS personnel is recognized as a key issue in global information systems by multinational IS managers (Deans and Ricks, 1991; Deans and Kane, 1992). Recruiting and training of IS personnel was rated as 13th out of 32 key issues with an overall rating of 4.6 on a seven-point scale (where 7 is high and 1 is low). The 32 issues ranged in ratings from 5.3 for the top issue, educating senior personnel, to 3.5 for export restrictions. It should also be noted that in the survey recruiting and training was described by the sentence: 'Shortages of qualified personnel, especially in some countries, may increase the importance of recruiting and training'. In a sense this concept encompasses a variety of staffing issues and ought to include establishing the mix of skills, including both abilities to handle IT and knowledge of business conditions at one or more sites.

It comes as little surprise that staffing issues would be viewed as significant in the area of global IS considering that human resource issues are viewed by general IS managers as highly important. In a broad survey of MIS managers (Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1991), human resource issues are ranked as the fourth of 25 key IS issues in the 1990s. In fact, there is much historical support for human resource management as a key IS issue. Since 1980, in a series of surveys conducted approximately every three years in the USA, human resource management has been ranked 7th, 8th, 12th, and most recently 4th (Niederman, Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1991). Additionally, Watson and Brancheau (1991) report this issue as being ranked second in Australia in 1988, seventh in Europe in the same year and fifth in Singapore in 1987. Furthermore, the movement towards internationalization of human resources in general is of growing concern to personnel managers (Milliman, Von Glinow and Nathan, 1991; Adler and Bartholomew, 1992).

The purpose of this paper is to identify staffing issues for international IS operations with a particular focus on problems and opportunities presented when IS personnel are transferred to assignments outside their home country. Section 1 reviews the IS personnel literature; section 2 reviews the expatriate literature; section 3 discusses models for understanding the expatriate phenomena; section 4 presents a research agenda for testing the experience of IS professionals against the expatriate models; section 5 suggests some ways that IS managers can profit from the existing knowledge from the expatriate literature; section 6 discusses the IS personnel and expatriate experience in the broader context of cultural diversity; and the final section presents concluding remarks, identifying topics requiring further research.

## 1. IS personnel

Literature regarding IS personnel has focused on three issues: whether IS personnel vary from non-IS counterparts (and whether those variations merit different management techniques for productivity); the degree to which IS professionals perform varying task assignments; and how to deal with high levels of turnover.

A significant body of evidence has been compiled suggesting that IS professionals systematically differ from non-IS counterparts (Couger and Zawacki, 1978a, b, 1979; Couger, Zawacki and Oppermann, 1979; Bartol and Martin, 1982). IS professionals were primarily distinguished from non-IS employees by higher levels of need for individual growth and lower levels of social need. Couger and colleagues also found that IS managers rated their job significantly higher on core job dimensions of skill variety, task identity, task significance and autonomy.

Studies by Ferratt and Short (1986, 1988), however, found no differences between IS and non-IS employees (i) in the pattern of motivational factors that would influence productive work (the researchers controlled for different job levels comparing IS to non-IS clerical, technical, or managerial workers separately); (ii) in the way in which they were managed; (iii) in levels of productivity based on differences in the work-unit environment (as a surrogate for management style). The study did find, however, that regardless of type of manager the highest performance followed from high levels of support and attention to production; lowest performance was from high attention to production with low levels of support.

These differing findings may result from changes over time, from differences in the approach and questions of the researchers, and also from differences in the sample of IS professions chosen for study. Couger's sample must have been drawn in the mid-1970s whereas Ferratt and Short's was probably drawn in the 1980s. As a result it may be that over time IS professionals have come to resemble other managers, particularly with the increasingly wide distribution and use of personal computers. Duliba and Baroudi (1991) argue forcefully that, 'IS personnel perform work today which differs dramatically from work performed twenty years ago' (p. 114). Additionally, Couger polled high level MIS professionals from lists derived from the Society for Information Management, whereas Ferratt and Short sampled from employees in the Midwest of the USA in the insurance industry.

While IS professionals generally focus more on technology than their general management counterparts, IS professionals vary greatly among themselves. Studies often distinguish between programmers and analysts or between data processing ‘operations’ and ‘development’ personnel (Couger, Zawacki and Oppermann, 1979; Chadwin and Cross, 1983). Myers (1991) adjusted for the volatility of the IS field by contrasting not only IS professionals against non-IS professionals, but also by sub-dividing IS into ‘traditional’ and ‘non-traditional’ job categories based on tools used and tasks performed (p. 34). Traditional IS professionals were those whose tasks more closely resembled the more prominent tasks of the 1970s data processing shop. This study supported Couger et al. as far as distinguishing traditional IS professionals from non-traditional IS professionals and non-IS professionals; but also supported Ferratt and Short in that the combined traditional and non-traditional IS professionals did not differ from non-IS professionals.

Several studies of IS personnel, particularly up through the early 1980s, suggested that turnover among IS professionals was extremely high (Bartol and Martin, 1982; Baroudi, 1985) with estimates ranging from 20 to 28 per cent turnover rate. Kaiser (1983) suggested that turnover rates were inversely related to the age and number of years' experience of the IS professional; that as IS staff grew older and more experienced, they tended to remain longer and have more loyalty to a particular organization.

In a classic study of IS personnel attitudes focusing very specifically on turnover, Baroudi (1985) suggested that IS work typically involved high levels of role ambiguity (where expectations of the employee are not clear) and high levels of boundary spanning (employee activities that span both their own and other organizational units). This should be particularly true among systems analysts or end-user consultants. Baroudi showed that high levels of role ambiguity among IS personnel correlated with lower levels of job satisfaction and organizational commitment. High amounts of boundary spanning activities led to high levels of role conflict, but also to high levels of organizational commitment. High levels of job satisfaction correlated with high levels of organizational commitment. Finally, high levels of organizational commitment implied low levels of intention to quit. While this study is insufficient to support the reverse logic — if intention to quit is high (demonstrated by actual turnover figures), then role ambiguity and role conflict must be high — it does raise interesting research questions regarding the nature of IS work and suggests high value in managers providing clarity and definition where possible.

Even though our ability to characterize the IS employee is imperfect, with the increasing emphasis in the world economy on both globalization and IT, it is likely that some mobility of IS labour has been and will continue to be created. Although the author is aware of no studies focusing on IS personnel as expatriates, the IS manager embarking on or sending an employee on a foreign assignment may learn a great deal from the literature on the expatriate experience, which will be briefly summarized in the next section.

## 2. Expatriate literature

The processes of selecting, training and evaluating the impact of foreign assignments on employees has been the subject of significant amounts of research. As of 1973, it was estimated that more than 35000 US employees represented US firms abroad (Miller, 1973). Although assigning personnel outside their home country provides opportunities for transferring technological skills and building organizational human resources, it also poses some significant problems. Such an assignment can be expensive and failure, in terms of non-completed assignments or poor performance, even more expensive (Baker and Ivancevich, 1971; Misa and Fabricatore, 1979). Moreover, there are non-monetary costs to the firm such as sub-optimal career paths for the employee (Harvey, 1989). This is of particular concern to US firms which have historically shown a significantly higher failure rate than Japanese or European multinationals (Tung, 1988).

Traditionally, organizations have either tried to reduce the potential for failure through selection and training programmes or, quite often, have ignored the problem. Tung (1981), for example, finds fully 68 per cent of surveyed companies with no formal programme for assigning and training personnel for work outside their home country.

The literature on expatriate human resources is large but can be divided into seven major categories (Harvey, 1989) by roughly following stages of progression as viewed by the expatriate employee.

Selection and selection criteria for expatriate assignment (Borrmann, 1968; Miller, 1972, 1973; Hays, 1974; Miller and Hill, 1978; Lanier, 1979; Tung, 1981, 1988). According to Tung (1981), the preponderance of foreign assignments is based primarily on technical qualification, however successful completion of assignments is often based on ability to adjust to the host country (Miller, 1973). As IS professionals often perform highly technical tasks, it is likely that in this area there would be additional tendency to make a selection based on technical qualification. However, we can expect that tasks will vary in their requirements for adjustment to the host country. For example, within the IS departments, one employee may be involved in installing local area networks (a highly technical task that may not require either intensive or extensive communication with host country nationals) while another develops an end-user information centre (a task that is likely to include extensive communication with host country nationals).

Miller and Hill (1978) discuss a related issue: how to persuade the employee to accept an expatriate position after having been selected for the post. They point out that while many similar considerations are expressed by employees changing domestic jobs as those going to a foreign post, expatriates tend to hold greater expectation of career and/or economic advancement as a result of their experience. It may be more difficult or require more specialized inducements to persuade IS professionals that advantages outweigh risks when accepting a foreign assignment.

Training for adaptation to culture (Tung, 1981; Harvey, 1982; Mendenhall and Oddou, 1986; Mendenhall, Dunbar and Oddou, 1987). Mendenhall and Oddou use three dimensions (self-orientation, others-orientation and perceptual-orientation) to differentiate skill levels in potential expatriate employees. They create a taxonomy of potential personality types by varying high and low levels of skill in these three areas. These categories can be used in fine-tuning understanding of expatriate experiences and tailoring training programmes even though the authors stress that all potential expatriates should receive training prior to their new assignment. The length and intensity of prior training, however, can vary according to expected length of stay, type of involvement in the culture, cultural toughness of the host country relative to the country of origin, family differences, expected interaction with host-nationals, and likelihood of future expatriate assignments.

## Information systems personnel as expatriates

Acculturation once transferred (Mendenhall and Oddou, 1985; Pinder and Schroeder, 1987). To the extent that we can apply findings from the more general study of time to proficiency following job transfers (Pinder and Schroeder, 1987), perceived levels of support once at the new location may be a key element in successful transfer of personnel. Mendenhall and Oddou (1985) suggest four specific dimensions in which to enhance acculturation: self-oriented (reinforcement substitution, stress reduction and technical competence); others-oriented (relationship development and willingness to communicate); perceptual ability (making correct attributions regarding and being less judgemental when evaluating host-nationals' behaviours); and cultural-toughness (that cultures differ to varying degrees and it is more difficult to adjust to cultures that differ more from one's own). The authors suggest using these dimensions for assessing managers prior to foreign assignments and tailoring acculturational training programmes to address each dimension. IS professionals may need to develop some abilities to apply highly specialized competencies in less than ideal circumstances — for example software development specialists may need to do some hardware installation before they can perform their more specialized tasks. Both others-oriented and perceptual ability may be critical for 'softer' IS work such as identification of information and data requirements and supervising lower level IS personnel.

Impact of foreign assignment on family (Tung, 1982, 1988; Black and Stephens, 1989). There is a significant literature showing the potential negative impact of difficulties of the employee's family in adjusting to life in a new country (Tung, 1988), and a connection between these difficulties and the tendency to finish the assignment. However, there does not seem to be any reason to expect this to be different for those working in management information systems (MIS) compared with other professionals.

Return on investment (ROI) of expatriates versus foreign nationals (Hays, 1974; Misa and Fabricatore, 1979). While Misa and Fabricatore (1979) discuss the costs of failed assignment overseas, there does not seem to be any literature providing a detailed look at the relative value of staffing key posts with expatriate versus foreign national employees that can be extended to the specific situation for MIS managers. We might expect that development teams with mixtures of local and 'home office' personnel might be able to best create systems that are adapted to local conditions but also address the concerns of headquarters. The literature does not suggest any basis for determining an ideal mix of local and expatriate personnel for a given operation. We might expect that general personnel policies for a firm would tend to be applied to the IS department along with other departments such that companies that tend to maximize the number of host country employees might be expected to maximize the number of host country IS employees, and vice versa.

Stress of foreign assignment on the executive (Mendenhall and Oddou, 1985). By its nature, taking a foreign assignment involves an increase of stress on the individual and family. This stress will result from the accumulation of changing work role, residence and life style patterns. Additionally, there may be adjustment to a new language and new culture. Articles in the trade literature have asserted that the role of the IS professional, regardless of location of assignment, tends to be highly stressful. However, an exploratory study based on questionnaire response data gathered by Ivancevich, Napier and Wetherbe (1983), finds little evidence that the IS professional faces more stress than vice presidents, managers or supervisors. The study does not rule out the possibility that the IS role is more stressful, but simultaneously attracts individuals better able to cope. We might expect high levels of stress for expatriate IS professionals, however, there may not be any reason for stress levels to increase more rapidly for IS professionals than for other managers.

Repatriation (Harvey, 1982, 1989; Tung, 1984, 1988; Mendenhall, Dunbar and Oddou, 1987). Financial and cultural readjustment issues should generally be similar between IS and other personnel, but we would expect that due to the rate of change of technology, keeping up with the latest technology may create career path problems for the technically-oriented returning professional.

Research following the stages of movement from domestic to expatriate employee provides some indication of what firms need to plan effectively for returning IS professionals. Careful selection, pre- and post-departure training, and programmes for expatriate readjustment are important components of a plan for aiding expatriate IS employees. The degree to which programmes designed generally for management employees will effectively meet the needs of expatriate IS employees as well as the degree to which IS employees will be better served by programmes is more specifically tailored to their needs have not been determined.

## 3. Models of international adjustment

Black, Mendenhall and Oddou (1991) review and integrate international and domestic personnel literatures regarding the adjustment of employees to a new setting. Their model lists factors that influence ‘anticipatory adjustment’ or the expectations of the employee prior to departure. Anticipatory adjustment and a variety of other factors in turn influence the ‘mode and degree of adjustment’ once on the job (see Figures 1 and 2). The assumption being that mode and degree of adjustment either influence or serve as a surrogate for level of performance and duration of stay overseas.

In this model, anticipatory adjustment is influenced by both individual (training and previous experience) and organizational (selection mechanisms and criteria) factors. The authors hypothesize that both individual and organizational factors will affect levels of anticipatory adjustment. The level of anticipatory adjustment joins with five other factors to influence mode and degree of adjustment on the job. The five other factors are individual (self-efficacy, relation skills and perception skills); job (role clarity, role discretion, role novelty and role conflict); organization culture (organization, culture novelty, social support and logistical help); organization socialization (socialization tactics and socialization content) and non-work (cultural novelty and family-spouse adjustment).

Due to the breadth and complexity of this model, in predicting dependent variables of interest such as adjustment or ‘success’, we can anticipate potential influence from a large number of variables that can take on many different values. For example, the variable ‘past experience’ might vary greatly from individual to individual in terms of specific job related, knowledge of languages and cultures, and abilities to adapt in cross-cultural settings. This may vary to the point where it is difficult to form homogeneous categories consistently including like individuals and excluding different ones. It may also be difficult to account for all possible interactions among these variables. For example the effectiveness of different training programmes may vary greatly with the past experience of selected individuals and both may then influence the level of anticipatory adjustment.

Nevertheless, this model provides a basis for targeting specific areas of intervention for providing the expatriate employee with the maximum probability of a successful adjustment. It also provides a framework for further investigation regarding the relative impact of various factors and combinations of factors on expatriate success.

Prior research by Black (1988) studied two distinct facets of adjustment for expatriate personnel: adjustment to work and adjustment to the general culture. A survey of expatriate Americans in Japan (most of whom were senior executives with only 1.5 per cent of the respondents being technical personnel, p. 283) found that work adjustment was negatively related to role ambiguity and positively related to role discretion. This implies that expatriate workers will perform better when their past successful behaviours can accurately predict current successful behaviours and when they have more freedom to modify their new role in keeping with their abilities and habits. This is consistent with Baroudi's (1985) findings that high role ambiguity goes with lower job satisfaction discussed above.

Interestingly, although not discussed in the paper, Black's (1988) findings included a non-significant and slightly negative correlation between work adjustment and general adjustment. This runs counter to the expectation that adjustment between work and general life would be mutually reinforcing. A different set of factors, pre-departure knowledge, association with host nationals and family adjustment, influenced general adjustment. As Black suggests, care should be taken in supplying pre-departure knowledge about the host country to make sure that it supports adjustment and effectiveness. It is possible that with some prior knowledge of the host country employees may have made less adjustment to their role (in order to be more culturally sensitive) but that may have resulted in less role flexibility and less overall work adjustment.

Due to the low number of technical personnel in this sample, findings are not necessarily considered to be general. Therefore, it would be reasonable to test whether this finding also holds for technical and, more specifically, IS personnel. Until counter-evidence is presented, however, firms would probably be well advised to reduce role ambiguity, or clarify their expectations regarding expatriate staff, and, to the extent possible, provide opportunities for employees to tailor roles towards their own personal style when taking up expatriate positions.

## 4. Testing the expatriate literature in the case of IS personnel

As discussed above, some evidence suggests that IS personnel differ from other employees, particularly in their low need for social contact and high need for achievement. Although the degree to which these differences exist and the implications of these differences are not fully established, differentiating IS professionals from other expatriate managers represents an opportunity to test the robustness of theories of expatriate adjustment and post-assignment readjustment. It also offers potential insight into the mechanisms contributing to successful completion of expatriate IS assignments and guidelines for managers charged with making such assignments.

Applying the Black, Mendenhall and Oddou (1991) model to the IS personnel literature suggests a series of important research questions. Following the dependent variables in their model, we may ask:

1. Do significant predictors of anticipatory adjustment differ between IS personnel and general managers?

2. Do significant predictors of mode and/or degree of adjustment differ between IS personnel and general managers?

3. For IS personnel, are there significant relationships between anticipatory adjustment, mode and/or degree of adjustment and long-term career success (in terms of advancement to senior management, turnover, job satisfaction, etc.)?

Anticipatory adjustment. Following Black, Mendenhall and Oddou (1991), anticipatory adjustment is a measure of the preparedness of the individual for the overseas assignment. This variable is influenced by training (amount and type), previous relevant experiences, accurate expectations, the mechanism by which the individual was selected, and the criteria by which the individual was selected. Do training and selection mechanisms and criteria for overseas posting differ or are they the same for technical and general management staff? Assuming them to be generally the same, we can see that they may have different impacts on personnel of different types. If they are different, we may investigate whether the use of different training and selection mechanisms are justified for the different personnel types. It is not unreasonable that some firms may be very careful and thorough in training and selection of general managers before posting overseas, but haphazard with technical personnel, assuming that requirements of the position ought not to vary from location to location.

The other two variables, previous relevant experiences and accurate expectations, may be trickier to pin down. We need to distinguish between the importance of these variables and the relevance or accuracy of the particular instantiations. In other words, to assess the influence of prior relevant experiences, we need to measure both how relevant the specific experiences were and how important they turned out to be. Since the relevant experiences of technical staff and general managers will differ from each other, we will need two scales. We can also measure expectations, but again we will need to assess both how accurate they are as well as their importance. It is quite possible that the amount of relevant experience and accurate expectations are equally important for both technical staff and general managers, but they may be more difficult to deliver in one case or another (e.g. the population of technical specialists may include more or fewer individuals with prior overseas experience).

Following the expatriate literature, both individual and organizational attributes influence the anticipatory adjustment of the individual to the new post. Firms, particularly American firms, have historically selected individuals to expatriate positions based on their job-related competencies without much regard for cultural adaptability (Tung, 1982, 1988). To the extent that the IS position requires high levels of technical skills, selection based on job-related competencies would seem prudent. Where the overseas posts demand technical competence this makes sense, but where communication and understanding are needed (e.g. during requirements determination and/or installation and implementation of new systems), we might expect IS people to have a more difficult adjustment. While low social need as a motivating attribute does not necessarily predict ability at social dealings, we might do well to be concerned about the possibility of such a connection. To the extent that IS employees do indeed have higher needs for achievement and lower needs for social contact, and where the expatriate position in itself presents more challenging human interactions, some concern is justified that the result will be statistically higher levels of poor adjustment and less successful expatriate experiences.

Mode and/or degree of adjustment. Adjustment to the new assignment is viewed as central to the successful completion of that assignment. Black, Mendenhall and Oddou (1991) suggest six different variables influencing the mode and/or level of adjustment by expatriate personnel. These variables revolve around individual skills, the nature of the job and its match with previous positions, the organizational culture, the socialization provided by the organization, the non-working environment and the level of anticipatory adjustment. Individual skills and the nature of the work of IS personnel can be expected to vary systematically from those of general managers. To some extent, we would expect that social oriented skills of general managers would aid in adjustment to a new culture. IS professionals, on average, cannot be expected to display as high a skill level in this area. The work of the IS professional can be expected to include higher demand for technical and specialized proficiency. On the other hand, there would be no reason to expect the non-work environment, the overall organizational culture and the socialization provided by the organization to vary between the IS professional and general manager. Anticipatory adjustment can be expected to vary, according to the model, based on factors described above.

Long-term career success. Although not directly addressed by Black, Mendenhall and Oddou (1991), the long-term performance or success of the employee is of concern both to the employee and organization. In some cases, an overseas assignment will be so important that a firm might be tempted to trade-off long-term benefits from a successful relationship with the employee for the short-term completion of the project. In light of the potential costs to the firm and individual, this trade-off ought not to be made lightly.

It would seen reasonable, following Black, Mendenhall and Oddou (1991), to examine the impact of identified variables preceding and during overseas assignments on long-term success. Measuring long-term career success should take into account at least the following variables: time for readjustment to domestic assignments, career advances over time, variance between desired and achieved promotions, and the number of years before moving to another company.

It can be expected that overseas assignment will have stronger impacts on the long-term career of IS professionals than on general managers. Due to the rapid development of new information technologies and the obsolescence of old ones, there is particularly strong pressure on IS personnel to remain current with the very latest technologies. These employees stand to be most vulnerable to falling behind from being outside the flow of changing technology. On the other hand, as Miller (1973) points out, ‘Americans overseas may be expected to demonstrate generalized skills requisite for maintaining and guiding a relatively small scale subsidiary facility.’

In IS terms, a specialist in database administration, in addition to defining data elements and constructing access paths, may need to write retrieval programs and, perhaps, even input data. Of course, this may be a function of moving from any larger department with highly specialized roles to a smaller unit with a lesser volume of activity and more diverse roles for individual staff members. To the extent that a specialist in a more developed operation is transferred to a smaller subsidiary operation, we might expect to find higher levels of mismatch between the expected and actual job and, as a result, higher levels of dissatisfaction among IS expatriates. On the other hand, IS personnel assigned specifically for utilizing a particular skill, such as installing local area networks, may be able to keep up with changing technology whatever country they are in.

It may be of particular long-term interest to see if there is any impact from overseas assignment on movement from IS into senior management ranks. It has been asserted that IS professionals have less access to promotion to senior management levels in many firms (Kaiser, 1983; Lasden, 1983). This assertion in itself presents a testable hypothesis: whether, in fact, IS professionals find it more difficult to reach senior ranks and, if this is true, what creates this situation (e.g. the characteristics that make them successful within IS make them less successful in senior ranks; negative views of IS as a service rather than profit centre; or that senior managers choose others that resemble themselves, with former marketing managers choosing marketing managers). Assignment for overseas tasks for IS professionals may represent an opportunity to gather a broader range of skills, thus increasing the probability of eventual ascension into senior ranks. However, it could also represent a distancing from central management and years that are lost for lobbying existing senior management. Long term study of IS personnel reaching into senior management might profit from considering the impact of overseas posting on the probability of IS personnel reaching senior management ranks.

Research methods. A number of research strategies can be employed for testing the robustness of these general expatriate literature theories in the context of MIS personnel. For example, case studies following IS personnel over time may observe in great detail which of the postulated variables have an impact in particular settings and whether stated contingencies capture the appropriate constructs for understanding and predicting successful IS adjustment to overseas assignments. Additionally, data gathered through surveys of expatriates both within and outside of IS functional areas, can examine whether the experiences of these managers differ in critical areas. Using the survey method, adjustment may have to be made for other factors, such as organizational size and structure, prior experiences and knowledge, and particular assignments, so analysis can be attempted to account for other potential influences besides functional area.

## 5. Applying the current state of cross-culture knowledge to aid the IS expatriate now

There is a large and growing literature regarding cultural differences as applied to learning, conflict resolution and communication. These can be helpful to MIS managers in guiding work and policies where people of different cultures must work together. This literature generally presents contingency models (based on the expatriate work role, time to be spent overseas, the cultural distance between home and assigned country) and increasingly sophisticated levels of training for the overseas role. For example, training for assignments that are shorter, more technical and in countries with more similar cultures (e.g. USA, UK and Australia; Portugal and Brazil) may be satisfied with background information about the history, politics, and customs of the country to be visited. On the other hand, training for assignments that are longer, involve higher levels of communication, and in countries with more dissimilar cultures (e.g. US and Hungary [Pearce, 1991]) may require experiential training along with background materials.

More generally, cultural understanding can play a significant role in the successful diffusion of technology across borders (Harris and Moran, 1987). In a sense, the development and deployment of information systems throughout an organization will involve transferring and inventing new technology. Stamper (1988), building upon Hall (1959) provides an anthropology based model for evaluating the cultural impact from the introduction of new technologies. Conceptually the introduction of any innovation into an environment holds the potential for changing the culture of those inhabiting that environment. Stamper presents a taxonomy of 10 categories representing a rough accumulation of potential cultural impacts. He also proposes a method for assessing the impacts (and cascading secondary and tertiary impacts) that may derive from that innovation. Although the concept is primarily illustrated with non-IS examples, the point is made that this technique can be used in assessing possible impacts from introducing new IS technology. The method does not guarantee that all cultural impacts from introducing the new technology will be predicted, but rather asserts that a broader knowledge of the cultural impact will be obtained by using this method. No rigorous testing of this method is presented, but it has reasonable face validity. The 10 categories are listed in Appendix 1.

Cross-cultural studies also provide insights into improving global information systems in the area of decision-making where people from multiple cultures are involved. Harris and Moran (1987) present a psychological model of cross-cultural decision-making based on attribution theory. They propose that individuals develop values or ways of perceiving and acting within a cultural framework that create a tendency for individuals raised within the same framework to have similar values and for individuals raised in different cultures to have different values. Values come into play when we observe behaviours from people of a different background and try to understand that behaviour. We tend to interpret those actions based on our own values, when they may have a very different meaning based on the values of the other person. The extent to which we base these interpretations upon our own cultural values varies with the degree of flexibility we show in clustering individual instances into categories (Detweiler, 1975). Harris and Moran (1987) suggest a five-step method based on the premise of needing to resolve, or at least communicate about these different values, for problem solving among people of different cultures (the five steps are listed in Appendix 2).

Both models are general and can be applied in the IS area in a number of ways: the Stamper/Hall model can help us anticipate (and hopefully prevent or reduce) problems when introducing new technologies; the Harris and Moran model can be applied among members of work teams that include people of diverse cultures (perhaps even between IS and end-users).

## 6. Related multi-cultural issues

Although this paper has been oriented largely towards Americans going abroad, cross-border travel goes in multiple directions. Significant numbers of non-US born students remain in the USA following technical education and take up posts in local industry, commerce and government. Similarly, there are growing numbers of technically qualified people from South-east Asian countries who remain in Australia following study there, and increasing movements of people within the European Community following the implementation of the ‘Europe 1992’ legislation. Insights gained in studying IS expatriate personnel, particularly issues of cultural adjustment, may be applicable in settings comprised of individuals who are all permanently resident in the local country but have cultural and national origins from many diverse locations.

In a series of articles, Couger and colleagues (1986, 1989, 1992) compared attitudes of IS professionals of different countries. One study (Couger, Borovits and Zviran, 1989) compared IS professionals in the USA to their Singaporean and Israeli counterparts. The study indicated significant differences on several dimensions. Singaporean IS professionals rated their jobs lower than Americans on four dimensions (skill variety, task significance, autonomy and feedback from the job). Israelis also rated task significance lower, but rated feedback from the job significantly higher than Americans. This might be explained by IS work differing between countries; by people in different countries perceiving differently essentially the same work; or by some combination of these. Both Israeli and Singaporean IS professionals rated satisfaction with pay/benefits significantly lower than did US counterparts. This could signal different pay rates, different expectations among workers, or different labour supply curves in different countries. Israelis also indicated higher levels of goal clarity and feedback on goal accomplishment and lower levels of goal difficulty. In another study contrasting IS workers in six Pacific Rim countries (Couger, 1992), Australians showed higher levels than American employees on both growth-need strength and job-motivating capacity; however, respondents from Hong Kong, the People's Republic of China (PRC), Singapore, Taiwan, and Thailand all showed significantly lower levels of job-motivating capacity and both the PRC and Thailand showed significantly lower levels of growth-need strength among employees.

These findings suggest important new questions regarding the degree to which IS jobs and personnel differ across borders. To the extent that these effects are due to differences in the jobs themselves, then IS expatriates need to be prepared for either adapting to changes (perhaps subtle changes) in their overseas work or to rearranging their work environment. To the extent that these effects are due to differences in the employees (whether resulting from culture, training or some other source) IS personnel need to be prepared for operating in new ways (or in spite of new ways). Of course it is possible that there is influence from both differences in jobs and in employees (perhaps there is a feedback loop where employees of different culture or background are inclined to arrange their jobs differently which in turn reinforces different cultural norms) and so the IS expatriate would be wise to anticipate adjusting to both different jobs and ways of working.

To the extent that this literature relaxes specifically geographic boundaries and addresses issues in relationships among people of differing cultural background, it connects to the topic of cultural diversity. As pointed out by Cox, Lobel and McLeod (1991), 'The American work force is becoming increasingly diverse' (p. 827). They point out that large percentages of the people to be entering the workforce will include women and non-white men, with Asian, Black and Hispanic Americans and immigrants especially prevalent. Cox (1991) emphasizes that this growing diversity presents both challenges and opportunities. Challenges include the increased potential for misunderstanding and unresolved conflict. People of different cultures may hold dramatically differing assumptions regarding the pace and nature of work as well as the meaning and objectives of particular assignments. Moreover, their behaviour in new groups may not be predictable based on their behaviour in homogeneous groups (Adler and Graham, 1989).

As shown in Cox, Lobel and McLeod (1991), in at least some situations, people of different cultures vary in their tendency towards competitive versus cooperative behaviour. This diversity presents opportunities for utilizing the skills and knowledge of a broader range of people and for transferring that knowledge into sensitivity to a broader range of customers. It also offers the opportunity to challenge what Janis (1989) calls ‘groupthink’ or the tendency to escalate commitment to a homogeneous body of ideas without adequately challenging those ideas from other points of view. For firms concerned with adapting both to the global environment and to internal cultural diversity, there may be opportunities for developing programmes that address both issues, particularly in extending specific skills, such as communication and decision-making, to a broader range of environments and conditions.

## 7. Conclusion

Although knowledge regarding IS personnel and expatriate personnel has been increasing in recent years, examination of these literatures shows that very little is known about the intersection of these two areas: IS or other technical personnel assigned outside their home country. This lack of research presents an opportunity to test the robustness of current thinking about the expatriate experience by differentiating experiences of technical from general managerial employees. Also, with the growing density of transcontinental telecommunication and global enterprise reliant on information technologies, it may stimulate consideration for managing in an area while or before it becomes highly problematic.

A number of issues identified in this paper call for further study. It is important to develop a descriptive framework to better describe current practices of international transfer of technical personnel. How many technical personnel are currently working as expatriates? Does their selection and preparation for these assignments differ from general managerial personnel? If selection and preparation do not differ, do outcomes for technical and general managerial personnel differ? If selection and preparation do differ, are these differences systematic and effective? Research of these questions will require systematic data collection from multinational firms with personnel located in many countries.

A second, and fundamental issue, targets the measurement of successful performance by expatriate technical and general managerial employees. These measures would surely include quantifiable variables such as remaining in the assignment for the allotted time and fulfilment of pre-defined objectives, but should also consider long-term movement towards ‘globally competent’ staff (Adler and Bartholomew, 1992). Presumably it would consider costs (both out-of-pocket and opportunity costs) as well as benefits and would consider potentially differing interpretations by the firm and the employee. For IS personnel it will need to account for systematic differences between countries on how IS work is organized and personnel attitudes that result from the range of work environment, compensation and related issues. Dealing with this issue requires careful conceptualization of success in this context and the development of appropriate measurement instruments.

A third area for study involves the optimal mixture of domestic and expatriate personnel for a given office, project or task. To move in this direction will require (i) defining ‘optimality’ in this context which may differ across competing organizational goals; (ii) identifying factors upon which the optimal mix is contingent; and (iii) measuring the relative impact of the identified factors. Research in this area may require in-depth observation of various settings across industry, nation, firm, with a mixture of different ratios of domestic and expatriate personnel. The concept of equifinality suggests that in open systems there are multiple directions to the same goal — if different mixtures of domestic to expatriate personnel lead to similar levels of performance, are these at different costs or might they require different kinds of managerial adjustment?

More generally, a fourth area of questions implied by Couger's work aims at distinguishing between IS professionals working in domestic versus multinational firms. To what extent do multinational firms impose a culture on their employees and to what extent are they influenced by the cultures in which they operate? Do IS personnel significantly differ in job needs or perceived job motivating capacity based on the nationality of their firm?

As both international commerce and an IT infrastructure continue to grow, understanding the proper management of personnel in a global environment is likely to become a key factor for successful multinational enterprises.
