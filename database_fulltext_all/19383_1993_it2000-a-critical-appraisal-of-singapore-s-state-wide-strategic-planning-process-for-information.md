# IT2000: A critical appraisal of Singapore's state-wide strategic planning process for information technology

Christina Soh, Boon Siong Neo and M. Lynne Markus\* Information Management Research Centre, School of Accountancy and Business, Nanyang Technological University, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 2263

In April 1992, the Singapore Government announced its information technology (IT) vision and plan for the next 15 years to turn Singapore into ‘an intelligent island’, providing a strategic regional switching centre for expertise, goods, services and information. At the heart of this plan is a national information infrastructure that would capitalize on information as a key factor of production and an important ingredient for enhancing the quality of life of its people. Code named ‘IT2000’, the plan was developed by 60 senior managers at the National Computer Board in 1991 with participation and inputs from about 200 business and industry leaders, domain experts and academics. This paper describes the background of Singapore’s drive into the information age, the IT2000 planning process, the roles of various participants and the main recommendations of the plan. An evaluation of IT2000 is made in terms of its objectives, its unanticipated results and its impacts on industry participants. Implications and lessons are drawn for strategic IT planning at the national or sectoral level.

Keywords: information technology planning, strategic planning, national policy, information technology infrastructure

In recent years, the role of governments in stimulating national economic growth and in achieving national competitiveness has attracted increased attention. Scott (1982), for example, has concluded that the rapid growth of the newly industrialized East Asian economies, relative to the major North Atlantic nations, can be attributed in part to explicit industrial strategies, translated into technology and human resource investment policies. In the same vein, Porter (1990) has argued that governments can play a key role in shaping national competitiveness through policies that influence the determinants of national productivity at the industry or industrial segment level (such as demand conditions and firm strategy and structure).

If, as is widely believed, information technology (IT) has the potential to create competitive advantages for firms and to alter industry structures, then IT is also likely to play a significant role in the competitiveness of nations. As Porter (1990) has argued, the competitiveness of nations no longer hinges on comparative advantage in the factors of production, largely due to the ability of technology to overcome resource constraints. Thus, because IT can transcend space and time, it can, for example, enable distant countries with few natural resources to compete effectively for white collar jobs.

However, the ability to achieve such benefits from information technology seems to depend critically (just as it does inside individual firms) on a sound IT infrastructure, comprising integrated telecommunications networks, trained information professionals, and a healthy IT sector of hardware, software, and services providers. To quote a recent Business Week (1992) article:

... in the 1990s, building up a (national) communications infrastructure that can support the information-intensive industries ... is critical. A communications conduit made up of fibre-optic cables and high-speed digital switching equipment ... could have an economic impact such as the interstate highway system did in the 1950s and 1960s.

And, because many components of national IT infrastructures are public goods, national governments are likely to play a role that individual firms cannot in the formation and maintenance of national IT competence.

While there is consensus on the need for an industrial policy even in the traditional capitalistic economies, there is far less agreement on the content and feasibility of such a policy. Phillips (1992) notes that, in the USA, many conservatives are sceptical about government's ability to pick winners and losers, and others fear that the formulation of a rational industrial policy is impossible in the face of pressures from interest groups. We are aware of these concerns as we present the recent Singapore experience relating to technology planning. Many problems of national planning are reduced because of Singapore's small size and the absence of strong special interest lobbies and political opposition. Nonetheless, we believe that there are lessons to be drawn from the Singapore technology planning experience, particularly as it uses a sectoral planning framework which is becoming ‘more and more acceptable — and, indeed, fashionable’ in the USA (Phillips, 1992).

According to Scott (1982), the ability of governments to translate national strategy into effective investment policies requires non-adversarial government/business partnerships and governmental institutions that are ‘competent, committed, and able to wield (sufficient) political clout’. In this case study, we examine the institutions involved in Singapore’s IT2000 planning process and the tactics they used to create a national consensus, and we evaluate the effectiveness of their planning efforts to date. We enumerate the factors contributing to the success of the IT2000 planning process, and we discuss the implications for national and industry-level IT planning.

## Background and approach

In the early days of the IT2000 planning effort, Singapore's National Computer Board (NCB, the initiator and key player in the effort) invited a number of academics to join NCB staff members and senior managers from industry in a series of planning meetings, described more fully below. The academics were expected to make substantive contributions about the capabilities of IT and its application in different industry sectors. The first two authors of this paper were among those invited to participate. Intrigued by the opportunity to observe and evaluate a unique phenomenon, we designed a systematic data collection method to supplement our experience as participant observers. Since we had no involvement in designing the planning process, nor did we change its course of execution, we believed that we would be able to conduct a relatively unbiased assessment of the process, despite having participated substantively in the planning meetings.

The data sources for this descriptive study of the IT2000 planning process include:

\- detailed notes taken during participation by the first two authors in a total of eight meetings in two different sectoral groups;

\- structured observational notes taken by other academic colleagues who participated in different sectoral meetings;

\- press releases, publicity materials and reports associated with IT2000;

\- interviews with key NCB personnel, conducted by the authors throughout the planning effort to document the history of the effort and the reasons for its structure.

The main foci of this descriptive case study are the actual planning process, both structure and execution, and the outcomes of the planning process, both anticipated and unexpected. However, to set the stage for this description and evaluation, we first present the context of the IT2000 effort. After a brief description of Singapore, we discuss the history of events leading up to IT2000, and we describe the NCB, initiator and key player in the process.

## Context and history

Singapore, a small and relatively young island nation, is situated at the tip of the Malayan peninsula, 50 miles north of the Equator. Singapore's modern political history began in 1959 when it gained independence from the UK. Since then, the democratic nation has been governed by a single political party.

Singapore first began to thrive economically in the early 19th century, when the British recognized its advantages as a trading port. Since its political independence, Singapore has achieved impressive development of its manufacturing, financial, commercial and tourist sectors, despite its small land area (26 by 14 miles in size) and limited natural resources (Singapore imports almost all its food and water as well as other materials).

In view of these limitations, the government of Singapore recognized that national economic vitality could only come from trade and industry, and that the most important natural resource of the country was its people — a diverse population of 3 million of many races, with Chinese in the majority but also including Malays, Indians and Eurasians. In support of this strategy, the government explicitly pursued policies intended to maintain free markets, to attract foreign investment through excellent infrastructure (e.g. airport and telecommunications) and tax incentives, and to upgrade the education and skill level of the population.

Singapore's government plays a role in the nation's economic life that can be characterized as somewhere between the minimal role of the governments of Western nations like the USA and the strong central planning role that formerly characterized the governments of Eastern European nations such as the former USSR. Singapore's government has attempted to maintain a capitalist economy with few trappings of a welfare state. However, it is not averse to economic interventions. For example, the government exerts considerable influence on wage levels by setting wage guidelines in consultation with a council of employers and trade union representatives. In recent years, the government allowed wage levels to rise significantly as part of its policy of shifting the nation's labour force into ‘higher value-added’ jobs, e.g. those in high technology industries rather than in low-skill, low-cost manufacturing. The government also does not shy away from forecasting the future: it identifies areas of industry or commerce that may represent the next wave of Singapore’s industrial development and actively encourages companies to invest in those areas through such policies as creating tax incentives and funding institutions to train people in the requisite skills.

## Singapore's history of national IT planning

The Singapore government has always been keenly aware of the country's scarce natural resources, and continually looks for ways to leverage these scarce assets for economic growth. Of necessity, the government has for many years emphasized the importance of a skilled and productive labour force. In the late 1970s, the government initiated an economic restructuring process to move the economy into high value-added products and services. Wages were increased substantially for three years to force marginal businesses to upgrade or to encourage labour to move to other sectors better able to absorb the high wages. This policy recognized that while labour was one of Singapore's critical resources, it was also in short supply, given the small population size, and that the labour force therefore had to be both productive and effectively utilized. Information technology was identified as a key enabler of greater productivity, economic growth, and human resource development.

In 1980, a high-level ministerial Committee on National Computerization (CNC) was chartered to explore how Singapore could benefit from developments in IT. This centralized approach to setting and implementing policy has its roots in Singapore's post-independence history, when its early leaders found that it was the fastest way to get things done, given a relatively unsophisticated electorate. Over time, networks comprising civil servants and industry leaders have developed which add to the smooth functioning and rapid deployment of policy. Although significantly more educated today, the electorate is still largely content to let the government drive most economic initiatives, because the government is seen to be responsible for Singapore's favourable economic performance, and is considered to be relatively free from corruption.

The CNC made four major recommendations in its report, which was accepted by the government:

1. Development of adequate numbers of IT professionals.

2. Development of the computer software industry.

3. Computerization of the Civil Service.

4. Establishment of the National Computer Board (NCB).

The NCB was created in 1981 as the government agency responsible for implementing Singapore's IT policies. The culture at NCB reflects the character of its first chairman, Philip Yeo, whose response to the challenge of computerizing the government Ministries was to ‘move fast’, and computerize 10 Ministries ‘all at one go’. The NCB workforce, made up largely of young professionals, also tended not to dwell on how difficult the task ahead was, but rather ‘assumed that somehow or other they could get it done’ (National Computer Board, 1991). Today, NCB employs over a thousand people to run the computer services for Singapore’s government departments.

By 1985, NCB had made considerable strides in the areas of its initial charter — IT manpower development, software industry development and civil service computerization. However, NCB leaders felt that new developments in technology, in particular the merging of computer and communication technologies, gave rise to new opportunities for further economic development. It was noted that, although ‘Singapore has the components of an IT infrastructure and industry . . . they are not well integrated . . .’. Further that ‘IT is too critical to our future economic and social well-being for its development to be left to a fragmented arrangement with different agencies tackling separate segments’ (National Computer Board, 1985). Therefore, a second planning effort was launched in 1985 by the National IT Plan Working Committee, composed of representatives from the NCB, the Economic Development Board, Singapore Telecoms and the National University of Singapore. The Working Committee, which reported to the CNC, recommended a ‘new strategic framework’, noteworthy for its focus on the local IT industry, IT applications to support business competitiveness, and telecommunications infrastructure. These recommendations were accepted by the government as part of the 1986 Report of the Economic Committee, headed by the Minister for Trade and Industry. This report received much attention as Singapore was suffering negative economic growth for the first time in 25 years.

By 1990, there were several indicators that the policy of building national IT infrastructure and encouraging IT use had had some success. Examples that are well known to the international community include the well publicized case of Tradenet — an interorganizational system to automate the flow of trade documentation (King and Konsynski, 1990; Neo, King and Applegate, 1993) and the top ranking given by the World Competitiveness Report for Singapore's telecommunications infrastructure. Less well known but equally important, are reports that the number of IT professionals had grown from 850 in 1980 to 14 000 in 1991, that the IT industry had grown at a compound rate of 30 per cent between 1982 to 1990 to reach 2.15 billion in revenue, $^{*}$ and that responsiveness of government services had increased by orders of magnitude. These numbers suggest that IT policy has indeed contributed to national economic development and the quality of life for ordinary Singaporeans.

The ideas for IT2000 came together as NCB reviewed Singapore's IT progress in its first 10 years, and sought to determine the focus of IT development for the next 10 years. Their thinking was also influenced by the societal vision published by Singapore's new political leadership which emphasized increased economic prosperity and gracious living (Government of Singapore, 1991). IT2000 is, in part, NCB's response to this vision. As NCB's Chief Executive, Ko Kheng Hwa explained:

We seem to have a strategic long-term IT plan every five years or so, but this was not by design . . . it just seemed to happen that way. The plans were conceived to respond to new national challenges and technological opportunities. The IT2000 project was no different. There was no single flashpoint. We began thinking about the idea of a new IT masterplan to support Singapore's thrusts to become a developed country during our corporate planning sessions in early 1990. The scale of commitment to the project evolved slowly. However, over the few months, we were able to persuade senior NCB managers about the project. (Source: authors' interview)

## The IT2000 vision

Early press releases described the goal of IT2000 as the exploitation of IT to ‘improve business competitiveness and enhance the quality of life’ (The Straits Times, 1990). A major objective was to move away from isolated networks towards a ‘national integrated network’ (NCB press release, 11 February 1991). Later publications articulated the vision in more colourful and people-oriented terms — to turn Singapore into an ‘intelligent island’ where ‘IT is pervasive in the very fabric of society, where computers in homes, offices, schools and factories are interconnected to one another — and to the world’ (National Computer Board, 1990/1991 Annual Report, Chairman’s Review).

Like the previous two IT planning efforts, IT2000 was concerned with increasing the overall competitiveness of businesses in Singapore, and leveraging the scarce human capital was an implicit goal. However, IT2000 differs from the previous two IT plans in a number of ways, only some of which are evident from the above quotes, including the following:

1. There was more focus in IT2000 on encouraging effective deployment of IT in the private sector, whereas the first IT plan was focused largely on the public sector.

2. The IT2000 planning effort involved much larger numbers of participants, mainly business leaders from the private sector.\*

3. The IT2000 planning was carried out on a sectoral basis, in recognition of the various sectors' diverse needs.

4. The IT2000 approach was more ‘needs-driven’ or ‘demand-pull’, so that ‘actual needs’ would ‘drive the development of the necessary infrastructure’ (Source: authors’ interview).

5. The IT2000 approach was based on the recognition that an IT infrastructure that would support business needs could also be used to change the way people live.

## The planning process

This section describes several key aspects of the IT2000 planning process: planning for the planning process, project structure and selection of participants, the sectoral planning process, the reports of the IT2000 project, and the IT2000 effort as it stands today.

## Planning for planning

The IT2000 planning process was officially announced in December 1990 by the Chairman of the CNC, Dr Tay Eng Soon (also the Senior Minister of State for Education). By this time, however, the NCB had been planning for IT2000 for several months. One of the initial activities of the IT2000 Secretariat (the project coordinating team, composed of NCB staffers) was to visit US corporations and universities that were conducting large scale IT planning projects. They found no IT planning efforts of the scale of IT2000 and came back convinced that they would be charting new territory in designing the IT2000 planning process. An NCB director explained:

The IT2000 Secretariat engaged a major US consulting firm specializing in IT to help design the IT2000 planning process. However, the engagement was perceived as unsuccessful and was terminated. According to one NCB director, the consultant's proposed methodology met with 'massive disapproval', because it appeared to be a variant of IT planning methodologies readily available on the market, and the NCB already had many years of experience using its own, similar, planning methodology. The feeling among the senior NCB managers was that existing IT planning methodologies, including their own, were too detailed and too oriented towards data flows or budgetary issues to be useful in high level planning for entire industry sectors.

Eleven industry sectors were selected for the planning (see Table 1). All sectors chosen were important for the Singapore economy, but a few important sectors (e.g. defence) were excluded because they were not accessible, and others (e.g. oil refining) were excluded because they were dominated by a few large firms. According to an NCB director, ‘the tasks and competitive needs for coordination are much less’ in concentrated sectors. The sectors selected for the IT2000 process were those with many firms, where there is greater potential for ‘enhancing sectoral coordination and competitiveness through inter-organizational systems’. \*

The planning methodology that eventually emerged was really a broad framework rather than a cookbook. The project was organized into three major phases, each with required deliverables. Phase 1 was intended to generate the inputs to the Phase 2 sectoral planning processes: (1) the IT2000 ‘scenario’, covering Singapore’s national agenda and socioeconomic trends as well as developments in information technology and (2) an analytical framework for understanding the sectors. Phase 2 consisted of a series of meetings, by sectoral group, to identify potential strategic IT applications, culminating in a written report for each sector. Within a set of guidelines about meeting formats and sectoral report outlines, much leeway was given to the sectoral planning teams. They were tasked to produce plans that were to be actionable: the intention was to identify specific projects for subsequent systems engineering. Phase 3 entailed consolidating the sectoral reports and producing summary reports for the Singapore cabinet and for public dissemination. This final phase would also result in identifying common infrastructural requirements across sectors.

## IT2000 project structure and selection of participants

The structure of the IT2000 project is shown in Figure 1. The IT2000 Secretariat, the main coordinating body for the project, was a small group of NCB professionals, who designed the planning structure and processes and integrated the efforts of the 11 sectoral groups. Oversight for the project was provided by a Steering Committee, consisting of the chairs of the 11 sectoral groups and representatives from Singapore Telecoms, other government bodies, and universities. The IT2000 Steering Committee was chaired by Tan Chin Nam, the chairman of NCB.

The basic planning activities were conducted in 11 sectoral teams, chartered to identify strategic applications for a particular industry sector (see Table 1). Each sectoral group consisted of 10 to 15 chief executive officers (CEOs) and senior managers from leading organizations in the sector and a team of five NCB employees led by an experienced IT manager. Each group was chaired by a prominent CEO from the sector.\*

The IT2000 Secretariat gave considerable thought to the selection of industry and

NCB participants in the sectoral planning process. Industry participants were often industry leaders and, in many cases, were known for their visionary use of IT. An NCB manager explained why these characteristics were important:

Their [senior managers'] participation will lend weight to the plans . . . developed. They can help [NCB] mobilize resources and support for the plans. (Source: authors' interview)

It was recognized, however, that senior executives had limited time to devote to the planning effort. To keep the required time commitment to a minimum, the Secretariat decided that the sectoral groups would have only five three-hour meetings over a four-month period. Industry participants were expected to make their major contributions during the meetings, by helping ‘pick the really good and workable ideas’ for IT applications and to provide feedback that would help ‘to keep [the NCB planners] honest’.

To make this limited involvement of industry executives work, they assigned five NCB IT professionals to each team to (1) assist the sectoral group chair in facilitating the meetings, (2) conduct detailed analyses, (3) perform prework for the meetings, (4) write the reports — in essence, to do all the necessary staff work. These NCB staffers were handpicked by the Secretariat from among the ranks of NCB employees. None of them were full-time planners; they participated in the IT2000 planning process as an additional assignment concurrent with their full-time jobs as IT managers, systems analysts, etc. Those selected for IT2000 were among NCB's 'best people', able to 'understand technology and also . . . to interact with all levels of business people'. A member of the Secretariat noted that the ability of NCB employees to juggle schedules and work under pressure was tested yet again during IT2000 since 'they still had to perform their usual duties at the ministries or at headquarters' and, even though the IT2000 effort was supposed to be 'part-time', it:

... took up much of their time and energy. During that period it was not uncommon to hear complaints from team members that other aspects of their lives, including family time, were being sacrificed. (Source: authors' interview)

## The sectoral planning process

Table 2 summarizes the sectoral planning process for one of the groups we observed, including the work performed by the NCB staffers and industry participants prior to the meetings as well as the meeting agenda. While there was some variation across the sectoral groups, our interviews suggest that they followed a similar pattern.

The sectoral support team began by performing a ‘functional decomposition’ of the key tasks, information requirements, and information flows in the firms in their sectors.\* They collected data through observation, interviews, and review of documents from several sector companies. The findings were documented in diagrams reviewed by managers in sector firms. This exercise served several purposes; most immediately, it gave the NCB support team a thorough understanding of the sector and helped them probe and interpret industry participants' reactions to the proposed strategic IT applications discussed at the

Table 2 shows that the meetings tended to follow the pattern of industry members reacting to materials prepared and presented by NCB staffers. The NCB team expended a great deal of effort between meetings in preparing interim and final reports, such as a report on industry trends, an outline of potential IT applications and IT trends, a feasibility study of the suggested IT applications, and the final sectoral report. These reports were used to inform the industry participants of NCB's broad planning framework (indeed, much report material was geared towards the requirements of the final sectoral report), and to initiate discussion at the sectoral meetings. The outline of potential IT applications prepared by the NCB support team drew on published material on IT applications abroad, and on suggestions from business managers during preliminary interviews. During the sectoral meetings, the industry participants also suggested additional applications. However, the major contribution of industry participants, made during the meetings, was a high-level, practice-relevant perspective on the strategic IT applications most appropriate for their sector. The participants ranked the applications, and only a portion of the potential applications initially listed made it into the final report. They also validated a statement of industry trends and commented on feasibility analyses of the proposed applications.

One drawback of this sectoral meeting process was that time available for discussion was limited by the amount of material to be covered: presentation of report contents took up at least half of the total meeting time. In the sectoral group observed, the second meeting was particularly low on group participation. The busy executives had not had time to read carefully the 66-page report on potential strategic IT applications that the NCB staffers had distributed prior to the meeting, and the staffers appeared disappointed at the lack of feedback they received. Diagnosing the problem as one of information overload, they decided to give the executives more time to digest the report and revisited the material at the next meeting. Similar problems were found in the other sectoral group we observed.

## The IT2000 reports

The final product of the sectoral planning process was a report, from each sectoral group, in a common outline, covering:

• overview and economic profile of the sector;

\- the current IT application portfolio of the sector;

\- the impact of industry and IT trends worldwide;

\- national aspirations and a vision for the sector;

\- descriptions of potential strategic IT applications;

\- descriptions of the major infrastructure issues that need to be addressed before the proposed strategic applications can be successfully implemented.

The strategic applications listed in the sectoral reports were not subjected to formal cost-benefit analyses at this stage. There was some discussion of their technical and operational feasibility and the major benefits expected during the sectoral meetings. However, formal cost-benefit analyses would only be conducted as NCB identified and negotiated with industry partners for each project.

It was then up to the IT2000 Secretariat and the leaders of the NCB sectoral support teams to consolidate the sectoral reports into an overall summary and recommendations. The most important recommendation of the final report called for the creation of a National Information Infrastructure (NII). The NII, described at a relatively high level, consisted of the three components of ‘conduit’, ‘content’ and ‘compute’:

Conduit refers to the physical ‘pipelines’ that carry information. Examples of such pipelines include voice and data lines, broadcast and cellular transmission. Content refers to information that flows through the conduit. Examples of such information include multimedia courseware, entertainment programs, government database records and payment instructions. Compute refers to the processing of content in the NII. Examples of such processing include user authentication, billing, and processing of permit documents. The three components of the NII are under the purview of several government agencies and involve the private and public sectors. (National Computer Board, 1992a, p. 23)

The NII is intended to turn Singapore into a more efficient switching centre for goods, services, capital, information and people, and to contribute to the competitiveness of every sector of the economy, particularly those where information is a key factor of production. NII is also expected to affect the private lives of individuals by providing opportunities for lifelong learning, extending the ‘personal reach’ of Singaporeans, creating electronic communities of people with similar interests, and increasing individuals’ leisure time and opportunities through increased efficiency in performing essential tasks.

The report also specified the key organizations to be involved in the development of the NII: a steering committee, Singapore Telecoms, Singapore Broadcasting Corporation, Project Specification Teams (NCB Application Groups in partnership with the relevant private and public organizations), and a newly formed NII Group within NCB.

Previously, in early September 1991, a more detailed version of this report had been presented to the cabinet for endorsement and approval. Later that month at NCB's 10th anniversary celebrations, Prime Minister Goh announced that the government had accepted the IT2000 report. While the cabinet report was not available for our inspection, interviews with NCB executives involved in preparing the report revealed at least three significant differences between the cabinet report and the report made available to the public. These differences concerned the discussions of strategic applications, 'soft' infrastructure and popular implications.

First, unlike the public report, the cabinet report included a list of 60 strategic applications derived from the 11 sectoral reports. An NCB director explained that the applications were not included in the public report in order to avoid raising unrealistic expectations — not all 60 applications could be developed at once, and detailed system engineering might prove some of them to be unfeasible. The applications are aimed at raising the overall productivity of the sector and are not meant to increase the competitiveness of any one particular firm vis-à-vis that of another in the sector. One example of such an application is the intelligent central distribution centre proposed by the wholesale/retail/distribution sectoral group. This application aims to reduce the processing and distribution inefficiencies inherent in the current situation where many retailers deal with many suppliers. The sectoral group envisions a fully automated centralized distribution centre, through which the flow of all types of goods from the many suppliers to the many retailers will be efficiently managed with the aid of integrated stock management systems, automated route planning and EDI systems.

Second, the cabinet report contained greater discussion of what the public report calls the 'soft' infrastructure of IT2000. 'Soft' infrastructure includes such necessary supports for the NII as a legal and regulatory framework for the protection of intellectual property, privacy and security and for multi-lingual access to NII. Given the conservative social values prevailing in Singapore, it is likely that the cabinet report discussed controls on the dissemination of pornography and other material that might be construed as undermining Asian values, racial harmony or the authority of the ruling party. The Singapore government has often made it clear that the cultural values transmitted through state-of-the-art IT must be appropriate to local conditions:

While it is often useful to learn from the experiences of other countries, Singapore has to make its own choices on the basis of its evolving national values and traditions. (National Computer Board, 1992a, p. 29)

Third, the cabinet report probably contained lesser emphasis than the public report on the popular implications of the NII. Early press announcements and NCB materials make it clear that the initial focus of IT2000 was primarily on increasing national competitiveness through industrial use of IT. Indeed, the major concern during the sectoral planning meetings was to identify strategic applications useful to companies in the sectors. However, when the Prime Minister endorsed the IT2000 plan in September 1991, he gave particular emphasis to making IT accessible to the average citizen. Following the Prime Minister's announcement, the vision of IT2000 articulated at NCB was broadened to include a new emphasis on the private individual's use of IT and its implications for family and social life. The new emphasis was reflected both in the final public report (National Computer Board, 1992a) and in NCB's subsequent plans for implementing the IT2000 vision. In our interviews, NCB executives frequently discussed the NII in terms of personal services such as ‘edutainment’. One director observed that, as a result of IT2000, NCB might add to its current ‘competencies’ in bringing IT to the government sector and to private industry the competence of ‘bringing IT to the people’.

## IT2000 today

Cabinet acceptance of the IT2000 report almost immediately set into motion a number of structural changes to support the implementation of IT2000 recommendations. One change reconstituted the CNC, the high ranking IT policy body that had recommended the formation of NCB 10 years previously, into the National Information Technology Committee (NITC). The committee received a new charter and membership as well as a change of name — reflecting the importance to NII of ‘conduit’ and ‘content’ as well as of ‘compute’. NITC’s new charter is to:

Provide policy guidance in addressing social, political, cultural and economic issues arising from NII implementation (National Computer Board, 1992b).

The importance of ‘conduit’ is signalled by the inclusion of the Singapore Broadcasting Corporation and the Ministry of Communications as new committee members. Another new member — the Ministry of Information and the Arts, whose portfolio includes information dissemination and censorship — will oversee NII’s ‘content’.

Organizational changes also occurred within the NCB. A new division was formed — the National Information Infrastructure Division — with two main departments: (1) the Planning and Infrastructure Department (incorporating what was formerly the Planning Department), and (2) the IT Application and Promotion Department (incorporating the former IT Application Division). A new director was appointed to head each of the two new departments, and both now report directly to the Chief Executive of NCB. The new division is charged with spearheading the implementation of IT2000; it is responsible for IT ‘master planning’,\* for IT policy analysis, and for preparing detailed specifications for the 60 strategic IT applications identified in the sectoral planning process, and for promoting the use of IT in both businesses and homes.

Within the Planning and Infrastructure Department, a team has recently been formed to articulate in greater detail 'What constitutes the NII' (Source: authors' interview). A major area of discussion within the group concerns the telecommunications layer: 'What technologies will be best for the nation?' To date, they have few answers, but they have established some guidelines for identifying a solution: (1) the NII must be an 'open' system, (2) it must be secure, and (3) the services available through it to individuals and organizations must be affordable. A second area of discussion deals with the enormous software development requirements of the IT2000 strategic applications. Efficiency and flexibility considerations suggest an object-oriented approach with reusable objects and code. But interfacing new systems developed via this approach with the numerous existing data repositories is a new technological frontier, and practical resource constraints prohibit the complete abandonment of existing databases. NCB planners began by tackling this issue at a rather high conceptual level; they are asking, for example, 'What are the "objects" in a national object model of

Singapore?' The director in charge of this effort frankly admits that his team is unsure whether and how this approach will work, and that it may be five years or more before there are any concrete results.

Since the release of the IT2000 report, NCB has been working closely with a number of partners from industry and research institutions to develop a working model of the NII. In August 1993, these industry and academic partners were invited to watch a 'proof of concept' demonstration of some of the NII components. A number of prototypes of selected NII core applications, such as a procurement system and telemedicine were demonstrated. The team acknowledged that they are still some way from implementation on a national scale as they have not yet found good solutions to the problems of scalability, compatibility and security.

Within the IT Application and Promotion Department, work has begun on drawing up specifications for a number of strategic IT applications identified in the sectoral planning process. While announcing IT2000 plans in April 1992, the Chairman of NCB indicated how the initial development projects would be selected:

Focus will be given to applications that exploit currently available telecommunication infrastructure and information technology such as electronic data interchange, Teleview, and ISDN. Approval for each project will be considered following the feasibility study. We will also experiment with the application of advanced technology and the high speed computing networks such as broadband ISDN and wireless computing. (The Straits Times, 2 April 1992)

It should be noted that, while the IT Application and Promotion Department will develop the specifications for the strategic applications, the expectations are that NCB may not build them; NCB directors recognize that they add value, not by in-house development, but by integrating the efforts of vendors and other external experts:

We can't do everything. We are like a giant systems integrator — getting people with different expertise to work on different aspects of the overall plan. We will coordinate their work and integrate them into an entire whole.

(Source: authors' interview)

Many of the initiatives of the NII Division are completely new to Singapore, and NCB executives recognize the need to import expertise from other countries. They are particularly on the lookout for ‘policy development type people, information technology architects (for working on the telecommunications layer), and people to work on specific technology R&D projects that will bridge the gap between what technology offers today and what the NII needs’ (Source: authors’ interview). A staff recruitment drive was launched in March 1992, and sources at NCB estimate that they will recruit 50 additional IT professionals. A significant number of these will be involved in planning activity under the newly constituted Planning and Infrastructure Department, as NCB intends to increase its planning personnel from five to at least 15.

In conjunction with the recruitment drive and as part of the overall effort to flesh out the IT2000 implementation plan, NII Division members recently went on study visits to IT vendors, universities and field sites in the US, Japan and Europe. The purpose of the visits was to learn more about how the targeted applications and technologies might be successfully implemented and to make contacts with potentially useful experts, should they be needed. An NCB manager explained that focus of this effort was to find what would work in Singapore and people who could make it happen:

We need to understand what's going on in certain IT applications in more advanced countries, and adopt or adapt what is useful to Singapore. We need to find the people that are already doing what we have envisioned and let them do it in Singapore. (Source: authors' interview)

## An evaluation of IT2000

Ultimately, the IT2000 planning process will be judged by its fruits: attributions of success or failure will focus on what is eventually built in the way of a national infrastructure of telecommunication services and the strategic IT applications and personal information services that depend on it. Building these technologies and systems (or failing in the attempt) will take place over several years. Thus, any assessment conducted today is provisional at best. Nevertheless, we can think of at least three dimensions on which a provisional evaluation of the IT2000 planning process can meaningfully be conducted now: evaluation against NCB's stated objectives for the IT2000 process, evaluation of the planning process itself, and evaluation of IT2000's unanticipated effects.

## Evaluation in terms of objectives

According to our interviews with NCB executives, the IT2000 planning process was expected (by NCB) to realize six major objectives. The objectives and an evaluation of whether they were achieved are discussed below.

1. Better understanding of industry sectors. The NCB support team assigned to each sectoral planning group developed this in-depth knowledge. As part of their prework, team members interacted closely with industry representatives in developing the ‘functional decomposition’ of the sector, in identifying key industry trends, strengths, and weaknesses, and in acquiring knowledge of relevant IT trends and applications locally and abroad.

2. Strategic IT applications by sector. Those involved in the sectoral planning process clearly understood that a list of strategic applications was a required deliverable, intended to form the basis of a new five-year agenda for the NCB, and thus the sectoral process was structured to generate and prioritize strategic applications. The result was the list of 60 strategic applications from 11 industry sectors included in the cabinet report.

3. A national data model. This objective was not achieved. Originally, the NCB Secretariat had hoped that the functional decompositions performed by the sectoral groups could be integrated into a national data model. This model was intended to show clearly the interrelationships in the dataflow among sectors and to help eliminate redundancies during application development by identifying common data needs. It was also envisioned as a comprehensive model against which individual applications could be mapped. It is now apparent to NCB planners that this objective was too ambitious given the time-frame and resources available for the overall IT2000 planning phase.

4. Support of industry IT champions. NCB executives believe they have achieved this objective. Not only were they able to identify promising champions for the strategic applications during the first phase fo the IT2000 effort but, in many cases, they were able to develop close ties with these industry leaders by working with them in the sectoral planning meetings.

5. Governmental approval of the plan. This was necessary so that the resources to implement it would be forthcoming. This objective was clearly achieved. Not only did the cabinet formally endorse the IT2000 effort, but the NCB has been given the go-ahead to add 50 new positions to implement its recommendations.

6. Widespread popular support for the plan. This objective was achieved through judiciously timed press releases and other public announcements. The Prime Minister gave the project (and NCB) a boost in credibility by announcing government endorsement of IT2000 at the NCB's 10th anniversary celebration.

In short, with the exception of the national data model, the objectives of NCB planners were largely realized by the IT2000 planning process, and on this dimension the effort can be considered to have been relatively successful.

## Evaluation in terms of the planning process

There were clearly aspects of the planning process that helped to achieve the planning objectives, but there were also certain characteristics of the process that, with hindsight, could have been improved. First, a consideration of the processes that worked well.

1. Broad-based involvement. Involving more than 150 business leaders from 11 sectors was a good way to build broad support in the business community for the plans that came out of IT2000. The main challenges in such a strategy were getting so many busy senior managers to volunteer their time and coordinating such a massive planning effort.

2. Sectoral organization of planning activity. This was a good way to cope with the heavy coordination load given that more than 200 people (including the NCB staff) from many organizations were involved in the planning effort. Planning within sector allowed the participants to address more homogeneous needs, and many participants already had professional or personal relationships with others in the sector, thus reducing some communication barriers. In addition, the sectoral focus allowed each NCB support team to acquire greater depth of knowledge about their assigned sector and, more importantly, to establish good links with the business leaders in the sector.

3. Support for planning teams. Five NCB staff were assigned to each sectoral planning group. Their role was critical in that they did all the research groundwork, meeting facilitation, and report writing. Their efforts enabled the planning deliverables to be met while keeping the time that industry participants had to spend to a minimum. In the process of supporting the sectoral planning groups, the NCB teams also acquired an understanding of the sector in terms of major trends, process and data flows, and key players.

Our post-planning interviews with NCB planners and an analysis of our observations of the sectoral planning groups also highlighted a number of areas that did not work out as well. Many of the problems could arguably be traced to the rapid execution of the IT2000 planning effort — six months from the time that the NCB support teams were formed and industry participants identified to the writing of the final sectoral reports.

1. Standardization of methodology and coordination among NCB support teams. Some of the prework was rushed. Partly because of the novelty of the planning effort, the details of the methodology were not fully fleshed out, and the process relied heavily on the team leaders' experience and initiative. At least one NCB team leader felt that the planning would have benefited from 'more effort on internal management processes and a consistent methodology across sectors' (Source: authors' interview). Had there been more time, the team leaders might have had more opportunities for 'cross-fertilization of ideas'. For example, one director noted that there was wide variation in the amount of effort different sectoral groups put into functional decomposition. Apparently, not all of the NCB sectoral teams 'bought in' to the idea of doing the functional decomposition; some thought that the effort would be too time-consuming in light of the many other tasks they had to complete for IT2000. As a result, the level of analysis was not uniformly detailed for the purpose of drawing up a national data model.

2. Depth of industry participants' involvement. Partly because NCB was concerned that the planning effort should not lose momentum by taking too long or requiring too much time on the part of industry participants, the sectoral planning meetings were tightly scheduled over a four-month period, and participants mainly responded to prepared material. During the scheduled brainstorming sessions in some sectors, participants were not particularly active. There are several possible explanations. Participants may have been inhibited by the presence of competitor organizations and preferred to take a wait-and-see attitude, or they may not have been prepared for the session, because there was relatively little time to read and mull over the background reports on industry and technology trends that the support team had prepared. While five planning meetings may sound like an adequate number, at least the first and last meetings were not focused on actual planning. Several participants came to the first meeting not knowing what to expect, and time was spent outlining the background of IT2000, the broad planning process and the deliverables. The focus on the last meeting was on finalizing the sectoral report.

## Evaluation in terms of unanticipated effects

The IT2000 planning effort also resulted in some learning that the NCB planners had not anticipated when they initiated the process. Analysis of our interview notes indicates a gradual shift in the NCB planners' perception of their role over the past year. In particular, we were struck that they now define themselves as 'system integrators' rather than as experts or as doers. Recognizing that they have neither the number of staff nor the expertise needed to implement the IT2000 plans on their own, they have accepted the need to identify experts wherever they exist and to coordinate the efforts of these experts. According to one NCB manager we interviewed: 'NCB's job is to understand our [Singapore's] own context, and then go out and get those who can implement'.

We also found that the NCB planners were considerably sobered by the enormity of the task they had undertaken and its attendant risks. As one NCB manager put it:

The more I think about it, the more wary I am . . . [we] are actually dictating how people will live. It [what we are doing] is really social engineering. (Source: authors' interview)

Awareness of this new role may have been fostered by the recent emphasis in IT2000 on ‘bringing IT to the people’ at home and in the schools. When asked how he felt about the ability of information technology to ‘enhance the quality of people’s lives’, another NCB director said that he was ‘feeling the weight’ of the responsibility.

Only time will tell the full story on the success of IT2000, but the project has realized most of the NCB's objectives, and it has produced some salutary (in our view) changes in their self-perceptions. On this score, we would provisionally evaluate the IT2000 project as largely successful.

## Lessons and caveats

What can be learned from the IT2000 planning process by other countries or industry associations that desire to engage in national or sector-wide IT planning? In this section we consider some of the issues that such planning efforts raise.

## Who should direct the planning?

The large coordination effort and the authority required to mount such an exercise suggest that planning be undertaken by a central body. Another reason for having a central agency, such as the NCB, undertake planning is that the most important outputs of planning are infrastructural, having many of the characteristics of public goods. The investment size and diffuse benefits of such investments tend to discourage private sector initiation. In the Singapore context, there are additional reasons (likely to be found in newly developing countries) such as the need to push the country's level of development and the relatively small size of local firms that might take significantly longer to adopt sophisticated IT applications. In more developed countries, it is possible that industry associations may undertake such planning efforts for their particular industries.

## Who should be selected to participate in sectoral IT planning?

The IT2000 experience suggests that, if the objectives include validation of IT proposals and support for future efforts to implement them, participants should be IT champions and opinion leaders in their industries. In selecting participants with these characteristics, IT2000 planners were counting on participants' ability to favourably influence their peers during the implementation phase. This strategy appears to be particularly useful when, as was the case in IT2000, the IT planning effort itself is novel and massive, when a suitable planning methodology does not already exist, and when the large number of participants impose huge coordination costs. The task is daunting enough without the additional burden of sceptical participants.

## What is the best role for senior industry participants in a sectoral IT planning effort?

In the IT2000 planning model, senior management were assumed to have little time available and were expected to provide their major input in face-to-face meetings by reacting to staff work performed by others. This model clearly has the advantage of making it relatively easy to recruit participants, who also get public credit for performing a ‘national service’. It is consistent with a consensus-building approach to strategic IT planning.

However, in our evaluation of the IT2000 we identified several potential problems with such a highly circumscribed role for senior executives. First, because IT2000 participants suffered information overload from the volume of information presented to them by NCB staffers, they may have missed an opportunity for more substantive learning about IT and its strategic uses in their industries. Since staffers are less knowledgeable about industry specifics, they are not in a good position to make judgements about what information to present and what to leave out.

Second, due to reduced involvement in the specifics of the plan, industry participants may experience little ownership of the final proposals. The potential negative consequences of lack of ownership may only become apparent when it is time to implement the proposals, especially if industry participants are expected to contribute funds and other resources. Clearly, finding a balance between substantive participation and manageable time requirements is a central issue in structuring the involvement of senior representatives of industry.

## Who should be selected to support the planning effort?

The options are full-time planners and IT professionals on a temporary, concurrent assignment. The IT2000 project followed the second approach, in part because there were not enough full-time planners in NCB to provide staff support to 11 sectoral groups, and the few full-time planners were needed for coordination and integration. In addition to minimizing the number of full-time staff needed, the IT2000 approach had two significant advantages. First, it ensured that the planning effort did not stray too far from operational reality. Second, it increased the number of people with planning skills distributed throughout the organization.

There are obviously certain trade-offs involved in the concurrent assignment approach. The part-time planners may be very overworked for the duration of the effort, unless they can be relieved of their other duties. Overwork may affect the quality of their regular work and of the planning support they provide. Even if quality of work is not affected, quality of working life surely will be, and the stress is likely to spill over into personal life. In the case of IT2000, the part-time planners received very good reviews for the quality of their work from industry participants and the Secretariat, but they complained about the effects on their lives at home. Looking back on their experience, most were happy to have been involved, because it developed new skills and gave them better exposure to industry. However, these personnel were considered to be among NCB's best, and the NCB culture embraces novelty, challenging tasks and tight deadlines. It is not clear whether people in other cultures would respond as favourably to such a demanding assignment, or whether even NCB could mount a similar effort in the future with the same people or a new high-quality handpicked group.

## How long should IT planning take, and how often should it be conducted?

The IT2000 planning effort was extremely expeditious: a mere six months elapsed between the first sectoral group planning meeting and the government's approval of the cabinet report. This speed ensured that the effort maintained momentum, and it enabled NCB to capitalize on narrow windows of opportunity such as that provided by its own 10th anniversary celebrations. It also supported NCB in positioning their report as a timely response to the government's published national agenda (Government of Singapore, 1991). The downside of such speed is that some of the prework may have been rushed, and the pace certainly took a toll on the NCB staffers.

Regarding the question of how often a nation should perform national IT planning, we note that Singapore has done so every five years, although this last effort was the most public and the most involving of people outside the government. Given the increasingly rapid rate of technology change, some might argue that planning should take place more frequently. But shorter planning cycles do not accommodate the time required to coordinate a planning effort on this scale, nor the time required to show significant progress towards implementing the recommendations of the previous plan. On balance, a five-year horizon for sectoral IT planning seems to us to be just about right.

## What is the transferability of the lessons learned in the IT2000 planning process?

A primary enabler of such a planning effort is the culture of collaboration between government and business that exists in Singapore. This has come about in a number of ways. First, government ministers have been leaders in the labour movement for many years, and quasi-government for-profit organizations, whose leaders are closely identified with the government, exist in many sectors of the economy. Second, IT2000 was similar to other strategic economic planning forums carried out in Singapore in one important respect: large numbers of senior industry managers, union leaders, domain experts and academics were involved together with government officers in intensive discussions over a limited period of time to surface critical issues facing the nation and evaluate policy options that may be needed. For example, before the IT2000 planning process, many CEOs and industry leaders met in committees to formulate a ‘strategic economic plan’ to move Singapore into a fully developed nation. Many CEOs who participated in the IT2000 sectoral planning process had also participated in other national planning forums with government agencies and officials. Nations without a similar collaborative planning framework between government and business leaders may find it much more difficult to achieve widespread participation of industry leaders. It is also likely that the time-frame for setting up and executing such a planning effort would be longer in countries where networks of influence and collaboration are looser. Where government-business-labour relationships are more adversarial, closure and consensus may not be so easily obtained in planning meetings as was the case in Singapore.

Nonetheless, many of the lessons of the Singapore experience are applicable to other settings, particularly when planning is performed within a sector. For instance, when it is initiated by a relatively neutral party such as an industry association. Even at the national planning level, the lessons regarding coordination through sectoral groups, the need for planning support, and the required time-frame are useful, although the context of government–labour–business relations might dictate adjustments to the scope of planning and the selection of industry participants.

## Conclusion

To our knowledge, Singapore's IT2000 national IT plan is the first effort of its magnitude in terms of the number of sectors examined and the number of senior industry managers involved. While it is still too early to assess its ultimate results, it should serve as a concrete model for other nations and industry associations that recognize the need to plan for information technology in the year 2000 and beyond.
