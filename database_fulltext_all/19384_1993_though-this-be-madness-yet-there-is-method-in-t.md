# ‘Though this be madness, yet there is method in’t’\*

Ian O. Angell and Bernhard H. Straub\*\*

Information Systems Department, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

The paper is a synthesis of the growing doubt and dissatisfaction felt by the authors about the direction that the study of ‘information systems’ (IS) seems to be taking. The first author has over 20 years’ experience, as an information technology/IS academic, as a consultant, and as a co-author of a large and commercially successful software package. The second author is relatively new to the subject, but is working full-time in a commercial IS environment. His views reflect the real uncertainties felt by many practitioners who put current IS theory into practice. Together the two authors confront some of the many methodical features that the discipline considers self-evidently true, but which they have come to see as bizarre.

Keywords: information systems, methods, methodologies

The practice of information systems (IS) is maturing, and hand in hand comes the establishment of authority. Led by a particular interpretation of the nature and role of information technology (IT), IS methods and methodologies $^{†}$ embody that authority. It is the context of technology and management, which has helped so much in establishing an authority in IS, that in turn has encouraged the development of these IS methods. But the good intentions, which are bringing this development in the study of IS to maturity, are not balanced by a recognition of the consequences that this establishment of authority generates.

It is, therefore, appropriate that attention is drawn to the way in which the early business successes of computer installations have generated and reinforced the platform of authority for applying IT, and have laid claim to a scientific legitimacy, from which to justify further action. On the basis of an unreflected bias — the belief in a virtuous technology — ‘utopians’ claimed that societies and organizations were moving into an Information Age. The computerized power of knowledge would drive a wedge between those who grasped this great opportunity and those who missed it, and the strategic use of computing would polarize the world into the information rich and the information poor. Large parts of the business community, and practically the entire IS community, have each used their self-proclaimed authority to exploit this assertion. By colluding, the authority of both communities is reinforced, extended, and enhanced, and this feeds back to make the claim even more compelling. Knowledge is substituted with a contrived understanding, which assumes for itself the ideological stance of a superior interpretative power, by way of an evolving consensus towards an explanatory framework that is grounded in the IS methods and methodologies. Against this background, the alliance of business and IS professionals has created the arena for methodologies. Thereby, what can be known about IS is replaced by what can be explained within the limited applicability of these systematic frameworks. Consequently, the basis for decisions shifts from ‘objective knowledge’ to a justification that is grounded in the consensus authority of self-propagating IS methods.

## Information technology in a management context?

As argued above, the concept of ‘information system’ is often understood to be a natural extension of an untarnished IT into the social and organizational context. Despite the growing number of failures of computer systems, and the manifest absurdity of ‘systems’ that confuse means with ends, form with content, functionality with solution, the proselytes of IT still whisper in the ears of businessmen that computerization really is a competitive advantage. For businesses are haunted by images of competitors gaining a commercial lead by introducing computerized IS. This undercurrent makes it easier to justify expenditure on computerized schemes — when everybody else is doing it — rather than to explain how they should invest, and perhaps why they should not. But it is ideological blindness, and not the reality of advantage, that is bouncing businessmen into these decisions.

It must not be forgotten that the computer is an electronic machine, and that its programs and systems are logico-mathematical in nature. Thereby, they all encourage a thinking in terms of the tradition of natural sciences. An ensuing history of technological achievements, including the development of the computer itself, nurture a scientific optimism that is apparent in the readiness with which the challenge of computerization is taken up. Such unbridled confidence has meant that, until the recent recession, IT has escaped relatively unscathed from the fall-out of negative side effects (such as environmental pollution and depletion of scarce natural resources) that have adversely affected the reputations of other technologies. Hence, IS professionals have often portrayed IS as the ultimate Taylorist dream, allowing technological rationality to enter business through the back door in the guise of ‘scientific management’. The build-up of an optimistic ideology leaves unchallenged the portrayal of IT as a controllable tool, in a vicious circle of the blind leading the blind. As IS professionals probe the application of new technology, not knowing whom they serve — the company or the technology — they simultaneously promote a peculiar form of ideological imperialism (Straub, 1991), and so their role becomes increasingly ambiguous, almost schizophrenic.

The sophistication of this new technology has prevented many from realizing that it furnishes neither a prescription for, nor even a proscription to, action, but merely a way of interpreting the commercial environment. Technological reasoning has insinuated itself into the business IS domain, and its legacy is a rigid framework of 'social engineering'. The pre-eminence of technological thinking in masterminding computerization has even led to a call for standardization on the inappropriate basis of the obscure opportunities of IT, with far-reaching effects on every organization. This has inclined to the creation of technical installations and procedures that ride roughshod over the subtle nuances of social context and that are completely unsympathetic to the organizations they are supposed to serve. Many organizations have consequently been burdened with an overdose of technology and bureaucracy. A case in point is the US Department of Defense standard DOD-STD-2167; computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools used by potential contractors actually reinforce the DOD bureaucracy and spread it into the contractor organization by creating, as a by-product, the 27 documents required by the DOD (Fisher, 1988). In any particular context these documents assume unquestioned authority, yet they are likely to be untouched by human hands, let alone by human heads.

## The arena of methodologies

Thus IT has entered the management context with an entourage of consequences that have increased, rather than decreased, the need for management. But why, then, is computerization such a compelling aspect of this ideology? IT is just another in a long line of technological enterprises whereby mankind feels it can subjugate nature by mere force of will. The sheer power of an all-pervading ‘optimistic rationality’ insists that things will get better, and that ‘progress’ is achievable through rational thinking, particularly when expressed as scientific thinking. Because of the ‘ideological thuggery’ of this dominant mindset, it was inevitable that organizations would find computer-based IS, and its baggage of systems and development methodologies, thrust on them by ‘intrapreneurs’ (Pinchot, 1985) and ‘corporate champions’ as the obvious solution to their business problems — and all with the highest of motives. Indeed, in many situations little thrust is required. With businesses and individuals so keenly promoting themselves as ‘successful through computerization’, others were equally keen to emulate such successes.

But individuals and organizations have been brutalized by this insensitive application of technology. Such excesses are not easily shrugged off, because personal and organizational destinies are tied to that technology. It is all too easy to forget that, unlike classical engineering, the relevant determinants of the application of this technology are not physical and deterministic, but societal and unpredictable. However, by incorporating information as an essential corporate resource which can be managed by means of IT, IS methods promote this technology for processing smoothly, systematically, methodically, scientifically, what otherwise would be chaotic, and thereby wasted. On account of this mirage of manageable information in the arena of methodologies, businessmen are seduced into accepting an equivalence between the functionality of computerized models and the behaviour of 'real problems', where control over the model is taken to be control over the underlying problem. For many this lure of control and of properly and efficiently working IS was too much to resist. It is not apparent without reflection that the 'answers' derived from such technical wonders are often inert—they neither reflect the nuances of 'being there' nor the consequent differences between the model and the modelled. Indeed, the model often takes on 'a life of its own'. What is then interpreted as reality, and therefore seen as important, is the contrivance of the model, rather than what is being modelled. The friction between this contrived reality and the business context is often an incentive to build even more 'sophisticated' IS.

## The ritual of systematic overkill

Profligate application of computers, using software, engineered to process people as mere data, and formulated to make decisions, has failed to see information as a basis for negotiation and compromise. Too often the response to commercial uncertainty is the ritual application of yet more computerized systems, displaying the bureaucratic tinge of IS methods, and forming rigid and inadequate structures that in turn lead to deadening conformity and repression. Yet IS are being challenged, not least by problems that they themselves have created. The mounting strain that IT loads onto organizations will lead to a major rethink about its applicability and the role IS methods play in this context. There is a growing prospect of a ‘wait and see’ position in respect to time and cost justification of new products, and a broader questioning both of the ‘self-evident value’ of IT products and of return on investment. Similarly, IS are no longer seen as the harbinger of organizational effectiveness. It is interesting to note that, in the latest recession, companies have been particularly badly hit across the whole spectrum of the IT/IS industry, from consultancy to computer sales — is this a sign of a changing business attitude towards the new technology?

The focus on ‘positive usability’, aggravated by a dangerous pretence of understanding business organizations, has precluded the acknowledgement of ‘negative usability’ of the very same technology. Negative side effects manifest themselves, but not necessarily within the framework of applying the technology; many problems lie outside the immediate scope of the frameworks. The systemic nature of business situations challenges their systematic interpretation within the limits of method, because unforeseen and unintended effects are nevertheless causally connected to the introduction of the technology in spite of an undeterred belief in the possibility of achieving a ‘wish list’ (IS jargon: requirements) through the proper application of IS methods. Emerging effects can have negative consequences because of the reckless transcription of the technological concepts of ‘communication and control’ into the realm of business IS. This approach, perfectly valid and appropriate in the development of technology, is made without any adjustment for the changes caused by, but not managed by, the underlying positivistic reasoning as practised in an organization. For what IS methods claim as communication is merely contact and connection; and what they call control is merely the formulating of intentions and the precipitating of actions; this is a long way from ‘being in control’ of the consequences.

The optimistic claims that this is the dawn of an era of strategic computing must be questioned. It is about time that it was recognized that we have stalled in fourth-generation computers. Surely the imminent failure of the fifth-generation hype (Straub and Wetherbe, 1989) will stop the nonsense ideas about sixth-generation systems and beyond. For if we are honest we will admit that our methods are not working particularly well even with the third-generation systems! The real uncertainties felt by many practitioners are a consequence of the growing unease with the ‘self-evident truths’ of such classifications, which fail under any close scrutiny.

## Systematic or systemic?

As the idiotic 1980s give way to the no-nonsense 1990s, at least in the business world, it is high time to reconsider the trajectory of past, present, and future developments within the discipline of IS. So what should be stressed in the study of IS? Most of the issues are mentioned somewhere in the multidisciplinary literature. Focusing on one aspect or even several aspects of a situation is unsystemic, and at best systematic. A systemic approach would be to emphasize that ‘everything is of (potentially) equal importance'. But there are problems in being multidisciplinary, for given the disparity of sources there is much that is incoherent, even inconsistent, and it lays us open to the accusation of being 'Jacks-of-all-trades'. The question then is whether we IS professionals have done our best in satisfying the perceived broad technical and organizational needs, or whether we have merely succumbed to a systematic pursuit of answers? Checkland (1988) suggests that it is time for the potential of systemic thinking to be developed within the study of IS. He opines that systems thinking denies the possibility of coming to terms with a problem, for coming to terms with it would itself change those very terms.

The systematic nature of IT clashes with the systemic nature of IS. Efforts to systematize, with the use of ordering methodologies, face the chaos of feedback. Coping with this tension between systematic and systemic natures is a challenge which has to be taken up by the IS profession, where so far the general response is tentative dabbling with qualifying and marginal issues. A reluctance, both to break with the belief in the virtue of technology, and to question the power of systematic management, leaves many inappropriate methodologies secure and intact.

Authority sanctions methodology and vice versa. Tidiness in method is encouraged, because it is an option on scientific and professional respectability, which in turn is a means of forging identity and conformity among practitioners. The technological pedigree of the study of IS supports this orientation toward tidy, rational, systematic efforts. However, untidy consequences proliferate in a truly systemic fashion. The authority of technological communication and control is usurped by the organismic consequences of hierarchy and emergence that are evident in all active systems.

## Authority and methodology

In a climate of confidence in progress through technology, developments in IS have tried to meet technical and organizational goals. And vice versa, organizations have tried to understand IS in order to apply them profitably. The meeting ground is the methods and methodologies for the development of IS. Over the past two decades, methods have evolved rapidly (Hirschheim, 1985), with rationales ranging from those that are strongly technical to those with a strong organizational orientation — with, in between, some esoteric mixtures and eclectic varieties. Yet only the most powerfully supported methods and systems have survived. It has led many to believe that these methods have acquired authority because of their quality, when in fact the opposite interpretation is just as convincing. The authorities of technical, social, and organizational expertise have hammered out between themselves a set of methods that serve their particular purposes. Some have become successful simply because they have been produced within an authority, and that fact in itself is enough — the full backing of that authority may not even be necessary (e.g. Unix). These approaches thus create and convey a position of authority for themselves, without any accountability. The original authority may even find it difficult to replace these approaches, as defence mechanisms will develop around them within the user community (e.g. DOS and Cobol).

There has been rapid expansion of computer consultancy companies based on the claim to authority of their own exclusive methodology. But now these ‘methodologies’ are showing their age, and their monolithic structures prevent them from being extended in line with present-day requirements and practice.

In the UK, the Structured Systems Analysis and Design Method (SSADM) (Downs et al., 1988) backed by the government's Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency (CCTA) demonstrates another, but slightly different scenario. The CCTA was created to oversee the use of computing in government agencies, but in a fit of technological determinism and bureaucratic self-fulfilment, and with the best of intentions, it helped in the formulation of a ‘right way’ for these agencies to develop computer projects — the SSADM method. Thus the CCTA became a stakeholder in SSADM; thereby, bureaucratic certainty and a ‘will to systematize’ replaced critical independence. They have now found themselves involved in the promotion of what is to many of its critics a highly questionable approach, and the control mechanism is subsumed into what it is supposed to control. Perhaps the same criticism can be made of DOD-STD-2167?

We really must expand on our understanding of technology as the basis of many IS methods. In combination with information, technology is usually embodied as computers and telecommunications. Taken alone, however, it characterizes an approach that stresses the objective aspect of the human predicament. This approach treats causes and effects as possible inputs and outputs of a 'black box', while social influences merely manipulate the inputs and mitigate its outputs. By contrast, a sociological approach stresses the interpretative aspect of the human predicament, and causes and effects are themselves in constant within a system of social interaction. Technical influences are subsumed into this social system by means of interpretation and ritual. However, neither technological nor sociological aspects are necessarily systemic. Both refer to systematic interpretations as they too are developed by some authority. But systemic interpretation, where 'everything' is of (potentially) equal importance', must not resort to such authority. Systemic interpretation is essentially anarchic!

We see methodologies as being ‘hijacked’ by technological and sociological authorities. Technological regimes stress the powers to store, process, amplify, transmit or filter information, whereas sociological regimes stress the importance of alternative perspectives, social dynamics or competing hierarchies. To care about IS, the IS community has to allow for more variety than either regime affords. Otherwise, the intended outcome of an investigation or a development of an IS merely correlates with the vested interests of an authority.

## Tidiness as a veneer of order

The focusing on ‘successfully working computer systems’ tends to result in various storage structures for data, and in transformations between the structures. The focusing on a smooth fit of social influences results in the imposition onto a ‘system’ of a structure of mediating agents and their views. In both cases, the underlying understanding of what an information system is, and how it should be treated, is responsible for the ‘syntax’ of the methodologies. This leads to phenomena where the use of a particular method will invariably lead to a particular prefigured outcome, whatever the situation (e.g. the large databases created with BSP, or a software estimation method such as COCOMO (Boehm, 1981)). Consequently, no challenge to the authority will arise. The general statement of the process for drawing up a conceptual model automatically produces a formalized approach, which in turn fosters the development of a comfortably secure bureaucracy, under the assumption that all is meaningful information. The ensuing surrogate method, which designates formal categories to observations, can actually become detrimental to the informal organization that surrounds it because formality and informality are not assigned contingently.

Invariably, some sort of ‘bubbleware’ (Straub and Angell, 1992) supports each methodology, and often a pictorial or pseudo-mathematical representation becomes an end in itself. A plethora of frameworks, dataflow diagrams, two-by-two matrices, ‘Weltanschauungen’, ‘magic’ triangles, etc., constitute the stock in trade of such methods and methodologies. Such constructs generally reflect the individual ideology (Straub, 1991) of their creator, and to a certain extent the collective ideology that is IT. But is it really meaningful to reduce an IS development project into neat categories of dataflow diagrams, two-by-two matrices and the like? It is widely accepted among practitioners that such constructs are much too tidy for them to cope with a messy reality. This tidiness cannot hope to represent the emergent non-linear causality of dynamic feedback. For there is a whole spectrum of particular biases driving the evolutionary process; non-linear feedback from the most peculiar and unexpected events in the product environment will be factored in. Just because many of the early IT developers ‘got it right’ with their original formulation of technology applications does not mean that their view of developments, ‘more of the same’, will maintain the present unanimity among users. In practice, professionals are required to negotiate the problems that arise. They have to negotiate problems that arise even from the very tidiness of their chosen methodologies, as they sprout new extensions when the going gets messy.

Nevertheless, tidy methodologies are being propagated. Not only do they support authority, but also their perceived importance is that they are assumed to give answers in an environment with few answers! Their usability is contagious, and the result is a readiness to adopt them in IS teaching and consultancy or in organizational procedures — they are of a ready mechanistic form that can be easily absorbed, and they thereby reinforce the authority of teacher, organization and methodology. The true expertise for those methodologies is retained by high-priesthoods, who develop the science of method to a point where they feel sure that few can challenge their grasp and authority. However, in the light of developments, a crisis of credibility is in the offing. Tidy methodologies, based on questionable and confusing theories, are difficult to apply effectively without great experience. Yet, by the elaboration of methodologies, expertise and experience are effectively separated. Under such circumstances IS are created that either depend on the often too simplistic understanding of methodologies, or they are all-out attempts at the ‘perfect’ IS. Both attempts are doomed to fail in an ever-changing reality.

Such developments are all the more surprising given the existing body of literature on systems, where issues such as complexity and multidimensional factors are commonplace. By adopting them into a method, these issues degenerate into a multiplicity of (numerical) variables (e.g. project management methods). In effect they assume that organizational reality can be represented and communicated by means of neat categorizing. Such clear-cut tidiness in methods is odd, where the reality in projects is so messy. This is widely acknowledged and most would agree that professionals expend a great deal of effort adapting methods to their projects and, worse, adapting projects to their methods. These methods are not supposed to be an end, but a means. However, it is not unheard of that companies invest in particular pieces of hardware and software, and in methodologies too, explicitly to attract graduates (e.g. the commercial use of expert systems) or to tie them in to the company (e.g. a proprietary methodology of a consultancy company). Means do become ends, and tidiness is forced onto an untidy reality!

## The return of untidy consequences

A purely functional perspective, a solution looking for problems, is driving much of applied computing. Design is seen as a matter of intent. In their methodologies, designers (bureaucrats by another name) include, and wish to impose on the user community, their sophisticated expectations and their ideological need for tidiness. Yet this tidiness denies variety. Designers can see only a snapshot of fragments of transitory ordered functionality and usability, in a sea of misconception. All their ‘achievements’, of course, are welcome, but there is a price. For each approach gives only a representation of a particular stable veneer that covers a more chaotic whole. But even the veneer is transitory, and in time, as that stability disintegrates, each design is prone to reinterpretation and quite often dismissal, and the authority that backed it is compromised and loses credibility. Users know they are working in operational environments that are messy and vague, much of it caused by the debris of detail in the unfolding history of a system and its environment, which lies outside the design. This debris can be reconstituted by a particular contextual significance and relevance, and thereby emerges as either an erratic and unpredictable nature and the potential of disorder or beneficially as the seeds of real commercial opportunities. But rather than minimizing the risks and maximizing the opportunities nourished by this systemic behaviour, many computer systems and methods are built on barren narrow intentions and simple preconceived goals that lead inevitably to the foregoing of emergent opportunities and to counterproductiveness.

In this context, methods should have the responsibility to communicate the systemic nature of IS. But by being limited to ‘bubbleware’, their influence is systematic and likely to blinker observation against the unfolding changes of IS. The consequences of these changes are the key to what any particular IS is going to become. Based on what an IS has been, at any particular moment, the system will develop into something different. The installation of a design must not be confused with system — and a method must be seen as adaptable, not ‘carved in stone’ (Checkland and Scholes, 1990). The behaviour of an IS is ‘new every morning’. Being competitive or cost-effective, or damaging today, can evolve into the opposite tomorrow. We must recognize this perverse nature of consequence, and reject the notion that a method forms a causal link between intention and consequence. It is, therefore, appropriate to assent with Nietzsche when he wrote: ‘I distrust all systematizers and I avoid them. The will to system[atize], shows a lack of integrity’ (Levy, 1911).

## Methodology: servant not agent

So management is a world of intentions, but business is a world of consequences. The task of IT management is to cope with the systemic manifestations and consequences of technological and organizational change. Against a background of commercial uncertainty, business decisions will require a broad-based and solid understanding. This stance must be more than just a token tribute to philosophical problems and questions of meaning. It is not good enough to respond to these questions with an anti-intellectual 'so what?', for that is a smug statement of complacency towards any questioning of cherished beliefs, and hardly the attitude that should be taken by a strategist in the face of profound uncertainty. To be strategic, understanding must be disconnected from the prevalent authority, whose legitimacy is anchored in its ideological stance. Hence there is a crying need for a reassessment of the approach to IT in IS, and of the problems associated with the expansion of technological systems as integral parts of organizations. As new technology is confidently applied in ever more unstructured environments, the unsuitability of its instrumental rationalism brings about more frequent and increasingly disastrous consequences, and less scope for management of change. The designers of grand schemes optimistically believe that, by mere intention, they can confine the consequences of using a computer installation to the achievement of a 'wish list' of their original goals. They fail to see that an evolving system is not the original installation, but everything that accrues to it — what it has become, what it will become, and not what it was intended to be.

However, standing systems and methodologies have, after all, become both the training ground for future generations of IS professionals and the cornerstone of the present study of IS. A radical reorientation has to take place to accommodate the changes we understand to be necessary. For with failure, the discipline of IS will decline into an ancillary service for management studies and the natural sciences. This lamentable and thoroughly unintellectual state of affairs should be of great concern. It is not good enough to teach tidy methods and to encourage their uncritical use. Already we see that computer systems are no longer the competitive weapon they were supposed to be (Hopper, 1990). The British Computer Society (BCS) programme for the development of ‘hybrid managers’ (Skyrme and Earl, 1990), as well as a means of reinforcing and enhancing BCS authority, is a consequence of the fact that the career paths of IT professionals are severely restricted. Do IS professionals want to suffer the same fate? Practitioners should think more about systemic issues, for otherwise they find themselves groomed to be the ‘grease monkeys’ of tomorrow’s organizations.

We are calling for a new approach, one that must relate to the consequences of technology, as embodied in the observed behaviour of organizations. This approach must balance the advantages against the limited applicability of computers and of their baggage of methodology, and be grounded in a theory based on experimentation and contingencies within the feedback of applying methodologies and standing systems, and on a sympathetic reaction to the disposition of the social and commercial environment. It must not be based, naively, on the belief that a description of the situation via models and methods will enable us to be ‘in control’. It is a delusion both to assume that we are in control of the application of IT, and to conclude by extension that organizations can be controlled accordingly. Even our vocabulary conspires in this self-deception. ‘Organizations’ are not totally organized; there is much that is disorganized and unorganized, even unorganizable; they are systemic and not systematic — and hence there is madness in the adherence to method.

But it is because of this very lack of control that IT has a major role to play. The future applications of IT must be as a means of enhanced communication and of meaningful calculation, so enabling organizations to maintain flexibility and to adjust tactical intention towards making short-term gains, while relating consequences to perceived strategic aims in the long term (Straub and Angell, 1991). It is, therefore, essential that we emphasize information and its communication among people; calculation should be used only where appropriate. We must separate information from technology; we must see information, not as a commodity, but as a consequence of context. IS must be continuously updated, based on learning from on-going experimental observation of behavioural feedback within each organization. This must be a major role for any methodology, but it is just not good enough to pass off fundamental systemic change as a backward pointing loop in the ‘bubbleware’, relegating it behind labels such as ‘rework’, ‘functional changes’, ‘enhancements’ or ‘maintenance’. A new attitude is needed if we are to overcome the widespread cynical opportunism in those who are promoting ‘certainty through information technology’, preying as they do on the
