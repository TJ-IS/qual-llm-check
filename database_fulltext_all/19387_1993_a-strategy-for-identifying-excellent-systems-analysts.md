# A strategy for identifying ‘excellent’ systems analysts

M. Gordon Hunter

School of Accountancy and Business, Nanyang Technological University, Nanyang Avenue, Singapore 2263

One of the key issues confronting information systems (IS) executives relates to the effectiveness and efficiency of their staff in actually implementing their organization's IS strategies. As a result of this and in the light of the current development and maintenance backlog problem and a desire to increase systems analyst productivity, a research project was initiated to identify the qualities of 'excellent' systems analysts. The project included 53 research participants from two companies representing five groups of individuals, or audiences, who interact with systems analysts. The data were gathered using Kelly's RepGrid technique and represent audience perceptions of what constitutes an 'excellent' systems analyst. Overall, the more important aspects that emerged from the data include communicate, attitude, knowledge, investigate, and experience. The detailed definitions of these aspects, as determined from the research data, varied among the audiences and reflected audience objectives. For instance, user audiences emphasized content-related aspects, such as knowledge. Alternatively, systems analysts and their supervisors differentiated on process-related aspects, such as investigate. Each audience has its own interpretation of the relative importance of what constitutes an 'excellent' systems analyst. Based on these research findings, the companies involved in the research project have revised their approach to hiring and training systems analysts.

Keywords: information systems, systems analysis, productivity

This paper presents a discussion of what constitutes the qualities of ‘excellent’ systems analysts. A relative differentiation of ‘excellent’ is determined from the research data. The data reflect perceptions of five groups, referred to here as audiences, which interact with systems analysts. The current problem with respect to the information systems (IS) backlog is presented. It is suggested that instead of investigating, again, the application of tools and techniques to solve the IS backlog problem, a consideration of the individuals involved could provide new solution ideas. These ideas will contribute to the development of a corporate strategy for identifying ‘excellent’ systems analysts. Therefore, a research project is discussed, which was conducted to determine a better understanding of systems analysts skills and personal characteristics, whether it is possible to differentiate systems analysts, and how the results of the above may be applied. The result of accomplishing these objectives will assist in the identification of the appropriate types of systems analysts for the organization. The research technique is described and the results discussed. Finally, the results are reviewed in light of the initially identified IS backlog problem.

## The problem

In many organizations there is a significant accumulation of incomplete IS projects, both in development and maintenance. According to Senn (1985), it is generally known that there is an excess demand for IS development. Senn estimates that this backlog of projects would take a number of years to eliminate. In addition to this identified development backlog, Senn suggests that there also exists a hidden backlog, which represents those users' plans that are not even submitted as requests because of delays resulting from the identified backlog. Furthermore, according to Hussain and Hussain (1988), recent statistics suggest that 50 per cent of data-processing budgets are allocated to maintenance. The backlog in development and excess expenditure on maintenance is a problem because business operations rely heavily on IS. An IS that does not perform well produces inaccurate and untimely information, which may detrimentally affect business operations. Within individual projects there also exist concerns regarding completion on time and within budget. Laudon and Price Laudon (1991) have identified some development projects that are as much as 30 per cent over budget and eventually require 50 per cent more time than originally estimated. Understanding the reasons for these circumstances, and identifying ways of reducing them, are clearly of concern to both the IS professional and the functional user.

The majority of research effort has been directed towards the development of tools and techniques. While this approach may somewhat increase systems analyst productivity, such an increase is contingent on the systems analyst knowing when to apply specific tools and techniques. This emphasis on tools and techniques is analogous to providing a sailor with a boat and sophisticated sailing equipment, while ignoring the sailor. Stated another way, if the objective is to improve sailing performance, which may mean completing a race course faster, then it may not be sufficient simply to acquire a well equipped boat. It may be necessary to develop the skill of knowing how and when to use the available tools and techniques and a fuller understanding of where to sail. With regards to IS development and maintenance, it is also necessary to be able to determine when and where to apply available tools and techniques. While training may initially seem to be an appropriate response, it is considered insufficient in this situation. For instance, the question of what kind of training to provide would still need to be answered. Furthermore, there may exist non-trainable aspects. Thus perhaps an investigation of the individuals involved with IS development and maintenance might provide further information regarding the resolution of IS backlog problems.

The research reported in this paper determined and compared the interpretations of relevant groups or audiences about what constitutes the qualities of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst (see Hunter, 1992). The audiences, depicted in Figure 1, were identified based on the anticipated characteristics of audience-member interaction with systems analysts.

Each audience interacts with systems analysts during an IS development or maintenance project and brings a different perspective to this interaction. Therefore, while the audience members would be in a good position to judge whether the systems analyst has performed well, the different perspectives may result in different conclusions regarding what constitutes an ‘excellent’ systems analyst. The identification of relevant audiences relates to Checkland's concept of 'Weltanschauung', which suggests that the description of any purposeful human activity system must be from some declared perspective of worldview (Checkland and Scholes, 1990; Checkland, 1981). Further, this concept purposes that multiple perspectives on a particular subject will always be available because individual, and audience in this project, interpretations may be unique. This project has gathered the interpretations from these audiences and considered them to be an important source because of their interaction with systems analysts. The difficulty in evaluating professional work, including that performed by systems analysts, is to obtain a consensus about what is considered ‘excellent’. Thus it is necessary to adopt a research method suited to measuring a relative concept when determining and comparing the qualities of systems analysts.

In relation to the problems outlined above, three research objectives were identified. The first objective for this research project was to provide a better understanding of ‘excellent’ systems analysts. The project gathered data about how audience members interpret what constitutes the qualities of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst. The second objective was to determine whether it is possible to differentiate systems analysts. While it may not be possible to identify an ‘excellent’ systems analyst, it may be possible to identify what contributes to an interpretation of ‘excellent’. A final objective was to apply the research results, through action research, to determine their validity as seen by the research participants, and whether there could be any potential benefit.

Galliers and Land divide their taxonomy of IS research approaches into traditional and newer approaches. It is interesting to note that they associate 'observations' with traditional approaches. This association provides a description of the traditional un-involved researcher in a researcher-subject relationship. On the other hand, 'interpretations' are associated with the newer approaches. This association suggests the kind of approach described by new paradigm research which, as described by Reason and Rowan (1981), is about how researchers attempt to make sense of human action and experience. As Reason and Rowan state, 'What we are building in new paradigm research is an approach to inquiry which is a systematic, rigorous search for truth, but which does not kill off all it touches' (Reason and Rowan, 1981, p. xiii). This research project falls within the domain of new paradigm research.

It is considered important to determine what emerges from the data regarding interpretations of the qualities of ‘excellent’ systems analysts. If a researcher approaches a problem with no preconceived ideas, then a framework must necessarily emerge from the data. This process of the framework emerging from the data is referred to in the literature as ‘grounded theory’. The major proponents of grounded theory, Glaser and Strauss, argue for grounding theory by generating it from the research data. They define grounded theory as ‘the discovery of theory from data systematically obtained from social research’ (Glaser and Strauss, 1967, p. 2). A category which stands by itself as a conceptual element of the theory to be generated ‘emerges’ from the data. A property is a conceptual aspect or element of a category. Both categories and properties are concepts indicated by the data, and are not actually the data. A major strategy used in discovering grounded theory is comparative analysis, whereby emerging categories and their properties are compared. As Glaser and Strauss state, ‘By comparing where the facts are similar or different, we can generate properties of categories that increase the categories’ generality and explanatory power’ (Glaser and Strauss, 1967, p. 24).

The theory of personal constructs was first proposed by George Kelly (1955, 1963). A psychologist, Kelly developed the theory from his work in attempting to help individuals analyse their own interpersonal relationships. Kelly regarded individuals as ‘scientists’ and suggested that for an individual to gain control of his/her environment, it was necessary for the individual to be able to interpret and predict (construe). Thus Kelly proposed that individuals, based on their experience, would devise a system of personal constructs which would assist them in dealing with current or anticipated situations. Kelly used his theory to identify and attempt to assist those individuals whose personal construct system was unable to help them construe.

A technique developed by Kelly to study personal construct systems is the role construct repertory test (RepGrid). While the RepGrid technique was originally devised by Kelly to assist in counselling his clients, the technique has subsequently been applied in a number of other areas. Some articles discuss the use of the RepGrid technique in general, or for specific problem construction and market research (Bannister and Mair, 1968; Corsini and Marsella, 1983; Eden and Jones, 1984; Eden and Wheaton, 1980; Fransella, 1981; Shaw, 1980). To attempt to understand what constitutes the qualities of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst, audience members were asked, using the RepGrid, to discuss their own experiences in dealing with systems analysts. This process allowed the documentation of each participant’s personal construct system in relation to the research subject.

Subsequent analysis and application of the results, then, was based on an accepted and proven research method.

To gather data regarding the qualities of ‘excellent’ systems analysts, interviews were conducted with research participants at two fieldwork locations. The interview was based on Kelly’s RepGrid technique. Extensive interview notes were taken, which provided descriptive support for each construct in the RepGrid. The RepGrid technique served as the vehicle to facilitate the elicitation of participant comments, which, in turn, became the detailed interview notes. As with most qualitative research of this kind, the problem of how to organize and analyze the interview notes had to be resolved. The remainder of this section describes the resolution of this problem.

Applying the RepGrid technique to the interview process resulted in data which the participant provided in support or ‘definition’ of a general statement. As part of the RepGrid triadic elicitation process, the research participant would offer a general comment about how two elements (systems analysts) differed from a third. This general comment would be pursued to obtain, from the research participant, a detailed statement about what was meant by the general statement. This process led to extensive interview notes. A paraphrase of the discussion regarding one RepGrid construct and its supporting interview notes has been reconstructed as follows:

Participant: These two systems analysts have good user rapport. The other

has no user rapport.

Researcher: What do you mean by ‘. . . user rapport . . .’?

Participant: The user feels more comfortable.

Researcher: Why does the user feel more comfortable?

Participant: Well, one reason is that this systems analyst determines and remembers user interests.

Researcher: How does he do that?

Participant: He's a good listener. This other systems analyst is not a good listener.

Researcher: What is another reason the user may feel more comfortable?

Participant: The systems analyst speaks in terms the user can understand.

Researcher: Anything else?

Participant: Yes, these two systems analysts take time to answer user questions.

Researcher: What about the other systems analyst?

Participant: He has no concern for the user.

Researcher: Do you have any other comments about how the user feels more comfortable?

Participant: No.

Researcher: What does it mean when the user doesn't feel more comfortable?

Participant: Well, when dealing with this other systems analyst, the user is intimidated.

The problem to be resolved then is how to organize such interview data for subsequent analysis. The data contain ‘laddered’ relationships which can be used to organize the comments in sequence from general to specific. Based on the above example, the following relationships could be extracted:

```txt
good listener
... leads to ...
determines and remembers user interests
... leads to ...
user feels more comfortable
... leads to ...
good user rapport
```

There were 2727 individual constructs documented in the detailed interview notes for 53 participants from the two fieldwork locations. Thus some kind of automated approach was essential. Because the data for each participant could be unique the use of generic database software, which requires the establishment of consistent data relationships, was eliminated. The COPE (COgnitive Policy Evaluation) software package (see Eden and Ackermann, 1989) was selected because it supports the documentation of hierarchical relationships that exist within the interview notes. COPE ‘can be used by social scientists in the analysis of qualitative data derived from interviews and documents’ (Cropper et al., 1990, p. 346). COPE implements the personal construct theory developed by Kelly and was developed to assist in supporting group decision-making processes in a business environment (Eden, 1989).

Continuing with the example introduced above, Figure 2 represents the COPE map of the interview notes. For this research project, a 'path' is defined as a series of related action statements leading to a RepGrid construct. Each path which leads to the RepGrid construct 'good user rapport' in the resulting tree-like structure has been highlighted. Note that COPE attaches a unique numeric identifier to each concept or interview note. In Figure 2 these identifiers range from 510 to 515.

Within the context of this research project, a ‘theme’ represents a general category for the concepts which form a path. The actual narrative content of each concept along a path was used to determine a category of the subject matter pertaining to what the participant was discussing. In this example, all of the themes happen to relate to ‘communicate’.

## Results

Figures 3 and 4, which present the data gathered at the two fieldwork locations, show the occurrence of themes by audience and a summary. The values may be used to represent the relative importance of each theme.

Analysis of the results indicates that while the identified skills and personal characteristics may be considered ‘universals’, the values which indicate relative importance portray ‘local’ interpretations. These local interpretations are supported by each audience’s detailed statements about each skill and personal characteristic. These comments are further explained in the following paragraphs.

First, certain universal themes emerge from both fieldwork locations. These themes relate to how the audiences interpret which skills and personal characteristics contribute to an interpretation of an ‘excellent’ systems analyst. Each company and to some extent each audience interprets ‘excellent’ with regards to a systems analyst relative to these universal aspects.

Second, local interpretations emerge from the data. Certain aspects emerge which relate to specific audiences. In general, the user-oriented audiences interpreted ‘content’-related aspects as relatively more important, while the systems analysts and business systems managers interpreted ‘process’ aspects as relatively more important. The aspects, along with the related themes, have emerged as a result of the respective audience goals and objectives. User-oriented audiences are more concerned about what the information system does (content), perhaps to support the activities of the functional area. On the other hand, systems analysts and business systems managers are more concerned with getting the job done (process), perhaps because their job is to develop IS.

Further, while a theme may emerge from a number of audiences and is considered a universal, its relative importance may vary from one audience to another. This implies that each audience will have its own interpretation about the relative importance of what constitutes an ‘excellent’ systems analyst. Also, there will exist a unique corporate interpretation reflecting its history and resulting corporate culture. These factors contribute to local values for the universal aspects.

To attempt to use the themes in some way it was necessary to provide a source for their definition. Since the themes emerged from the data, so should the definitions. Therefore, the data in the format shown in Figure 2 were reviewed in more detail. The result of this analysis provided a further categorization of each theme, consisting of several subthemes. Further, each subtheme was defined by the detailed interview notes. This process provided a source for the definitions required to apply the emerging themes by providing users of the data with common reference points. The subthemes provided a middle ground in support of the understanding of themes and a category to which detailed interview notes could be assigned.

## Decisions about systems analysts

Recall that the third research objective was to apply the results in a decision situation regarding the evaluation of an individual in relation to each skill or personal characteristic. Thus in the context of general decision-making theory, the list of skills and personal characteristics may be referred to as the decision criteria. Also, the relative importance of the skills may be taken to be the weights. Subsequently, ratings could be developed for the individual which would indicate strengths and weaknesses of the individual relative to the identified skills and personal characteristics for the adopted interpretation of what constitutes an ‘excellent’ systems analyst.

This description fits a model based on the concepts of Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA). The automated MCDA shell which was used is Visual Interactive Sensitivity Analysis (VISA), developed by Belton and Vickers (1989). The interactive nature of VISA allows sensitivity analyses to be conducted by varying the criteria weights to determine their effect on the overall results. This interactive sensitivity analysis supports the ‘fine tuning’ of the model for each specific decision situation.

One of the fieldwork locations agreed to use the research results to review a hiring decision made before the current research project. The decision-maker rated each candidate, using VISA, based on the skills and personal characteristics identified in this research. While the results supported the original decision, this process has led to the consideration of other decision factors (see Hunter, 1992). The decision-maker has revised and expanded the current process to incorporate the results of this current research project. The other fieldwork location decided to use the research results to attempt to develop a training plan for three systems analysts. Again the decision-maker rated each systems analyst using VISA and the data obtained in this research. Training plans have been developed for each systems analyst and the decision-maker plans to expand this process throughout the department. The fact that these two organizations have been willing to use the results of this research indicates there has been a contribution to a better understanding of systems analysts and how to differentiate them based on skills and personal characteristics.

## Conclusion

In general, ‘excellent’ can only be defined in relative terms. This situation is made even more complex because various audiences approach their interpretations from different viewpoints. This paper has identified the qualities which contribute to an interpretation or a perception of ‘excellent’ according to those individuals who interact with systems analysts. These identified qualities form the basis for a relative definition of ‘excellent’.

Examples of the most important skills and personal characteristics identified in this research are communicate, attitude, knowledge, investigate, and experience. While these example qualities are considered ‘universal’, there are also two ‘local’ aspects which emerged from the data. First, the weight of the differentiating factors varies and seems to depend on the audience and, to some extent, the organization. Second, the theme definitions differ among the audiences. All of the above, taken together, may be used to indicate how to differentiate systems analysts based on the identification of their skills and personal characteristics.

The fieldwork discussed in this article applied the research results in the areas of selection and training. There is a need for an organizational strategy regarding the evaluation of systems analysts in a number of other areas. Future research will investigate the application of the research results in the following areas.

## Assignment

An improved method of manpower resource allocation may be achieved by more closely matching the skills and personal characteristics of systems analysts with the task requirements to which they are to be assigned. For team-based projects, the combined profile of the systems analysts may create a relatively highly rated, composite, ‘excellent’ systems analyst.

## Career path

To provide the systems analyst with experience that would improve long-term performance, specific career paths could be planned. This experience may be gained through a combination of users, IS or project teams.

## Job description

Job descriptions for systems analyst positions might be restructured to reflect more appropriately the relationship between the skills of available systems analysts and the skills necessary to perform currently required tasks.

## Project team risk assessment

Each member of an existing project team could be evaluated in a similar manner to provide information about aspects on which the project manager should concentrate.

## Career counselling

If assessment of an individual systems analyst identifies weaknesses which may not be improvable, another more appropriate career could be suggested.

Finally, the results of this research may be used to assist in dealing with that aspect of the information management strategy (Earl, 1989) concerned with IS staffing issues or what Galliers (1991) terms the implementation strategy, which he sees as an integral part of an IS strategy framework. More specifically, it may be used to resolve the IS backlog problem outlined at the beginning of this article. The current response to systems analyst productivity has resulted in the application of a plethora of tools and techniques. Thus an investigation of the individuals involved in IS development has provided information regarding another important component of systems analyst productivity. To improve productivity, systems analysts should be more carefully selected and should be trained according to their skill deficiencies. The basis for systems analyst selection and training will vary from one organization to another. The approach which should be used has been described in this paper.
