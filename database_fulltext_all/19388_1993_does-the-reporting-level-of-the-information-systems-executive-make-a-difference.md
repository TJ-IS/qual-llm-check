# Does the reporting level of the information systems executive make a difference?

Bhanu Raghunathan and T. S. Raghunathan

College of Business Administration, University of Toledo, Toledo, OH 43606, USA

This paper examines the role of the information systems (IS) planner in relation to the organizational reporting level of the top IS executive. It is suggested that as the reporting level of the head of IS changes, the roles played by the IS planner change too. This notion is tested empirically with a random sample of 192 organizations. Results indicate that the IS planner plays an expanded role when the top IS executive reports at the highest levels. The implications of these results for the organization and the IS planner are discussed.

Keywords: information systems planning, information systems planner

The strategic role played by information systems (IS) in many organizations has led to an increasing awareness of the importance of the IS function and to the related importance of IS planning, which has been recognized as a critical factor in determining IS group performance (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Dickson et al., 1984; Niederman et al., 1991). The IS planning function and the role\* of the IS planner have, consequently, taken on new importance.

The purpose of this paper is to explore the IS planner's role in the context of the organizational reporting level of the top IS executive. The organizational reporting level of the top executive is chosen for study because previous research (Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1989a) has indicated that the IS functional head's position in the organizational hierarchy significantly influences and is influenced by the importance given to various aspects of the IS function in that organization.

## Importance of the IS planning function

The accelerated decline in the relative cost of information technology (IT) and the greatly improved power and speed of computers have helped to change IS from its traditional focus on providing support service (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; Ives et al.,

1980) and given it a more strategic orientation (Cash et al., 1992). Some organizations have begun to view their IS as a critical area of operations, with the potential to provide opportunities for exploiting information-based competitive advantage (Bakos and Treacy, 1986; Benjamin, 1983; Parsons, 1983; Porter and Miller, 1985; Rockart and Scott-Morton, 1984). The role played by IS is considered critical to the success of many organizations in the 1990s (Straub and Wetherbe, 1989), and investments in IS are expected to provide direct competitive advantage in world markets (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991).

This changed role of IS, as a result of changes in the internal and external environment of the organization, has introduced complexity into IS planning. If IS is to be used strategically, it becomes increasingly critical that IS planning is congruent with corporate strategies. In planning for new technologies, IS must have a long time-horizon and must anticipate the technologies that will have to be in place when the time comes for the company's strategy to be implemented. This becomes more difficult as the rate of technology change increases. Another problem for the planner is the increased uncertainty in organizational environments; he/she can no longer depend on the past as a reliable guide to what may happen in the future. These complexities in IS planning have made it one of the more important IS management issues in recent times (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987; Dickson et al., 1984; Hartog and Herbert, 1986; Leitheiser and Wetherbe, 1986). Leading IS professionals overwhelmingly ranked improved IS planning among the top IS management issues in surveys published in 1984 (Dickson et al., 1984), 1987 (Brancheau and Wetherbe, 1987) and 1991 (Niederman et al., 1991). Rackoff et al. (1985) have suggested that, to uncover strategic opportunities through IS, a new foundation for IS planning, based on a firm's worldwide competitive strategy, will be required. The importance of this issue is also evident from more recent empirical research efforts that have been directed at it (Lederer and Mendelow, 1988 and 1989; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1988 and 1990; Zviran, 1990).

This increased emphasis on the planning aspects of IS management suggests that individuals responsible for the IS planning function need more actively to initiate and manage the evolving role of planning in the changing IS environment. Rather than view IS simplistically at the level of efficient operations of systems and systems planning, they now have to think in terms of the strategic use of IS, exploring the types of new systems and new technologies that can be used to achieve IS-initiated strategic advantages. The IS planner's role is thus becoming an evolving and increasingly important one. Previous research (Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1989a) has indicated that the nature of this individual's job is an integration of different roles — roles that have evolved over the past few years from a passive to a more active orientation. This paper adds another dimension to this characterization of the IS planner's job by suggesting that, while the individual may, in fact, play these roles, the emphasis on these roles will change with the organizational status of the IS function.

The next section presents a description of the different roles the IS planner plays, drawing on role descriptions offered in the literature, followed by a discussion of the relationship between the IS planner's roles and the organizational reporting level of the IS functional head. It is proposed that as the organizational reporting level of the head of IS changes, the roles played by the IS planner would change too. This expectation is empirically tested using data collected from a survey of industrial firms. The research methodology, results of the analysis, and implications of the results are then described in the remaining sections.

## IS planner's roles

This study uses the categorization developed by Raghunathan and Raghunathan (1989b), which follows Camillus (1979) and consists of the following roles for the IS planner:

\- decision-maker

\- coordinator

\- internal consultant

• developer of action plans

\- source of external data

\- idea generator.

As IS increasingly becomes an indispensable organizational resource, top management would expect IS to take on a leadership role in planning and implementing new technologies and new systems. This enhances the decision-making role of the IS planning executive in that (s)he becomes responsible for charting out the future course for IS in the organization. In the coordinator role the planning executive would be responsible for monitoring the execution of the planning process and consolidating the elements of the various subsystems. Given the high-growth, constantly changing environment that IS faces, new technological advances and their impact on the organization have to be constantly monitored by the IS planner to facilitate appropriately planned responses to these changes. This would require the IS planner to scan the environment continuously and, by keeping up-to-date on these changes, be a source of external data. By sharing his/her expertise and knowledge of internal and external data, the IS planner would be serving as an internal consultant. In this role (s)he will be bringing new technologies to users and be able to recommend appropriate ones while planning future systems portfolios. In the idea generator's role, the IS planner would be identifying significant issues and strategic areas where IS can be used to enhance organizational effectiveness. Finally, for the purpose of bringing together the various functional systems and for effectively integrating and implementing plans, the IS planner would serve as a developer of action plans.

These roles are not easily separable and, in fact, represent different aspects of an integrated job. Camillus (1979) has proposed that these roles vary along the four inter-related dimensions of:

\- technical-managerial

\- passive-active

\- advisory–decisive

\- output orientation-process orientation.

Technical roles emphasize the responsibilities of planners in establishing schedules for implementation of the planning process, applying specific techniques such as forecasting, and so on. Managerial roles, on the other hand, highlight the planner's responsibility to manage the overall planning function by integrating the viewpoints of various functions and hierarchical levels in the organization. In some roles the planning manager is essentially passive or reactive, responding to requests for technical and analytical assistance. In other roles the planner plays an active or catalytic role, influencing both analyses and decisions. This passive-active classification correlates highly with another variable — the extent to which the planner acts in an advisory capacity as opposed to a decision-making or decisive capacity. The planner's roles also vary in the extent of responsibility assumed in the process of arriving at a plan, as opposed to the output or content of the plan. This, again, is closely related to the technical-managerial continuum. At the technical end, concern with the process rather than the output is more common, while the reverse is true at the managerial end.

Because of these inter-relationships among the dimensions, the managerial roles also tend to be more active, decisive, and output-oriented, while the technical roles tend to be more passive, advisory, and process-oriented. An organization's requirements for planning will dictate whether the planner's job will tend to be more managerial (and more active, decisive, and output-oriented) or more technical (and more passive, advisory, and process-oriented). This, in turn, will affect the emphasis placed on the different roles of the IS planner.

Based on the descriptions of the planner's roles and the four dimensions, a matching of the roles against the dimensions is presented in Figure 1. While the possibility for some interchangeability between adjacent ranks may exist, some roles emerge as being more managerial, active, decisive, and output-oriented, while others are more technical, passive, advisory, and process-oriented. The roles of the decision-maker, idea generator, and developer of action plans fit into the former category, while the roles of source of external data and coordinator fit into the latter. The internal consultant role requires both technological expertise and the managerial skills necessary to be an effective consultant in dealing with the management of technology.

## The planner's roles and the reporting level

Due to the increasing recognition of IS as an important organizational resource, the leadership of the IS function in an organization and its location in the organizational hierarchy can be viewed as powerful and perhaps critical elements in determining the organizational impact of the IS function. The reporting level of the top IS executive is an aspect of an organization's IS that has particular relevance to the IS planner.

Results of that study indicate that the higher the reporting level of the IS head, the greater the organizational acceptance of and support for IS planning and the greater the importance given to the strategic role of IS. Cash et al. (1992) identified the status conferred by the reporting level of the IS manager as a corporate environmental factor that influences IS planning. They stated that IS planning 'must be tailored to the realities of the organization's environment. Importance and status of the IT manager, physical placement of senior IT staff in relation to general management, . . . all influence how IT planning should be carried out' (p. 641). Previous studies thus suggest that the IS head's reporting level can have implications for the IS planner's roles. The more managerial roles of the IS planner can be expected to receive significantly greater emphasis when the IS head is at the highest reporting level; likewise, the more technical roles of the planner will not receive significantly greater emphasis when the IS head is at the highest reporting level. Thus it is proposed that the roles described for the IS planner would vary in relation to the reporting level of the IS functional head. Therefore, proper identification of the IS planner's roles can, for a particular organization, be an important step towards increasing the effectiveness of IS planning. Camillus (1979) has suggested that 'defining the appropriate role of . . . the planning manager can be critical to the effectiveness of planning'.

The remaining sections of this paper describe an empirical test of the above proposition using data from a sample of 192 organizations.

## Research methodology

## Data collection

The data used in this research was obtained by using a mail survey. A self-administered questionnaire instrument was mailed to heads of IS in 800 organizations. These were chosen at random from a mailing list of 3000 top IS executives. A total of 202 responses were received, of which 192 (24%) were usable. Table 1 provides the classification of the sample by industry type. Table 2 provides information on company revenues. Companies with revenues in the ranges above \$50 million are well represented in the sample. The results may therefore be more relevant to companies in this size category.

## Operationalization of variables

Roles of the IS planner This study proposes that the emphasis on the different roles of the planner described earlier varies in relation to the reporting level of the IS function head. The survey instrument listed six role descriptions and requested the respondents, who were heads of the IS function in their organizations, to indicate the extent to which each of the listed items accurately described the roles for the IS planner in their organization\*. Responses were on a five-point Likert-type scale. Thus 1 indicated a highly inaccurate description and 5 a very accurate description.

Organizational reporting level of the IS functional head Information on reporting level was obtained by asking the respondents (to the questionnaire survey) to indicate the number of levels below the chief executive officer (CEO) that the head of the IS function was located. These were then ranked 1 (one rank below the CEO), 2 and so on, so that rank 1 would refer to a higher organizational level than rank 2, and so on.

Table 3 presents information on the number of companies falling into each of these categories. Interestingly, there was one company where the IS functional head was also the CEO. For analytical purposes, these companies were then classified into three groups. Companies in ranks 0 and 1 were grouped together as group 1 companies, companies in rank 2 as group 2 companies, and companies in rank 3 and below as group 3 companies. On a relative scale, group 1 companies have their IS functional heads at the highest reporting levels, group 2 companies at the next higher levels, and group 3 companies at the (relatively) lowest reporting levels.

## Analysis and discussion of results

This section describes the test of the research proposal that the roles played by the IS planner will vary significantly, depending on the organizational reporting level of the IS functional head. The data were analysed from two perspectives:

\- The mean values of responses on the accuracy of descriptions as it pertained to each role was compared among the three groups to test for significant overall group difference in the mean values.

\- The differential impact of the reporting level of the IS functional head on each of the individual roles was tested by comparing two groups at a time.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test for the differences among the three groups for each of the six roles described earlier, namely, decision-maker, coordinator, source of external data, internal consultant, idea-generator, and developer of action plans. ANOVA is essentially concerned with tests of group mean responses. The F value allows us to accept or reject the null hypothesis that all group means are equal. The results of ANOVA runs are presented in Table 4.

The very high levels of significance of the F values indicate strong support for the expectation about the differences among the groups with respect to each of the roles of the IS planner.

These F statistics, when considered together with the increase in mean values from group 3 to group 1, indicate that the emphasis on each of the roles is significantly greater in group 1 organizations compared to group 3 organizations. In the absence of any imputations of causation, these results indicate that the organizational reporting level of the IS head has a significant relationship to the different roles played by the IS planner. Note, though, that the Table 4 results only provide evidence of overall differences in group means. Because of the direction of increase in means in moving from group 3 to 1, it can be inferred that group 1 and group 3 means must be statistically different. Table 4 results do not indicate however, whether there are statistical differences between group 1 and group 2 means, or between group 2 and group 3 means.

To explore further whether these group means were statistically different, i.e. to explore whether there were changes in these roles, in relation to differences in the reporting level of the IS head, t tests of group differences were conducted between groups 1 and 2, 2 and 3, and 1 and 3, for each of the six role descriptions. The results are diagrammatically presented in Figure 2 and suggest significant differences between the groups with respect to individual roles. These differences, when interpreted in the context of the differences that are not statistically significant, yield some interesting insights into the direction of changes in the roles vis-à-vis changes in the reporting level of the IS head.

For the roles of decision-maker, internal consultant, and developer of action plans, while the differences between groups 1 and 2 were significant (at the 0.01 level), the differences between groups 2 and 3 were not. These results suggest that these three roles become more important when the IS head is at the highest reporting level. The results for the roles of decision-maker and developer of action plans were consistent with the expectations stated earlier that these more managerial roles should receive more focused attention because of the increased importance derived by IS planning when the IS head is at the highest organizational level. The authors had expected the internal consultant role to be a mix of managerial and technical aspects. What has emerged here suggests a more managerial orientation for this role.

The results also indicated that the roles of ‘source of external data’ and ‘coordinator’ stand out more in group 2 organizations compared to group 3 organizations, but were not emphasized (significantly) any more in group 1 organizations compared to group 2 organizations. This result too was consistent with expectations for these two roles because they are more technical than managerial.

Finally, the role of idea generator indicated relationships that were very different from the other roles. There were no statistical differences between the group 1 and group 2 companies or between group 2 and group 3 companies. There was a gradual change in emphasis in moving from group 3 to group 1 organizations, unlike the more clearly delineated shifts indicated for the other roles.

These results provide evidence of the differential roles played by the IS planner in the context of the reporting level of the IS head, and provide overall support for the authors' expectations. The implications of these results are discussed in the next section.

## Implications of results

Previous research analysing the IS planner's function has suggested that the stereotype of the planner as a passive staff functionary may no longer be a true representation of that position; instead, the existence of a more active, varied and demanding role is envisioned for the planner (Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1989a). The results reported in the present study represent a further extension of previous research by analysing individual aspects of the IS planner's job. While the relationships tested in this study are within the context of the hierarchical reporting level of the head of the IS function, the reporting level itself can be viewed as a proxy for the organizational importance of IS. Cash et al. (1992) stated that the IS manager's status aligns with the role that IS play in the overall operation and strategy-formulating process of the company. They further added that in the environment where IS have strategic importance, the reporting level of the IS manager would be 'very high' (p. 634), and that, in a company where IS are serving only a support function, a lower status is appropriate for the IS manager (p. 637). The results of the present study acquire added significance if the IS planner's roles are viewed as being related to not only the organizational reporting level of the IS manager but, by implication, to the organizational importance of IS itself.

The implications of this study for IS planners result from the shift in role descriptions in relation to the reporting level of the IS functional head. The results of this study indicate that IS planners play a more managerial role in organizations that have IS heads reporting at the highest levels, and the more managerial orientation could mean more responsibilities for the IS planner. The results also suggest that organizations that have IS heads reporting at higher levels are likely to expect greater performance from IS planners.

IS planners in organizations that had assigned low priority to their IS in the past may find a sudden change in the demands on them when the organization is caught in the midst of the information revolution and discovers an exciting range of IS options that can be used to gain competitive advantage. The role of IS and IS planning may be suddenly elevated into prominence as a result of a change in the reporting level of the IS head; so too the roles of the IS planner, who should be prepared to meet the challenges of the new demands on planning. He/she may have to assess the relative importance of, and the emphasis to be placed on, each of these aspects of the job, and tailor the job so that it addresses adequately the varying organizational demands on the function.

From the perspective of the organization, the results suggest the need to recognize that the planning function can take on a more managerial orientation as the reporting level of the IS head moves up to the highest levels in the organization. Organizations where IS play only a support role should note that their IS planning efforts have a more technical flavour. These distinctions in the content of the planner's job can provide some guidance in the choice of individuals responsible for planning. Given the increased emphasis on managerial activities for organizations which have high reporting levels for IS heads, the same criteria that are used for selection of individuals to fill managerial positions within the organization may also have to be used in the selection of IS planners. In contrast, in organizations that do not currently view their IS as a key or critical organizational resource, the selection of IS planners can be made from a pool of individuals capable of handling a job which is primarily technical, advisory, and passive. Further, the managerial–technical aspects of the job and the qualifying criteria for the individual responsible for planning have to be viewed not only in the context of the current importance of IS in the organization, but also within the context of the future course of IS in that organization. If IS are expected to have great organizational importance in the foreseeable future, the individual responsible for planning should have the potential to take on the additional demands of the job in future.

IS planning has received focused attention as an important issue for IS practitioners and academic researchers only within the past decade — a status enjoyed by general organizational planning since the early 1970s. Almost two decades ago, a study by Peter Lorange (1973) suggested that ‘there are two types of planners, reflecting different planning tasks, and that the two have separate management profiles’ (p. 16). The first type of planner focuses more on the substance of planning, is more management-oriented, and is more of an active partner in top-level decisions. In contrast, there is the process-oriented planner, whose task is broadly specified in terms of improving ‘the formal planning procedures so that planning can be done better by others’ (p. 16). The study concluded that ‘there is need and room for both types of planners, depending on what job is to be done’ (p. 16). The results of the present study, though applicable to the specific context of IS planning, rather than to general organizational planning, which was the subject of Lorange’s study, provide some parallels to that earlier study. The idea that planning, whether it refers to general organizational planning or IS planning, is a multifaceted task and varies in focus in relation to the organizational environment in which planning takes place, emerges from both studies. The idea in Lorange’s study that a planner’s role is a dual one is paralleled by the emergence of the idea of differential emphasis on roles in the present study.

While it is interesting to note that some basic planning ideas have as much recent relevance as they had two decades ago, the identification of similarities between organizational planning and IS planning serves a more useful purpose. It suggests that the recent trends that are affecting the role of the general organizational planner can have implications for the IS planner's function. In the past decade the idea of strategic planning has filtered into the IS arena and has resulted in a major refocusing of interest in the use of IS strategic planning as a key management tool to help promote competitive advantage through the strategic use of IS. This refocusing has been instrumental in the rise to prominence of the IS planning function in many of today's organizations and has had implications for the IS planner's job. More recently, major changes have taken place in the general planning arena as a result of significant changes in the ways organizations are restructuring themselves. An example of this is the trend towards downsizing. Similarly, downsizing and outsourcing are two key trends in IS today. Both of these trends have implications for the IS function, IS planning, and the IS planner's job. Depending on the extent of downsizing and the nature of outsourcing, the size and structure of the IS function is likely to change. This, in turn, will have an impact on the planning function and roles, by adding another degree of complexity to the planning environment.

The greater the complexity in the IS planning environment the greater the need to be responsive to the issues that are critical to the effectiveness of planning. As pointed out earlier, defining the appropriate role of the planner is one such issue. The results of this study indicate the need for a clear understanding of the roles an IS planner can play in the context of the organizational importance of IS. Such understanding can help IS planners ‘reduce any lack of congruence that may exist between the roles they play and those they would prefer to play’ (Camillus, 1979). The relationships analysed in this study can also be of value in defining appropriate roles for IS planners in the context of specific organizations and help maximize the contribution of IS planning to overall IS effectiveness.
