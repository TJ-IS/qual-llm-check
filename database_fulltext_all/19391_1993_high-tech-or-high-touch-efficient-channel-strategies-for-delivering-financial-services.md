# High tech or high touch? Efficient channel strategies for delivering financial services

Uday M. Apte and Ari P. J. Vepsäläinen \*

Cox School of Business, Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX 75275, USA

With the progress of information technology, financial service institutions are restructuring their delivery channels. The delivery channel applications now range from direct sales and agency systems to all-electronic, customer-accessed networks. A key issue in the design of a delivery channel is achieving a proper mix of new technology and traditional human-centred delivery approach. Moreover, the delivery channel design should be properly matched with the characteristics of the service being offered. Based on a model of economic trade-offs, a theoretical framework, called the service channel strategies (SCS) approach, is proposed for identifying, analysing and designing appropriate delivery channel applications in the financial services industry. An example from banking is discussed to highlight the scope and normative focus of the proposed theoretical framework.

Keywords: service strategy, delivery channel, information technology, channel systems, financial services

The financial services industry has seen dramatic changes during the past decade and a half due to factors such as inflation, deregulation and advances in information technology (IT). The frontier of IT applications has shifted from internal, back-office support systems to externally oriented systems such as new sales support and service delivery applications, also known collectively as channel systems. The impact of information systems (IS) in reaching out to customers for the delivery of financial services has been particularly dramatic in commercial banking where, for example, many banks have made a conscious effort in the past decade to augment their traditional ‘brick and mortar’ strategy of branch banking with the deployment of automated teller machines (ATMs) and point-of-sale (POS) devices.

As pointed out by several observers, however, the financial services industry has realized — painfully — that increased use of computers does not necessarily raise productivity. As Wessel (1988) observed, productivity — as measured in output per hour worked — actually fell in the finance, real-estate and insurance industries between 1973 and 1986. Some of the common sources of problems in applying IT, not only in financial services but throughout the service sector, include:

\- clinging to old operational practices

\- insufficient planning before deploying computers

\- getting bogged down in expensive projects that do not pay off.

Hackett (1990) observed that the promise of productivity improvement with IT is yet to be realized since operations and technology are usually decoupled and, in many cases, operational strategies are not well grounded in the marketplace and competitive requirements.

This paper argues that the above disappointing results are observed, at least in the case of some channel systems applications, because insufficient attention is being given to the design of the overall delivery channel, including the channel system, and because the design is not always properly matched to the service being offered. The authors have already proposed a framework to address the underlying issues raised above (Vepsäläinen and Apte, 1987). This framework, which is called a model of service channel strategies (SCS), explicitly recognizes that, in addition to channel systems, the delivery of financial services involves participation of human intermediaries, such as agents or company employees. Hence the issue here is how to find the proper balance between IT and human intermediation in configuring delivery channels for various services. The SCS approach also proposes principles for matching the design of the delivery channel with the type of financial service package under consideration.

This paper starts by presenting the theoretical basis of the SCS approach. The economic trade-offs underlying the efficient design of delivery channels and the formulation of alternative service packages are captured in two graphic illustrations, the channel matrix and the service matrix. These matrices help the practical application of the SCS approach by facilitating communication between systems developers and marketing managers. The model is summarized in the form of the matrix of SCS, which demonstrates the suitable matches of channel designs and service packages. The third section illustrates the scope and normative focus of the SCS approach by discussing the evolution of retail banking services. The fourth section concludes the paper with a discussion of the managerial application of the proposed framework.

## The model of service channel strategies (SCS)

## Motivation for the model of service channels

As discussed at length in the emerging service operations literature (Sasser et al., 1978; Fitzsimmons and Sullivan, 1982; Heskett, 1986; Murdick et al., 1990; Lovelock, 1992), services differ from manufacturing in several important dimensions, including intangibility and idiosyncrasy of output, and simultaneity of production–consumption where customers participate and have contact with the delivery channel in the service creation process. As an example of intangibility, consider the example of life insurance. In this case, a part of the value of insurance to the policyholder comes from the agent's advice about what cover to buy and the psychological comfort of knowing that the insurance company will pay for losses should they occur (Light, 1986).

The existence of customer participation and contact is also an important factor to be considered in designing a service and its delivery channel. Chase (1981) proposed one of the earliest theory-based approaches for designing services using a customer contact approach. The degree of customer contact is defined in this theory as the ratio of time during which the customer is in direct contact with the service facility to the total time required for the creation of the service. Customer contact theory holds that the services that entail a high degree of customer contact are inherently less efficient due to the variability and uncertainty that customers introduce in the creation of service. The theory implies that to achieve greater efficiency the degree of customer contact should be reduced and/or improved. In the same article Chase outlined a number of customer contact reduction and improvement strategies.

In extending the customer contact approach, Apte and Mason (1992) suggested that customer contact be conceptualized as taking two forms. The first is where a physical presence involving face-to-face contact between the customer and service provider/facility is required for service creation and consumption; the second involves a symbolic contact where the main purpose of the customer's presence is to exchange the information necessary in service creation and consumption. It is evident that with an improved IT benefit-to-cost ratio, a substantial opportunity exists for substituting face-to-face customer contact with IT-based symbolic contact. The increasing use of IT in front-office environments can be seen as an actualization of this concept.

A mechanism that a service provider uses to deliver services to its customers is called a delivery channel. As an example of a delivery channel, again consider the life-insurance business. Life-insurance policies are sold to individual customers by independent insurance agents who, in today's computer age, use portable microcomputers to collect the necessary customer information and immediately give a premium quote (Apte, 1985). The processes of underwriting and insurance policy preparation follow next. In this scenario, the insurance agent, together with the microcomputer-based software application, can be considered as that part of the total service delivery channel with which a customer interacts. The delivery channel in this case also includes the back-room activities that get carried out in the home office of the insurance company.

Thus, the delivery channel, in this case, consists of both front-room and back-room operations where human efforts and IT support are combined in appropriate proportions to create the service needed by customers. The distinction between backroom and front-office activities, as made by Chase (1978), is an interesting one since the emphasis in the past decade, as indicated earlier, has been to improve the front-office operation through use of IT.

The topic of IT-based delivery channels has received considerable attention both in the trade press and research literature. Thiemann (1986) presents a service delivery matrix where the ‘level of service’ is considered in conjunction with a customer’s ‘ability/willingness to pay’ to draw conclusions concerning the positioning of banking services. Gordon and Fisk (1987) discuss some issues of service location, customer contact and service process flows in electronic service delivery. Huete (1988) suggests a matrix for matching service content with delivery channels in a dynamic framework where technology progress impacts the ‘natural area of match’ between service content and delivery channel. Kelley (1989) analyses the efficiency of humanistic and/or technological approaches to the delivery of a wide range of services as characterized by the extent of service customization, the level of judgement exercised by service employees, the nature of service act and the types of customers involved in the service.

## Efficient design of delivery channels

In general, a delivery channel for financial services consists of two main components — channel organizations and channel systems. As illustrated earlier through the example of life insurance, a financial institution has the choice of combining these two components in various proportions to create an appropriate delivery channel design for offering its service. Channel organizations consist of human intermediaries in the roles of outside agents or employees of the service-providing firm. In certain services several intermediaries are needed to reach a customer, but in others, a customer may be reached directly using IT with almost no human intermediary. This is called a market network organization. Along a spectrum of possibilities from a high level of human intermediation to a low level, three channel organizations can be identified:

\- external agent (independent agents as well as dedicated agents)

\- company employees (back-room staff members and salespersons)

\- market networks for direct customer access with minimal human intermediation (for example, services such as ATM or pay-by-phone).

This order of channel organization, specifying available options for the deployment of human intermediation, also reflects the economics of the channel. The variable cost of delivering a service is typically higher with an independent agent (due to the commissions paid) and lower with direct access by a customer. In terms of the fixed costs, the ranking is exactly reversed. The direct customer access has large up-front investment in developing the IS needed for service delivery and large fixed operational costs.

The channel systems, i.e. the IS used in delivering the financial services, span a variety of technical designs. A classification is suggested below on the basis of the degree of connectivity that a channel system offers to the various players involved in the service delivery process for providing access to information and processing. From a low level of connectivity to a high level, two channel system designs can be identified:

\- A centralized IS with batch processing where information exchange takes place mostly through paper-based transfer (low level of connectivity).

\- A distributed IS with online, interactive processing capabilities connecting remote and central processors (high level of connectivity).

One major difference in these designs from a user's (i.e. either agents or customers) point of view is the connectivity that each design offers to users in the accessing and processing of data. A centralized/batch system allows access in a limited sense, whereas a distributed/interactive system can link a large number of users to a central data repository and processing site with rapid response. Another method of comparison is the diversity of services that a channel system can handle. A centralized/batch system is usually designed to handle a wide range of service transactions, but a distributed/interactive system, being already complex enough from a technological viewpoint, must be specifically designed to handle only a limited range of service transactions. Thus a centralized/batch system allows greater flexibility in serving customers in unique ways. The human intermediaries have a large role to play in creating the service. Consequently, the service-providing firm exerts limited control on the performance quality of the service, whereas the channel intermediary exerts a relatively greater control over the quality and productivity of the whole service delivery process. A distributed/interactive system requires that all the processing modules be well integrated in terms of interfacing standards. This tight coupling of processing requires standardization in customer service and indicates a higher degree of control by the service-providing firm on the process of service delivery to customers.

These systems also differ in terms of the costs associated with establishing and maintaining them. A distributed/interactive system involves a high initial development cost, but the variable, per-transaction cost of operating such a system is typically low. A centralized/batch system requires less initial investment, but the total variable cost of system operation, including automated and manual processing, is relatively higher.

The above classifications of channel systems and channel organizations form a two-dimensional matrix of alternative designs of delivery channels, as shown in Figure 2. For example, ATM services, involving distributed/interactive systems, display a high level of connectivity and provide a classic example of service offered through market networks for direct customer contact. ATM services are thus located in the lower-right corner of the channel matrix in Figure 2. The cost trade-offs associated with different positions in this matrix are analysed, using arguments similar to those (product-process matching) of Hayes and Wheelwright (1979a and 1979b), to determine economically efficient design alternatives for delivery channels.

In this study of financial services, a small sample of which is discussed here, the delivery channels were observed to gravitate towards the diagonal. This is hypothesized in SCS to follow from economic efficiency and organizational feasibility considerations. In terms of economic efficiency, the channel designs above the diagonal, such as IVANS, involve high-variable-cost agents using distributed systems that also require heavy investments in technology. Thus these delivery channels cause excessive cash outlays and out-of-pocket costs in the form of commission and systems development and maintenance. Conversely, channel designs below the diagonal, such as the benefits system SHOWCASE, solicit direct customer access but provide limited link with batch systems. In this case, the opportunity of offering more services and options, and the ability to complete transactions within a network, are not exploited, resulting in high opportunity costs. Economic efficiency requires that these two costs be properly balanced, which leads to a preference for the diagonal positions. The feasibility considerations also lead to the same conclusion. Direct customer contact, for the most part, requires distributed/interactive systems; centralized/batch designs just will not do. Independent agents, on the other hand, tend to shun the technological constraints, and the associated loss of independence, implied by distributed/interactive systems. These feasibility arguments also indicate a preference for delivery channel designs along the diagonal.

The above intuitive arguments must be further evaluated on a more rigorous basis. An analytical approach involving a modelling of the economic trade-offs underlying the channel systems and organization is useful in this regard.

The efficient channel designs fall along the diagonal into two generic classes shown in the channel matrix in Figure 2. The delivery channels of the upper-left corner may be termed as the 'high touch' or human-centred channels that build on rather centralized systems which provide data recording and document processing for agents under the control of the service-providing firm. Examples are investment counselling and personal lines insurance. In contrast, the delivery channels located in the lower-right corner, which are called the 'high tech' or technology-centred channels (exemplified by ATMs and credit cards) are the means of bypassing all human intermediaries through distributed/interactive systems by providing customers with direct access to service transactions and central databases. If a service channel does not resemble one of these efficient designs, the SCS indicates that such a channel is likely to be inefficient and needs to be adjusted. The farther away from the diagonal the current channel is, the more alternatives there are for adjustment that can and should be considered.

## High tech or high touch?

## Efficient packaging of services

Financial services are customarily classified in terms of the basic financial needs of a customer — deposits, loans, payments (cash, cheques, etc.), investment management and risk management (primarily insurance). This classification, however, does not shed much light on how a service may be defined or how a delivery channel may be designed. Lovelock (1983), reviews a number of alternative frameworks for classifying services to gain marketing insights. Based on these classification frameworks, financial services can be characterized as a service that:

\- is an intangible action directed at people

\- is continuously delivered on a membership basis to customers

\- is customized with a high degree of judgement being exercised by the customer contact personnel

\- has smaller variability in both demand and supply

\- is delivered to customers at multisite locations.

Though useful to a marketeer of financial services, these classifications shed little light on how a financial service might be characterized in a way that helps the design of service delivery channels. In the proposed framework, a financial service is viewed as a service package that is defined through two main dimensions: customer contact strategy and complexity of a service contract.

The first factor, the customer contact strategy, may vary between two extremes, a transaction-based strategy and a relationship-based strategy. With a transaction-based strategy, the delivery process is designed to make transaction processing efficient. The emphasis is on low-cost, rapid completion of standardized transactions rather than on giving individual attention to each customer's needs. Under this strategy the costs vary mostly in proportion to the volume of transactions, with economics of scale being present in most cases. An example of a transaction-based service is ATMs. In relationship-based marketing (Levitt, 1986), in contrast, the attention is given to the needs of individual customers and the emphasis is on building a long-term relationship with them. Major investments under this strategy are targeted to improve the communication process between the firm and its customers. Investment counselling constitutes an example of a relationship-based strategy.

The second factor, the complexity of a service contract, may span a spectrum from a contract with a small and simple domain to a contract with a large and complex domain. The complexity of service domain can be assessed by the number of options and contingencies to be considered in establishing a service contract suitable to all players and the information complexity associated with delivering the contracted service. Again ATMs illustrate a small and simple domain and investment counselling services a large and complex domain.

Different service packages are illustrated in the service matrix of Figure 3. For example, the ATM service is located in the lower-right corner. In this study of financial services, most services were again seen to be close to or on the main diagonal of the matrix, suggesting some inherent efficiency for service packages along the diagonal.

The preference for diagonal positions arises from the consideration of economic efficiency along the arguments of transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1985). According to this view, economic transactions — exchange of products or services — should be arranged so as to minimize the sum of production and transaction costs. In financial services, for example, the trade-off is between the efficient routines for processing transactions, causing the ‘production costs’ of the service, and the establishment of customer relationships and easy access to the service, causing the ‘transaction costs’ of service. More specifically, above the main diagonal, where simple services are offered through customized procedures, excessive processing costs are incurred. Below the diagonal, complex contracts get offered through a standardized transaction-processing system without the appropriate relationship management.

Inefficiency is created here due to the excessive costs of service establishment and access (i.e. the difficulty on the part of a customer in establishing the service contract and in making use of standardized transactions to satisfy a complex need without receiving customized help). The sample services seem to be well balanced by avoiding excess of both the transaction processing and the service establishment/access costs. Being on the diagonal, though more efficient, should not be considered as a strict rule for service package creation. For long-term strategic reasons, a firm may choose to be in an off-diagonal position for a certain period. What SCS argues is that in the long run the preferred packaging strategy will be on the diagonal.

The bubbles termed ‘customized service’ and ‘mass transactions’ in the service matrix of Figure 3 represent preferred service packages. Customized services are those that have a complex domain and require close customer relationships. Examples of customized services include investment counselling and commercial risk management. In the other corner there is mass transaction services, which are characterized by a simple service domain and transaction-based customer contacts. ATM services and credit cards are typical mass transaction services.

## Matching of service packaging and delivery channel designs

So far, efficient choices for service packages and delivery channel designs have been identified. To ensure competitive performance, these two components of a service delivery strategy need to be matched appropriately. SCS argues that the matching of service packages and delivery channel designs is determined by the interplay of individual interests and actions of all parties (such as service provider, channel organization and customers) involved in the process of service delivery. For example, consider a service-providing firm and an independent agent. They are involved in a situation of cooperation and conflict in delivering a service to customers. Each party strives to control the service, lower the costs and extract the value-added in service delivery from their own perspective. The customers prefer lower cost, objective advice and reliable and convenient service. Thus, in a more complete sense, it is this multi-player, multi-objective game that determines the appropriateness of a given delivery channel for a given service package. The arguments of the SCS approach presented below simplify this complex situation in capturing the critical trade-offs.

The resulting match between service package and delivery channel is shown in the SCS matrix of Figure 4 for the sample services. For customized services, the factors important to a customer are objective advice on the domain configuration of the service to be selected, and a close and confidential relationship. Human intermediaries, agents and/or employees, having the ability to exercise judgement, are best suited for this role. Hence, despite the higher variable costs entailed in using outside agents in some cases, the human-centred channel is seen as the preferred delivery channel design for customized services. Another way of explaining this is by using economic arguments — the benefits or the value-added associated with the service being offered should be sufficient to cover the cost of intermediation efforts involved in the delivery channel. The value-added in customized services is high enough to more than offset the high variable costs of human intermediation. On the other hand, if a customized service were to be provided through a technology-centred channel, the effort is likely to fail due to the undue complexity of defining and accessing customized service from a customer's viewpoint, and high fixed costs and volume from the service-providing firm's viewpoint. Thus the SCS approach indicates that a human-centred channel is the matching delivery channel for a customized service. Mass transaction services are best delivered through technology-centred channels, because of the convenience of direct access to customers and low variable cost to providers. Shared or third-party networks are usually preferred since the investment of establishing the network can be shared over a large number of customers, providers and services.

## Use of the SCS approach in strategic planning

The previous section makes it clear that by capturing the trade-offs underlying the design alternatives of the delivery channel and service package, the SCS framework can be particularly useful in identifying appropriate delivery channel designs and service packages. This feature makes it a beneficial tool in strategic planning for financial institutions.

The application of the SCS approach involves use of three matrices discussed at length above — the delivery channel matrix, the service package matrix, and the service channel strategy matrix, matching the service package and delivery channel design. It should be noted that within the context of a given scenario only a subset of the above diagrams may need to be used. For example, if a service package were to be considered as given, one may only need to use the delivery channel diagram in analysing the matching of channel system and channel organization.

## SCS illustration: teller services in commercial banking

As an illustration of SCS's power to identify a suitable delivery channel design for a given service, and, therefore, in being useful in strategic planning and delivery channel design activities, consider the evolution of teller services in the commercial banking over the past 25 years. Teller services in retail banking include deposits and withdrawals, balance enquiries, account management, cheque cashing, and fund transfers. Using the SCS approach, the evolution of these services and associated delivery channels over the past two decades is discussed. In the late 1960s, commercial banks provided teller services mainly through the branch office network. The human tellers were the only form of channel organization used. Current experience shows (Steiner and Teixeira, 1990) that certain simple teller services, such as withdrawals, deposits and balance enquiries, have now been shifted to the ATMs, even though the human tellers are still seen to support a majority of more complex transactions. These services were offered through a customer contact strategy that was a mix between relationship-based and transaction-based approaches. The current situation indicates that many banks now offer these services through two complementary approaches: ATMs for mass transactions and human tellers for both types of services, with the main emphasis on services of medium complexity. ## Guidelines for efficient service strategies

Thus the full potential of the SCS approach can be brought to bear in strategic planning by conducting the analysis in two steps (Mäkelin and Vepsäläinen, 1989). The first step involves the analysis of industry and competition. The SCS framework allows meaningful comparisons of a firm's service packages and delivery channels with those of its competitors. In addition, it can be used to analyse long-term industry trends, in terms of the services, delivery channels, and corporate structures, to uncover patterns of evolution that can be used in identifying competitive opportunities or potential competitive threats. The second step involves the analysis of corporate resources and business unit strategy. By analysing available corporate resources within the context of a portfolio of service packages to be offered, the SCS helps identify strengths and weaknesses of a given corporation. This analysis can be translated into specific action plans for resource acquisition and restructuring of a corporation's delivery channels or service packages. This allows the positioning of a business unit and associated delivery channel to achieve a competitive advantage.

So far certain fundamental concepts underlying the SCS approach have been discussed. There are two other aspects that need to be considered for developing a more complete understanding of the SCS. First, the relationship between the SCS approach and Porter's well known competitive strategy framework, which encompasses three generic strategies — cost leadership, differentiation and focus (Porter, 1980). Referring to Figure 4, it may be interesting to note that the above three strategies are encountered sequentially in moving diagonally from the lower-right to the upper-left end of the preferred matching region. For example, cost leadership is the preferred strategy in the lower-right corner of the matrix. Generating a high volume of sales and production, this strategy rests on gaining competitive advantage through offering low-cost, mass transaction services. Differentiation is the preferred strategy for standard services within the central zone of Figure 4. Differentiation is seen to be accomplished by offering a new combination of basic transactions, or by introducing new products or channels. The focusing strategy targets specific customer groups or market niches by offering customized services within the upper-left zone of Figure 4. A firm can create a focus by customizing a set of services specifically for a given customer group.

The second interesting aspect of SCS is related to the stages of a typical customer service life-cycle (Ives and Learmonth, 1984) and the possibility of requiring different delivery channels for supporting different stages of this life cycle. For example, consider a service such as discount brokerage, whose customer service life cycle may be conceived in terms of two stages: sales and after-sales service. Discount brokerage is a direct-response business where customers are primarily solicited by direct mail, coupon advertisements and statement inserts (Coler and Ratner, 1988). Most customers either mail in their application or telephone their information to a broker in opening an account and they rarely visit the brokerage office thereafter. In view of this one may be tempted to conclude that it is possible to run a national discount brokerage business from a single office. This would indeed be a mistake. Most customers also implicitly require that their brokers be easily accessible in case they ever need to resolve any matter in person. Just having a local telephone number is not enough. This dictates that having nationwide branches for occasional after-sales service is a key to success. Understanding this concept is what made Charles Schwab and Quick and Reiley so successful. Quick and Reiley recognized that it did not need to have a branch in a high-rent district. All it needed was an easily-accessible office with a staff of two or three. As this case shows, successful discount brokerage operation requires two delivery channels: direct-response marketing for sales and branch offices for after-sales service. Thus, in general, the SCS approach should be separately applied for analysing the delivery requirements of separate stages within the customer service life cycle of a given service.

## Conclusions

A theoretical framework has been outlined, called service channel strategies (SCS), for identifying, analysing and defining appropriate delivery channels for financial services applications. A key issue in the design of a delivery channel is achieving a proper mix of 'high tech' and 'high touch', i.e. to balance the applications of new technology and the traditional human-centred delivery approach. The SCS approach recognizes this in determining efficient designs of delivery channels by balancing relevant tangible and intangible costs. The delivery channel design should be properly matched with the delivery characteristics of a service (or a service package in the authors' terminology). The model subsequently provides guidelines on creating efficient designs for service packages and suitable matching of a delivery channel to a service package.

The theory of SCS provides an analytical basis for planning strategies for delivery channels in the financial services industry. In analysing financial services and associated delivery channels, the SCS model also deals with the issues concerning the evolution of services, availability of corporate resources and positioning of individual business units. An example from banking illustrates how the SCS approach can be used to predict the dominant delivery channels for the future. This insight can then be leveraged in strategic planning by a practising manager for efficient implementation and competitive pricing of financial service packages and associated delivery channels.
