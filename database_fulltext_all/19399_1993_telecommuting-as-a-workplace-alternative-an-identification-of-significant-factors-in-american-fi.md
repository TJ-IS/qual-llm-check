# Telecommuting as a workplace alternative: an identification of significant factors in American firms' determination of work-at-home policies

Mark N. Frolick, Ronald B. Wilkes and Robert Urwiler \*
Memphis State University, Management Information Systems and Decision Sciences, Fogelman College of Business and Economics,
Room 300, Memphis, TN 38152, USA

Due to the changing demographics of the American workforce, telecommuting programmes for mid-level corporate workers are receiving a great deal of attention. This research presents factors that firms have found to be most significant when considering telecommuting policies. Results show that telecommuting programmes increased productivity, decreased absenteeism and turnover, and decreased in non-salary related expenses to the firms. Attachment of telecommuters to the corporate culture of the organization was an initial issue of concern and was explicitly addressed through a set of similar policies by all the organizations. The research was conducted using semi-structured interviews to investigate questions of interest to firms considering telecommuting programmes. This approach provided candid, inside views into some of the most successful telecommuting programmes in the USA.

Keywords: American workforce, telecommuting programmes, teleworkers

Recent studies show a dramatic shift in the demographics of the American workforce. The demand for qualified technical labour will continue to exceed the supply well into the next century. The increasing numbers of women and working couples in the workforce have forced organizations to become sensitive to family-related issues. To remain competitive, organizations must continually seek new ways of attracting and retaining the best and brightest technical workforce without sacrificing product innovation. One way that organizations can compete in such an environment is to offer employees work flexibility.

Work flexibility in most organizations consists solely of flexible working hours. Flexible hours give workers the freedom to tailor work hours to meet personal and family needs. This arrangement has proven to be an effective way for organizations to meet many of the needs of the modern workforce (Kay, 1990). It also allows them to compete more effectively with other firms in recruiting the best employees. A natural extension of flexible hours is the flexible workplace. The term telecommuting has been coined to refer to employees of organizations working from their homes or satellite offices near their homes, keeping in touch with the main office mostly by telephone or other telecommunications technologies (Nilles et al., 1976).

Telecommuting, or telework, programmes are becoming increasingly popular throughout the world (Bailey, 1989; Gann, 1991; Kinsman, 1989; May, 1990; Risman and Tomaskovic-Devey, 1989; Yap and Tng, 1990). The number of US workers classified as full- or part-time teleworkers is projected to reach 90 million by the year 2030 (Hudson Institute, 1987). In Sweden, not only have remote work centres been instituted but some train carriages have been equipped as remote offices (Kinsman, 1989). While the term telecommuting or telework may refer to working at home, at satellite offices near workers' homes, or, as in Sweden, even train cars, this study looked only at 'work-at-home' telecommuters.

Several benefits from telework have been cited. Dramatic increases in worker productivity, reduced turnover, and a reduction of expensive workspace are being reported by organizations currently offering the telework alternative (Cross, 1986; Christensen et al., 1990; Gordon, 1990). Teleworkers claim that elimination of stress associated with the daily commute, avoidance of interruption, and flexibility to tend to family and personal issues without affecting job related commitments are directly linked to their level of job satisfaction and increased productivity (Cascio, 1989). Organizations offering telework are also finding an increase in the size of the available technical labour pool due to the ability to hire individuals with special needs such as those with physical disabilities, geographic restrictions, or special family related concerns (Cascio, 1989; Di Martino and Wirth, 1990).

The research described in this paper explores factors that an organization should consider before implementing a telecommuting programme. The relationship between worker isolation and attachment to the corporate culture was investigated. Factors linking workplace and work hour flexibility and its effect on productivity, employee tenure, remote management styles, and non-salary related employer expenses were also studied.

The findings indicate a significant increase in productivity among teleworkers, almost universal adoption of objective-oriented management of teleworkers, decreased employee turnover, decreased absenteeism, and a decrease in non-salary related expenses to the organization. Concern that telecommuting would result in detachment from the corporate culture was not supported by the research but this may have been prevented by programmes specifically instituted for this purpose in all the participant organizations.

## The literature

In their 1976 book, Nilles and co-workers identify almost all of the issues that organizations today must deal with in instituting telecommuting programmes. Jack Nilles, who coined the terms ‘telework’ and ‘telecommute’ in 1973, is responsible for much of the significant research to date on the subject. In 1990, his California-based consulting company, JALA Associates, completed the final evaluation of the State of California’s telecommuting pilot project which he was instrumental in establishing in 1985. Data used in the evaluation was obtained primarily through questionnaires distributed to telecommuters, their supervisors, and a non-telecommuting control group. Other data was obtained via interviews and focus group meetings. The evaluation consisted primarily of tracking measurable change in an established ‘effectiveness rating’ over time (Nilles, 1991b). Many of the findings in the study reported herein are consistent with Nilles’ findings.

The Hudson Institute's 1987 study of changing demographic and labour force issues entitled ‘Workforce 2000: Work and Workers for the 21st Century’ found that there will be a drastic decrease in the supply of qualified technical workers entering the workforce to meet the increasing demand. To remain competitive, organizations must implement programmes to attract and retain the best employees with the same vigour and innovation with which they market their products.

Methods for attracting and retaining the best technical employees vary from industry to industry, but one factor seems to be constant: most workers want to see significant improvements in the quality of work life (Cascio, 1989). As a result of the changing demographics and attitudes of today's workforce, many organizations are offering much greater work flexibility.

Telecommuting is seen as a potential solution to the problems involved in employee commuting and to the labour shortage that will continue to plague management through the 1990s (Kelly, 1988; Gordon, 1990). Yap and Tng (1990), found that the availability of telecommuting would help attract female computer programmers into the Singapore job market where their skills are in short supply. 'There is also widespread consensus that large productivity gains result from telework. These are attributed to the lack of interruptions and improved concentration; increased motivation and job satisfaction; higher dedication and morale; and a higher energy level on the job due to the elimination of wasted time and frustration of commuting' (Di Martino and Wirth, 1990). 'The impact of telecommuting on the telecommuters has been very positive, as determined by a variety of measures besides effectiveness changes. Quality of life changes, both at work and at home, have been particularly important to the telecommuters, with improvements in the ability to concentrate on crucial tasks and to get more done, freedom from interruptions, increased creativity and improvements in personal life steadily increasing over the life of the project.' (Nilles, 1990).

The Telecommuting Advisory Council (TAC) was formed in 1985 ‘to advocate the benefits, planning, design, and appropriate application of telecommuting and to serve as an information resource for telecommuting to the private and public sectors’. TAC cites reduced costs of office space, competitive advantage in hiring and retention, availability of a broader labour market, decreased turnover, and increased productivity as potential benefits of telecommuting. The employee benefits cited include: decreased commuting costs, increased flexibility and autonomy, evaluation based on performance instead of appearance, and closer bonds to family and community (Telecommuting Advisory Council, 1991).

The success of existing telecommuting programmes is due primarily to the benefits of flexibility offered to both the company and the telecommuter. The more flexible the working arrangements, the more likely that a worker will become motivated to perform in an effective, results-oriented manner (Nilles, 1991b). Improvements in management effectiveness have also been widely noted. This is attributed to the results-oriented nature of managing employees in a remote location (Newman, 1989). The notion of managing results rather than an employee's presence is increasingly seen as a key factor in the success of telework (Di Martino and Wirth, 1990).

Recruitment and retention of employees is seen as a significant benefit of telework programmes to employers (Christensen et al., 1990). Recruiting becomes more effective because the flexible nature of a telework programme can be marketed as a unique benefit. Qualified employees with special needs such as a disabling injury or a geographic restriction can still be offered full employment status, thus giving the employer a competitive recruiting advantage in that additional labour markets are tapped. Employees are more likely to remain with a company where such flexibility is offered as long as career paths are defined for the teleworker (Young, 1991).

Companies instituting telecommuting programmes have felt the need to keep telecommuters ‘visible’ by requiring them to come into the office at least one day per week (Gordon, 1990). This is considered necessary in order to keep them from becoming completely isolated from their peers and the social aspects of the office environment. Attachment to the corporate culture and the peer network is seen as being essential to career advancement as well as ensuring that the teleworker is having his or her work-related social needs met (Olson, 1983; Olson and Primps, 1984; Risman and Tomaskovic-Devey, 1989). Excessive isolation has been identified as a demotivational factor for teleworkers (Christensen et al., 1990). A recent study states that 60 per cent of teleworkers polled named isolation as the greatest disadvantage in their situation and that the stimulation of exchanging ideas with colleagues would be missed by a majority of the teleworkers (Di Martino and Wirth, 1990).

Formal employer/employee contracts or agreements are common in telework arrangements. Most telework contracts stipulate working hours. This is important for complying with workman's compensation regulations in case of injury while working at home. Many formal agreements include job responsibilities, expense reimbursement policies, and safe physical workspace requirements. Generally, employee/supervisor orientation programmes are also required in order to familiarize both the employee and supervisor with exactly what to expect from telework in terms of balancing work and family issues, results oriented management, and related topics (Di Martino and Wirth, 1990).

Despite the apparent benefits to both employers and the teleworkers themselves, trade unions have shown a concern over telework since it began spreading in the mid-1980s. It is perceived as being a modern extension of industrial home work, which is traditionally associated with low wages and substandard working conditions. The unions feared that an isolated, fragmented workforce was particularly vulnerable to exploitation. Unions are currently encouraging regulation of home-based work in order to prevent exploitation (Gordon, 1990).

Most prior empirical research has used case studies to evaluate the effects of telecommuting on workers and organizations. There have been very few quantitative studies. Many of the conclusions reached have been based largely on the subjective opinions of researchers dealing with organization case studies. The issues surrounding evaluation of many telecommuting projects are often concerned with the establishment of proper controls by which success can be gauged. 'One of the problems plaguing most studies of innovation is that of acquiring the necessary evaluation data. It is important during any implementation to keep an accurate account of changes produced in order to decide whether to expand beyond the pilot phase' (Nilles, 1991b).

## Issues in instituting telecommuting programmes

The literature discussed above identifies the following telecommuting implementation variables: attachment of the employee to the corporate culture; productivity; employee tenure; management style; and non-salary related expenses to the firm. Even though employee absenteeism was not a significant topic in most of the literature, it appeared to be a significant potential benefit of telework programmes and was included for investigation.

## Attachment to corporate culture

For employees to remain a part of the corporate culture of an organization, they must remain visible within the organization. Visibility in terms of physical interaction with peer groups in the formal office setting contributes significantly to the notion of a corporate culture. The corporate culture describes the attitude of the workforce as a whole. Social interaction with those outside of the immediate work group helps to give an employee a sense of belonging to an organization working towards a common goal. Extended isolation from the office environment can eventually isolate or detach an employee from the corporate culture. In the traditional office environment, corporate culture can significantly affect the overall mode of operation, employee morale, and even the success of the company as a whole (Gordon, 1990; Christensen et al., 1990; Di Martino and Wirth, 1990; Telecommuting Advisory Council, 1991). Another potential reason for concern of telecommuters for not being in the office is that they will be overlooked in the review and promotion process (Bailey, 1989).

## Productivity

The relationship between workplace flexibility and productivity is of particular interest to an employer. As a teleworker is able to optimize his or her motivational periods around a flexible work schedule in an informal setting, productivity, or the number of tasks effectively completed in a given timeframe, are expected to increase accordingly. This is due not only to the motivational factors, but also to the lack of outside interruption and resulting increased level of concentration reached by the teleworker (Gordon, 1991; Nilles, 1991b).

## Employee tenure

Workplace flexibility also has a relationship to employee tenure. Since the number of telework programmes currently active in America is relatively small, workers who find themselves in situations where they are able to work from home may consider this one of the most desirable benefits or privileges offered by the organization. This will affect the employee's desire to seek employment elsewhere (Gordon, 1990; Di Martino and Wirth, 1990; Telecommuting Advisory Council, 1991).

One particularly appealing benefit of telecommuting is avoiding the trip to the office each day. This issue would appear to be more significant within more urbanized areas. This should provide a greater push for the creation of telework programmes in Western Europe and Japan where 90+ per cent of the population reside in urban areas as contrasted with the USA where less than two-thirds of the population live in urban areas (May, 1990).

## Employee absenteeism

A reduction in teleworker absenteeism could naturally be expected but has not been widely discussed in the literature. Perhaps this is because the meaning of absenteeism among teleworkers has not been adequately defined. But this issue is important and is consequently addressed in this study.

## Management style

Flexible work arrangements and a non-resident workforce additionally affect management style. Many adaptations have been observed by organizations currently offering telework programmes. The primary observation has been a shift towards results oriented management. Managers now develop schedules in cooperation with the teleworkers and manage the units of work completed according to these schedules. In a non-telework environment, managers often evaluate workers based on their presence in the office or on the perception of the employee's dedication to the job. Too many times, exempt employees are seen as 'taking advantage' or being lazy or even uncommitted when they show up late occasionally or consistently take a long lunch, even if the time is made up by working extra hours. This perception can easily jade a manager's view of an employee's overall effectiveness and result in a micro-management style that is generally considered to be detrimental to all parties involved. The results-oriented manager is much more concerned with the work being completed effectively as opposed to being completed while adhering to that particular manager's idea of an acceptable working style (Di Martino and Wirth, 1990; Nilles, 1991a).

## Non-salary related expenses

The implementation of a telecommuting programme can also affect certain non-salary expenses. Expenses such as medical benefits paid due to routine sickness should significantly decrease for those workers involved in telework. Workers who are not exposed to the various ‘bugs’ circulating around the office environment are less vulnerable to the type of minor sicknesses that are most likely to send them to see a doctor or call in sick for work. In addition, reduced exposure to the elements and reduced stress from the lack of a daily commute will decrease their chances of developing minor sickness and thus decrease absenteeism and doctor visits. When teleworkers are feeling ill and would call in sick for work, they now have the convenience of alternating rest and occasional work from home and integrating any doctor visits with convenient work hours (Di Martino and Wirth, 1990; Nilles, 1991b).

## Research questions

The preceding discussion provokes the following questions:

\- Do telecommuters become detached from the corporate culture?

\- Are telecommuters more productive than their non-telecommuter counterparts? Is their productivity related to the flexibility of their work environment? Do telecommuters tend to work longer hours as a result of their telecommuting arrangement?

\- Does telecommuting affect employee job turnover?

\- Does telecommuting affect absenteeism?

\- What management styles are adopted by telework managers?

\- What is the impact of telecommuting on non-salary related expenses?

## Study method

To address these questions, semi-structured, taped, telephone interviews were administered to 45 individuals in 10 organizations. The semi-structured interviewing approach was developed to define the scope of the interviews and to ensure that the research questions were properly addressed while allowing for 'probing' questions to gain greater understanding and insight into the issues. The interview guides are shown in Appendix A.

The participants in this study are from American organizations that were identified in the literature as participating in telework programmes and recommended by the Telecommuting Advisory Council. Interviewees were directly involved in telework programmes either as teleworkers, telework managers, or telework administrators. Organizations were only included in the sample if at least one individual from each of these three categories could be interviewed. As a consequence of this selection criteria, 10 telework managers, 10 telework administrators, and 25 teleworkers were interviewed. Of the teleworkers interviewed, 14 were programmer analysts (56 per cent), three were legal professionals (12 per cent), one was a technical writer (4 per cent), and seven were engineers (28 per cent). The average time that each of the interviewees has spent in telework programmes is 2.3 years. The types of industry and geographic regions of the participating organizations are shown in Tables 1 and 2 respectively.

## Results and analysis

All the interviewees seemed enthusiastic about the overall uniqueness of telework and the opportunity to be involved in telework in any capacity. They candidly shared their opinions and experiences with the researchers during the interview process. Their responses provided the basis for the following discussion.

## Attachment to corporate culture

Preserving the degree of teleworkers' attachment to the corporate culture was a fundamental concern to a majority of the subject organizations. Employers considered this a high priority requirement in the development of telework programmes.

Detachment of teleworkers from the corporate culture was addressed by all the subject organizations through the inclusion of policies that require telecommuters to come into the office at least one day per week and, in some cases, that they participate in ‘focus’ meetings with other telecommuters and non-telecommuters on a regular basis. In most cases, the maximum that an employee could work from home was four days per week.

Exceptions to the requirement to spend some part of the work week at the office were made in special cases such as those involving workers classified as temporary teleworkers due to short-term disabling injuries or illnesses. A unique exception was that of a programmer/analyst whose participation in telework consisted of a ‘production support’ role in which telecommuting was offered as an option for up to seven days in a row during each month. The employee in this case was ‘on call’ for a period between 1700 and 0800 on each of the seven days.

A few organizations make extensive use of support groups. These meetings are held periodically not only to aid in measuring the effectiveness of the programme, but also to act as a means by which employees communicate with one another to share experiences of telecommuting with colleagues.

The need by teleworkers for attachment to the corporate culture appeared to vary greatly. While some teleworkers required a great deal of social interaction with peers in order to keep a feeling of ‘belonging’, others were perfectly content being out of the office for extended periods of time without a negative effect on their attitudes toward work. One telework manager observed, ‘She seemed to be glad to be away from here. With the political environment around here, I can’t say that I blame her.’

In response to the question, ‘Do you feel “in tune” with the corporate culture?’, 17 of the teleworkers (68 per cent) responded yes while only eight (32 per cent) responded no. Two of the latter explained that they did not feel ‘in touch’ with the corporate culture even when they were working at the company full time.

One respondent had an opposing view of the importance of attachment to corporate culture within the organization. He described his work atmosphere as ‘oppressive management styles, excessive bureaucracy, and inadequate facilities resulting in overcrowding and poor working conditions’. The promise of telework as a way to escape that atmosphere was among the most attractive aspects of the programme. This situation was observed in a large city government organization and was not seen as common to other work environments. Aside from this exception, participants valued attachment to corporate culture and considered possible detachment a situation that should be avoided.

One concern regarding working at home is that teleworkers will be ‘out of sight’ and consequently ‘out of mind’ when promotions and raises are being considered (Bailey, 1989). This fear was not expressed by any of the teleworkers in this study. Additionally, evaluations of teleworkers’ performance (discussed below) indicate that their work does not go unnoticed or unrewarded.

Another related issue may be a cause for concern. Fourteen of the teleworkers (56 per cent) indicated that ‘no one to bounce ideas off of’ was a disadvantage of telework. Ways to address this issue in the context of telework need to be identified.

The overt effort by organizations involved in telework programmes to ensure attachment of teleworkers makes it difficult to answer fully the question of the impact of telecommuting on detachment from the corporate culture, but sufficient concern was shown by the participants to warrant serious consideration of this issue when developing a telecommuting programme.

## Productivity

Perhaps the most extensively discussed issue in telework literature is the effect of telecommuting on productivity. Every teleworker and every telework manager reported that productivity had increased as a result of their telecommuting programmes. Positive effects on performance evaluation scores were reported by many of the participants. Responses of telework managers varied somewhat, from 'not vastly more productive, but more productive nonetheless' to 'his work quality was vastly improved'. But in every case, the respondents stated that teleworker productivity was higher than that of the non-teleworkers. Average increases of approximately 20 per cent were cited by the study participants. This figure is consistent with other research findings (Nilles, 1990).

Most claims of productivity to date have been attributed to a lack of interruption and the ability of the teleworker to schedule his or her work in a flexible manner. In this study all the teleworkers cited ‘fewer interruptions’ as a contributing factor to their productivity and 17 of them (68 per cent) cited ‘greater flexibility’ in performing their jobs.

Defining and measuring productivity were found to be controversial topics. Several respondents suggested that organizations find themselves struggling to quantify productivity when designing telecommuting programmes. Telework managers reported using ‘deadlines’ or ‘agreed upon deadlines’, and ‘on-time work and quality’ to manage and measure teleworker productivity.

Several participants expressed that they ‘are still looking for a way to measure teleworker productivity’. Measuring the productivity of teleworkers appears to be an area that needs further work. Poorly developed measurement methods can actually have a detrimental effect on the quality of work produced. Initially, organizations have a tendency to focus on quantity with complete disregard for quality and other effectiveness measures. The findings suggest that qualitative as well as quantitative factors should be built into measurement tools. An example given by one telework manager was that of computer programmers being measured by the number of ‘lines of code’ produced over a given period. Although this measurement might seem legitimate to a person unfamiliar with tasks involved in computer programming, those familiar with the job know that this measurement actually gives very little information as to the effectiveness of a programmer. Much of the analysis involved in the construction of a computer program relates to streamlining procedures in order to simplify and increase the efficiency of the computer program itself. The end result of an efficient, well-thought through programming effort, is usually a program containing fewer lines of code than one not well designed. A measurement which encourages programmers to write lengthy programs can have a detrimental long-term effect on the effectiveness of the organization as a whole. Nilles (1990) stated, ‘We avoided using the word “productivity” in our questionnaires (used in evaluations) because of the common bias toward thinking of productivity as directly related to the quantity of one’s output . . . In our use of “effectiveness” as the preferable term we hoped to focus attention on doing the right things — and doing them right.’

All teleworkers considered productivity measurements at their particular organizations to be effective; however each participant did consider themselves to be a proven high performer prior to becoming involved in telework. Employees participating in telework programmes are usually chosen based on their proven track record of productivity and ability to work independently within the office environment. This was found to be true for the teleworkers in this study. The observation by one telework manager that ‘while they are more productive, they were shining stars when they were in the office’ is illustrative of the telework participants. A group of highly independent, above-average performers should be expected to show an overall higher productivity level than the group of ‘average’ performers who remain in the office (Nilles, 1991a).

The length of time that the reporting organization had been involved in telework should be considered when evaluating claims of increased productivity. Many organizations perform their first formal evaluation of a telework pilot project after completion of the first six months of the programme. The interviews found that administrators and managers of telework programmes contend that a minimum of one year's worth of data is necessary to measure performance changes effectively. The novelty of working from the home may result in an initial burst of productivity due to the increased motivation of the participants as well as the pressure put upon the participants by non-telecommuting peers to make the project successful and thus, expandable. Administrators reported that the extended period prior to evaluation is necessary to allow norms to be established within the telecommuting workforce.

Teleworkers reported initial instability in their level of effectiveness due to the uncertainty of what could be accomplished in the period away from the office. A tendency to bring home ‘too much’ was common for fear of ‘running out of things to do’. This resulted in a feeling of pressure to complete all work brought home. Although most teleworkers participate in telework orientation sessions, initial confusion was still common across the organization. However, once workers become comfortable with their own telework ‘pace’, a level of stability is reached which is considered to be more productive than that of the office environment.

An important consideration in evaluating claims of higher productivity is the increased level of planning necessary prior to days out of the office. Workers usually make very detailed plans as to exactly what they expect to be working on while telecommuting. This focus on work details prior to beginning work contributes to the level of productivity experienced while actually working from home. Increased productivity would likely occur if better planning was performed by non-teleworkers within the office environment.

One of the most controversial subjects encountered during the research was that of the length of a standard telecommuting work day. One organization has a strict policy of eight-hour work-days for all salaried employees including those involved in telework. Regardless of the amount of work accomplished in a day, the teleworker must continue the pace until a full work-day is complete. Fifteen of the teleworkers (60 per cent) reported working a ‘standard’ eight-to-five or nine-to-five day while 10 (40 per cent) reported that the length of their workday ‘varies’. Nine of the teleworkers (36 per cent) felt that a disadvantage of their participation in telecommuting was that they ‘worked too many hours’. Teleworkers felt that if they produce eight hours worth of work in seven hours, the extra hour, or some portion of that hour, should be given to them as personal time. An organization’s inability to recognize this concern could result in a feeling of exploitation among the teleworkforce over an extended period of time.

Aside from these concerns, teleworkers and managers of teleworkers agree that productivity gains directly resulting from telework are being achieved in their organizations. In addition, they encourage administrators to be cautious of creating feelings of exploitation among the workforce by offering some form of compensation for performance that exceeds norms established by the general employee population across the organization.

## Employee tenure

Claims of decreased employee turnover were found in all the organizations. All the telework managers reported cases where telecommuting was a decisive factor in retaining an otherwise departing employee. In the majority of these cases, the employee considered leaving the company because their spouse was relocated; however, since telecommuting was available, they were able to remain. Due to the uniqueness and flexibility of the programme, telecommuters expressed a desire to remain with their respective organizations. One telework manager stated that, 'Our programmers on telecommuting seem to stay with us longer than the ones that work in the office'. Another reported, 'One person told me that he had another, rather attractive job offer. The only reason that he didn't leave was our telecommuting program'. Perhaps the most significant observation of the impact of telecommuting programmes on turnover was, 'I feel this program allows us to keep our most talented people that might have gone to other firms'.

Administrators of telework programmes cite decreased turnover as one of the most desirable long-term benefits of telework to the organization. The cost of replacing mid-level employees ranges from \$100 000 to \$150 000 in most US corporations (Nilles, 1991b). In response to the decrease in the overall supply of qualified technical workers and the cost of replacing workers, any programme that can avoid employee turnover is extremely valuable. The availability of telework has allowed employees who would normally be forced to leave the organization the flexibility to remain. Spouse relocation and the increased flexibility for workers to adapt to special personal needs were cited as the most common problems that telework can help to overcome.

Elimination of commuting time when involved in telework also affects both employee turnover and absenteeism. One Los Angeles telecommuter reported a daily commute of up to two hours each way when travelling to and from work during peak traffic periods. Telecommuting has allowed this individual to reduce stress significantly by avoiding this situation a few days each week. Elimination of commuting appears to be one of the most influential factors in teleworkers' decisions to remain with their organizations.

## Employee absenteeism

Every administrator and manager of telework programmes reported a lower absentee rate among teleworkforcers compared with workers as a whole. Teleworkers supported these statements by admitting that the likelihood that they call in sick for work had become significantly less since becoming active in telework. Teleworkers universally stated that the likelihood that they would miss a complete day of work significantly decreased due to the flexibility offered by telework. Many claimed that they had worked productively while feeling ill and if they could not have worked from home, they would have called in sick on some occasions. Teleworkers also stated that they were less likely to miss partial days of work due to personal or family-related needs. These observations may require further analysis. While one telework manager stated, 'My feeling is that even if the person is too ill to come to the office they will do some work at home', another felt, 'this is hard to measure. Yes, there has been a decrease, but I'm not sure that they would call in sick. How would I know?'

Study participants cited a variety of factors contributing to the decrease in absentee rates. Teleworkers agree that the flexible nature of the telework arrangement has allowed them to adjust their work schedules to accommodate various appointments, family-related needs and other personal activities requiring attention during the normal working day. Teleworkers also stated that they were more inclined to work at least a partial day when feeling ill. This was attributed to the ability to alternate work and rest and to stop work completely when feeling overwhelmed.

Telework administrators reported cases where the telework alternative was offered to employees in lieu of short-term disability status or in order to decrease the length of time spent in that status. This was especially popular in instances where extended leave was requested to accommodate a disabling injury or even pregnancy.

This research indicates that a decrease in absenteeism can be expected as a direct result of telework, and that this decrease in teleworker absenteeism contributes to the increase in productivity in the telecommuting environment. In spite of these findings, it appears that more work may be required to define and measure absenteeism in the telework environment.

## Management style

Most of the participants in the study indicated that initial concerns over the most effective management style for teleworkers was reconciled by adopting a ‘management by objective’ approach to management across the programme. Administrators felt that the management of a remote workforce required an approach that could accommodate the unique circumstances involved in telework. An increased focus on results-oriented management styles was associated with adoption of telework programmes. All contended that management by objective was anticipated to be the accepted style of management for telecommuting projects. In addition, the majority of managers and teleworkers interviewed participated in either formal or informal training sessions on the subject of management by objective prior to being permitted to participate in the programme.

The management by objective approach provides teleworkers with agreed upon objectives expected to be completed in a given timeframe. The effective attainment of these objectives becomes the criteria by which performance evaluations are conducted. The concept of management by objective was well received in a majority of the cases encountered. There was a single exception to this management style. The telecommuting administrator involved attributed this to the nature of management within a large bureaucracy such as a city government organization. He stated that a majority of the managers had been with the city for most, if not all, of their careers and had become rather resistant to any change that challenged the ‘old ways’ which had proven effective over the years. This resistance to change has had an overall negative effect on the establishment of their telecommuting programme.

Several managers of teleworkers noted that there was an initial tendency to micro-manage teleworkers in an attempt to keep track of their remote work activities. They indicated that although management by objective training had taken place, there is often an initial feeling of insecurity and distrust which causes them to continually ‘check in’ with teleworkers to ensure their productivity. This tendency to micro-manage, however, decreases over time.

Administrators of telework programmes admit that because most teleworkers are selected for participation due to proven performance, they are unlikely to require a high degree of management attention to remain productive. They contend that a management by objective approach is most effective with employees of this type due to the ‘self-motivating’ nature of their work ethic. An exception to this philosophy was one organization which requires that teleworkers keep a detailed daily work log when working from home in order to justify each hour of their time. This approach to managing teleworkers greatly enhances the potential for micro-management and can have a detrimental impact on the effectiveness of the teleworker.

Teleworkers found the results-oriented nature of the management-by-objective style to be effective. Given the trust of management to attain pre-defined objectives effectively, teleworkers experienced much better relationships with supervisors. The trust aspect also provides an attitude boost in perceptions of quality of work life.

All participants reported an overwhelmingly positive experience with the management-by-objective approach. Consistent with the literature, managers reported that management-by-objective made them better overall managers. This can be attributed to the fact that they focus on work results instead of their perception of productivity.

## Non-salary related expenses

All the telework administrators found that non-salary related expenses to the organization were reduced as a direct result of telework. Several telework programme administrators related experiences where the offer of telework reduced or completely avoided costs associated with short-term medical disability (usually due to minor injury or pregnancy). These cases confined workers to their homes but did not affect their ability to perform work-related tasks effectively. No data on stress-related medical claims or lawsuits filed related to discrimination and/or harassment involving participants in the programme was available. Several administrators pointed out that a relationship did, most likely, exist between telework and frequency of these occurrences but none could substantiate their opinions. Although all of those interviewed agreed that there is a great potential for employer savings in this area, none could cite incidents other than those mentioned above.

Overall, our research indicated that telework administrators feel that a relationship between telework and non-salary related expenses to the firm does exist. But, additional research is necessary to investigate this belief and quantify these benefits.

## Conclusions and recommendations

The research method used to gather data for this analysis was found to be effective in terms of soliciting candid opinions of those involved in telework. Participants in the research were very cooperative and willing to provide detailed data regarding their experiences with telecommuting. The semi-structured interview approach, using questions based on a review of relevant literature, gave the researchers a wealth of information for better understanding the pitfalls as well as the benefits from telecommuting. While the observations of the subjects supported current wisdom in the field, their responses provide major insight into the functioning of telecommuting in their organizations.

The most significant conclusions that firms have reached during planning and operation of their telecommuting programmes include:

\- Participants must remain attached to the corporate culture through required

days in the office and participation in employee focus groups.

\- Productivity/effectiveness measurements by which success can be evaluated must be established. Productivity should increase as a direct result of telecommuting.

\- Employee tenure and decreased absenteeism can result from the widespread availability of telework options.

\- Management by objective is felt to be the most effective management style in a telework environment. Both employees and supervisors should be properly trained in the technique.

\- Certain non-salary related expenses to the firm can be reduced as a result of telework programmes.

In addition, organizations considering the telework option should restrict its availability to only those jobs which will benefit from the telecommuting environment. Many firms today require that employees and supervisors who wish to participate in telework present a written justification outlining exactly how telecommuting will increase the efficiency of a particular job.

Issues that need additional research include: the long-term effects that employee detachment might have on the employee and, consequently, on the general effectiveness of the organization; what mechanisms, beyond existing ones, can be used to combat this detachment; how productivity can and should be measured; how to insure that higher-performing teleworkers are not exploited through longer hours and/or lack of recognition for increased productivity in the same number of hours; what absenteeism means and how it can be measured among the telecommuting workforce; and the effects of a non-resident workforce on the legal expenses of an organization. With the increasing number of lawsuits being filed against employers, especially with regard to discrimination and harassment, it would seem likely that a workforce virtually ‘unaware’ of office politics would be less likely to participate in or become a victim of discrimination or harassment.

There is debate on the role of technology in the advancement of telecommuting programmes. Katz (1987) observes that the growth in telecommuting ‘can be attributed, in part, to the proliferation of low-cost personal computers, electronic messaging networks, and office automation’. Contrarily, Risman and Tomaskovic-Devey (1989) contend that, ‘despite the technological feasibility of workplace flexibility that microcomputers create, little reorganization of American industry has occurred. Instead, the managerial concern with employee control has stymied such changes.’ The growing international popularity of telecommuting programmes may allow us better to address this question. Will growth in technological capabilities in countries that do not currently possess the levels of technology available in the USA facilitate and thus cause increases in telecommuting or will management styles and cultures of these countries dictate the way that work is performed?

One additional interesting issue regarding the international implications of telecommuting does not deal with the status or issues of telecommuting within a country but rather the potential for telecommuting to transcend international borders. To date, the expansion of geographic flexibility of the workforce has been fairly limited. With the increasing expansion of telecommunications infrastructures, some organizations may, in the future, have teleworkers throughout the world.

Telecommuting is potentially one of the most effective white-collar workplace programmes recently introduced to corporate America. The potential benefits to both the employee and employer have been well documented. Organizations who recognize these benefits and pursue the telework alternative aggressively can find a resulting competitive advantage in addressing the workforce related issues that will be confronting managers through the next decade. But this pursuit must consider the potential pitfalls and be prepared to address them.
