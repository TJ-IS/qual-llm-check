# Market as opportunity? Case studies in outsourcing information technology and services

Leslie Willcocks and Guy Fitzgerald\*

Fellow in Information Management, Oxford Institute of Information Management, Templeton College, University of Oxford, Kennington, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

Outsourcing information technology (IT) and services has received little academic research attention in the UK. The paper details four case studies on sourcing decisions made by UK based organizations. It also draws upon preliminary evidence from 16 further cases to arrive at an assessment of the most favourable conditions under which IT and services may be outsourced. It finds that the management of certain contractual and non-contractual elements of outsourcing have critical parts to play in the subsequent effectiveness or otherwise of a decision to outsource. Strategic systems and systems differentiating the organization from its competition tend not to be outsourced, but the definition of such systems is heavily influenced by the political contexts in which decisions are being made. The paper suggests that the issue is better cast as not whether to outsource IT, but as whether or not, and if so how, to use the market for IT and associated services for organizational advantage. The case study findings enable a working through of this research question.

Keywords: outsourcing, information technology, decision factors, contract management

In the UK the outsourcing of information technology (IT) and associated services is a rapidly growing phenomenon, the market expanding at 20 per cent per annum to a predicted 1995 revenue of £1013 million (International Data Corporation, 1991). Additionally most of public sector IT is being open to market testing, the outcomes from which may greatly increase this figure (Willcocks, 1994). Outsourcing IT has also been the subject of considerable debate, not only at the level of how best it can be done (see for example Earl, 1991), but also in terms of its implications for organizational forms and management (Grant, 1992; Huber, 1993; Quinn, 1992). However, to date, the UK phenomenon has not been the subject of detailed academic research, unlike for example the USA (for example Buck-Lew, 1992; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1992; Loh and Venkatraman, 1992a,b).

A research organization, specializing in reports for business, commissioned an independent study to address this gap, and the present research was designed to address their research questions and additional issues identified by the authors. The present paper reports on this ongoing research by providing details of four of the 20 case studies that have so far been completed. The approach at this stage is essentially exploratory in nature. The details of these case studies are related to other issues emerging from our research so far and the literature currently available on experiences of outsourcing.

In what follows, IT is used to refer to the supply of information-based technologies. Information systems (IS) is used as organizational applications, more or less IT-based, designed to deliver on the information needs of the organization and defined stakeholders. To introduce much needed conceptual clarity we use the phrase 'IT outsourcing' narrowly — it is the commissioning of third-party management of IT assets, people and/or activities to required result. This can and often does involve a degree of transfer of assets and staff to the third-party organization. So defined, outsourcing does not exhaust the ways in which markets can be used. Thus a key distinction can be made between contracts that specify a service and result which the market is to provide ('outsourcing'); and contracts which call for the market to provide resources to be deployed under the buyer's management and control. Elsewhere we have described the latter as 'insourcing' contracts (Feeny et al., 1993).

## Research questions and methodology

A range of studies have already been carried out on outsourcing IT and related services. However there are virtually no published studies of the IT outsourcing phenomenon in the UK context. Lacity and Hirschheim (1992, 1993) have focused on total outsourcing decisions in the USA. Loh and Venkatraman (1992a,b,c) have produced a series of studies looking at stock market reaction to IT outsourcing; the sources of influence on the adoption of outsourcing as an organizational innovation; and the relationship between the degree of outsourcing and business and IT cost structures and IT performance. There has been work on the global outsourcing of information systems and processing services (Apte, 1990; Apte and Mason, 1993). There has been a great deal of theoretical work, amongst the most recent being that on the outsourcing decision structure and on efficient outsourcing relationships (Clark and Zmud, 1993; Klepper, 1993). A number of detailed case studies have been produced on US corporations (see for example Buck-Lew, 1992; Huber, 1993; Moad, 1993). More recently still there has been a series of case studies produced from various European countries (for example Auwers and Deschoolmeester, 1993; Griese, 1993; Heinzl, 1993; Saaksjarvi, 1993).

The overall aim of the present research is to complement this work by investigating, by means of an empirical study, the outsourcing of IT/IS activities in the UK. In particular we seek to identify patterns of outsourcing in terms of resources, decisions and activities being outsourced, the reasons for, and risks of outsourcing, criteria for success, the difficulties being experienced, how they are and can be handled. This paper presents limited findings on these issues in the form of four detailed case studies and preliminary evidence from 16 further case histories. A further objective of the main research project, not dealt with in this paper, is to identify where outsourcing has contributed to new forms of organization and new ways of managing those forms.

We will outline here our approach to the overall research project before introducing the frameworks developed for analysing the case study evidence. Mintzberg (1979) suggests that successful research requires both systematic and anecdotal data, i.e. both hard and soft information, the hard — to uncover relationships — and the soft — to help explain such relationships. The researchers are sympathetic to this view and adopted a research methodology that attempted to collect both kinds of information. To this end, and aware of the limitations but also the use of the approaches available, it was decided that the study should integrate a variety of investigative techniques. Firstly, there would be a series of telephone interviews based on a relatively open-ended set of questions to validate the parameters for the study. A set of frameworks and hypotheses would then be developed for testing. Secondly, a detailed postal questionnaire was to be administered. Thirdly, this should be followed up by a series of semi-structured interviews with a range of participants involved in outsourcing, and finally a number of detailed cases would be developed. Thus the methodology sought to gain triangulation at two levels: firstly at the level of the range of techniques employed; secondly, in interview sets and cases studies, at the level of targeting respondents from senior management, IT, vendor and user backgrounds.

The major objective of the telephone interviews was to establish rapidly whether the assumptions that we made about outsourcing based on our own experiences and from the literature survey were viable in the context of the UK. We selected large companies that were more likely to have IT outsourcing experience from different economic sectors. Firstly we asked people to react to our stated approach and the list of the issues we proposed to address. These had been sent to the participants in advance. Had we addressed issues of relevance? Were there others that should be included? What could we leave out? We then asked the following questions concerning their experience of outsourcing: Have you outsourced any IT functions? If not, why not? What have you outsourced and on what basis? What contract stages are you at? What has been successful and what difficulties have you experienced? What was the process of making outsourcing decisions and who was involved? Has it resulted in or been linked with new forms of organization and management?

Sixteen telephone interviews were conducted with senior managers responsible for IT departments in large UK organizations. The interviews lasted on average about 20 minutes each and established that all except one person was very keen to talk about their outsourcing experience and that it was a topic of great current relevance. (The one person that refused to talk was under a company embargo.) They were keen to hear about the research and their experience suggested it was a complex issue that was not easily navigated. They felt they needed to know more and would value hearing of the experience of others. Almost all offered to be a case study for the research. The outcome was that our assumptions were largely appropriate but there were some areas that we should focus upon more directly. Firstly that outsourcing was not just an IT issue and that the outsourcing culture of the organization needed investigation, secondly that external drivers were important, for example the financial health of the company, its competitive position, government pressures; thirdly that there was a discrepancy between those that made the decisions on outsourcing (senior, mainly non-IT management) and those that had responsibility for finding out about and implementing outsourcing, and fourthly that we should investigate issues of contracts and how disputes between parties are resolved. In general terms we also discovered the varieties of types and activities that were outsourced and that it was not a homogeneous phenomenon. These findings helped to shape the subsequent stages of the research.

The questionnaire was devised to establish the quantitative issues and give a picture of what the situation concerning outsourcing is like in the UK. We also attempted to investigate a few softer issues of perception concerning success, key issues and reflections. The questionnaire was amended as a result of the telephone interviews although a number of the issues highlighted were not amenable to investigation in the questionnaire but could only be explored in the interviews and cases. The questionnaire was piloted on a small group of senior managers, and revised in March 1993. Targeted at senior IT managers/directors in medium and large sized organizations it has been dispatched to 1200 organizations. The conduct of and results from the questionnaire is not the subject of the present paper since, at the time of writing, we are still receiving returns from respondents.

We have conducted 20 sets of interviews based on a semi-structured set of questions. These interviews will form the basis of detailed case studies. The present four case studies represent preliminary analyses of the data returned from selected case studies. Interviews were held in the organizations and were scheduled for one-and-a-half hours each. All the interviews were recorded. The interviews reported here were with senior managers responsible for IT management in their organizations. The interviews have been backed up by the collection and analysis of relevant documentation, including copies of outsourcing contracts.

Initial evidence is limited in extent and is basically from pilot interviews, early responses to the questionnaire, and 20 sets of case study interviews in large UK organizations. It is clear however that a majority of organizations in the survey sample are thinking of outsourcing IT or have done so to some or even a significant extent already. The character of outsourcing also seems to have changed from that portrayed in the late 1980s literature, with a wider range of technologies and IT services, and in some cases a major part of the IT function being put out to contract. There are also examples of organizations in several sectors, especially the oil, defence and public sectors, contemplating or signed up to total outsourcing, but in many cases a common critical factor seems to be a significant corporate financial problem being undergone. At the same time other companies reveal a wariness about long-term outsourcing contracts, and seem to be preferring more flexible contracts of 3–5 years duration. One interesting finding is that some companies outsource their older systems for a relatively short period in order to buy time and release resources for the in-house IT department to develop new or replacement systems.

The literature review, refined by our pilot interviews and early data, has allowed us to develop some working ideas and frameworks for testing. A number of factors do emerge in relationship to the degree to which organizations outsource IT. These factors include the cost reduction attractions of the outsourcing option, the stability of the IT activity being outsourced, the desire to access IT expertise in short supply, IT as a business differentiator or not, access to new technologies, and dissatisfaction with the in-house service and/or its cost. This is a tentative list of merely the most cited factors so far. Our evidence is not yet robust concerning the relative priority of these factors, or the conditions under which such a priority would form. Many of the issues, however, emerge from the case studies detailed below.

An emerging framework we have developed and would wish to test further is one which identifies six factors as critical determinants of successful outsourcing decisions (see Feeny et al., 1993). The factors emerge from reviewing the literature on sourcing decisions, previous research by ourselves and colleagues, and pilot interviews. The relevant factors are:

1. The degree to which there is sufficient in-house experience relevant to IT use and intentions, coupled with the degree of stability/certainty associated with the technology (i.e. 'technology maturity'). This is suggested by research carried out at the Oxford Institute of Information Management by Feeny et al. (1989).

2. The degree of business uncertainty through environmental change and changing requirements. This influences the degree to which technical requirements can be predicted and known. High business uncertainty would suggest retaining flexibility through an in-house function or a flexible long term contract with a strategic partner.

3. The degree of systems interconnectedness and complexity. Some IT/IS activities may have simple interfaces with the rest of the organization's systems and with business users, and so be easy to isolate and contract out. Others may have complex and extensive interactions with a wide range of other systems, implying that there will be high switching costs involved.

4. The degree to which IT is a business differentiator (i.e. 'core competence') or a commodity that can best be outsourced if an external agency can do it cheaper than the in-house IT function. This is suggested in research by Quinn et al. (1990) and Quinn (1992).

5. The activity's relationship to business strategy. Strategic activities are integral to an organization's achievement of goals and critical to its existing and future business direction. Organizations need to maintain control of these. Useful activities can make incremental contributions to the bottom line. As one example a major European ferry company we have researched retains in-house control and IT skills relating to its central reservation system. This handles 10–12000 reservations a day and its operation is critical to present and future business success.

6. In-house capability relative to that available on the external market. Even though there may be high internal capability, increasingly the external market may well be able to match the in-house skills of even the biggest user organizations. This is suggested as a critical factor in the research of Rands (1991), in investigating software make/buy decisions.

In different combinations these factors would point to a range of decisions on types of relationship with vendors and what resources, activities and decisions can be effectively outsourced. These issues will be investigated below by means of the four illustrative case studies. As the research progresses it may well be that this framework has to be modified, and indeed different factors may emerge as the more critical determinants of effective outsourcing decisions.

A final framework we have identified and wish to test is one that applies the concepts of IT as core competence and strategic differentiator in business terms (see for example Drucker, 1991; Prahalad and Hamel, 1991; Scott Morton, 1989). Where large amounts of IT resource, personnel and activity are outsourced it may well be that the shape of IT management could move to operating three roles. These would be the ‘business consultant’ (to retain control over strategic decisions), the integrator/specialist (to run and coordinate in-house IT) and the ‘informed buyer’ to manage outsourcing contracts (Feeny et al., 1993). The research will investigate the degree to which this is occurring in UK organizations and is recognized as a viable model for managing new forms of organization. This issue is dealt with in so far as it arises in the following case studies, but will receive some attention also in the subsequent discussion.

## Case A — the NHS Regional Health Authority

## Background

This case shows how a period of radical organizational change necessitated, but was also used as an opportunity for rethinking IT/IS needs and arrangements. It details the application of a selective outsourcing approach, and the rationales for the decisions made.

From the mid-1980s the National Health Service (NHS) in the UK has been the subject of radical initiatives to introduce general management, devolve organization structures, and introduce competitive tendering and market testing into its services. In 1989 a government White Paper 'Working For Patients' proposed widespread reforms along these lines, but whose delivery would be heavily dependent on the implementation of effective information systems. At the same time the content of these initiatives — in particular the encouragement to privatize, market test and competitive tender — also fell upon how IT/IS was to be organized and run. By 1990 nine of the 14 Regional Health Authorities (RHAs) had privatized their computing operations through outsourcing or through management buyouts. However, these initiatives have a history of mixed success with privatizations in Wessex and West Midlands RHA, for example, recording high subsequent costs, and little user satisfaction (Gibbs, 1991; Collins, 1993; Cross, 1993; Kelsey, 1993). At the same time the government was planning for 1991 more detailed further radical reorganization of all RHAs. It is in this context that the case study RHA considered its sourcing options in 1990–91. As at 1992 the RHA served a population of 3.5 million, had an annual expenditure of £1.5 billion, 51464 employees, 22 major hospitals and 2033 family doctors.

## Details

On the technological front the central RHA IT service was from 1986 moving away from providing to the 13 District Health Authorities and its hospitals a batch bureau service on payroll, personnel and financial reporting systems. These were being replaced by interactive central services based on an ICL VME mainframe, requiring a mainframe data centre, a wide area network (WAN), service level management and tailored support services. Local hospital systems were also being developed in this period. However in April 1991 the government introduced radical organizational reforms that cut across these technological arrangements. These led to a devolution of management and responsibility, and the introduction of purchaser-provider contractual relationships across the NHS. In the case study region the 13 DHAs stayed as purchasers but in addition many of their powers were passed to 29 newly created NHS trust hospitals (providers of services) and seven Family Health Service Authorities (purchasers of services). Structurally this would suggest the need to develop IT/IS responsibilities, but also the technological infrastructure. However the reality was that many of the 49 separate organizations were ill-equipped to handle IT/IS responsibilities and would need to continue to purchase services and support from the RHA IT function. In practice more than half of the Districts took responsibility for their computing in the 1990–91 period, while the rest went 'live' on the new payroll, manpower and finance systems run by the RHA computing centre. However it was clear that mainframe systems were no longer part of the long-term strategic direction of the NHS.

A number of options faced the RHA Information Systems function. One was to contract the whole unit out to an outsourcing vendor. The others were to convert the unit into a trading agency to sell its services in the region but also on the open market. The others were to contract out parts of the unit, or go for a management buyout. As at summer 1990 the RHA preferred to sell the unit to an outsourcing vendor because it was short of money due to a drop in land values. However the unit had no forward contracts, there was little likelihood of profit and there may have been problems in persuading District, Health Service Authority and hospital users to use the services. The 110 staff and computing managers also had little enthusiasm for a management buyout. Moreover outsourcing vendors could probably not provide certain services requiring NHS-based knowledge and expertise.

An analysis of RHA IT/IS existing and required skills revealed the following picture. These were strong in the batch bureau area but this operation needed to be wound down by 1995. They were weak but growing in the data centre/service level agreement management area; stronger and growing in telecommunications; and strong in the applications support area. On applications development there were few in-house skills but the policy had been to move to package solutions. Project management, planning and consultancy had already been outsourced. As can be seen on human resources, staff were not highly skilled across all the activities undertaken by this RHA IT/IS function. In the face of this skills diagnosis and the changing context in which the RHA IT/IS function had to operate and offer services a selective outsourcing approach was adopted. A £9 million five year facilities management contract was given to a vendor for bureau/data centre operations and network support, with the cost recharged to the DHAs to whom these services were being supplied. Thirty-two of the 37 staff agreed to join the vendor. Originally the vendor wished to move the mainframe off-site to its own data centre to achieve economies of scale. However staff concerns about moving sites had to be addressed. Eventually the company agreed to stay on the original site, and in fact moved in an additional 30 staff from its other London sites. The ICL 3980 mainframe, still owned by the RHA, was moved to the vendor's data centre in December 1991 but was operated remotely from the RHA site. The estimated savings were £2 million on computer operating and maintenance costs. Certain other items were outsourced separately on ad hoc contracts. These were in project management, applications development and on specialist items. It was felt that applications development needed no great specialist skills or NHS knowledge since the policy was to use packages already available wherever possible.

However in certain areas it was felt to be important to retain the considerable healthcare systems skills and knowledge within the NHS region. To achieve this a RHA owned, customer funded trading agency consisting of 60 staff was established for two years to provide application support, network development and new services required by users. Its services were made available within the region but also across the NHS. On application support it was felt that outsourcing this would cost more, provide no additional quality but would provide additional uncertainty to the service. On the WAN development this was identified as a strategic activity since the WAN was to be implemented across the region. Moreover the in-house staff did not possess enough knowledge and expertise on this area to be able to manage an outsourcing contract. It seemed a better policy to build up internal expertise in this area with a possibility of offering this expertise as a service at a later date on the open market to other NHS regions. Certain functions, identified as core, were not outsourced, but were retained in-house as RHA managed and funded. Thus planning, liaison and consultancy — previously outsourced — were now brought in-house, while standard setting and the planning of training were kept as core functions. Interestingly many staff for core functions were recruited from outside — an example of ‘in-sourcing’. Subsequently this unit has developed strong teamwork and has been made responsible for national pilots of new systems in other NHS regions.

These arrangements have been in operation for over 18 months at the time of writing. They would seem to be successful, though with some problem areas. The trading agency has a large cultural legacy to deal with but its progress was helped by freedom from having to operate the mainframe and dealing with large cost overheads. The trial was extended for a third year. Users have tended to test its value against private sector companies, and the agency has tended to focus on assisting the weaker IT units in the region. The outsourcing arrangement solved a lot of old problems, raised service availability to higher levels, and reduced costs, though the latter could have been done in-house. It also freed up in-house staff to focus on more strategic areas. Some problems emerged over the vendor wanting to reduce its own costs and also over the details of some aspects of the contract that needed to be made more clear and revised on some issues. There are still a lot of interface issues outstanding on the management of the services offered by the trading agency, the outsourcing vendor and the end-user involvement and influence upon service levels and quality. As the WAN comes on-stream and the mainframe is wound down by 1996 it may be that WAN operations could also be outsourced, if this seems the most cost-effective route.

## Summary

There are a number of distinctive features in this case. Theoretically a total outsourcing option could have been adopted. However a major sticking point was staff reluctance on this and the need to make future arrangements that in-house staff could live with. This emerges as a critical issue commonly mentioned across the 18 organizations we have so far researched. The importance of differentiating between a strategic and a useful or commodity IT/IS activity is also observed, with strategic activities being retained in-house, and only the latter outsourced. However, there is the possibility of outsourcing network operations once these have been developed and are understood and controllable by the in-house function. This links with two ideas thrown up by our research so far. The first is the need, when outsourcing, to retain in-house certain management, and possibly some technical skill, capability. Elsewhere we have suggested that the minimum management skills would be: ability to help and specify and manage ‘rightsourcing’ (the right mixture of contracting in, contracting out and in-house activities); ability to work with business management and specify IT requirements over time; and ability to track assess and interpret changing IT capabilities and relate these to the needs of the organization (Feeny et al., 1993). The second is the concept of technology maturity — the degree to which the technology is stable/unstable and the degree to which there is relevant in-house experience with the technology being utilized. The case endorses the findings of Feeny et al. (1989) that in situations of low maturity an ‘insourcing’ approach is preferable, as in the case of the RHA core functions, and in retaining in-house control over WAN development. Outsourcing is more pertinent where high technology maturity exists, as in the case of the mainframes/data centre in the case, and perhaps future WAN operations.

## Case B — a major high street retailer

## Background

In 1989 this company was heading for a financial loss and was deemed to have lost strategic direction. The business formula that had proved successful in the 1970s and 1980s had not been changed and was no longer proving effective. A new chief executive was appointed to turn the company around. He put into effect a threefold strategy. Firstly he removed levels in the hierarchy, secondly he decentralized the organization and thirdly, and in this context most importantly, he focused on the core competencies/skills of the business. These core skills were identified as essentially buying and selling, and from this analysis the philosophy of outsourcing was developed. The argument put forward was that only core activities needed to be world class and that non-core activities could/should never be delivered by in-house staff to world class standard. Thus the corporate philosophy on outsourcing was evolved with anything that was not buying and selling becoming a potential candidate for outsourcing. For example distribution has been outsourced and has been reduced in size from 250 staff to three; quality control, packaging, and design activities have followed a similar pattern. Security and cleaning are currently in the process of being outsourced and the only non-core activity still in-house is finance. The company is now one that manages contracts and demands services.

## Details

The outsourcing of IT thus fitted into the overall strategic thinking but it still needed to be shown that, if a particular activity was outsourced, a better service would be provided. The feeling of senior management was that the company had never properly leveraged the benefit of what was basically good IT. Outsourcing was seen as providing the opportunity for improvement by introducing rigour, and more detail and thought into requirements and specifications. The issue of whether IT could provide competitive advantage was addressed. It was concluded that it was not the provision of IT itself but the way that it was used that might provide advantage; that could be harnessed irrespective of whether the IT itself was in-house or out.

The decision was made to outsource the whole of IT including the data centre, PCs, development and support. Some time was spent discussing whether all or parts should be outsourced but it was felt that if the company really believed in the philosophy it had to be everything. Therefore there was negotiated what is essentially a single contract worth £11 million over a contract period of almost 11 years. The deal started in January 1993 and involved the transfer of 116 people to the vendor.

The process involved the selection of a shortlist of vendors, not by issuing an open invitation to tender but by selecting four vendors that the company, based on its existing knowledge, felt to be capable of handling such a contract. The company provided a brief to these four who were invited to provide an initial response. As a result of this response two of the vendors were dropped and one was put on hold whilst the other was invited to go into detail. The winning vendor was felt to best understand the objectives of the company, especially in the area of development rather than just assuming that it was a simple data centre deal with a few extras as some of the other companies apparently did. The selection was made on the basis of a compatible partner as well as competence. Indeed the notion of the partnership is currently being tested in a new area, with the vendor suggesting that they sell some IT equipment and software they have developed for the company to a third-party on a partnership basis.

Almost all the details of the company's performance requirements in IT had already been defined in detail over the past three years, especially the key requirements of their stores and for buying and merchandising. This was felt to be an essential prerequisite. A series of hard negotiations were completed and the contract agreed and drawn up with help from outside legal specialists (already outsourced — there is no legal department, only two legal people, one being the company secretary).

The benefits, although it is only the first year of operation, are estimated to be 35 per cent savings on the computing hardware, although the company do not regard this as very significant in the long term as the prices of machines are declining very rapidly anyway. For the rest it is about 5 or 6 per cent savings, but more importantly the company has shifted the bulk of £11 million from fixed to variable business overhead and given themselves financial, service and technical flexibility. For example, where the company previously had three years backlog of work in development, such work can now be begun within six months if so required because of the increased resources available from the vendor company. This would have been impossible for the company to achieve previously.

There are now only five IT jobs left in the company. One is the manager of the contract relationship. The other four are concerned with business development using IT, and getting close to the business. The transfer of people to the vendor is described by the company as having been remarkably smooth. The deals were relatively good, the contracts in terms and conditions were essentially the same as previously held, and people knew that the IT labour market had shifted over the previous few years from one favouring the employee to one where they were at some disadvantage. Some viewed the change as positive because IT was the core business of the vendor and they felt that there might be greater career opportunities with the vendor.

The company felt that pre-existing outsourcing experiences within the organization was very important and had helped to smooth the way for IT outsourcing. It enabled them to understand the importance of putting the effort into building the contract and getting it right. It gave them the confidence to go for a long contract and seek a partnership rather than base everything on price considerations. The length of the contract is relatively unusual these days but the company argues that if you are convinced by the philosophy and the deal why not ensure the benefits over the longer period. According to company respondents, short contracts are for companies that are experimenting and are not really convinced, longer contracts are for companies seeking partnership relations.

## Summary

This company is an example of one where outsourcing has been driven by the overall strategy of the company and not just for the cost-cutting reasons commonly encountered (although cost-savings were also felt to accrue). IT is not seen as core to the organization and therefore should be outsourced. The company has sought a partnership with the vendor rather than a transaction-by-transaction approach and has entered a long term contract to enable that partnership to flourish. Despite the notion of partnership this has not meant that the contract and the detail of performance measures and service levels were left open; in fact they were specified in great detail. Similar attention was paid to the transfer of personnel.

The company is still in the very early stages of the relationship and it remains to be seen what happens but the company's experience in other areas with outsourcing suggest to them that similar success will result. It will be interesting to discover whether this proves to be the case.

## Case C — multinational consumer products manufacturer

## Context

This company had a history of tight cost control long before the 1989–93 economic recession. After some years of vertical and horizontal integration the business returned via a series of divestments and acquisitions to focusing on its core businesses. This proved very successful in business terms. In this climate IT was regarded as a non-core back office activity and was subjected to rigorous cost containment pressures. As a result it was identified as a candidate for outsourcing.

## Details

In 1984 virtually the whole of its IT in the two European head offices was outsourced. The deal included all hardware and staff and also involved the taking over of accommodation. The hardware included two large mainframes, a number of minis, modems, communication lines and generators. A total of 190 staff were transferred. Transfers of staff and equipment included operators, programmers, systems analysts, help desks, PCs, word processors, hardware maintenance and training.

The negotiations for this contract were long and complex. One significant problem was that the company had not fully understood the need for fully defining to the necessary detail its required service levels; nor did it have a good feel for its own internal costs. Nevertheless a deal was done. A five-year contract was negotiated in 1985 with a get out clause after three years for a contract value of £8 million.

The vendor was viewed as providing good service levels in its fulfilment of the contract and certainly the service provided was regarded as an improvement on the company's own previous performance. However it became clear that the vendor was not able to make money on the contract in the way that it had been negotiated. As a result the vendor attempted to recoup its losses by increasing its charges wherever possible in grey areas of the contract and on any upgrades and new systems development that were the subject of new agreements within the contract. This resulted in continual wrangling between the company and the vendor. This wrangling absorbed a great deal of the time and effort by the company as did its system of billing its internal users.

The deal had been struck at a general level, rather than in close detail, and the company paid the vendor a large amount annually. The group that managed the contract then had the task of recouping this cost from the users of the resources in the product-based business units. This they did by introducing a billing system which was the subject of much dispute and contention. In the first year of operation the billing system failed to recover the necessary amount and the users were surcharged to make up the difference. In the second year the charges were increased and the users received a rebate. However the billing system was not a success and many of the users felt hard done by. Many users felt they were being charged more than they should have been, mainly because they were now paying true costs from which they had previously been protected. Some users experienced the high costs of upgrades and new developments where the vendor was trying to recoup its perceived losses, and others were finding that the costs of PCs from the vendor were very high compared with list prices. As a result there was great user dissatisfaction which overcame the benefits of undoubted service improvements that the vendor was providing in certain areas. As a result when the contract approached its three-year review the option to withdraw was taken.

The contract had been negotiated with such a situation in mind and it was supposed to provide for the returning in-house of the systems. However this proved impossible, mainly because the people were no longer available to return. Many of them had left the vendor or been absorbed into positions within the vendor from which they did not want to return.

In the event some of the users negotiated their own deals with the original vendor, some changed to an alternative vendor, whilst some developed their own in-house capabilities. Some found that by switching to mini or PC based systems (in-house or with new vendors) they could save themselves significant amounts of money, due to the changes in technology and the market place that had occurred during the three years of the original contract.

## Summary

Although this experience might be regarded as an example of an unsuccessful outsourcing venture the company does not view it completely in this light. The vendor is absolved by the company of blame because they performed well and proved that they could provide a successful service. The problem was that they underestimated the profitability of the contract. A learning point might be that it is wise to allow the vendor to make a reasonable return. The company was critical of itself for not being able to deal with its internal billing system effectively. It learnt that it should not do grand deals in this way but rather outsource, or not, on the basis of individual systems or projects. This way the businesses (rather than head office) cut the deals and they own the result. In this way the company, including head office, now has many successful outsourcing contracts. It should be noted that the company encountered higher switching costs for ending the deal than it had originally anticipated, that the contract and its delivery foundered on financial aspects rather than levels of service, and that this largely related to the looseness of the terms of the contract, and the internal failure to run acceptable pay-for-performance control and chargeback systems.

## Case D — head office IT at an international manufacturing company Background

This case illustrates outsourcing decisions made in a difficult business climate with cost reduction and outsourcing being ordained as board level management decisions. In this context the group head office needed to reduce its headcount greatly, and the head office IT function was not excluded from this dispensation. The case shows how an organization adopted a gradualist approach to outsourcing possibilities, and pursued an approach that maintained leverage over the outsourcing vendor.

In 1992 this company had a turnover of £2.6 billion and a profit before taxation and after exceptional items of £77 million. This compares with a profit of £152 million on similar revenue the previous year. The company had experienced difficult trading conditions in the 1990–93 period in all its major markets. Thirty-seven per cent of its revenue comes from Europe, 28 per cent from the USA, 16 per cent from the UK and the remainder from the rest of the world. An important part of radical reorganization plans was to reduce significantly the size of the UK head office in terms of activities and personnel. This was to be done by focusing on core activities only, outsourcing as many services as possible or devolving services into lower level business units throughout the group.

## Details

In the 1991–92 period outsourcing of IT/IS head office services was actively considered. This was a discrete area, that is services offered to head office only. A number of options presented themselves to the IT/IS management. One of these was to form a stand-alone company that would then sell its services back to head office as well as on the open market. A second was to manage down the operation and phase in outsourcing arrangements. Both were rejected as risky, with doubts about the feasibility of the latter, and the financial viability of the former. The decision was to totally outsource all operations and new development to a large, reputable supplier. This would involve transferring virtually all staff, and selling all operational IT assets to the vendor.

The IT/IS management looked to achieve several objectives through taking this approach. Firstly an outsourcing contract could permit a transitional period wherein the supplier ran old systems and equipment while downsizing and streamlining could be carried out. However an interesting feature in this case is that the development of new systems was also outsourced to the same vendor. These included office and network systems and special applications being moved from the mainframe to a PC network. Secondly outsourcing would permit a site move, including the transfer of non-head office applications, where relevant, to business units. These included pensions, investments, training, accounts, payroll systems. It would also, hopefully, bring in wider, relevant expertise from a large vendor while also providing enhanced career opportunities for the company's IT staff, job security, and a broadening of their skills. Finally, two major objectives were to reduce substantially IT/IS service costs at head office, while maintaining service levels in a period of major change.

A two-year contract was signed with a very large vendor, with a target to move off the IBM mainframe by December 1993. The vendor was selected on a number of criteria. These included multi-vendor expertise — the company had different technological bases throughout the group, and also wanted to retain flexibility about how future equipment requirements could be serviced. The vendor also had to have financial stability, location flexibility, wide geographic support across the world to match the company's own spread, networking expertise and support depth and breadth. Finally and critically, there had to be a close trusting relationship with the vendor, together with good terms and conditions of employment and career opportunities for transferred staff. Clearly all this implied a long-term strategic alliance type relationship, yet interestingly the initial contact was short term for discrete, head office systems only.

A major feature of this outsourcing arrangement is the critical requirement to reduce risks commonly associated with ‘total outsourcing’. Thus a two-year contract with the option to renew responds to findings on the deterioration of service levels and costs savings after the fifth year of many long-term contracts (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1992; Symons, 1993). A critical concern was to avoid staffing problems and resentment. All staff were unionized but industrial relations problems were avoided. Thirty-seven staff moved to the vendor on the same conditions or better, three retired, while three key staff were retained at head office and operations research staff were absorbed into other parts of the company. A great deal of attention was given to: communicating with staff what was going to happen; their feedback; and individual discussions with the vendor. Much of this was handled by the in-house IS manager who was given, and accepted, the option of joining the vendor as account manager for the contract once it had been signed. This meant that staff who knew the business, including their ex-company manager, would be largely responsible for delivering the contract, while bringing in additional expertise formerly not available to the company for developing new systems. This avoided the dangers of becoming over-reliant on external staff who did not know the business, and who could not get sufficient knowledge in such a short contractual period. Another approach, as for example at First Fidelity Bank, USA, would have been to minimize risk by retaining the employment contracts of its systems developers (Moad, 1993). However this was seen as unnecessary in this case because of the quality of the relationship with the staff and vendor and the limited impact if the contract went wrong.

Risk was further reduced by retaining certain key activities, skills and roles in-house. Responsibility for IT/IS strategy was retained in-house. Also some critical skills relating to the ability to maintaining common standards and viewing IT/IS integration possibilities across the group. Also the ability to identify the IT/IS needs of business managers. Contact management and regular monitoring of vendor performance was also a critical set of skills identified for in-house management. This arose partly from disappointing experiences with outsourcing in Swedish and US companies where such skills had not been retained. Furthermore, leverage was retained over the supplier by offering the possibility of further work not just at head office but throughout the group, dependent on a record of success together with the establishment of a strong working relationship with that preferred vendor. Thus the company sought to gain many of the advantages of a strategic alliance type relationship without putting at risk critical systems, at least in the early stages, and having a short time period for feeling out the relationship while getting clear cost savings. Furthermore, while the company sold its existing IT assets to the vendor, the new systems being developed were the result of heavy investment by the company, who would own them, thus avoiding the risk of having to recreate them or buy them from the vendor should the contract not be renewed.

## Summary

At time of writing this ‘total’ outsourcing is considered successful from both vendor and company perspectives. The cost savings were real and substantial, the company has been able to migrate its systems while reducing significantly the size of the head office IT function, there is a developing relationship with the vendor who also sees the possibilities of further business throughout the group on a worldwide basis as a preferred supplier or even the formation of a strategic alliance at a future date. An interesting feature is the decision to outsource applications development — often seen by other respondents as a risky decision and usually avoided. In another company we found the outsourcing of applications development to have been disappointing because the vendor did not understand the business, and business managers were unable to specify their requirements clearly enough. In the present case this was avoided to some extent because a discrete section of the business was the subject of new systems application and the IT staff were knowledgeable of that line of business, while the new expertise required was acquired on almost a ‘contracted-in’ model thus building up the learning of former in-house staff, in a situation where technological, but not business, maturity was fairly low.

Further interesting features of the case relate to the strategy for managing the vendor adopted by the company. The aim was to retain flexibility on future technological options, while reducing costs, ensuring service levels and retaining leverage over the supplier. The approach adopted would seem to be carefully thought out and merit the title of ‘strategy’ whereas many other respondents in our research seemed to move safely and tactically rather than plan out the approach to form what could be called an outsourcing strategy.

## Discussion

A tentative approach to making sense of our results so far is shown in Figure 1. We will discuss Figure 1 in the light of our findings from not only the cases detailed in this paper, but also from preliminary analyses of the other 16 cases we have investigated to date. Figure 1 is based on a more concise version of the six factors framework for testing detailed earlier in this paper. The objective behind Figure 1 is to provide a mapping device for the results of the case studies. For mapping purposes only, we have conflated the differentiator-commodity and strategic-useful parameters into a 'core-commodity' continuum. Additionally we have conflated the 'technology maturity' and 'in-house capability relative to that available on the market' dimensions into a high-low 'relevant experience with technology' continuum.

The four case studies detailed above are positioned on the framework in Figure 1. Case A shows a set of decisions that have been deemed largely successful. In the case of A1 mainframes and data centres were outsourced together with staff. The staff had high experience with the relevant technology, a fairly short term contract was signed to minimize risk but also because the systems had limited usefulness beyond 1995, and the outsourced assets, including people were considered ‘commodity’ not ‘core’. Essentially the RHA were not outsourcing a problem here, and this is a typical pattern amongst the more successful contracts we have studied so far. In principle this does not explain A3 — the outsourcing of applications development — except that this was not considered a problematic area, with the strategy being to move to standardized packages. To balance the outsourcing, elements identified as ‘core’ were retained in-house, and where these were inadequate a strategy of ‘insourcing’ IT capability was adopted (A2).

Figure 1. Sourcing decisions: mapping the case studies

Case B is deemed by respondents as likely to be successful, but in fact is at a very early stage of the contract. B1 represents the outsourced IT including data centre, PCs, development and support — all identified as ‘commodity’. B2 represents what remained in-house, identified as ‘core’. There is already research evidence on long-term contracts and on total outsourcing contracts suggesting that claims for their success made at early stages of the contract should be treated with caution (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1992; Symons, 1993). This case was a total outsourcing example, large scale and for an 11-year period. There are also strong elements of what has been called a ‘strategic alliance’ here, with the partners agreeing to operate a joint venture together on selling mutually developed products onto the external IT market. Several factors favour the success of the contract however, according to respondents. Firstly, the company had consolidated and rationalized its IT function in-house before outsourcing. Secondly, it had a clear view of its strategic direction and where IT fitted in long-term. Thirdly, given the high systems interconnectedness of its systems, it was felt that a selective outsourcing decision could not be made, and that a strategic alliance on a long-term preferred partner basis was the better option.

Case C is an example of an outsourcing contract that was not renewed after three years, though some business unit users subsequently drew up their own contracts with the same vendor. A great deal of IT was outsourced in this case, but the problems experienced tended to relate to financial aspects rather than service levels. On one analysis the head office chargeback system to business users created considerable dissatisfaction because it had not been established on a credible and acceptable basis. Furthermore the vendor was put in a position where it had to look constantly to charge high for subsequent service and equipment above the base level in the contract terms. From another case in our sample in fact business users preferred to deal with the vendor through a head office IT group. However the difference between the two cases was the way in which the charging system was set up and was operated. In the latter case the contract has run for five years and will probably be renewed in 1993. The learning points here relate to the need to draw up a detailed contact and to understand the charging implications of the service levels required. Secondly, the company built up a risk in not retaining enough in-house capability in case the contract did not prove successful. There is a need to guard against the risks associated with the irreversibility of contracts.

Case D is considered a successful contract arrangement. D1 represents the outsourcing of ageing mainframes and data centres for three years. D2 represents the development of new replacement systems. In this sense this could be seen as an example of ‘transitional outsourcing’ except that the development of new systems is not normally outsourced in such situations, at least in the other examples we have researched. However the risks associated with ‘low experience of relevant technology’ in the case of D2 have been offset in several ways, as described above in the case study itself. These include: the use of their own ex-staff together with additional expertise from the vendor; a short term contract; and retention of the ownership of the assets being developed and acquired. A further factor is the retention in-house of relevant IT capability, strategic thinking and business knowledge, as well as contract management skills (D3).

In the research so far we have mapped a further 16 cases and analysed each in terms of whether the outsourcing has been successful or otherwise. The criteria for success, in number and type, vary from organization to organization. However, to generalize, the criteria fall within the following range: targeted cost savings achieved or better than anticipated; service levels maintained or better; user management satisfaction; low levels of vendor-client dispute; vendor responsiveness and attention; general favourable comparisons between objectives and outcomes; and decision to renew the contract. We have also endeavoured to establish the specific salient circumstances and factors which help to explain differing outcomes on the sourcing decisions made. The results so far from our work are summarized in Figure 2. The work so far suggests that outsourcing IT is best done on shorter term (one to five year) contracts, for commodity type IT, in situations of high in-house experience of relevant technology, where discrete systems or activities can be identified, and in conditions of some business certainty leading to confidence about technological needs of the organization for at least the life of the contract. It is also clear that outsourcing requires the retention of some critical in-house skills and capabilities. Where ‘core’ systems have been identified the general effective approach has been to ‘insource’ where appropriate. In situations of low ‘experience with the relevant technology’ again we have found the tendency to buy in resources, rather than outsource. However, as Figure 2 and Case D show, we have found organizations that adopt effective outsourcing approaches that minimize risks even in these circumstances.

However this discussion of effective sourcing decisions would not be complete without remarking on another set of provisional findings. Additional to the factors encapsulated by the Figure 1 framework, we are finding a number of factors emerging strongly from our interview programme as influential determinants of success or otherwise of outsourcing initiatives. These relate to managing the contract once the sourcing decisions have been made. In the 20 cases we have so far analysed, all respondents in their representations to us mention at least half of these factors as critical ones to get right when managing the contract across its lifetime. The first factor is the quality of the relationship with the vendor. This factor in fact cannot be separated out from, but is partially dependent upon the other factors being in place. These are, in no order of priority: careful handling of staff transfers; a hard-nosed contract establishing the business relationship; retaining leverage over the vendor — but not just by contract; retaining critical in-house capabilities, not outsourcing responsibility for business and IT strategy and business knowledge; and the fact that future changes are built into and allowed for in the contract. It is difficult to establish a general priority of these factors from the interview programme; this is partially because they are emerging as mutually interdependent. However the survey findings will enable us to attempt a more quantified ranking of these, and possibly other factors emerging from a much larger sample.

A final point that emerges strongly from our case study work is the importance of understanding the political context in which sourcing decisions are made and subsequently enacted. This issue has been discussed in some detail in a review of 17 outsourcing decisions in the USA by Lacity and Hirschheim (1993). Our own studies confirm broadly the picture they produce of sourcing decisions being shaped and enacted through political activity. It would perhaps be more accurate to describe the processes at work as rational-political decision-making, with the definitions of what constitutes rationality saturated by political context. As one example, a particularly interesting issue emerging from our own research is the degree to which the distinctions between types of system — differentiator or commodity, strategic or useful — may be determined by the political context, as much as by what the systems actually do. Thus in one large organization in which board level managers had determined on outsourcing most of its IT equipment and services, partly through financial problems, much of the IT was subsequently defined as ‘commodity’ and ‘non-strategic’. Lower level IT and business managers tended not to agree on precisely where the lines between these distinctions were being drawn, but also pursued their own political interests in wishing to identify more systems as of strategic importance — the implication being that such systems should not be outsourced.

## Conclusions

The paper reports findings on outsourcing decisions and practice from four detailed cases and preliminary results from 16 further cases. The paper represents exploratory work and conclusions must be tentative at this stage. A six-factor framework was developed for testing and has proved to be a helpful way of locating and explaining outsourcing decisions and their outcomes. The case studies also caught details of how factors interrelated, and the research methodology, as so far applied, enabled the identification of factors influencing the management of contracts from both a vendor and client perspective. We have identified the general conditions for effective outsourcing decisions — high ‘technology maturity’; vendor offers better deal compared with in-house; IT identified as ‘commodity’ not ‘core’, that is IT is not a strategic differentiator; discrete systems; in situations of reasonable business certainty across the life of a contract. However, even where all these favourable conditions apply there still needs to be in-house management action during the contract on a number of fronts which we have identified. Additionally we have found examples of organizations outsourcing IT even where not all, or even only some of these favourable conditions apply. But success seems dependent on taking careful action to minimize the additional risks to which the conduct of the contract then becomes exposed.

A number of cautions are raised from the research so far. From a management perspective the issue is perhaps better cast as not whether or not to outsource IT, but whether or not, and if so how, to use the market for IT and associated services for organizational advantage. In some situations ‘insourcing’ approaches may well be more appropriate, and this emerges as particularly important where IT-based systems and activities are identified as ‘core’ to the organization. An interesting aspect of the research so far is that in some respects when outsourcing IT, organizations are fairly clear about what then has to remain in-house. However a debate rarely entered into by respondents is the degree to which once IT has been identified as ‘commodity’ and outsourced, how far the future opportunities for identifying IT as a source of strategic opportunity and differentiation for the organization are then cut off. It may well be that the lack of identification of IT as potential strategic differentiator in many organizations now currently considering IT outsourcing reflects a lack of mechanisms in the past for identifying such opportunities. The question, then, is how far does outsourcing IT reinforce this tendency, or can outsourcing IT be the occasion for creating mechanisms for more effectively identifying strategic opportunities, and ways in which IT can differentiate the organization to advantage.
