# The impact of EDI on the components sector of the Australian automotive industry

David R. Mackay

Deakin University, Geelong, VIC 3217, Australia

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is an example of a cooperative inter-organizational system capable of providing competitive advantage. This article examines the inter-organizational impact that EDI has had on the first Australian industry-wide adoption of the technology — the Australian automotive industry. The research focused on the component sector of the automotive industry which supply parts primarily to the locally-based multinational car manufacturers. It was this sector that has felt the greatest impact of EDI adoption, and the sector that expected the least benefits. In fact, about two-thirds of suppliers indicated they adopted EDI only because they were told to by their much larger customers (the car companies).

Despite their apparent pessimism, the component sector did receive benefits from EDI adoption. In assessing the benefits, participants cited improved relations with trading partners, enhanced customer service, improved data accuracy and increased productivity as ranked in the highest group of claimed benefits from adoption. The greatest benefits were gained by organizations in which EDI was not only used, but fully integrated into their internal application systems. The analysis also indicated that senior management support was an important factor in achieving the stated benefits.

Keywords: automotive industry (Australia), electronic data interchange (EDI), electronic trading, just-in-time (JIT) management

The Australian Automotive Industry has been undergoing considerable change over recent years. Competition from imports has risen steadily, and may well continue to do so as tariff protection barriers are gradually reduced following the direction of Australian Government industry policy outlined in 1984 (known as the Button Car Plan, after its architect Senator John Button, then Federal Minister for Industry, Technology and Commerce) (Department of Industry Technology and Commerce, 1988).

If the issues of efficiency and structural change are not addressed quickly, it has been suggested that in five years or so, Australia may well find itself even less internationally competitive in motor vehicle production than it already is today.

This may mean Australia could well lose its domestic manufacturing industry and become highly dependent on imports of motor vehicles, with significant losses in both employment and Gross Domestic Product.

It has been argued that electronic data interchange (EDI) is one of those communication and information technologies that are capable of assisting an industry in improving efficiency and becoming more competitive. EDI is ultimately about addressing business issues, and is an example of a facilitating technology that is necessarily cooperative in nature, but can provide the basis for competitive advantage (Swatman and Swatman, 1992).

This article draws upon recent survey-based research into the impact that a requirement to use EDI for trade facilitation has had on a major industry sector — the Australian automotive industry's component sector (Mackay, 1992).

## Prior surveys of EDI

A range of surveys covering the implementation of EDI in different industries (including Government) have been conducted in a number of countries. Clarke et al. (1990, Appendix F) summarized a number of these studies covering the USA, Canada and Australia from about 1988 to 1990. The surveys were generally commissioned ‘consultant’ reports, with dissemination of the results somewhat restricted. Many of the questions specifically related to reasons for using and intending to use EDI, rather than the actual impact that EDI has had on an organization.

Perhaps the first serious study undertaken on EDI was by Emmelhainz (1990). In her book, Emmelhainz quotes a number of survey results used to enhance particular contentions. In particular, she highlights the four main areas of benefits from EDI as being:

\- improved internal operations from a reduction in time;

\- better responsiveness to customers;

\- improved channel management;

\- increased ability to compete, both domestically and internationally (p. 25).

Once again, apart from her own survey work, many of the presented results relied on commercial sources for information. One in particular that bears directly on this study, found that ‘. . . 87 percent of the respondents to a 1986 survey study believed that EDI improved relationships’ (p. 38), a theme picked up by Webster (1993).

Pfeiffer (1992) examined the diffusion of EDI in a broad-ranging survey covering the USA, UK and Europe. Pfeiffer found that except for high volume users, EDI generally offered very little in the way of immediate financial benefits. In most cases, organizations did not bother to conduct an economic assessment of the benefits they may or may not be receiving. They took EDI as a foregone conclusion, usually because of customer demand for its use in future trading partner relationships. Pfeiffer found evidence for strategic benefits of EDI citing respondents claiming a positive impact on customer service. Further analysis showed that EDI enabled users to avoid competitive disadvantage in contrast to suggestions that investment in EDI will in some way grant users lasting competitive advantages.

In Australia, experience of EDI pioneers has been disseminated in the works of Zinn and Takac (1989), Swatman and Swatman (1991, 1992), Swatman and Everett (1991) and Clarke et al. (1990, 1992). Kimberley (1992) recently completed a survey of South-east Asian countries' EDI implementations and intentions. The

Zinn and Takac study represented a thorough survey of various industry EDI implementations in Australia and New Zealand. Because of the timing of the study (1988–89), few Australian organizations had had much experience with EDI, and so many of their conclusions related to forecasts of EDI usage and expected benefits.

The Swatman and Swatman broad-ranging studies have focused particularly on the issue of integration and the benefits to be achieved from full integration of EDI into organizations' internal application systems. On this issue of integration, Swatman and Swatman (1992) state:

... while EDI per se is not a competitive weapon (except in the very short term), there is immense scope for competitive advantage to be gained from the way in which EDI is integrated into the organisation's structure. (p. 185)

The surveys conducted by Clarke et al. focused on providing information about government agencies awareness, practices and intentions in using electronic funds transfer (EFT), electronic funds transfer at point-of-sale (EFTPOS) and EDI. The initial study, published in 1990, was followed up by a further survey in 1992. The studies identified that there had been moderate growth in volumes within existing EDI schemes, but implementation was proceeding less quickly than respondents had expected. The systems had generally produced sufficient, and in some cases significant, benefits in terms of reduced cost, time-saving and improved client satisfaction. Many respondents regarded increased use of EDI as inevitable, but many also warned against excessively high expectations in the short term.

Chartered accountants Coopers and Lybrand in 1990, 1991 and 1992 surveyed Australia's Top 1000 Companies seeking various responses, including benefits received from adopting EDI. Response rates varied from 24 to 26 per cent. There was no specific industry data publicly available. The two principal conclusions reached in the 1992 survey were that nearly half of the respondents indicated improved speed in business transactions and reductions in costs as the major areas of expected benefit, with more than half the companies surveyed expecting to increase earnings by more than \$10000 per year with EDI (pp. 35–36).

With the exception of Clarke et al. (1990, 1992) who surveyed the EDI practice and intentions of the Australian Government, and perhaps Zinn and Takac (1989), all the surveys reviewed have generally been cross-sectionally based. Most of the benefits following EDI adoption referred to in these surveys tended to be anecdotal, stating that there are benefits to be gained (or anticipated), but providing little or no factual evidence in support of such claims. The contribution of these surveys is best related to outlining industry intentions to adopt EDI, including what they hope to gain from its adoption. Because of the varying state of EDI in many countries, an assessment of benefits and impacts was not always possible. In their survey of 560 Canadian enterprises, Bergeron et al. (1992) confirmed the absence of factual information relating directly to the impact that EDI has had on specific organizations — in particular, their assessment of benefits and costs.

In order to identify actual benefits from EDI implementation, a survey of the Australian automotive industry sector was undertaken between November 1991 and February 1992. The objective was to test whether or not the industry strategy has been achieved, or at least whether the industry is on the road to meeting some or all of the industry expectations. The study examined the benefits achieved by component manufacturers from adopting EDI, and how trading relationships between customers and suppliers were affected following adoption.

The author believes this case research is the first of its kind that focuses on the specific impact EDI has had on trading organizations in an industry that has had such high international significance as the automotive industry (Womack et al., 1990). This paper extracts key data from the first research report (Mackay, 1992) arising from the longitudinal study.

## EDI and the Australian automotive industry

Although only a small part of the global motor vehicle industry, the Australian automotive industry makes an important contribution to the gross domestic product of Australia. In all cases, the five (now four) manufacturers are subsidiaries of large transnational corporations based in the USA or Japan. If the industry was to survive in Australia, it needed to improve its efficiency and competitiveness. EDI was seen as a key element of automotive industry reform, which must improve both efficiency and quality of production to remain globally competitive.

## The Australian automotive industry

At the start of 1992, the Australian automotive industry comprised five major manufacturers with local production facilities (Ford, General Motors Holden (GMH), Mitsubishi, Nissan and Toyota), approximately 280 component parts manufacturers, and a number of motor vehicle importers and assemblers. By October, the number of local manufacturers had reduced to four following Nissan Japan's decision to confine its Australian operations to the reselling of imported vehicles from Japan.

The automotive industry remains a major segment of the Australian manufacturing sector (despite the general decline in manufacturing output in Australia). It is particularly important to Victoria, where Ford Australia, GMH and Toyota have their headquarters and principal assembly plants located.

Most of the producers' trading partners are located within Australia (principally within the major capital cities of Melbourne, Sydney and Adelaide), while a few are located overseas. Irrespective of size, they all have one thing in common: the need for the communication of material requirements (car assembly line needs) to their trading partners (the component manufacturers).

In 1990, the Australian Motor Vehicle Industry employed in excess of 63500 people in the assembly, component manufacturing and importing segments of the PMV sector (a five-year employment peak). By the end of December 1992 (and following the closure of Nissan's plant in Victoria) total employment had fallen to 41329 (Automotive Industry Authority, 1993).

Exports of vehicles and components have resulted in a major contribution to manufacturing revenue. The growth rate in the total value of automotive exports has reached a plateau, however, from an annual high of 56.2 per cent in 1990 (\$b1.0) to 3.3 per cent (\$b1.2) in 1992 (Automotive Industry Authority, 1992). The high growth rate was primarily due to sales of the Ford Capri produced in Ford's Victorian plant, destined for the USA market.

Unlike the automotive industry of the USA, the Australian industry is quite shallow in the number of suppliers available. Most of the Australian component producers supply more than one of the local car assemblers. In some cases, they produce for all five (four) local manufacturers, including a number operating in overseas markets. Communication difficulties have resulted from the different business practices of the local manufacturers. With the introduction of EDI, the industry believed, or perhaps hoped, that these practices would become standardized.

The complexity of this industry can best be summarized by an address given by Robert Copley, Executive Officer of the FCAI (Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries), who stated that:

In the car manufacturing industry, five companies produce Australian cars at about the rate of 1,500 cars per day. Concurrently, about 300 suppliers produce and distribute some 15 million parts for a day's production of cars, and fifty or more suppliers of materials provide several thousand tonnes of materials to the industry. It takes one car-carrying truck about ten hours to shift three minutes of the industry's output of finished cars. The total transport network for the industry involves thousands of sorties daily. The number of documents for ordering, forecasting, expediting, invoicing, receiving, debiting and paying amounts to millions. (Zinn and Takac, 1989)

As can be inferred from Robert Copley's address, there is much scope for EDI to assist the automotive industry in improving its communication effectiveness and efficiency among trading partners.

Kimberley (1991, p. 13) defines EDI as:

[EDI] . . . involves the electronic transmission from one computer system to another of any common business information, e.g., [purchase] orders, invoices, payment instructions, schedules and manifests.

This definition should really be modified to include the term ‘application to application’ rather than simply ‘computer to computer’, as potentially the significant benefits from adopting EDI are to be found when both trading partners have fully integrated EDI into their internal application systems (see especially Swatman and Clarke, 1990). The definition preferred in this study is:

EDI is the paperless transmission of business documents between trading partner application systems, via a computer and communications network, in a standard message format.

The automotive industry is the first Australian industry to introduce EDI on a coordinated industry-wide basis. It has been using EDI since the first electronic data transmissions commenced in 1988, to communicate the production requirements of the five car manufacturers to their component suppliers in order to meet the demands of the Australian and overseas motor vehicle markets.

In their review of literature on EDI and system integration, Swatman and Swatman (1992) point out that EDI is really a subset of inter-organizational systems (IOS). EDI is ‘the pre-eminent example of a cooperative information system that can still provide competitive advantage’ (p. 200). There are other well-known IOS systems such as the US Airlines reservation system — SABRE and the SWIFT inter-bank clearing house for international transactions. These examples represent systems designed for achieving competitive advantage, whereas EDI is an IOS that possesses the ability to enhance both cooperation between organizations as well as provide a platform for use as a competitive weapon. Swatman and Swatman state:

One of the most outstanding differences between the type of strategic IOS referred to by the majority of academic authors and EDI systems is that EDI gains its major leverage from cooperation between organisations to their greater benefit, rather than providing an immediate competitive weapon to the using organisation. (p. 183)

EDI was acknowledged by the automotive industry (in particular the FCAI, and FAPM — Federation of Automotive Product Manufacturers), as one of a number of [cooperative] technologies that would assist in its re-structuring plans following the conditions placed upon it in 1984 as a result of the Button Car Plan. To this extent, it can be regarded as falling into the category outlined in Swatman and Swatman's review. To the extent that the industry, or firms within the industry use EDI as a competitive weapon is yet to be determined. This is certainly an avenue for further research as EDI becomes more fully exploited within the industry.

The fundamental policy objectives of the Button Car Plan for the Australian automotive industry included:

\- creating a time frame in which to restructure and modernize;

\- increasing the industry's efficiency;

\- holding down vehicle prices;

\- minimizing disruption during restructuring; and

\- reducing job losses and the provision of job stability.

The principal strategies whereby these objectives were to be achieved included:

\- increased economies of scale (fewer models);

\- gradual increases in competitive pressures (through tariff reductions);

\- encouragement of a stronger local industry, with commitment to innovation and design skills (supplier co-operation to retain local manufacturing industries); and

EDI was one of the communication and information technologies to be employed by the automotive industry in their response to achieving some of the Button Car Plan's objectives.

One of the principal objectives of using EDI was to achieve efficiency in communicating the needs of the manufacturers' production lines to the suppliers of component parts. Electronic trading was expected to bring about a number of benefits to the industry, including improvements in general logistics, increased productivity, improved product quality, enhanced customer service, and a movement toward lower inventory requirements as manufacturers moved towards just-in-time (JIT) production. Through this industry cooperation, individual component companies were placed in a better position to compete for overseas contracts to supply parts to other than their local customers, thus gaining a competitive advantage directly from their adoption of EDI.

## The automotive industry survey

There has been much written about global manufacturing and the adoption of new technologies, particularly in the USA and Europe (see van Tulder and Junne, 1980; BRIE, 1991; Butler Cox, 1991). However, little is known about Australia's role in the global picture. Particularly, the impact of industry initiatives, such as EDI, on groups such as component suppliers.

The application of EDI is generally seen to be between customers (the multinational car companies located throughout the world) and their suppliers (both local and overseas). In discussions with local component companies, senior managers have stated that in endeavouring to gain access to international markets, they have found that EDI capability is an essential prerequisite for winning lucrative supply contracts. However, the initial impetus for adoption in Australia has primarily come from the local manufacturers (Ford, Toyota etc.), with the cooperation of a few enterprising suppliers.

The principal objectives of this survey were to elicit responses from two groups of automotive industry suppliers (those currently EDI capable, and those suppliers who are not) as to both the impact that EDI has made on component suppliers, and an identification of the economic benefits (if any), together with the costs, implications and problems derived from using EDI.

## Survey method

The survey was undertaken using a mail questionnaire sent to industry component suppliers, most of whom were members of the FAPM. The questions were compiled following an extensive review of literature, from both international and domestic sources, claiming benefits from the introduction of EDI in various organizations. The questions used were then tailored to reflect the use of EDI in the Australian automotive industry, and so reflect the originality of the survey. They could be applied to other industries following appropriate modifications.

All EDI-capable respondents were registered with Telecom Plus as users of the proprietary EDI software called Tradelink (Auto).\* To offer anonymity, Telecom Australia provided the mailing facilities for the questionnaire, which included an accompanying letter from the FAPM explaining the reason for the survey, and seeking a positive response from their clients. The usual follow-up letter was sent, via the Telecom facility, for organizations not responding to the initial request. Organizations were asked to supply a contact name and telephone number for response clarification purposes (only a handful of organizations failed to enter this information). Further compliance was gained through the visits made by the FAPM EDI project manager to member organizations during the survey period.

## Surveyed population

As far as can be determined from sources which include the FCAI, FAPM, and key suppliers, there is a total population of component manufacturers to the automotive industry of about 280. Some are relatively insignificant suppliers.

For the purposes of this survey, two distinct (but related) groups of industry suppliers were identified:

\- Currently EDI capable suppliers trading electronically with at least one of the car manufacturers and who were registered with Telecom Plus as Tradelink (auto) users.

\- Those suppliers that, for whatever reason, were not EDI capable at the time of answering the questionnaire.

The Telecom Plus registered group consisted principally of original equipment (OE) manufacturers who in aggregate supply between 72 and 92 per cent (depending upon which customers they supply) of component parts to the passenger motor vehicle assembly lines, and spare parts divisions. The total number of industry respondents represented 51 per cent of Telecom users and 34 per cent of non-EDI users. This represented an effective response rate of 47 per cent.\*

Figure 1 shows the size of organizations responding to the questionnaire, validated by the FAPM from their specialized knowledge of the sector, as being representative of Australian component suppliers. The largest category of firms surveyed (34 per cent) fell within the \$5.1 million to \$20.0 million range, with a less than even spread above and below that category. One firm had a turnover in excess of \$200 million, 62 per cent of firms had turnovers of less than \$20.0 million.

## Profile of EDI capability

Figure 2 outlines the flow of EDI documents currently implemented in the automotive industry, together with future documents projected for adoption by the industry EDI committee. These are discussed later in the article.

The survey showed that 81 per cent of suppliers to the automotive industry were to some extent EDI capable. These suppliers were at least able to receive a materials release schedule (MRS) — a document something akin to a purchase order — from their car manufacturer customer and/or send an advance shipment notice (ASN) to their customer (a document indicating delivery details and the nature of the goods which are ready to be sent to the customer).

Of the remaining organizations, nearly two-thirds stated their intention to become EDI capable within the next 12 months.

The results which follow relate to those industry suppliers who stated that they were able to send and/or receive an EDI document. Clearly, some were able to both receive an MRS and send an ASN document. A number were also receiving final confirmation documents, such as JIT documents from one of the manufacturers. One supplier had trialed JIT electronically, but did so using facsimile transmission. A relatively few suppliers were able to send MRS documents to their own suppliers (sometimes called second-tier suppliers).

Figure 3 summarizes the implementation of EDI amongst EDI-capable suppliers by year of registration. Although not obvious from the exhibit, it was the larger, most significant suppliers that came on line during 1990 and 1991. The smaller suppliers were encouraged but not expected until later.

The dramatic increase in EDI registrations in 1990 and 1991 coincided with the appointment of the FAPM Project Manager in February 1990. His principal role was to assist FAPM members (component suppliers) to become EDI capable. This was achieved through awareness (EDI newsletters), education (seminars and workshops held in key centres) and through direct contact with suppliers within their own organization (project manager visits).

Clearly the same exponential adoption rate will not be maintained, as the remaining (smaller) suppliers are encouraged to come on line. Additional suppliers have become users of Telecom's EDI electronic trading software (Tradelink) during 1992–93, but the growth rate has slowed down significantly as the smaller and less dependent suppliers assess the viability of adopting EDI and, therefore, remaining in the industry. The FAPM estimate that about 92 per cent of component suppliers were trading electronically by March 1993 (see Mackay and Ridge, 1993).

It is also considered likely by the FAPM, that both the number and range of 'industry approved' documents electronically transmitted within the industry will increase significantly during 1993–94 as customers, suppliers and vendors critically assess the benefits of trading electronically.

## Reasons for adopting EDI

Suppliers were asked what motivated them to adopt EDI in the first place (Table 2). They were presented with a list of 11 possibilities (including an open category) and asked to rank them in order according to the weight given to the particular issue for their organization.

Two-thirds of suppliers adopting EDI did so, not primarily because of the perceived benefits of EDI to their business, but because they were ‘instructed to’ by their major customers. This result would seem to indicate the dominance of strategic factors over economic (cost/benefit) factors. The claimed economic benefits of EDI (reduction of: inventory, transaction costs, staff levels) all rated in the lowest three of the suppliers’ ranking. This appears to indicate the coercive nature of EDI implementation in the automotive industry over the past four years. This is certainly not meant to conclude that suppliers failed to gain those perceived benefits, only that they adopted EDI principally for survival reasons.

## Hardware and software used

The respondents were asked to indicate the type of hardware link they were using to Telecom Plus's mailbox network, i.e. whether a stand-alone personal computer (PC), a PC linked to a mainframe, a mainframe directly, or some other type, such as a private or corporate bureau service. The majority (nearly two-thirds) of users were trading using a PC, with only 3 per cent using a mainframe computer.

The integration of EDI with internal application systems (sales/order entry, finance or distribution) is seen by the industry as an important development following the adoption of EDI. Maximum benefits from EDI are seen to come through complete integration with other internal applications. Respondents were given an opportunity to outline any difficulties they had with integrating EDI into their internal application systems. The main difficulties were:

\- considerable variation in the way each of the car manufacturers used the various EDI documents, i.e. the business rules varied from customer to customer, making integration far more complex and costly than needed to be for suppliers;

\- absence of off-the-shelf EDI packages integrated with suppliers' existing application software, for example, MRP (material requirements planning) software;

\- perceived lack of integrity of electronically transmitted data from their customers. For example, some suppliers felt that not all transmitted order volumes could be accurately relied upon without subsequent confirmation by telephone or fax;

\- lack of a sufficiently high data processing priority within their organization, possibly due to inadequate management commitments to EDI.

## Benefits and costs

Supplier costs of implementing and operating EDI

The costs of implementing EDI fell into three categories:

• one-time establishment costs;

• annual fixed costs;

• volume-dependent variable costs.

The average cost of establishing an EDI facility was \$19685. The minimum reported establishment cost was \$1500 and the maximum \$175000 — clearly a significant variation. There was only one supplier who indicated costs as low as \$1500 (early adopters were not charged for software or initial transactions). The considerable variation can best be explained in terms of small suppliers who paid for the minimum entry-level to the very large suppliers fully integrating EDI on their mainframe at the time of their initial decision to adopt electronic trading. A few suppliers were given EDI software, already had a PC for other reasons, and therefore only needed to purchase a modem for communication.

If the extreme outliers are removed from consideration, i.e. if the largest implementation cost suppliers (top 3) are removed from the computed list (costs in excess of \$90 000) because they are not representative of most implementations, the average implementation cost falls to \$15 805. This average cost is supported by the FAPM following evidence gained from individual member-supplier organizations. The median was \$13 000.

## Benefits to suppliers from adopting EDI

A survey of literature provided a list of 15 areas of claimed net benefits from introducing EDI into an organization. To test this proposition, respondents were asked to indicate the net benefits and net costs which their organization had achieved following the introduction of EDI. The range of possible answers were in the range 1 = significant net cost, to 5 = significant net benefit (Table 4).

The benefit ranked the highest was improved relations with trading partners. Sixty-four-and-a-half per cent believed that their relations with the five car companies (or the ones they supply) had generated either marginal or significant net benefits. Only 2.5 per cent believed that they had actually brought about net costs.

The second ranked net benefit was improved customer service, i.e. how well the supplier services the customer. Approximately 50 per cent believed that customer service had generated either marginal or significant net benefits through the use of EDI. The third and fourth ranked benefits were data accuracy (36.3 per cent marginal or significant net benefits) and productivity (33 per cent marginal or significant net benefits).

Suppliers were asked to state approximately how many hours of actual staff time were employed to process MRS documents to their customers both before and after the introduction of EDI into their organization. Responses are summarized in Table 5. Respondents indicated an average 30.5 per cent reduction in processing time of electronically generated MRS documents compared with their former manual systems. Given that 85 per cent of suppliers became EDI users some time during 1990 and 1991, this is a dramatic result that provides some evidence in support of the savings which are possible for organizations who adopt EDI.

If the suppliers with EDI integrated into other systems are separated from those that are not, the results are even more dramatic (Table 6). Typically it was the larger organizations with significant numbers of monthly transactions that had undertaken integration. Virtually all of the suppliers who stated that EDI was of little use to them and only added to their costs, had not integrated EDI into their internal business systems.

## Changes in inventory

Suppliers were asked whether or not their level of inventory (finished goods) had been affected in any way because of the introduction of EDI into their organization. The information supplied was limited, in that the majority indicated no change. However, one feature is worth reporting. For those with a change in inventory level attributed to EDI, the average reduction in inventory was close to 2 per cent. Such a reduction is not unexpected with the industry's move towards JIT production. A more significant reduction is to be expected, following a greater integration of EDI into all aspects of trading, together with the adoption of other techniques designed to improve overall industry efficiency. However, at this early stage of adoption, the effects of EDI on inventory are really quite insignificant, and would instead be expected to increase as customers adopt JIT techniques, passing inventory costs onto their upstream suppliers. First-tier component suppliers would only expect a reduction in inventory the more they are able to use JIT techniques with their own suppliers, further up the value chain.

## Changes in trading partner relationships

All suppliers were asked to evaluate whether and how their relationship with their customers had been changed following the introduction of EDI into their organization. Respondents were asked to specify how their trading relationships had changed in the range of 1 = significantly worse, to 5 = significantly improved (see Figure 4).

While the great majority of respondents indicated that there had been no change in their trading relationships with their customers, about one-third of suppliers believed that EDI was bringing about some improvement. A number of suppliers stated their belief that it is the car companies that stand to reap most of the benefits from suppliers having been ‘forced’ to trade electronically. They believed that the distribution of benefits were quite one-sided. Evidence, as outlined in the earlier section entitled ‘Benefits to suppliers from adopting EDI’, and including Table 6, suggests that this is not the case. Indeed, some suppliers have shared and will continue to share in the benefits, but have to take the necessary steps to gain those benefits through, for example, appropriate integration of EDI with their internal application systems.

## Issues of concern to EDI capable suppliers

At this relatively early stage of adoption, it is important to highlight the issues respondents believed were of major concern and which needed to be addressed by the relevant industry committees, if EDI is to be more successfully integrated into the electronic trading environment.

Table 7 lists, in rank order, the issues considered of most importance to electronic trading component suppliers. The rank order is determined by the position of importance of the issue given by each respondent, and the number of respondents selecting that particular position. Suppliers were asked to elaborate on their ranking by outlining specific issues of concern they would like to see addressed. The number one ranked issue, suitable industry standards, was referred to and in a number of ways. Firstly, there was a lack of uniformity by all five customers in their use of MRS, ASN and JIT documents with their suppliers. Suppliers reported that quite often each company applied their own business rules in how they made use of these documents. For example, one company may use a combination of MRS and JIT documents to achieve JIT delivery, while another customer uses the daily scheduling ability within the MRS to achieve the same end.

A second example of suitable industry standards related to the use of the ASN document. The ASN can be used for three functions: receiving, planning and accounting. Not all customers use ASNs for the same three functions, nor the same combination of functions. Suppliers indicated that such a lack of consistency across manufacturers adds greater complexity to the task of integration of EDI, which further impacts the cost of implementing EDI internally.

## Future EDI developments

Suppliers were asked what the other EDI developments they would like to see in their industry. They suggested:

\- the introduction of EFT (electronic funds transfer) arrangements for faster payments of suppliers' invoices upon receipt of the ASN, including the use of the electronic remittance advice document;

\- the development of an improved EDI application interface for transfer to personal computers (e.g. to Lotus and dBase software formats);

\- enhanced/improved software from Telecom Plus, including more comprehensive, ‘readable’ reference manuals;

\- continuing the progress toward full implementation across the industry which focuses on closing the business loop;

\- the development of interactive EDI.

Only 36 per cent of suppliers indicated that they had integrated EDI into their internal application systems. From an industry perspective, this would seem one of the next major challenges — to demonstrate to suppliers the benefits of such integration. Through this process, suppliers and their customers should gain additional benefits from improved efficiencies.

## Summary

This article reports on the impact EDI has had on the component sector of the Australian automotive industry. The results of the survey suggest that EDI has had an important impact on the automotive industry since the first live transmissions commenced in 1988. As at February 1992, approximately 81 per cent of component manufacturers were capable of trading using EDI, and that had risen to approximately 92 per cent by March 1993.

## EDI implementation

While the rate of EDI adoption has not been as fast as the five manufacturers would have wanted, virtually all of their major suppliers are trading using EDI (representing up to 92 per cent of supplied parts for some customers). The reasons for this apparent slowness in adoption can be traced to a number of sources on both sides of the communication's link.

First, at least two of the five major customers have had organizational difficulties establishing EDI within their own internal application systems. This has caused considerable difficulties for suppliers who themselves have adopted EDI, only to find that they cannot effectively trade electronically with all of their customers.

Second, while many component suppliers have felt coerced into adopting EDI, many have readily agreed that EDI is one of a number of measures necessary for the continued existence of their industry. Perhaps this point has not been as clearly communicated to the smaller suppliers, many of whom have stated that EDI only increases their costs, at a time when customers are negotiating reduced prices for their components.

## Benefits received

The survey results show that many component suppliers are receiving at least some benefits from EDI adoption. The benefits are both direct (e.g. improved data accuracy, salary savings) and indirect (e.g. improved product quality and trading partner relationships). Respondents indicated that they are receiving more indirect benefits at the present time than direct.

The evidence provided from this survey suggests that once EDI has been fully integrated into internal systems, significant benefits are achievable (see especially Tables 5 and 6 and Figure 4). As 64 per cent of EDI users have not integrated, this remains one of the greatest challenges the automotive industry faces over the next two years or so.

## Reasons for adoption

Despite the majority of suppliers (67.5 per cent) stating that they were told to adopt EDI by their customers, many placed strategic and other business reasons in the top four ranking of reasons for adoption. Clearly, from the responses to open-ended questions, a number of suppliers are still smarting from the coercive approach to EDI adoption by their customers. While the drop dead date of 1 April 1991 passed with little acknowledgement, implied pressure from individual customers ensured that at least some key suppliers met the industry deadlines with reluctant compliance.

## Management of EDI

There is some evidence to support the contention that the success (and indeed failures) of EDI in the automotive industry may be traced back to the level of internal support EDI has attracted from senior management. Over half of the firms surveyed stated that not only did they have the support of senior management (up to CEO level), but that senior managers were actively involved in the process of EDI implementation. Similarly, where there was a designated EDI co-ordinator, that person was normally at least at manager/senior manager level.

The issue of primary concern to most suppliers, was seen to be the establishment and maintenance of suitable industry standards. Approximately 36 per cent ranked this issue as their number one concern. Having adopted world recognized trading document standards (ANSI X.12 and soon the United Nations EDIFACT standard) the only conclusion one could reach is that suppliers really have little to be concerned about, so long as the industry continues to use such recognized standards.

## Concluding remarks

This paper has provided empirical evidence in support of the contention that electronic trading, in the form of EDI, has led to improvements in trading partner relationships within the Australian automotive industry. In particular, it is one of the positive responses made by an industry to government economic policy (primarily through the Button Car Plan) in an attempt to make Australian industry more competitive in both local and international markets. The focus of this study has been on an assessment of EDI's impact on a particular industry. The research has demonstrated:

\- the importance of industry collaboration in achieving individual benefits (both direct and indirect) from EDI adoption;

\- the extent of potential benefits flowing to individual firms from full integration of EDI with internal application systems;

\- that despite being coerced into adopting EDI, smaller trading partners were able to achieve benefits from electronic trading;

\- the importance of senior management involvement and active participation in the EDI process.

## Future directions

It is evident that EDI will continue to not only have a major impact on changing the way organizations do business, but to ensure their very survival. To date, previous research has not examined closely the impact EDI is having on trading partners and how it is changing the way organizations do business with each other. Mostly, this is due to the relatively embryonic stage of its use, particularly outside the USA and Europe. Further research should centre on gaining longitudinal information within and between industries currently using EDI. A focus on individual cases is a useful way of providing the richness of information upon which industry policy may be shaped. Such research could examine:

\- the extent to which claimed benefits are sustainable in the longer term;

\- how well industry cooperation in establishing EDI has led to individual firms gaining competitive advantage;

\- the extent to which EDI has assisted the move to a JIT production process;

\- the nature and extent of benefits achievable from closing the loop.
