# Successful use of information technology in a small tourism enterprise: the case of Country Holidays

Alistair Mutch

Nottingham Business School, The Nottingham Trent University, Nottingham NG1 4BU, UK

The contribution which information technology made to the growth and success of a small tourism company, Country Holidays, is examined. The successful use of information technology was due largely to the identification of a business need by a non-technical owner. The key success factor for small businesses might not be information on hardware or software, but an appreciation of the information they need to compete successfully.

Keywords: tourism information systems, small business information systems

Tourism is one of the UK's fastest growing and largest industries. However, a recent National Economic Development Council report (1992) concluded that ‘the UK tourism product is starting to lose its competitive edge in the increasingly fierce international market place’. The same report drew attention to the important role that access to information played in successful tourism products and the need for the dissemination of best practice. This case study seeks to present such best practice as exemplified in a company held up by the NEDC as an exemplar — Country Holidays.

When one begins to consider the use of information technology (IT) in tourism an initial problem is the difficulties that surround the very definition of ‘tourism’. Much academic ink has been consumed in the attempt to come to a clear and agreed definition of what tourism constitutes (Ryan, 1992). This difficulty of conceptualization is reflected in the heterogeneity of the industry and in turn in the wildly varying patterns of IT usage. On the one hand we have the global networks of the international carriers, using the power of IT on a massive scale; on the other are large areas in which usage is rudimentary or non-existent. This comparison highlights another factor: the wide variation in size of organization, from the multinational operating on a global scale to the owner-manager confined to the local area. Thus, the examples of IT use available are largely confined to particular sectors and within them to large organizations.

There is no doubt, for example, of the impact of some tourism systems on the literature on competitive advantage through IT (Harvey, 1986; Large, 1986). The problem remains that for many tourism operators, and for many who wish to make their careers in the industry, such examples do not speak to their experience. That experience may well be better paralleled in the problems revealed in a study of the hotel industry, where less than half of the organizations studied had never conducted any form of feasibility study and where ‘use of the term “decision process” now seems highly ambitious; for some establishments this consisted of a series of ad hoc and uncoordinated decisions based on vague intentions’ (Whitaker, 1987). Because of this a case was sought which would show how IT could be used effectively in a representative mainstream tourism company, one which might be able to indicate how IT could be used in the smallest of companies. From this came the Country Holidays case study. Country Holidays, founded in 1979, is now the clear market leader in the UK cottage holiday letting industry.\* It now claims to handle the bookings for over 7000 properties, giving it over 30 per cent of the market. It also has an agency for booking similar accommodation in France and a growing telemarketing wing. It can be said to have used IT as a central part of its business strategy in three ways: it is able to handle much greater volumes than its competitors; the size of its investment in IT has erected a substantial barrier to entry; and IT has enabled it to branch out into new business areas. What follows is a discussion of the industry context, followed by a brief account of the history and development of the company. This then sets the context for an examination of the development of IT usage to be a central part of the company’s operations. The case concludes with some thoughts on the implications of the Country Holidays experience for the small tourism enterprise. The broad thrust of the paper is the primacy of business needs. It is argued that success for Country Holidays came through the ability of a non-specialist to identify the appropriate use of IT and to manage its introduction. The crucial success factor for small businesses may not be information on specific hardware or software, but an appreciation of what sort of information they need to conduct their businesses.

## The industry outlined

Cottage holidays form an ill-defined part of the self-catering holiday industry. They can be seen as forming the top end of a spectrum that starts with camping and caravanning and moves up through purpose-built chalets. There has been a long term shift in the demand for self-catering accommodation of all types. Over the 10 years from 1972 self-catering moved from being 49 per cent of nights spent in paid tourist accommodation to 60 per cent (English Tourist Board, 1984). One great problem is defining what a cottage is. Brochures from letting agencies deal in images rather than definitions, and a whole paper might be devoted to a discussion of those images and their resonance. Accordingly, the figures presented on the industry contain a high degree of estimation. Mintel has estimated that around three million adults took cottage holidays in 1988 (Mintel, 1990). One reason for this increasing popularity is the increasing number of consumers taking a second holiday in Britain in addition to an overseas holiday. Social Trends reported in 1991 that the proportion of adults taking two holidays each year rose from 12 per cent in 1971 to 15 per cent in 1989, with the increase being concentrated in social class AB (HMSO, 1990). Cottage holidays can also seem attractive to those with children, avoiding the difficulties of foreign travel. There may also be the impact of the rising level of concern for, and interest in, country matters, which manifests itself most obviously in various environmental campaigns but is also evident in popular magazines like Country Living. Given the difficulties of definition, one has to be cautious about trends in the industry. One estimate is that whilst overall demand has been static over the 1980s, the growth seen was in the upper ends of the market (Beioley, 1990).

The trends on the supply side of the market are somewhat easier to isolate. The steady decline in agricultural labour requirements has left many empty cottages. In addition, the increasing trend towards larger farms has made whole ranges of farm buildings redundant. Modern methods of production also leave many attractive, solidly built structures without purpose. Many of these buildings in the past have been allowed to decay, but there are new pressures on farm income. The change from a production based system of subsidies to a relatively free market has caused severe squeezes on farm incomes in some sectors (e.g. milk production). This seems likely to be a trend that will affect all sectors of the agricultural industry, and so farmers are looking to diversification. One attractive source of income is the former cottage or barn that can be turned into high quality self-catering accommodation. Restrictions under the Rent Acts make these unattractive for long-term lets. In addition, planning permission is now considerably easier to obtain, as planners in rural areas have relaxed restrictions in an attempt to preserve buildings that add a great deal to the quality of the environment (Evans and Ilbery, 1989; Haines and Davies, 1987; Slee, 1987). Those who have cottages to let have two choices when seeking customers. One is to advertise directly. There are many outlets for potential advertising from tourist boards to local and national newspapers. Once a regular clientele has been built up a considerable amount of trade can be generated by word of mouth. However, there are problems with this approach. Few owners will have any skill in media selection and this can lead to expensive failures — indeed it has been argued that many farmers have a completely different idea of what urban consumers are looking for from what is the case, and this may also cause them to misdirect their efforts (Slee, 1987). It is also the case that few will be able to afford national coverage. Some groups of owners with multiple properties can pursue this route successfully. One example is the National Trust, which runs about 200 properties. Another strategy is to combine in cooperative groups. One such body is the Farm Holiday Bureau, which seeks to coordinate and promote the efforts of local groups.

The other option is the use of a letting agency. These are able to offer much higher occupancy rates on the basis of greater advertising coverage and their skills in matching customers with accommodation. There has been something of an explosion in this sector over the 1980s. Again, it is difficult to separate out those agencies dealing solely with cottages from those covering the full range of self-catering. Beioley, for example, estimates that there are some 142: the research for this case found a smaller number, but this may well be due to the exclusion of those companies offering multiple types of accommodation (e.g. Hoseasons) (Beioley, 1990). There is a wide range of companies operating in this sector, from companies with local groups of cottages (e.g. North Norfolk Holiday Homes), to regional operators covering several counties (e.g. Devon, Dorset & Cornwall Cottages) to the largest operators offering nationwide coverage. Of these, the largest is Country Holidays with some 7500 properties, over 30 per cent of the market. Other major players are English Country Cottages (who offer national coverage through two additional brochures, Welsh and Scottish Country Cottages) with 2575 cottages, Blakes (1293 cottages) and Mackays (3000 cottages). The latter is based in Edinburgh and offers predominantly Scottish coverage, but also offers properties in the north of England. Despite the existence of these larger companies, the sector is one typified by a multitude of small firms (Table 1).

## Country Holidays: origins and growth

As Beioley (1990) points out, ‘self-catering letting agencies have expanded dramatically over the past 20 years’. When Country Holidays was founded there were few such agencies and those in existence were somewhat traditional and slow moving organizations. Its founder, Philip Green, came from a long established farming family in Gargrave, West Yorkshire. His intention was to go into farming, but the outlook for dairy farmers was bleak in 1978. Out of necessity he began looking for alternative sources of income. He was aware of a demand for reasonable quality rented accommodation from friends he had met in both the south of England and the USA, but he was also aware of the difficulties they had finding suitable places. They had to seek out and contact individual owners, and had no guarantee of the standard of accommodation they would find until they arrived. Equally, Green knew of local farmers with suitable properties which they would like to obtain an income from, but who faced difficulties in attracting custom. Discovering to his surprise that there was no central list of such accommodation, he set about creating one.

His aim from the beginning was to offer good quality accommodation at reasonable prices and it was clear fairly quickly that this was meeting a real demand. Starting off in 1978 and operating out of his parents' farm pantry he had 200 properties on his books in 1979 and doubled the number of properties in the following year. This necessitated a move to his wife Jennifer's cottage in the village and the taking on of four staff. Growth continued as cottage owners heard of the success of existing owners and applied to be taken on. More staff and more properties meant another move, this time to premises over the local Co-op.

This growth continued and by 1986 the company was outgrowing its accommodation. It began looking for suitable office accommodation in the vicinity, but none was available. Casting his net further Green found a derelict weaving shed in Earby, seven miles away and just over the Lancashire border. This offered ideal space for growth and the local council, keen to attract service jobs into an area over-dependent on manufacturing, offered a grant to assist with conversion. This was completed for use by late 1987.

The firm was of such a size now that Green began to look for professional staff in areas such as finance. In October 1988 Chris Aldcroft, formerly finance director of Beauty Care Drug Stores, was appointed as finance director. One of his responsibilities was IT, and he moved to bring in somebody with experience. These changes, and other strains associated with the growth of the company, convinced Green that this was no longer the company he had founded, and in September 1989 the company was sold to Aldcroft and Roger Pickering, the operations director, for £14 million (Financial Times, 1989). The buyout saw ambitious plans for expansion, with considerable investments in new technology and the construction of more office space in Spring Mill. The new international call centre was opened by Viscount Ullswater in 1990. Country Holidays was by then reporting a turnover of £50 million, four times that of its nearest rival, English Country Cottages.

## Use of IT

Having outlined the development of both the market and the company, attention is now switched to a discussion of the developing use of IT by the company. The company started with a card index system. Details of each property were held on cards, and a manual invoicing system was used. This worked well until customers came on with a complex query, requiring multiple searches through some 200 cards. Green realized that this was a prime candidate for the use of a computer and, with no knowledge of computers, he set out to look for a suitable system. The first one selected went disastrously wrong: 'the software I'd specified didn't bear any resemblance to what was actually delivered, they hadn't done any testing, it was a complete disaster' (interview with Philip Green). After this introduction the search continued and eventually the choice settled on an Altos micro with four screens, running software specially written by a programmer in Bradford. This was essentially the manual system, the index cards and the invoicing, transferred to the screen. However, the great advance was that it incorporated a search facility. This was key to its success: 'from day one, it was a marketing and selling system with administration and accounts bolted on rather than the other way round, (interview with David Jones). However, the next problem came with the rapid expansion of the business, necessitating extra staff. This is when the limitations of the machine were exposed, as it was not possible, not even with a direct visit to the manufacturers in California, to go beyond the four screens.

With this experience the new search embodied definite criteria. Hardware had to be a micro that was infinitely expandable to cope with what was obviously a business with tremendous growth potential. Crucially, it had to be able to run a database capable of storing 100000 names and addresses and 2000 properties with all their associated details. It also had to supply fast response times, critical in meeting customer demand. This was a tall order in June 1982 but a machine called an Alpha Micro was eventually selected. This used a proprietary operating system and by good fortune Green found a software house in Rawtenstall, Lancashire, who could program in Alpha Basic. A system was written in two months under pressure from Green and introduced in 1982.

One immediate consequence of the new system was the need to recruit staff specifically to maintain it. Because the system had been written so quickly it was full of bugs and a programmer was taken on to iron these out. His job then expanded to enhancing the system and extracting additional information from it, and two juniors were recruited to assist him. Over the next six years both hardware and software were expanded to meet the rapidly evolving needs of the business. The final hardware configuration was 85 screens and seven printers with a processor that was at the top of the Alpha Micro range. The software also was pieced together as time went by: 'A lot of it was patched together, cobbled together and programmers have looked at it and said "my God look at that, heavens it's not supposed to do this, oh that's dangerous" but I didn't care about all that, it worked and it did what we wanted' (interview with Philip Green).

Before the move to Spring Mill the company was using 'key and lamp' telephony. This relatively unsophisticated technology was being phased out. Unable to find a satisfactory replacement Green was forced to look elsewhere. At a telemarketing conference in London he became aware of the new automatic call distribution (ACD) systems available. The ability of these to not only handle the distribution of calls to operators, but also monitor and record traffic on particular lines brought whole new opportunities, as adverts could be given a specific number and the responses recorded. A system from Datapoint was installed in 1987.

One consequence of this investment was business diversification. The system gave the facility to use a thousand telephone numbers, but this capacity was barely being used: 'we only used it in large quantities in the months of January and February. For the other 10 months of the year the machine was being used for less than a 10th of what it was capable of'. To use this capacity, and the skills the company had acquired in telephone selling a tele-marketing agency called Rushton Response was set up. This ran short campaigns for other companies, giving them the use of the sophisticated facilities at Country Holidays.

This system was transferred to Earby and continued to function until the review instigated by David Jones, the new IT manager was brought in by Chris Aldcroft soon after his appointment as Finance Director.

## The professionalization of IT use

David Jones was an experienced IT professional, with considerable experience in the travel industry at coach firms Wallace Arnold and Smiths Shearing. He was alarmed by a number of aspects of the systems he found in place: the level of staffing, the resilience of the existing system and, most importantly, its ability to handle existing capacity and planned growth. Jones started by recruiting extra operators to allow for cover during business hours. He then undertook a thorough review of the existing system and future needs which culminated in an Invitation to Tender being issued. The contract was given to FSS Travel and Leisure Systems, a Berkshire software house specializing in leisure and travel applications. The system was fully implemented in October 1990.

The new system centred on a Data General MV30000 central processor rated at about 10 MIPS with 96Mb of memory. There were 4 gigabytes of disk storage with a 2 gigabyte tape unit for storage. Access was via 120 terminals, with eight printers, mostly laser printers, for output, all connected by a local area network. The actual database of property details took about 1 gigabyte of this storage, but there was also 500Mb of digitized property pictures. The system was able to give a response time, when fully loaded, of under 3 seconds to a customer query. At peak volumes it was able to handle six people processing 2000 bookings a day, with a further 100 people taking 4000 provisional bookings and 4000 brochure enquiries. The database was written in DG/SQL, the relational database supplied by Data General and conforming to the SQL standard.

The system could search on up to 45 parameters ranging from geographical area to availability. Available properties were presented on screen in random order. (Properties were stored in property number order, so retrieving them directly would be unfair to those owners with higher numbered properties. The presentation of details was therefore randomized, by Country Holidays staff.) All the text in the brochure was presented, together with supplementary notes. Holidays were sold on a daily basis, so short breaks could be offered if appropriate.

If a customer was interested in a property and made a provisional booking, two prints were generated. One was a personalized letter detailing the costs and confirming the dates, the other a detail sheet on the property complete with an outline drawing. On receipt of a firm booking, either by post or by telephone a confirmation letter was sent, containing directions to the property and any special instructions, together with an invoice for any outstanding balance.

Details of all customers were stored, including any brochure enquiries, with each customer being given a unique number. This enabled selective mail shots for former customers who had not yet booked. The confirmation letter also carried on its reverse a customer enquiry form covering quality and value for money. All responses were input to the system and analyses produced of areas in which either properties or Country Holidays service were found wanting. Regular reports were run after normal business hours providing daily analyses of bookings, performance tables for properties and other regular reports. In addition, ad hoc queries could be written using PRESENT, the Data General fourth generation language (interview with David Jones).

In addition to these developments the Datapoint ACD system installed in 1987 had handled between 2000 and 3000 calls a day. The company's growth meant that these volumes were growing and reached 7000 calls a day in 1990. The Datapoint equipment was upgraded in the autumn of that year and an additional digital switch was added. In addition, a call management system was installed which gave instant information on telephone traffic presented on terminals. Colour coded bar charts on the screen gave team leaders an immediate indication of the state of lines. Colours could be set to change with the volume of calls waiting to be answered to enable extra staff to be brought in to handle peak demand. Extra lines were installed to give a total of 130 lines, together with a direct dial in facility enabling calls to be routed directly to the people responsible. These changes were intended to relieve congestion and ensure that calls were answered promptly by the correct person. Together, these developments represented an investment of some £1 million. They not only ensured that the company was able to continue to handle growing volumes, but also represented a source of competitive advantage. To what extent the company owes its success to the use of IT is the subject of the next section.

## IT for strategic advantage?

In what ways can IT have been said to have given Country Holidays strategic advantage? Just as importantly, how sustained has this advantage been and can it continue to be maintained? The answers to these questions can conveniently be grouped into three areas, which are those outlined in the introduction: dealing with volumes, barriers to entry and new business areas.

There is no doubt that the ability to handle business volumes effectively and efficiently is a key benefit bestowed by the use of IT. This was demonstrated by Thomson's TOP system:

During the heat of the holiday price war in autumn 1985, the tour operators' booking systems were subjected to a rigorous examination. Thomson Holidays' videotex-based TOP system handled 3,291 passenger bookings in the first hour of the rush for 'standby' holidays bargains. Other operators did not fare so well. Some ground to a complete halt through acute overload. (Harvey, 1986)

This experience is mirrored in the ability of the Country Holidays' system to process all the business generated at peak periods of demand. Such an ability is vital in a business that is based on a speedy and effective response to customers' telephone queries. Whether such an advantage can be sustained is open to doubt. The success of such systems breeds imitators, although the lead that the successful use of IT can bring can be substantial. However, there are several other operators in the field who now make computers a feature of their sales literature.\* The following quotation from one such brochure encapsulates the trends nicely:

In my early years practising as Miles & Son some 45 years ago our ‘holiday letting’ effort comprised just a book with the addresses of a few properties in it and we referred people to the owners to make arrangements. In most years since then the service has been improved and in consequence, grown. We have now installed a computerised system and we have improved the detail and layout of the brochure which we hope will facilitate your making a selection and completing the booking. (Miles & Son brochure, 1992)

These systems are often based on personal computers, and the declining price of such hardware, coupled with the increasing power of PC based databases, will further erode the advantages held by Country Holidays. This will mean that even the smallest will be able to benefit from the data handling and manipulation capabilities that IT brings (Tregoning, 1991). In this context, it is the call handling capabilities of the ACD equipment that might prove to be the most sustained barrier. In addition to providing such a barrier, the ACD has prompted the development of new areas of business. Again, the size of the IT investment has both forced such a search for new business and enabled its introduction. The establishing of IT on a professional basis was the key foundation for such an expansion and so IT has both provided opportunities for strategic change and the ability to put the strategic change into operation. However, it is important to see IT in context. Before discussing the implications of the Country Holidays case for the use of IT in small enterprises, it is important to outline the other factors in its success.

A number of factors can be outlined that contributed towards the company's rapid growth.

## 1. A growing market

Country Holidays was founded just as the ‘Heriot Country’ idea was taking off on the back of the success in both Britain and the USA of the programmes about a country vet in the Yorkshire Dales. This created a strong demand for traditional cottage accommodation but of a reasonable standard amongst more educated and affluent sections of the community. This gave the company a strong local base. The move towards quality self-catering accommodation accelerated as disposable incomes grew in the early 1980s, and as attitudes towards British holidays changed. The activities of the firm as described below also played their part in expanding the market.

## 2. The company's approach

From the beginning, Country Holidays was aimed at the middle of the market, offering quality accommodation at reasonable prices. Philip Green identified that a major problem with existing cottage accommodation was the standard of facilities offered. He set out to drive up standards, offering owners not a higher commission rate but a longer season, thereby increasing overall income. In 1980 the expected length of season was 12 weeks, but Country Holidays set out to extend this to 20 weeks. They persuaded owners to take lower prices in the off-peak months and sold the idea of cottage holidays at these times of the year: 'we were noticing substantial numbers of customers who weren't just buying one holiday a year from us but were buying two, three and four holidays a year. February half-term, spring bank holiday, two summer weeks and October half-term' (interview with Philip Green). The average occupancy level reached a peak of 28 weeks by 1989. The success of this strategy was such that extra demand was created, as prospective holiday makers began to trust the quality they would receive. Supply was also increased, as successful cottage owners recommended the company to others. This approach differed from the self limiting approach of other companies, who confined themselves to the top end of the market.

## 3. Marketing

The company took an aggressive approach to marketing from the beginning. Considerable care was spent over the style and design of the brochure, which was seen as the shop window for the cottages and the company. Every effort was made to make the presentation of the information as clear and as easy to follow as possible:

Customers wanted a lot of detailed information but you had to make it easy for them to buy and easy for them to understand and easy for them to flick through and find what they wanted. So the whole thing was there not for our benefit in the administration side of the business but purely as our shop front, our sales aid. It was the only thing the customers ever saw of us and it had to be easy to use so that the whole product and service was easy to buy. (interview with Philip Green)

This was backed up by heavy spending on advertising: up to a third of gross income in the early years. This was reduced later when a substantial volume (70 per cent) of business was found to come through repeat bookings and referrals.

## 4. Quality staff

A key philosophy was that staff were not employed simply to act as telephone answerers, but as sellers of the cottages. A lot of time was therefore spent in selecting the staff for their personality and they were trained to know the company's properties well. This became important when a potential customer's first choice was not available. In addition, a management philosophy of giving considerable levels of responsibility was operated. It was found to be easy to recruit the right quality staff locally.

## 5. Location

The reliance of the business on the telephone and the postal service meant that a substantial business could be built without moving to high cost urban areas. Staff recruitment was easy and low cost office accommodation was available. The only potential drawback was the reliance on service suppliers, particularly British

Telecom. This was not a problem in the early years of the business, with the company receiving considerable cooperation from the Bradford area:

They used to have an engineer sat in the village exchange watching because our volume was so huge! They actually brought forward the investment programme in upgrading Skipton to System X and putting in the satellite exchanges in the villages because of the volume we were creating. (interview with Philip Green)

The success of the company cannot be crudely attributed to the use of IT. There were many other factors involved, and crucial to many were the vision of the original founder. The vision to spot potential and implement solutions was a crucial factor and one which is discussed in the next section in the context of the value to be gained from IT by small enterprises.

## Information technology and the small enterprise

Much of the literature on the strategic application of IT has been focused on the experience of the large organization. With the growing availability of hardware and software at steadily decreasing prices, the advantages offered by IT are available to smaller enterprises. However, there is little material on the implications and potential of IT use in shaping the growth and development of such enterprises (Raymond, 1992). Raymond's research, for example, puts an emphasis on the creative use of packages such as spreadsheets rather than the use of standard applications (such as accounting) as a means of gaining advantage. This reflects an experience of under-utilized information systems which have 'little impact on performance because the true information needs of the organisation are not met' (Massey, 1986). However, this offers little help in the current context, where the success was derived from the use of an operational system geared to the needs of the enterprise. The implication is that if small businesses understood their information needs better their use of systems might be more efficient. There is a germ of truth in this, but this drives us perilously close to recommending the use of the full blown apparatus of structured methods as used in larger firms. In fact Lees found that of the facets of structured methods that were examined, only consideration of future business needs was of significance. His conclusion on other areas was:

In contrast, the other three systems analysis factors (studying previous operations, defining computer functions, and performing a cost/benefit analysis) had very few or no significant associations with SBIS [Small Business Information Systems] success as indicated by satisfaction and computer usage. (Lees, 1987)

Rather than needing a detailed and structured analysis of information needs, the key success factors appear to be the involvement and creative thought shown by the users. Indeed, Lees found that the use of consultants to analyse needs produced a lower level of satisfaction. Relating this to Country Holidays, we find an owner with a self-confessed ignorance of computers at the outset, but a recognition of their potential value. During the whole process the use of IT remained wedded to the achievement of business objectives, rather than technical excellence. An important aspect was the ability to identify and utilize expertise, but this expertise was set clear targets based on business needs. The developing use of IT — the move to databases, the use of ACD equipment — was powered by the need for information with which to run the business. A powerful illustration of the way in which this information was used is supplied in an article by Philip Green (1992) on advertising response. In this costs are presented for bookings emanating from a range of advertising. The end product of such analysis may well be handled by a spreadsheet, but the data required to produce it comes from the storage capacity of a database. Green's success came in having the vision to recognize what information was needed to manage the business and to recognize how that information might be delivered. The other aspect of successful use of IT in the case of Country Holidays is the ability to recognize when another stage of development is required. Partly this threshold was reached with the increase in business volumes, when the enthusiasm of the user is no longer sufficient to ensure that the systems can cope with volumes. An important aspect is that at this stage the 'gut feeling' which confirmed the essential accuracy of figures such as the advertising responses is no longer sufficient as a benchmark. The means of having a trial back to source data is crucial:

I'm absolutely confident that we can reconcile whatever figures we produce, whereas in the old computer system that we replaced we just couldn't. If a figure was produced on a bit of paper we had to believe it as well, because I had no one still in the company who knew how those programs worked and whether indeed they were actually producing the information they purported to. (interview with David Jones)

This level of confidence could only be produced from a system which could cope with the volumes the business was generating. This in its turn demanded a level of professional expertise and has meant the growth of a systems ‘department’.

## Conclusion

Country Holidays is an example of the way in which small enterprises can use IT to assist in successful growth. The company was able to grow from foundation to market leadership in 10 years. Its use of IT has allowed it to both consolidate this position of leadership, by erecting substantial barriers to competition, and to branch out into new areas of business. However, this use was only one of several factors leading to success. The crucial factors behind the successful use of IT were:

\- the willingness of the owner to experiment with the technology;

\- the recognition of the potential of the technology, but a firm subordination of its use to business needs;

\- an imaginative approach to the information needed to run a business;

\- the ability to control the extra resources required;

\- the ability to recognize when systems had reached such an importance that a professional function was required.

Perhaps the overall conclusion is that those in a similar position need not so much training in what specific pieces of hardware or software are available, but primarily an understanding of what constitutes important information for managing their business. When this is understood, then the means of acquiring such information can be explored.
