# A model of EDI integration and strategic business reengineering

Paula M C Swatman, Paul A Swatman and Danielle C Fowler
Informatics Research Group, Curtin University of Technology, School of Computing, GPO Box U1987, Perth, Western Australia, 6001

Early research into electronic data interchange (EDI) stressed the greater speed, efficiencies and cost savings available from electronic document exchange. Despite EDI's cooperative focus, much of this research also described the technology as a competitive weapon for user organizations. Wider experience with EDI suggests, however, that rather than looking for short-term competitive advantages from EDI, it is in the areas of systems integration and business reengineering that EDI offers its greatest real benefits. This integration enables EDI to support a truly strategic approach to business, offering major comparative advantages to organizations, business groups, industry sectors and trading blocs. This paper develops a model of EDI integration as a series of four standard and recurring stages and then considers the case of a major Western Australian public sector organization which is in the process of making EDI a vehicle for business reengineering (the final stage). It also links this case study with current research into organizational growth and transformation. This research suggests that only organizations sufficiently mature to take a holistic view of information technology are likely to gain the comparative advantages which information technology offers. Building on this research, the paper suggests that, although EDI itself is a comparatively simple application in technological terms, the implications of its strategic use as an enabler of major organizational restructure and business reengineering are profound.

Keywords: electronic data interchange, business reengineering, strategic information systems

Industry conferences and journal articles frequently describe EDI as providing a ‘competitive edge’ for its users (e.g. Robinson and Stanton, 1987; Langton, 1988; Hudson, 1989). While short-term competitive advantage from EDI is possible for an organization which is the first in its market sector to link in suppliers, the arrival of competitors rapidly erodes any possibility of ‘sustainable’ competitive advantage (Clemons, 1986).\* Although some instances of sustainable competitive advantage through EDI do exist, these appear to be more likely where:

\- EDI is a factor in a substantial gain in market share being made and retained, particularly if some competitors are forced to withdraw from the market, e.g. the US airline industry's reservation systems SABRE and APOLLO (see Cash, 1985; or Copeland and McKenney, 1988);

\- alliances are formed which provide dominance over a market, e.g. the international banking community's closed-user-group SWIFT clearing-house (see Brearley, 1989; Swatman and Clarke, 1990), or the European airline reservation clearing-house SITA (see Amerongen, 1987); and/or

\- the market is substantially re-defined in the initiating organization's favour, e.g. the American Hospital Supply Company (now Baxter Healthcare Corp.) ASAP on-line ordering system (see Harvard Business School Case, 1985, 1988).

It is noticeable that, with the exceptions of the SWIFT and SITA clearing-houses, which clearly fall within the commonly cited definitions of EDI (see, for example, Coathup, 1988; Rochester, 1989; Metzgen, 1990), the majority of the examples of sustainable competitive advantage of this type relate to systems which many observers would consider only borderline EDI at best. Successful competitive systems appear to be based upon long-term ties between customers and suppliers (McFarlan, 1984), where barriers can be erected to prevent the entry of new competitors and switching costs are built in to discourage customers from moving to alternative system offerings.

Where attempts have been made to create competitive schemes of this type within the EDI world, it has been common to find the innovator's advantage rapidly neutralized, either because the original competitive scheme was replaced by a more successful cooperative venture, or because the innovators were forced by competitors or regulatory agencies to make the services widely available. The successful implementation of cooperative EDI schemes within such fiercely competitive market sectors as the automotive and pharmaceutical industries (see McNurlin, 1987; Hollands, 1989; Hill, 1988, 1989) implies that corporations view EDI as a factor in their industries' survival, rather than as a competitive weapon. In fact, the competitive SWIFT venture is itself an example of cooperation by the banking industry, to protect its market share against the rapidly expanding non-bank financial institution sector (Brearley, 1989; Burns, 1989). It is also worth noting that even the SABRE airline reservation system is moving towards the more open ANSI X12 document translation standards.

This failure to provide long-term sustainable competitive advantage implies that EDI, despite its strategic importance, is essentially a cooperative phenomenon: 'implicit in the early writings on EDI has been the assumption that these systems hold great potential for providing strategic advantage . . . [but] EDI applications, rather than being a competitive weapon, are increasingly a necessary way of doing business' (Benjamin et al., 1990. See also McNurlin, 1987; Rochester, 1989;

Swatman and Clarke, 1990). EDI's competitive advantages relate to the strategic advantage an organization can obtain by integrating internal/external document flows into in-house systems and structure — in fact to what is alternatively described as business reengineering, business process redesign, or even business process reengineering.

## Business reengineering

This concept is defined as ‘the analysis and design of work flows and processes within and between organizations’ (Davenport and Short, 1990, p. 11). Business reengineering (or business process redesign) is lucidly explained and illustrated in this seminal paper on the potential benefits available from linking business process redesign with information technology, which the authors believe can provide a substantial improvement in efficiency and effectiveness.

Davenport and Short point out that while process thinking has gained acceptance over recent years, the majority of organizations concerned about ineffective business processes tend to try to improve each individual process — with the result that overall processes may actually be rendered slower and more ineffective than before. This viewpoint is supported by Hammer (1990), who suggests that despite significant investment in information technology, organizations' tendency to use this technology to automate existing methods of doing business leaves existing practices intact (merely speeding them up). Hammer recommends the complete redesign of ineffective processes, in preference to attempting to improve them one by one. Morrow and Hazell (1992), while agreeing with the need for complete redesign, also suggest a brainstorming approach to alternatives and point to the need of senior management support for implementation of recommended changes.

Davenport and Short (1990) provide a list of pre-requisite steps for successful process redesign:

\- developing business vision and process objectives — taking a broad, strategic and cross-functional view of the purposes of organization into which the newly redesigned process(es) must fit;

\- identifying the processes to be redesigned — which can either be done by attempting to identify all the organization's processes, or by identifying the most crucial, high impact processes;

\- understanding and measuring the existing processes — to avoid repetition and provide a baseline for future improvements;

\- identifying information technology levers — rather than trying to ‘cobble’ technological aids onto processes after redesign;

\- designing and building a prototype of the process — using IT as a design tool, understanding the design criteria chosen and ensuring the prototyping of organizational processes.

Despite the wide acceptance which business reengineering has received, there are concerns which should be recognized by organizations considering such activities. Galliers (1994) points out that the history of information technology has rather more than its share of disasters — frequently as a result of failing to consider all relevant factors. When engaging in the revolutionary redesign of business processes, Galliers suggests that a thorough understanding of the organization and its technical and human resources is absolutely essential in preference to a partial view of the changes needed, based on technical/technological factors and perceptions:

It may well be not only a matter of simplifying the business processes in line with defined business objectives and automating some of these processes. It may not simply be a question of redesigning organizational entities, or even reconsidering the very nature of the business itself in line with the opportunities provided by IT. It is just as likely that the nature of the necessary change will include each of these aspects, together with changing attitudes, skills and even personnel. Certainly, IS/IT may well have a key role to play, but it will be but one aspect of the whole change process. (Galliers, 1994, pp. 4–5)

Vogel (1991) concurs that both the organizational and technical aspects of business reengineering are of importance to effective transformation, describing a synergistic method for integrating EDI by means of Enterprise Analysis which incorporates the concept of business reengineering. Enterprise Analysis is a technique for understanding and managing key business processes, originally created by Katz (1990) and steps in this process include:

\- meta modelling, which establishes a common terminology and agenda for reform;

\- project framing, which identifies existing problems and issues, defines goals and objectives, brings assumptions and constraints to the surface and identifies success factors and measures;

\- model building, which identifies processes (at all levels), actors and information and control flows;

\- model analysis, which incorporates both ‘soft’ and ‘hard’ techniques of analysis;

\- prototyping a solution; and

\- evaluation of the proposed solution.

Vogel suggests that ‘EDI especially opens new ways for thinking and reengineering of existing processes to take advantage of technological capacity’ (Vogel, 1991, p. 205).

## EDI as an enabling mechanism

The direct impacts of EDI (and those most commonly cited) include labour-savings in the areas of data transcription, controls, and error investigation and correction; and fewer delays in data-handling. Lists of the resultant benefits which are to be found in the business literature have tended to be remarkably similar, although more recent writers have begun to refer to EDI benefits in terms of their strategic/tactical potential. Annis (1992), for instance, provides the following list:

Strategic EDI benefits include the ability to:

\- enable implementation of cost-reduction programmes (e.g. just in time/quick response (JIT/QR));

● provide support for maintenance or improvement of market share;

\- improve certain business services significantly;

\- enable business units to expand the use of computer applications.

Tactical EDI benefits include:

\- faster and more efficient information exchange with trading partners;

\- improved quality — reduction of errors, omissions and lost or misplaced documents;

\- increased productivity;

\- reduced out-of-pocket expenses (labour, printing, postage, faxing, filing).

As organizations gain greater experience with EDI, the more far-sighted users have realized that significant benefits are to be gained through the integration of related functions across organizational boundaries. It is not the replacement of electronic messaging for formerly paper-based communications which provides EDI's major strategic capabilities, but the associated changes in operation and function within and between organizations, made possible by EDI links: 'if . . . one uses EDI simply to automate the same processes one used to do, the effects will be moderate and EDI will not be exploited to its full potential' (Sheombar and Wagenaar, 1991, p. 208).

An example of business reengineering using EDI as the enabling mechanism may be found in Rochester (1989). He cites the case of Levi Strauss which, by means of its Quick Response system LeviLink, has achieved a complete vertical integration of the company's entire apparel manufacturing and marketing cycle. This includes the replenishment of inventory, the management and reconciliation of purchase orders, the receipt of goods, the processing and payment of invoices, the capture of point-of-sale information and the analysis of market trends. The management of Levi Strauss sees EDI as a unifying factor in the organization's use of quick response technology (DuBois, 1990), providing the company with a significant edge in a highly competitive marketplace.

This focus on achieving integration across organizational functions and between organizations is what distinguishes EDI from other forms of electronic transaction (such as electronic mail) and makes EDI an effective enabling infrastructure for strategic reengineering of the organization.

This paper initially considers the principle of EDI systems integration and proposes that the integration of EDI with internal application systems can be defined as a series of standard and recurring stages. Case study research has provided support for this view and indicates that such integration does, indeed, occur in a relatively standard manner for the majority of EDI-using organizations (Swatman and Swatman, 1991c).

The paper expands on the investigation of strategic integration between EDI and functional restructure by considering a major public sector organization in the State of Western Australia which is in the process of making EDI a vehicle for business reengineering (Swatman, Fowler and Gan, 1992). Building on the findings of the earlier research into consistent patterns of EDI integration, this organization is creating an integrated system of applications and structures based upon an EDI infrastructure. We relate these experiences of EDI integration to models of organizational growth and business restructure (Galliers and Sutherland, 1991, 1993; Scott-Morton, 1991), suggesting that only organizations which have achieved sufficient maturity will be able to seize the initiative offered by EDI.

Finally we suggest that, while EDI itself is a comparatively simple technology, the implications of its strategic use as an enabler of major organizational restructure are profound.

## EDI system integration

Strategic benefits attributable to EDI depend upon two forms of integration:

\- integration of information received from external sources with existing organizational systems and practices;

\- integration of the internal organizational systems and practices themselves, which can change the entire structure of the organization.

While the first of these forms of integration has already received some attention in academic and industry journals (Boucher, 1989; Skagen, 1989; Benjamin et al., 1990; Thavasuthan, 1990; Payne and Anderson, 1991; Rynne, 1992), it is less common to see EDI regarded as a facilitator of business reengineering (Sheombar and Wagenaar, 1991; Swatman and Swatman, 1991c).

## Integration with existing organizational systems and practices

Figure 1 shows that EDI software performs two quite disparate tasks — and thus can be sub-divided into two separate categories:

\- in-house interface software, which translates outgoing information from unstructured, company-specific formats into structured EDI formats (such as ANSI X12 or EDIFACT) and places these structured documents in an electronic 'out-tray' (and, of course, deals with incoming structured documents in an analogous manner); and

\- network communications software, which transmits the now structured message to its recipient (using data communications standards such as those defined in the OSI Reference Model). For an extended discussion of this topic, see the Appendix to Swatman and Swatman (1992a).

In identifying the various process entities, objects and activities, Davenport and Short (1990) suggest that inter-organizational information technologies such as EDI are of most use in the redesign of inter-organizational processes (for example, the purchase of goods). Yet, although many prospective EDI users accept this view and concentrate on EDI communications software (which is generally provided by third-party network suppliers in any case), it is in the domain of the in-house interface software that the greatest potential for strategic benefit in the form of organizational restructure may be found.

Linking EDI messages with in-house systems can improve an organization's internal operations — reducing or eliminating administrative overheads such as overtime premiums, overnight courier charges, late/incorrect shipments from suppliers, excess inventories, poor forecasting, disruptive production schedules and incorrect order entry (Skagen, 1989). The long-term strategic importance of EDI lies in the potential comparative advantage to be gained from its use by organizations which integrate the technology in such a way that they can obtain additional functionality and cost savings through enhanced productivity (Benjamin et al., 1990).

EDI in its simplest form (as a replacement for paper-based document flows) is little more than a rapid electronic mail service. Boucher (1989) points out that significant changes to commercial operations only occur with the integration of EDI into internal application systems, allowing a seamless connection to functions such as purchasing, order entry, shipping, inventory management and accounts payable and receivable. Payne and Anderson (1991) see these links as equally critical to the US Defense Department's operations, pointing out that existing Defense Department applications are not well integrated with internal logistics management systems. These authors (and others — see for instance Lloyd, 1992) believe that the lack of such links not only impedes information flow, but also inhibits management's attempts to engage in business restructure.

The need for integration between differing types of electronic information is also starting to receive attention (Ellinger, 1989; Lloyd, 1992). A tutorial from the US Trade Data Coordinating Committee (TDCC) points to further benefits available from links between EDI and other electronic communications, enabling the value-added leveraging of EDI systems to exchange non-character information such as graphics, video and voice transmissions not covered by EDI existing standards (Watkins and Taylor, 1988). These concepts are exactly those espoused by the originators of the various CALS (Computer-assisted Acquisition and Logistics Support) programmes currently under development worldwide (see, for example, Gessin, 1992; Mitchell, 1992; Potter, 1992).

## Integration of overall business practice

The benefits offered by internal systems integration (particularly when combined with the integration of internal/external document flows) are already substantial. Still more significant long-term changes in strategy and competitive position can be built upon the technical foundation provided by system integration (see, for example, discussions in Somogyi and Galliers, 1987; Dampney and Andrews, 1989; Davenport and Short, 1990; Scott-Morton, 1991). Such strategic opportunities arise from changes in the organization's view of its structure and functions, rather than from greater and greater investment in information technology alone.

A number of authors propose the view that business reengineering cannot be based on either a technological or organizational model in isolation. Benson and Parker's Enterprise-wide Information Management (EwIM) model (Benson and Parker, 1985; Parker and Benson, with Trainor, 1988; Parker and Benson, 1989; Parker, Trainor and Benson, 1989) suggests it is possible to plan for information technology in business by linking technology planning to business planning.

Perhaps more immediately relevant to the consideration of EDI's contribution to business reengineering, however, is the MIT90s framework, developed as part of the MIT Management in the 1990s research programme (Scott-Morton, 1991), which suggests that successful organizational transformation must be based upon alignment of corporate strategy, information technology and organizational structure (see Figure 3). Galliers and Sutherland go even further by taking a broader view and developing a model of organizational maturity which, inter alia, enables the determination of an organization's ability to complete organizational restructure successfully (Sutherland and Galliers, 1989; Galliers, 1991; Galliers and Sutherland, 1991, 1993), that is, whether the organization is capable of the necessary alignment.

The identifying characteristic of EDI as an ‘information technology’ is its ability to link business systems in a seamless fashion. The internationally agreed standards for document translation (which are EDI’s unique contribution to document transmission) provide the consistent link between application systems no matter how or from whom derived (see Swatman, Swatman and Fowler (1991) for a more detailed discussion of this issue).

Although EDI itself offers little that is new in a technological sense (the majority of EDI schemes use comparatively old-fashioned batch technology), the ability to link input/output from a variety of application systems provides the necessary underpinning for a revised view of the organization's information needs. There is no longer a requirement to design information systems on the basis of paper-based information flows.

## A model of EDI system integration

The statement that ‘EDI is 90 per cent business and 10 per cent technology’ has now become almost an article of faith within the EDI community. Indeed, a wide variety of writers within both the trade and academic sectors provide support for the view that EDI should be regarded as a strategic issue, rather than as a technical problem (see, for example, Robinson and Stanton, 1987; Sadhwani and Sarhan, 1987; Lyttle, 1988; Patrick, 1988; Rochester, 1989; Skagen, 1989; Emmelhainz, 1990; Swatman and Swatman, 1991a, 1991b). While there has been no lack of propounders of this concept, however, there has been little research into the actual process of integrating EDI (Swatman and Swatman, 1992b).

The comparatively limited nature of the attempts to validate this view of strategic EDI is partly due to the difficulties of perceiving EDI's technical aspects separately from the organizational issues of implementation and integration. Prospective users of EDI have tended to view both aspects of the total EDI system as falling within the domain of their own IS department and, since the creation of an EDI communications system is unquestionably a complex and difficult task, it is not surprising that they have recoiled from contemplation of the issues involved in integrating EDI into their own organization (although in Swatman, Swatman and Duke (1991) the formal specification of an EDI communications system showed that the requirements of the inter-organizational communications functions of EDI are entirely separate from the requirements of the in-house EDI system).

A research approach which seemed to offer real possibilities was to determine the significance of integration with internal application systems, so that this aspect might be separated from the broader question of organizational restructure. A multi-phase research programme was developed, attempting to isolate the various aspects of this question (this research project is described in detail in Swatman and Swatman, 1991c).

The series of case studies focused on major Australian organizations which had been involved with EDI for between one and three years.\* The organizations chosen were all medium to large in terms of both staff numbers and annual expenditure on information technology and each participant organization was:

\- involved in at least one EDI scheme; and

\- could be considered relatively sophisticated in terms of its technological infrastructure — that is, they were organizations having a mature and sophisticated view of information technology and which had achieved at least stage 5 of the Galliers and Sutherland model.

The participants in the study were chosen to reflect, as accurately as possible, the categories of Australian organization involved with EDI — reported in two earlier Australian surveys (Clarke et al., 1990; Swatman and Everett, 1991) and were drawn from the Australian parent/subsidiary of organizations in the following categories:

\- an international airline;

• a large financial institution;

\- an automobile manufacturer;

\- a major supplier of parts to the automotive industry;

\- a large primary-sector manufacturing organization;

• a major national retailer;

• the Australian Customs Service;

\- two major State Port Authorities (in Australia, all port authorities are found in the public sector);

\- five State Government Agencies (one of these agencies also acts as a port authority for several minor regional ports).

The series of case studies of Australian EDI-using organizations resulted in a model of the stages of EDI systems integration (illustrated in Figure 2, where hollow arrows indicate points of entry to the model).

Stage 1 involves a PC with EDI software (translation and communication) and requires:

\- a member of staff to key-in outgoing EDIFACT/X12 messages;

\- the printing of incoming EDIFACT/X12 messages.

Stage 2 is composed of two alternate paths, either:

\- 2a — files created by the mainframe/mini application systems down-loaded to a PC having the EDI translation and communications software (with, of course, analogous facilities for incoming EDI messages). This replaces the keying-in and printing-out of messages with flat files, speeding up the process and making incoming messages (in particular) more useful, since they do not require rekeying prior to use by another system.

\- 2b — the EDI software is itself based on a mainframe/mini-computer. This is similar to the process described in 2a, save that the physical uploading/downloading is also eliminated.

2a and 2b are essentially the same stage of integration, since the physical location of the EDI translation/communication software is of little importance. The difference between these sub-stages is therefore merely technical in nature (rather than organizational).

Stage 3 involves seamless integration between EDI transactions and production applications such as purchasing, order entry, production scheduling, inventory management, accounts receivable/payable, shipping and so on.

Stage 4 is the final stage of integration, in which EDI is seen as an integral part of the organizational context and is a major factor in strategic and information systems planning. This final, organizationally centred stage results in the use of EDI links to influence the functional structure of the organization and the structure of the supportive information systems within the organization.

Membership of an EDI scheme, per se, appears to provide no impetus towards integration within the organization:

\- those organizations which progressed fairly rapidly through the model were either the initiating organization or had participated in the original pilot stage of the scheme in question, suggesting a proactive approach to EDI;

\- the participant/schemes which neither progressed, nor planned to progress, towards integration were all non-initiating members of EDI schemes.

These findings suggested strong correlation between the initiation of the EDI scheme and progression through the stages of integration — indeed, only one non-initiating participant showed progress (and that organization was an active participant in the pilot scheme).

At the time the research was undertaken (late 1990/early 1991) EDI was in its comparatively early stages in Australia. Later extension of EDI schemes, however, has shown that organizations which are initiators of one scheme tend to apply their proactive attitude towards any other schemes of which they may later become members (Swatman, 1993). It thus appears that the integration process is driven by the same factor which drives the initiation of an EDI scheme — an organizational perception that EDI is a natural extension of pre-existing internal operations. This does not mean that only initiators of EDI systems gain comparative advantage from integration, but that organizations joining pre-existing EDI schemes must take a top-down, strategic view if the benefits of comparative advantage are to be gained.

There was a significant difference between the experience of that group of respondents developing their first inter-organizational system and those building on prior experience in this area. While the hypothesized stages of integration (and the progress through these stages) was borne out by all respondents to the study, more mature organizations entered the model at higher levels and progressed both faster and further. Those organizations entering the model at higher levels appeared to be 'piggy-backing' on the experience of other EDI implementations with which they were involved. A significant number of case study participants had started work on more than one EDI scheme over a very short period and were thus able to incorporate the experience gained from earlier attempts.

The factor which had the greatest influence on progress through the stages of the model was an organizational perspective of EDI. Interviewees' comments suggest that the motivation for the integration process is a strategic perception of EDI as a natural extension of pre-existing internal operations. While non-initiating participants in EDI schemes have the ability to gain long-term comparative advantage from system integration, they frequently fail to take a top-down, strategic view which incorporates EDI. EDI systems are initiated by organizations which view the inter-organizational communication involved as central to their way of doing business and are therefore naturally inclined to integrate their IOSs with their existing internal systems.

The development of a model of EDI system integration provided support for the theories of EDI's strategic role. It was apparent that those organizations willing to use EDI as a platform for business reengineering could achieve significant advantages not readily available to less innovative competitors. The EDI Research Group at Curtin University in Western Australia has been involved with one such organization.

## Strategic EDI implementation — a case example

The Western Australian Department of State Services (DoSS) provides an example of an organization which is considering the use of EDI from a strategic viewpoint. These strategic plans for the EDI implementation are based upon a public sector procurement information system known as Supplynet. This is a joint development between DoSS and Telecom Australia's value-added service provider Telecom Enhanced Services (formerly known as Telecom Plus).

## Supplynet

Supplynet is a telecommunications-based information system which was designed to facilitate better, more cost-effective government procurement by providing an interactive link between the State Government (as a purchaser) and its suppliers (Western Australian Government, 1991).

In its original form, Supplynet is essentially database oriented. It provides both information and facilities for retrieval concerning:

• Western Australian State Government tenders;

• period contracts;

\- historical purchasing information; and

• forward procurement plans,

together with an industry knowledge base and directories of government purchasing contacts and supplier capabilities/catalogues. Information is also available on Commonwealth Government tenders and both Australian and New Zealand Defence Department forward procurement plans.

The incorporation of EDI into Supplynet

Two of the original primary objectives of Supplynet were:

\- to enable government agencies to achieve more effective purchasing by giving them ready access to up-to-date information on suppliers and their capabilities/prices; and

\- to assist suppliers (particularly small, locally based businesses) by providing easy access to relevant government purchasing information.

Supplynet development is now at a point where these objectives have been largely realized. While ongoing enhancements to the Supplynet database facilities described earlier will continue to be made, the major focus is now on achieving the next, perhaps more significant, planned objective for Supplynet — that of providing an electronic purchasing service throughout the State Government by means of EDI.

Integrating EDI into the internal purchasing systems of State government agencies offers substantial benefits to the public sector — including reduced costs and faster, more efficient/effective purchasing on a whole-of-government scale. In addition, as the first whole-of-government Electronic Trading system to be developed and implemented in Australia, Supplynet has tremendous potential not only to enhance the State's entire procurement process, but also to influence future development of EDI systems and their integration throughout the Federal, and other State governments. This potential has been recognized by the Victorian State Government, which has also adopted Supplynet.

In view of the substantial potential benefits and consequences of the project, the Department of State Services has taken a top-down, strategic approach to guiding the development of Supplynet. High level management within the State Government (not only within DoSS but other critical areas such as Treasury) see EDI as a strategic platform which will enable them to achieve seamless, integrated, electronic procurement across the whole of government. This support and involvement has been, and will continue to be, critical to the successful development of Supplynet.

## Supplynet development

DoSS foreshadowed the actual introduction of EDI via Supplynet by concentrating on raising the level of awareness of EDI and Electronic Trading throughout the State. The strategic plan prepared for Supplynet was released as part of the overall State Supply Strategy, and actually made up the first public document outlining the government's intentions for EDI and Electronic Trading (see Western Australian Government, 1991).

DoSS is currently undertaking the next phase associated with making EDI facilities available through Supplynet — that of trialling sample documents. ANSI X12 Purchase Orders (document 850) may now be sent by government agencies using the existing non-EDI Common Use Purchasing System (CUPS) to suppliers connected to Supplynet. The purchase order was chosen as the first document to be made available because its transmission was identified as the facility most needed by Supplynet users. Further documents will be trialled and made available, one at a time. Additional documents presently planned are the Purchase Order Acknowledgement (855), Request for Quotation (840) and Quotation Response (843). At present only ANSI X12 documents are being used, although a migration path to UN/EDIFACT is planned for the future.

Once these documents are available on Supplynet, DoSS will concentrate on the next forecast stage for Supplynet — the implementation of EFT facilities. Funds transfer facilities will provide the ability to achieve a seamless government procurement process.

The degree to which this goal is realized will in turn depend on the degree to which the government is able to use EDI as a platform to reengineer the functions associated with the procurement process; and to enable the redesign and restructure of the existing processes involved in government purchasing.

In terms of the model of EDI integration described previously, the majority of State Government agencies are presently at stage 2b (with most suppliers at stage 2a) and the change to stage 3 has already begun. Current Supplynet development, however, is already beginning to take on the qualities of stage 4 integration. Recent work has focused not only on making purchase orders available through Supplynet but, even more importantly, on redesigning CUPS (the existing government purchase system) to enable the look-up of supplier information and the posting of purchase documents via CUPS/Supplynet integration. This link will enable purchasing officers to send purchase orders electronically to suppliers having the necessary receipt facility, using Supplynet but from within CUPS.

If the anticipated result of Supplynet's development is fully realized, the Western Australian government will have obtained an integrated, whole-of-government electronic purchasing system by 1994. Regardless of the degree to which this objective is accomplished, it is certain that little of what has been accomplished would have been possible without:

\- a strategic plan outlining an EDI-based development for Supplynet; and
- the support of high level management within the government.

It is equally certain that future benefits to be gained from Supplynet will be limited by the degree to which the government's existing procurement systems are reengineered to take full advantage of the potential EDI offers as a platform for strategic redevelopment.

## Business reengineering through EDI

The evidence adduced thus far suggests that the benefits of EDI arise from the business reengineering and organizational restructure which EDI enables. The case studies on which our model of integration is based show that integration occurs in those organizations which perceive EDI as a strategic opportunity — and fail to arise in those organizations which see EDI as an isolated information technology. While the essential characteristics of EDI itself do not vary from one organization to another, the use which organizations actually make of this enabling infrastructure can (and do) vary widely.

We have argued above and in earlier papers (Swatman and Swatman, 1991a, 1991c) that organizations initiating or participating in EDI pilot schemes are those which view the inter-organizational communication involved as central to their way of doing business; and which therefore turn naturally to the concept of integrating EDI with their existing internal systems. We now suggest that scheme initiation may not, in fact, be the proximate cause — but that this predisposition is itself a consequence of an underlying maturity in the organizational approach to information technology.

The MIT90s framework (Scott-Morton, 1991) describes organizations in terms of five forces which remain in dynamic equilibrium as the organizational context changes (the MIT90s model is illustrated in Figure 3). Of these five forces, management processes, structure, and individuals and their roles are considered to be critical to any organizational transformation. If organizations are to change as a result of evolving technology, they must focus on the culture of the organization (as represented by the organizational structure and by the roles and tasks of individuals). It is clear that organizations which wish to enter a considered transformation (which we can equate with the term ‘reengineering’) must take a holistic view of these five forces and their interaction.

McKersie and Walton (1991) give detailed consideration to the implementation of this holistic view of cultural realignment, focusing on motivation, competence and coordination among users — and on the top management policies needed to bring about such a desirable state of affairs. These authors do not discuss the 'stages' through which organizations must pass to achieve a level of maturity sufficient to enable an organizational vision, although it is certain that many organizations are unprepared to take advantage of the MIT90s framework. While a number of models of organizational maturity have been proposed, we consider below only two of these — Nolan's models (Nolan and Gibson, 1974; Nolan, 1979), which are widely referenced within the literature; and the more recent and more apposite Galliers and Sutherland's (1991) Model of Stages of IT Growth.

Nolan defines three stages of growth which, together, are concerned with technology management — initiation, contagion and control. In these early stages organizations adopt a reactive approach to information technology which implies a view of information technology opportunities taken in isolation from the wider organizational perspective. It is only beyond Nolan's (1979) 'transformation point' that an organization is considered to have sufficient maturity to be proactive in its use of information technology and, consequently, to use information technology to 'enable' strategic organizational transformation.

The limitations of the Nolan model result from the difficulty of measuring an organization's level of maturity in relation to the model, without access to the

Nolan–Norton proprietary data. In their Model of the Stages of IT Growth in Organizations, Galliers and Sutherland (1991, 1993) provide a six-level classification, which is particularly useful in that it describes characteristics identifying organizations capable of proactive, integrated and strategic use of information technology.

The Galliers and Sutherland model cross-references the stages of IT growth (shown in Table 1) against the 'Seven Ss' model of Pascale and Athos (1981) which represents the organizational elements of strategy, structure, systems, staff, style, skills and super-ordinate goals. Organizations at stage 5 (or higher) of this model are clearly in a position to make effective use of information technology (and EDI in particular) as an enabler of business reengineering, focusing on cultural changes within the vertical ellipse of the MIT90s framework (see Figure 3). Such organizations have the following characteristics:

\- strategically, they are opportunity-seeking and their strategic horizons extend beyond the organizational boundary;

\- information systems are de-centralized and integrated both internally and externally (while being controlled centrally);

\- managerial style is individualistic — we find product champions capable of ensuring that initiatives with wide-ranging organizational implications achieve implementation;

\- the information systems manager is a member of the senior executive team and thus has an organizational and entrepreneurial perspective;

\- most significantly, there is no distinction made between the roles of corporate, business and information systems planners and therefore a holistic view of the MIT90s five forces is evident.

As organizations move from the simplistic ad hoc level of information management towards a coherent, well-planned and integrated view of their information flows, there is a co-existing need for a technical infrastructure which can link their often disparate application systems. EDI offers such a technical infrastructure and can therefore be seen as a platform for strategic business reengineering and/or organizational re-alignment.

## Conclusion

The need for synergistic integration of apparently disparate systems is already well recognized by industry commentators:

\- in the manufacturing sector, EDI-based just-in-time (JIT) inventory management systems offer a means of greater efficiency: 'JIT is not an implementation in itself. It is a way of thinking about the manufacturing process. It tells what is good and what is bad within the factory and how factory and products should be designed' (van der Laan, 1991, p. 43);

\- in the retail industry quick response (QR) systems utilizing EDI depend for their success on connectivity: ‘every stage in the retail chain, from production to sales, must be able to link in to achieve the turnaround and productivity improvements sought’ (Spriggs, 1989, p. 28).

One could imagine that all proactive EDI initiators would design their systems from the top down, considering the long-term implications for organizational work practices and functions. Case study research (Emmelhainz, 1988; Benjamin et al., 1990; Swatman and Swatman, 1991c), however, seems to suggest that EDI systems are developed as and when needed. A coordinated and holistic view of EDI is either added later — or simply never happens. As one might expect, reactive EDI users are less likely than proactive users to have considered the matter from a centralized or strategic viewpoint.

The problems of information systems integration are not, of course, unique to EDI. All information systems affect the way in which organizations go about their business (for example, the move to corporate databases). What does appear to be different about EDI developments is their lack of incorporation into existing IS planning processes, even in organizations where there are well-established strategic planning departments.

One reason for this ‘bottom-up’ approach to EDI implementation may be a desire to minimize potential organizational conflict by ‘sneaking’ EDI into a company or agency. Benjamin, De Long and Scott-Morton (1990) found in their case studies that EDI system developers intentionally minimized changes to work practices in an attempt to obviate resistance to the new system, although it is in these changes to structure and processes that the true benefits of EDI are to be found. These case studies indicate:

\- a lack of alignment between corporate structure, IT and organizational structure as recommended by the MIT90s framework;

\- a fragmented view of strategic, business and information systems planning, which indicates that the organization is still to reach stage 5 of the Galliers and Sutherland model.

This is consistent with the findings of a substantial body of unpublished experience collected by the various EDI councils within Australia, North America and the UK/Europe. Brief summaries of these case studies may be found in the members' bulletins published by these organizations (for example, EDI Oz, which is produced by the EDI Council of Australia, or TradaNet News, published by INS (a major British EDI value-added services provider)).

Another possible explanation is that value-added service providers, attempting to extend their market penetration, often persuade organizations to 'trial' an EDI system, which later becomes incorporated into company operations without having passed through the planning process. While both these explanations are readily comprehensible, it is imperative that potential EDI users take a proactive stance — at least where their own internal processes are concerned!

Organizations which take a proactive view of information technology are those most likely to benefit from the opportunity for reengineering offered by EDI. This is supported in case studies quoted by Benjamin et al. (1990) and Swatman and Swatman (1991c) and in major international ‘success stories’, such as the Levi

Strauss LeviLink implementation (Rochester, 1989; DuBois, 1990) or the successful British retail chains Tesco and Marks & Spencer (INS, 1991a, 1991b).

Organizations which have used EDI as an enabling infrastructure for strategic redefinition all provide evidence of:

\- the use of IT to change the business process and redefine areas of organizational responsibility — the strategic alignment recommended by the MIT90s programme (Scott-Morton, 1991); and

\- a unified and holistic view of organizational planning which incorporates strategic, business and information systems planning into a single, harmonious framework (see Galliers, 1991).

Indeed, the Tesco case study (INS, 1991a) makes substantial use of the MIT90s model in explaining the background to its strategic view of information technology in general and EDI in particular.

Smaller organizations, pushed into EDI by large customers/suppliers, are likely to find that they have merely purchased an unusually expensive fax machine. Larger organizations, which look upon EDI as just another technological innovation, will benefit to the extent of the ‘tactical’ advantages described earlier. Only those organizations which have reached a sufficient level of maturity and which are prepared to undergo a cultural re-appraisal will be able to gain the longer-term strategic benefits which internal/external system integration can provide. As Tesco’s Systems Controller, Geoff Warburton, says: ‘By redesigning the business process with our trading partners we will have taken cost out of the supply chain — a benefit that ultimately will be passed on to the customer’ (INS, 1991a, p. 4).
