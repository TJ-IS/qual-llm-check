# Organizational practice, information resource deployment and systems success: a cross-cultural survey

Varun Grover

The University of South Carolina, College of Business Administration, Columbia, SC 29208, USA

Albert H Segars

Department of Operations and Strategic Management, Wallace E Carroll School of Management, Boston Collge, Chestnut Hill, MA 02167, USA

Douglas Durand

College of Business Administration, Southwest Missouri State University, Springfield, MO 65804, USA

This study analyses differences and similarities in information technology (IT) resource, practice and perceived success across firms in the USA, France and Korea. Results are framed within the context of cultural characteristics and their influence on business practice as developed in international business, cultural anthropology and social psychology literature. Our findings indicate similarities in resource use across these varying cultures. Specifically, IT budget and deployment policies seem fairly consistent among the countries examined. In contrast, very distinct differences in IT practice are observed. Specifically, the role of IT, integration of strategic and IT planning, and risk taking in information systems (IS) projects varied among the countries examined. Korean entities, unlike their US and French counterparts, seem to view IT in a more traditional, operational vein with little tolerance for risk taking. Interestingly, perceived benefits of IT use also varied among countries. Consistent with their cultural profile, US firms seem positively to associate market level impacts with the measured dimensions of IT practice and use. The results of this study, along with the cultural context in which they are presented, should aid both corporate planners and researchers in recognizing and explaining cultural differences and their potential influence on IT policy and use.

Keywords: international information systems practice, systems success, cross-cultural studies, global information technology strategy

Geographically, the borders that have separated countries for years still exist. However, from the perspective of industrial and financial activity, these borders are rapidly disappearing. Recent world events, such as Europe 1992 and the opening of formerly closed markets in the Eastern Bloc, have heightened both expectations and anxieties among top corporations seeking to enter, sustain, or expand firm presence in international markets. The potential rewards of successful global competition include market expansion, economies of scale and, consequently, competitive advantage (Porter, 1990). However, to reap these benefits, corporate planners must develop organizational structures and supporting technology that facilitate both internal and external coordination. While advanced communications and computing technologies (IT) have, in some instances, moderated the numerous complexities of global competition, many organizations have yet to master the interaction and complexities that geographical language, organizational, regulatory, and cultural differences have imposed on their transnational information systems (IS) functions (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991a). As noted by Karimi and Konsynski (1991), ‘... while many global firms have an explicit business strategy, few have a corresponding strategy for managing information technology internationally . . .’.

Keen (1987) notes two sets of barriers which inhibit development of effective global IT strategies. The first set, regulatory and standards differences across countries, is in many cases beyond the control of business entities. The second set, of internal barriers, concerns management knowledge and appreciation for the subtle differences in culture, economies, education and business practice that influence the way professionals think about and interact with IT across national boundaries. As noted by Keen, too many telecommunications and information systems managers see the world through domestic-tinted glasses. Often they overlook important differences in IT organization and practice between US, European and Asian firms. Thus, domestic assumptions regarding IT management are projected onto a very different international context. In many instances both organizational and IT structures that have evolved in other countries are ‘written off’ as irrelevant when in fact these structures may be optimal within the regulatory and cultural environment in which they were created.

The complexities of international competition and its dependence on IT have created numerous managerial concerns of both technical and sociological dimensions. In addition, it has necessitated an important area of theory building and empirical research that, until recently, has been under-represented in IS. While much has been written about largely uncontrollable regulatory and technical issues associated with international computing (Sethi and Olson, 1991; King and Sethi, 1992), little is known about the sociological complexities or patterns in IT management across nationalities. Yet, the top-ranked issue in a recent survey of executive-level IS managers in US-based multinationals was the education of senior personnel regarding the differences in managerial and IT practice in the international environment (Deans et al., 1991). Clearly, much can be learned from examination of organizational IT practice across national borders. Given the global imperative of many competitive environments, research confined to a single cultural context is constrained in both theory construction and practical application. Important differences across nationalities in: end-user computing, Ein-Dor and Segev (1992); key IS executive concerns (Watson and Brancheau, 1991); and motivating stimuli for analysts and programmers (Couger, 1986) have each been uncovered through cross-cultural surveys.

In the spirit of these past studies, the primary goal of this research is to examine differences across cultures in IT practice and use. However, unlike these previous efforts, our secondary objectives are: (a) to broaden the spectrum of analysable traits to include organizational and resource characteristics that have routinely been associated with IS success, and (b) attempt to structure the complex notion of 'culture' to better explain any emergent differences. Sample data are drawn from business entities in the culturally diverse contexts of the USA (American), France (European) and Korean (Asian). Examination of these important issues should aid managers in the formulation of global strategies concerning management and deployment of IT as well as provide objective results for researchers interested in organizational, sociological and resource-oriented impacts of perceived systems success.

## Research model

Exploration of firm structural/resource characteristics and their relationship with information system attributes and subsequent effectiveness is well grounded in management information systems (MIS) research. In their classic programme for MIS research, Mason and Mitroff (1973) identify organizational context as a key variable impacting the resulting effectiveness of information systems. Organizational context is defined by these authors as ‘information pathologies’ that are typically associated with different types of organizational structures. Building on this framework, Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) identify relevant organizational context variables affecting the success and failure of MIS. These sets of variables are classified based on their controllability with respect to MIS function. They include:

(1) uncontrollable factors such as firm size, structure and industry dynamism;

(2) partly controllable factors such as firm resources, formalization and psychological climate;

(3) controllable factors such as IS executive rank and location, level and existence of the IS steering committee.

Following the same causal perspective of these frameworks, the premise of this study is that levels of IS success differ among firms with varying resource commitment, organizational practice and structural characteristics with regard to IT management. Further, we would expect that cultural influences might cause these relationships to be different across various nationalities. A brief discussion follows of the relationships of these variables from an IS research perspective, which concludes with a discussion of how cultural differences may alter these relationships across nationalities. Figure 1 illustrates the research model for this study.

## IT resources

The effectiveness of organizational information systems has long been linked to the quantity and quality of technological and financial resources within the firm (Keen, 1991). Firms differ in their financial commitments to IT, their distribution of processing throughout the organization, and the integration of their systems with others through telecommunications technologies. As the information resource evolves strategically, organizations may channel additional financial capital into the building of both internal (within-firm) and external (between-firm) networks of computing in order to obtain the technological flexibility necessary for competitive survival or the capability for a competitive strike. Conversely, the information resource may be viewed in some organizations as an operational support mechanism. In these instances, financial commitment to IT, distribution of processing and development of specialized telecommunications organizations may be limited. Importantly, in both instances the appropriateness of resource commitment for the prevailing context will be a large determinant of the level and type of success derived from IS (Nolan, 1979; Weil and Olson, 1989; Keen, 1991). Further, it is plausible that this level of resource commitment (and related perceptions of success) flows directly from the managerial beliefs, values and attitudes which are interwoven with cultural background (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1992).

## Organizational infrastructure

While IT resources can, in many instances, be rapidly adapted to shifts in managerial policy, the organizational infrastructure evolves over time and is relatively slow to change. Within the context of this study, infrastructure is divided into:

(1) organizational practice, consisting of: (a) the firm's policy towards user involvement in systems design, (b) the integration of IS and strategic planning, (c) the role of information technology in the firm's portfolio of competitive resources, and (d) the firm's posture towards innovation or risk taking;

(2) structural characteristics, consisting of: (a) the firm's organizational or coordinating structure, and (b) the amount of information sharing between departments.

Exploration of these contingencies in relation to levels of IS success is particularly poignant when examined on a cross-cultural basis. Although the same hardware and software are available across nationalities, differences in organizational practice, along with the perceived benefits of such approaches, are likely to vary with differences in cultural characteristics.

Organizational practice. Over time, organizations develop unique dispositions towards IT planning, development, competitive role and innovation. Firms may view technology as strictly overhead with little incentive to do more than follow industry practice. Conversely, firms may seek innovative ways to exploit new technology and, in doing so, gain asymmetric advantage over competitors. Within these scenarios, it is reasonable to suspect that levels of user involvement, integration of IS and strategic planning, and the overall role of information technology will be quite different.

User involvement in the development of IS projects is generally believed to be associated with increased system usage and favourable perceptions of system quality. Increased levels of user satisfaction are subsequently translated into improved decision-making and gains in overall firm effectiveness (Ives and Olson, 1984; Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991b). However, recent work by Doll and Torkzadeh (1991) suggests that in some instances users differ in their desire to participate and that satisfaction may be contingent on the congruence of the user's desired and actual level of involvement. Because of potential differences across cultures in levels of participatory managerial practices, exploration of user involvement and systems success across national borders may provide additional insight into the sociological complexities surrounding this important relationship.

The integration of strategic and IS planning and its potential impact on firm effectiveness has been widely covered by a variety of authors (see Das et al., 1991 for a complete review). In general, the integration of MIS planning and corporate strategic planning is viewed as necessary in assuring that technological infrastructures maintain both the capability and flexibility to support strategic initiatives in ever-changing competitive environments. Without a fusion of these plans, organizational computing architectures may resemble the vision of technical rather than strategic planners. Technology purchased and deployed under these conditions can often cause the firm to be inflexible in answering the competitive challenges of more innovative IT users. Therefore, the tighter the coupling of these plans, the greater the firm's potential effectiveness or market-level impact within its industry.

When an organization links IT with the overall strategic vision of the firm, other corporate resources may be more heavily emphasized in the quest for competitive advantage. The emergence of information networks interconnecting the value chains of vertical and horizontal market participants, IT enabled product/service differentiation and computer-integrated manufacturing have proved to be substantial competitive weapons in the search for asymmetric profitability and market share (Ives and Learmonth, 1984). However, the level of investment and associated risks of these IT-based market initiatives are viewed as unacceptable by many firms (Vitale, 1986). In these instances, IT is relegated to supporting roles within the firm's competitive plan. Only when innovative IT becomes proved or a competitive necessity do these 'followers' adjust their portfolio of system offerings (Clemons and Kimbrough, 1986). Still others may view IT as simply an operational resource with little thought of its competitive implications. These 'roles of technology' have their genesis in the training, experiences and viewpoints of top corporate management (McFarlan, 1984). Thus, cultural as well as competitive influences may dictate how these roles and risk-taking propensities evolve across nationalities.

Structural characteristics. Grounded in organizational theory (Galbraith, 1974), the structure of the firm has long been studied as a contingency in overall firm success. As IT has evolved, so have the possible combinations of organizational and computing structures. The long-standing view of IT theorists is that organizational and IS success depends upon the 'fit' between formal structures and supporting technology (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978, 1982; Olson and Chervany, 1980; Ahituv et al., 1989). Leifer (1988) notes that successful implementation of IS architectures requires that systems match the style and processes by which tasks are performed within the organization. Interestingly, results have been mixed regarding patterns in organizational structure, deployment policies, and levels of systems effectiveness. For example, Ahituv et al. (1989) found no relationship between structure and hardware deployment in Israeli firms. In contrast, Olson and Chervany (1980) report a moderate relationship among these factors in US entities. As noted by Ahituv et al. (1989), more research is needed in a cross-cultural setting in order to better understand the cultural, political and organizational complexities underlying these complex relationships.

While the direction of causality (IT emerging from structure versus structure emerging from IT) has been subject to extensive debate (George and King, 1991), resulting organizational and IT structures influence the amount of information-sharing or capacity of the firm and, therefore, impact effectiveness at both the firm and market level (Lee and Leifer, 1992). Like organizational policy, structures are functions of many ever-changing sociological and industry-related variables. Environmental, regulatory and competitive conditions, along with the background and experiences of top management, each influence resulting organizational structures and the degree of information sharing. Thus, how firms organize both organizationally and technically may depend to a certain degree on cultural influences and, therefore, vary across national boundaries.

## IS success

In a recent critique of IS research, Weil and Olson (1989) note: ‘. . . MIS performance is a multifaceted concept and most researchers have arbitrarily chosen a single measure as a surrogate. This choice therefore limits the amount of variance explained and contributes to low construct validity of the studies (analysed) . . .’. Throughout IS literature, measures of systems success have been consistently employed as dependent variables in models of environmental, organizational and technical phenomenon explaining complex systems processes. Traditionally, IS success has been based on internal firm measures such as user satisfaction, system usage, or productivity gains (Bailey and Pearson, 1983). However, as systems have evolved from operational to competitive resources, practitioners and researchers have begun to link IS success with market impacts such as improved customer service, increased profitability, innovative product offerings, and procurement advantages (Ives and Learmonth, 1984; McFarlan, 1984; Johnston and Vitale, 1988). Noting this phenomena, Delone and McLean (1992) suggest that multiple measures of IS success are tenable and that both internal (firm) and market impacts should be considered in future research designs.

Consistent with this call for multiple measures of IS success, this study incorporates both firm-level and market-level measures of systems success as response variables. Thus, unique insight can be gained into the specific impact of IS factors across cultural contexts. Studying the relationships between organizational and resource characteristics of IT and levels of IS success in a multi-country setting is a necessary first step in understanding complex cultural dynamics important in the formulation of effective IS functions within the international environment. As uncovered through several studies in international business (see Franke et al., 1991 for review), perceptions of success are deeply rooted in culture. Hence, what might be termed 'IS failure' in the West may be viewed as a resounding 'success' in another cultural context. Therefore, it seems critical, particularly within studies of this nature, to measure success across several dimensions. Only then can patterns in managerial perceptions regarding IS performance be developed across national boundaries.

## The role of culture

The division of the variables in Figure 1 into technical (IT resources) and behavioural (organizational infrastructure) aspects of information structures is consistent with the dichotomization used by Ein-Dor and Segev (1992) in their cross-cultural analysis of end-user computing. As noted by these authors, it is reasonable to suspect that cultural impacts would be most prevalent in behavioural versus technical aspects of IT structure and practice across countries. However, many studies conducted thus far have failed to specify both an operational definition of culture and its associate impact on organizational practice. We know differences exist between countries A and B but we do not know why. Perhaps a clearer, more objective, understanding of cultural dynamics and its implications for organizational practice is necessary in order to more meaningfully frame results generated through international survey. Utilizing literature from the fields of international business, cultural anthropology and social psychology, we now attempt to provide such structure. Specifically, the objective of this section is to set forth a formal definition of culture and detail its likely impact on managerial practice and, subsequently, the relationships modelled.

Until recently, the existence of a relationship between management and national culture was far from obvious to many. A widely accepted belief was that sound management principles were somehow ‘universal’, that is, if national or local practice deviated from these established principles, then it was time to change local practice. The logical outcome of what Hofstede (1983a,b) terms the ‘convergence hypothesis’ is a set of universally accepted management practices which would in turn lead to societies becoming more and more alike. Obviously, observed reality has been far different from this prescribed theory. Supra-national organizations such as the European Common Market, European Community and even the coalition of former Soviet States, which are very much founded on the convergence belief, have had to recognize and address the complexities of national differences (Hurwitz and Lequesne, 1991; Nugent, 1991). In addition, a great deal of psychological and international business literature has been devoted to, and successful in, uncovering organizational and managerial differences attributable to national character (Ajiferuke and Boddewyn, 1970; Kelly et al., 1987). As noted by Hofstede (1983a): 'The national and regional differences are not disappearing, they are here to stay. In fact, these differences may become one of the most crucial problems for management — in particular for the management of multinational, multicultural organizations both public and private'.

Culture can best be defined as collective mental programming. It is that part of our environmental conditioning that is shared with members of our nation, region, or group but not with members of other nations, regions or groups (Pettigrew, 1979; Hofstede, 1983a,b; Adler et al., 1986). Importantly, culture should not be taken to mean 'state' or 'nation'. Culture may well encompass many national states or a single state may be home to two or more cultures. In essence, government, legal systems, educational systems, industrial relations systems, family structures, religious organizations, settlement patterns, literature and architecture reflect traditions and common ways of thinking which are rooted in the common culture but are not generalizable to other cultures (Hofstede, 1983a). In turn, individual thinking and 'perception of reality' is partly conditioned by these factors. Although additional 'sub-differences' exist between groups within nations (social class, occupations, religion, age, sex etc.), distinguishable patterns in beliefs and ways of thinking can be identified across broader national boundaries. Unfortunately, these differences in national culture are too often characterized by superficial and subjective statements. The lack of a scientific language capable of describing the numerous complexities of culture, coupled with impressionistic statements rather than systematic study, confound attempts to adequately describe these cultural dimensions. However, important research efforts in the field of psychology and international business have begun to objectively uncover important dimensions of national culture and their implications for organizational practice. Further, the increasing amount of cross-cultural research within IS provides a beginning in extending these organizational influences into the context of IS management and deployment practices.

Perhaps the first and best-known research in the area of objectively characterizing national culture is presented by Hofstede (1983a,b). Utilizing over 116000 survey questionnaires from 40 countries, this author, through factor analysis, identified four distinct dimensions of national culture. These dimensions, in addition to their organizational influence and potential influence on IT practice, are summarized in Table 1. As shown, cultures can be differentiated based on degrees of:

(1) collectivism (collectivist versus individualist);

(2) power distance (small or large);

(3) disposition towards uncertainty (strong versus weak);

(4) role of sexes (masculine versus feminine).

In turn, these dimensions directly impact organizational culture through the experiences and beliefs of policy-making managers.

Collectivism and power distance. As noted by Hofstede (1983a), collectivism and power distance tend to be closely related. Within cultures characterized by high levels of power distance and low individualism, inequalities in power and wealth are more readily accepted and have become part of the cultural fabric. Hence, the members of these societies are willing to follow those in charge with little desire to participate or direct. In essence, it is part of their expectations that leaders lead autocratically. In the organizational profile of such cultures, these tendencies have been related to increased centralization of authority and autocratic leadership styles (Hofstede, 1983a). These cultural characteristics and related organizational profiles are typical of Asian countries such as Korea, Hong Kong and Japan. At the other end of the cultural spectrum, nations such as the USA, UK and Canada are characterized by levels of small power distance and low collectivism. Organizations within these countries are typically characterized by decentralized authority and participative decision-making processes. Though few in number, some European countries such as France, Italy and Spain exhibit levels of large power distance and high individualism, implying moderately centralized decision-making structure.

Within the context of this study, we would expect the cultural dimensions of social integration and power distance, along with its association with centralization, to impact IT practice through hardware deployment policies, end-user involvement, information sharing and the integration of IT and corporate planning. Within the field of IS, several studies have established moderate to strong relationships between centralization of organization decision-making and hardware deployment (e.g. Ahituv et al., 1989). King (1983) also notes the preponderance of studies which suggest that centralized control and/or location of decision-making activities implies more centralized IT structures. Centralized decision-making may also imply lower levels of user involvement in the development and planning of IS. As noted by Ives and Olson (1984), user involvement can be considered a special case of participative decision-making in which users and systems designers substitute for superiors and subordinates.

Uncertainty avoidance and role of sexes. Dimensions of uncertainty avoidance and masculinity refer to the risk-taking disposition and aggressiveness of cultural members. Countries with weak uncertainty avoidance and high levels of masculinity would be characterized by firms more aggressive in their approach to competition and innovation. Performance within such organizations would typically be emphasized and measured in tangible terms. Countries characteristic of this profile include the USA, Canada and the UK. Conversely, countries with high levels of femininity and strong uncertainty avoidance would be characterized by firms with less aggressive competitive postures and more conservative dispositions towards innovation. Performance, in terms of tangible results, is not so heavily emphasized in these cultures. Such countries include Norway, Korea and France.

Related to the variables of this study, we would expect the dimensions of masculinity and uncertainty avoidance to impact managerial perceptions regarding the role of technology within the firm's collection of competitive resources. As noted by Vitale (1986), competitive uses of IT are risky in nature. The ever-changing nature of IT, coupled with its resource requirements in terms of financial and human capital, require much higher levels of risk disposition and aggressiveness in contrast to alternative competitive initiatives. In essence, less aggressive firms may view IT as an operational resource and choose merely to 'play it safe' while more innovative entities may view IT as an exploitable competitive weapon and risk organizational resources for its perceived gains. This perceived role of technology should also directly impact the encouragement of risk-taking in IS projects (McFarlan, 1984), existence of formalized telecommunications activities (Keen, 1988) and, subsequently, metrics of success for IS efforts (e.g. market versus firm-level impacts).

Importantly, it should be recognized that characterizing a national culture does not mean that each individual or organization is programmed in the same manner.

Clearly, certain aspects of culture, such as education and economic development, may impact emergent organizational practice more than other factors such as religion, local traditions, etc. Thus, these dimensions should be conceptualized as a kind of average pattern of beliefs and values around which these entities vary. Although Hofstede's four-dimension model may not represent the absolute truth regarding cultural differences, it has thus far served as a useful framework for several studies seeking to expose patterns in management and organizational life across cultures (Kreacic and Marsh, 1986; Watson and Brancheau, 1991). Within the context of this and other studies of cross-cultural IS relationships, we believe the framework can better structure the somewhat fuzzy construct of culture and, more importantly, meaningfully frame research findings.

## Methodology

## Sample

Data for this study was collected by means of a questionnaire. The sample was stratified across firms in the USA, France and Korea. These countries were purposely chosen because of their differences along the cultural dimensions developed by Hofstede (1983a). As noted earlier, Korean business entities are representative of Asian cultural influences. In general, the cultural pattern of such countries includes low levels of individualism, high levels of power distance, high levels of uncertainty avoidance and low levels of masculinity. French business entities are representative of European cultural influences. The cultural pattern of these countries includes high levels of individualism, high levels of power distance, high levels of uncertainty avoidance, and low levels of masculinity. US firms are representative of Western cultural influences. This cultural profile includes high levels of individualism, low levels of power distance, low levels of uncertainty avoidance and high levels of masculinity. Hence, this sample provides an adequate cross-representation of cultural characteristics necessary to uncover the relationships of interest.

A directory of corporate information systems executives was obtained for each country and questionnaires mailed to a random sample from each directory. The questionnaire was translated into Korean and French for administration in those countries. A local representative in each country coordinated the mailings. Respective responses and associated rates were: USA — 155 (31.0 per cent); France — 121 (24.2 per cent); and Korea — 68 (28.3 per cent). Although the sample is not uniformly distributed, the uniformity of response rates across countries does provide evidence of sampling adequacy among the strata. Analysis of descriptive statistics revealed approximately equal representation among economic sectors (manufacturing, service) and firm size across strata. In each instance, larger manufacturing entities were predominantly represented.

## Variable operationalizations

A substantial criticism of research of this nature is the lack of uniformity in findings resulting from inconsistent variable operationalizations (Olson and Chervany, 1980; Ahituv et al., 1989). When possible, care was taken to follow operationalizations and analytical techniques of past literature. However, given translation considerations and the exploratory nature of the study, many scales were kept deliberately simple. Variable proxies were based on both organizational behaviour and IS research. Similarly, statistical techniques were selected based on the exploratory nature of study, the attributes of data collected, and past research practices.

IT resources. Consistent with studies by Nolan (1979) and Weil and Olson (1989), IT budget was captured via a ratio measure of the estimated percentage of the firm's computing budget to overall sales. Following studies by Ahituv et al. (1989) and Leifer (1988), as well as Olson and Chervany (1980), computing deployment policy was captured via a categorical classification of:

(1) centralized — all computing facilities are at one geographic location;

(2) distributed — processors are located at various geographical locations and connected by communication network(s);

(3) decentralized — systems exist at various locations with very little interconnection between them.

Similar to Kriebel's (1984) survey of telecommunications practices, the final variable of this set, telecommunications organization, was obtained by determining if a separate position for telecommunications activities existed in the organization and if specialists in various areas such as ISDN, LAN, PBX and international PTTs were retained on staff. These questions were nominal in nature, consisting of 'yes' or 'no' responses.

Organizational infrastructure. Organizational practice variables of user involvement, strategic-IT planning and innovation/risk taking were captured via nominal scales. Respondents were asked about their organization's tolerance or disposition towards these variables. Thus a typical respondent would indicate in the affirmative or negative to statements such as 'user involvement is encouraged on all information system projects' or 'our top management encourages risk taking'. Consistent with the discussion by Johnston and Carrico (1988), the role of information technology was captured via a three-level categorical scale of:

(1) traditional role — IT supports operations and helps decision support and administrative functions but is not strategy related;

(2) evolving role — IT supports organizational strategy but is not integral to the development of corporate strategy;

(3) integral role — IT is integral to organizational strategy, IS and corporate management coordinate on creating systems for competitive advantage.

Following previous studies in IS (Leifer, 1988; Ahituv et al., 1989) and organizational behaviour (Galbraith, 1974), organizational structure consisted of four categories:

(1) functional — divisions according to professional know-how and organizational processes;

(2) product — organization by product line or services;

(3) matrix — combination of product line or services;

(4) conglomerate — holding company for several firms.

Information sharing was captured via a nominal scale. Respondents were asked to indicate 'yes' or 'no' to the statement: 'Ideas and information are constantly shared between departments'.

IS success. Information system success was captured via multiple seven-point Likert scales. Each item consisted of three-point descriptive anchors. As noted earlier, both internal and market impacts were measured. Respondents were asked to rate their organization's information systems along market-based dimensions of:

(1) contribution to organizational profitability (negative benefit — no contribution — high contribution);

(2) improvement of services provided (deterioration — no improvement — positive improvement);

as well as firm level dimensions of:

(1) technology diffusion (not widespread — moderately widespread — very widespread);

(2) level of user satisfaction (dissatisfied — neutral — very satisfied);

(3) frequency of use (rarely/never — occasionally/intermittently — continuously/constantly).

In addition, an overall rating of organizational systems success was solicited (failure — some success — very successful).

## Results

## IT resources

Computing budget. IS budget as a percentage of sales was not found to be significantly different among the sampled countries. Specifically, the mean value of this measure was 2.20 per cent for the USA, 2.22 per cent for France and 2.60 per cent for Korea. However, when budget is associated with levels of systems success, differences among countries emerge. Utilizing lambda, a non-parametric measure of association (Hollander and Wolfe, 1973), a positive, moderate association between budget and the market level measure of contribution to profitability (tau(155) = 0.204, p<0.01) and firm level measure of technological diffusion (tau(155) = 0.237, p<0.01) is found in US-based firms. Interestingly, no relationship between measures of success and budget is found in French or Korean entities. Such results seem to underscore the more competitive nature of Western culture and the associated need to justify investment in tangible terms.

Deployment policy. In each of the countries analysed, greater than 50 per cent of sample respondents report the existence of distributed computing environments within their respective firms. However, a noticeable difference is observed in the number of centralized environments in Korea (41.8 per cent) versus the USA (36.13 per cent) and France (36.36 per cent). Decentralized computing policies were the minority in each strata sampled. Thus it would seem that the majority of firms in each of these countries are distributing processing and increasing reliance on advanced telecommunications technologies to support organizational processes.

Using analysis of variance (ANOVA), an overall effect of hardware deployment on the market measure of profitability contribution, firm measures of technological diffusion and system usage, as well as the aggregate measure of overall effectiveness is found in French-based firms. Applying Tukey's HSD procedure for pairwise comparisons, significantly higher ratings are found across each significant measure in distributed versus centralized and decentralized computing environments. An overall effect of deployment policy is also observed in the market measure of service improvement in US firms. Like French firms, pairwise comparisons within this strata reveal significantly higher ratings in distributed versus centralized environments. Interestingly, deployment policy did not test significant with any measure of IS success in Korean entities.

Although not as pronounced as anticipated, cultural dimensions do seem to match emergent deployment policies. In the USA and France, both high in terms of individualism, distributed and decentralized deployment policies form a clear majority. In addition, higher levels of performance measures are associated with distributed versus centralized policies. Thus in these countries the 'fit' between cultural influences and emergent IT structures seems evident in the resulting levels of perceived IS success. Korean entities, typically low in individualism, have a noticeably high percentage of centralized computing environments consistent with the autocratic management styles likely to pervade such a culture. Technology trends, however, are facilitating significant distributed environments in this country. Further, no measure of performance tested significant across any deployment scheme. Although further study is needed, this may suggest that Korean entities are in a state of transition from centralized to distributed computing and that organizational structures in terms of decision-making and control are not yet fully aligned with emerging IT structures.

Telecommunications organization. The existence of formal telecommunications activities and specialists varied significantly between Korea and its European and Western counterparts. Specifically, a formal position in telecommunications activities was found in approximately 70 per cent of French and US firms. This figure was a mere 19 per cent in Korean respondents. Of firms which house this activity, a significant number of specialists in local area networks (LAN) and PBX were reported in the USA and France. Interestingly, no country reported significant numbers of specialists in ISDN, PTTs or telecommunications standards.

T-tests revealed that success measures were significantly different with the existence or non-existence of a formal telecommunications organization within both France and Korea. In France, higher levels of service improvement and system usage are observed in firms with formalized telecommunications. In Korea, firm level measures of technological diffusion, user satisfaction and system usage are associated with the existence of these formalized positions. Surprisingly, US firms showed no differences in performance measures across this variable.

The widespread existence of specialized telecommunications functions in both the USA and France is consistent with the results noted earlier regarding a progressive view of IT as a strategic resource in both of these countries. In contrast, but consistent with the risk averse and autocratic nature of Korean culture, few of these firms report the existence of formalized positions. However, those which house a formalized telecommunications position report higher levels of firm related impacts. Thus, in Korea, unlike the USA and France, the role of telecommunications may be emerging as an important competitive resource.

In all, IT resource use seems fairly consistent across sampled countries. In particular, French and US firms mirror each other in hardware deployment and telecommunications activities. Although a larger percentage of centralized architectures and absence of telecommunications activities suggest a more traditional approach to IT use in Korea, the large amount of distributed computing environments coupled with the higher levels of success from firms with formalized telecommunications activities may be a prelude to a shift towards practices adopted by their French and American counterparts.

## Organizational practice

The role of IT. The role of IT as an operational (traditional) resource unrelated to overall strategic planning was reported in 19.35 per cent of US firms, 23.97 per cent of French firms, and a rather larger 58.82 per cent of Korean firms. An evolving role of IT (supportive of strategy but not integral in formulation) was reported in 54.19 per cent of US firms, 47.11 per cent of French firms and 25 per cent of Korean firms. Respondents which view IT's role as an intregal part of strategy formulation and competitive advantage include 26.45 per cent from the USA, 28.93 per cent from France and 16.18 per cent from Korea. Here it is interesting to note the similarities in the Western and European countries regarding the role of IT. In both strata at least 75 per cent of the firms sampled view IT as at least an important supporting resource of organizational strategy. Conversely, the majority of Korean entities view IT as an operational rather than strategic corporate resource.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) of success measures across the differing roles of IT resulted in significant overall effects for each country. In the USA a significant overall effect of IT roles is found across all measures of systems success. Pairwise comparisons utilizing Tukey's range (HSD) test reveal higher ratings across all measures for integral versus traditional roles of IT. Significantly higher ratings for integral versus evolving roles were found for the measures of contribution to profitability, service improvement and overall success. Significantly higher ratings for evolving versus traditional roles were found for the measures of diffusion of technology, user satisfaction and overall success. In France, overall effects are observed for all measures except user satisfaction. Pairwise comparisons reveal higher levels of success among all significant effects for integral versus traditional roles of IT. Higher ratings of success are also observed for evolving versus traditional roles in measures of contribution to profitability, system usage and overall effectiveness. Korean firms exhibit overall effects in all measures except system usage. For each of these significant effects, higher ratings of IS success are associated with integral versus traditional roles of IT. Higher levels of user satisfaction and overall success are associated with evolving versus traditional IT roles.

While the role of IT varied across sampled countries, the perceived measures of success associated with these roles were nearly identical. In each country, firms which viewed IT as an integral component of strategy formulation demonstrated higher measures of market, firm, and overall performance measures than those which view IT in a more traditional role unrelated to strategy. Although a greater number of US and French firms view IT as a contingency or integral part of the firm's strategic plan, the majority of Korean entities view IT as operational in nature. Thus, consistent with earlier findings, Korean entities seem to be more conservative in their view of IT resources. Again, this is consistent with the high uncertainty avoidance and feminine nature of this culture. The risky investment and modes of competition inherent in IT-based strategy are counter to the cultural influences of this nation. In contrast, notions of risk and competition are very ingrained into American culture. Therefore, the progressive role of IT observed for these firms seems consistent with underlying cultural influences on managerial behaviour.

User involvement. User involvement in the development of IT-related projects is encouraged in 92 per cent of US, 85 per cent of French and 75 per cent of Korean respondents. Differences in user involvement across performance measures were captured via t-tests. Interestingly, the market level measure of contribution to profitability tested significantly higher for US firms which encourage user involvement, unlike their French and Korean counterparts. In these countries, unlike the USA, measures of technological diffusion, user satisfaction and overall success are associated with organizational policies that encourage user involvement.

Thus, while the formal involvement of users in IT development was predominantly encouraged across all countries, the impact of this practice on systems success varied. Only in the USA were market level impacts observed. In contrast, only firm level impacts tested significant in French or Korean firms. Power distance may be a likely explanation for these differences. As noted earlier, French and Korean firms are typically characterized by large power distance. Therefore, autocratic leadership structures may preclude the participation of lower level managers and subsequently inhibit the development of more innovative and market oriented IT applications. Instead, systems may be focused on reporting or control and therefore have greater impact on firm level measures. Conversely, US culture is characterized by low power distance and therefore more levels of management may be included in the selection and development of IT applications. The diversity and competitive nature of these managers may influence the portfolio of applications developed resulting in market versus firm based impact.

Innovation/risk taking. Innovation and risk taking in IT projects is encouraged in 37 per cent of US firms, 38 per cent of French firms and only 14 per cent of Korean firms. Again utilizing t-tests, organizational orientation towards IT innovation and risk taking was tested across performance measures. Results indicated higher levels of overall success in more innovative firms across all three countries. However, significant differences in market level impacts were found only in French firms. Higher levels of user satisfaction and system usage were found in more innovative US and French firms, while Korean firms tested higher on only the latter of these measures.

Somewhat surprisingly, the majority of firms in all three countries discourage risk taking in IT projects. However, consistent with the cultural dimensions discussed earlier, this figure was more pronounced in Korean versus US and French firms. In all countries risk taking was associated with higher levels of firm and overall performance impacts. In particular, system usage was consistently higher in all countries that encourage risk taking. Thus, it would seem that encouragement of risk taking in the development of IS projects is a key component in the realization of overall levels of effectiveness and usage regardless of nationality. Given the advantages of this practice as suggested by these results, less conservative approaches to IS development may be in order.

Integration of IS and strategic planning. The formal integration of IS and strategic planning was reported by 74 per cent of US, 78 per cent of French and by only 44 per cent of Korean firms. Both US and French based firms with formal integration of these plans seem to realize significantly greater market level impact from their collection of IS resources as indicated through t-tests. However, this finding was not generalizable to Korean firms, which tested significantly higher only on the measure of overall success.

Reflecting the more traditional stance observed in deployment and telecommunications organization, the majority of Korean entities report no integration of IS and strategic plans. This is in sharp contrast to the large percentage of firms that integrate these plans found in both France and the USA.

Again, these findings may suggest a transition in progress among Korean entities. Although these firms have yet to realize the market level impact of this practice found in US and French respondents, this may be attributable to the existence of formalized structures which have been slow to change. For instance, the more autocratic type of management structure typically inherent in Korean culture is non-participative. That is, top management formulates the operating plan and lower levels formulate their plans to accommodate. In contrast, more participative cultures such as those in the USA and France may have an easier transition in terms of integrating the plans of top and functional level management. Further, it is likely that prescribed benefits of this practice will accrue faster within firms of these already participative cultures.

## Structural characteristics

Organizational structure. The overwhelming choice of formal organization among each of the countries sampled is functional. Approximately 64 per cent of US, 70 per cent of French and 72 per cent of Korean firms utilize this organizational structure. Product and matrix forms of organization were evenly distributed across countries and accounted for the majority of remaining responses. As an initial step, a non-parametric test of association (lambda) was calculated to determine if organizational structure was related to computing deployment policy (Hollander and Wolfe, 1973). In no country did this relationship test significant. Next, an ANOVA was performed to determine if levels of IS success were different across organizational structures. Again, no overall effects were detected across the sampled countries.

Consistent with earlier findings of Ahituv et al. (1989) in Israeli firms, no association between structure and hardware deployment was detected within the present sample. In addition, levels of IS success did not seem to vary with organizational form in any country sampled. These findings suggest that the flexibility of new technology may render the concept of 'fit' between structure and computing technologies moot. In essence, many technological solutions may now be feasible and adequate for any particular organizational form.

Information sharing. Results suggest that information sharing between departments is significantly higher in France (59 per cent) versus the USA (35 per cent) and Korea (28 per cent). To determine if levels of IS success were higher in firms which foster information sharing, simple t-tests were performed. Significant differences in all countries across the measures user satisfaction, system usage and overall success are observed. While no market level impacts are observed in French firms, both measures tested significant in Korean firms. In the USA only improvement of services tested significant.

As noted by Lee and Leifer (1992), for IS structures to be effective, they should possess capacities for matching information sharing requirements. Thus, the level of information sharing provides a metric for determining the appropriateness of IT structures in relation to organizational task characteristics, technological interdependency, work teams and networked structures. Consistent with this notion, our results indicate that market, firm and overall measures of performance are associated with information sharing between departments. Surprisingly, French firms showed significantly higher levels of information sharing than their US or Korean counterparts. Given the higher levels of individualism and power distance inherent in French culture, these results are somewhat counterintuitive.

Nevertheless, they imply a significant difference among this strata along this dimension.

Our findings suggest distinct differences in behavioural related characteristics towards IT practice across varying cultures. Importantly, the perceived impacts of IS on both firm and market levels also differed across the countries sampled. Thus a generalization of computing practice and associated benefits across national domains may be inappropriate. Clearly, cultural differences impact levels of success in addition to the way in which IT is viewed, planned for and, in some cases, used. Although exploratory in nature, the findings of this study suggest that quantifiable dimensions of culture are useful in more fully explaining observed differences between countries. Thus, results and underlying causes become more useful to practitioners who must formulate and implement global IT structures in the face of competitive and cultural uncertainty. ## Conclusions

This study has sought to identify patterns of IT usage and practice, along with associated measures of systems success, across a stratified sample of US, French and Korean firms. Knowledge of patterns in global practice and use of IT can only broaden both managerial practice and academic understanding of technical, organizational and cultural phenomena that may impact the successful operation of the increasingly global IS function. It is interesting to note that only firms in the USA associated higher levels of IT spending with market or firm level impacts. This may suggest that the masculine nature so prevalent in the US culture precludes IT investment which cannot be justified by quantifiable ‘bottom line’ performance measures. Additional evidence of this phenomenon is found in the USA column of Table 2. For each significant effect, US firms consistently realized higher levels of market related performance measures.

Our findings also suggest that US firms tend to be more progressive in their view of IT for competitive advantage than Korean and, to a lesser degree, French firms. This again may be attributable to the higher level of masculinity and weak uncertainty avoidance inherent in US culture. Notions of ‘competitive advantage’, ‘locking out potential entrants’ and ‘locking in customers’ are very descriptive of, and compatible with, organizational practice in such cultures. It may also further explain the observed association between IT investment and market related impact. Perhaps the notions of IT-based competitive advantage have yet to manifest themselves in terms of profitability in more risk averse and feminine cultures such as France and Korea. Unlike the USA, the cultures of these countries are somewhat counter to the ‘risk taking’, ‘aggressive’ philosophies underlying competitive advantage. Thus, IT investments may be made for other strategic reasons and therefore not be perceived as impacting overall firm measures.

The nature of cultural influences. What is the specific influence of culture on the IS function? Although no formal theory has been developed to address this issue, a great deal of research in the area of international business has set forth propositions on how culture influences organizational life. While this study has, in an exploratory vein, attempted to extend these notions onto practices and policies regarding IT, much more work is needed in order to fully understand this complex relationship. Perhaps certain dimensions of culture such as education, governmental structures and resources are more explanatory of emergent organizational practices than other sociological dimensions. Culture is a rather large concept. Importantly, a link between relevant dimensions of this construct and IT practice is necessary in order to explain in a scientific sense emerging world patterns in computing.

Framed within the context of Hofstede's (1983a) framework, our findings suggest the potential presence of cultural influence on the role of information technology within organizations. Specifically, the use of IT as an operational versus strategic resource seems directly related to the cultural dimensions of uncertainty avoidance and sex role. Thus, managers from cultures significantly varying in these influences may have a markedly different perception of IT's competitive role. Other cultural influences seem inherent in IS planning and innovation. The level of integration in IS and strategic planning, as well as tendencies in deployment policy, telecommunications organization and risk taking, seem linked to levels of power distance and collectivism among countries. Thus, the centralized autocratic structures resulting from higher levels of these cultural influences may yield IT policies quite different from the decentralized structures resulting from lower levels of these dimensions. Importantly, global IT strategies must be formulated with these influences in mind. Assuming that management is a universal set of understood principles is not consistent with both sensory and empirical evidence. A future challenge to IS researchers will be to more specifically identify these contingencies and formulate relevant theory regarding emergent global IT practice.

## Trends and effectiveness of IT practice

What are the current trends and associated levels of success in international IS management? Although this survey included only three countries, some general patterns in IT use and realized success transcend country boundaries.

(1) Our findings strongly suggest that firms with successful IS functions view IT as an integral part of their competitive resources. Thus, regardless of geographical context, organizational acceptance of IT's strategic role and formal integration of IS and strategic planning seem to be a precursor for systems effectiveness.

(2) Although the majority of firms in each strata discouraged risk taking in IS projects, higher levels of systems success were consistently associated with risk taking. Perhaps a second look at formalized policies for innovating and developing systems is in order. The lower costs and increased capabilities of IT may have redefined the traditional equation of risk and return regarding innovative systems initiatives.

(3) Consistently, firms with higher levels of systems success contained organizational structures which facilitate information sharing between departments. Thus, collaboration between departments seems to be an important determinant in the development of effective organizational systems.

(4) A clear tendency across these countries is towards distributed computing. Consistently, firms associated higher levels of success with this architecture versus centralized and decentralized environments.

These results would suggest that regardless of culture or country, recognition of the importance of IS, aggressive initiatives, within a context of distributed computing and information sharing/collaboration across parochial groups, would tend to be more successful. Unfortunately, exploration of a comprehensive set of factors related to IS success is beyond the scope of a single study. Additionally, some conclusions regarding the meaning of these results and the very notion of culture itself may be somewhat biased by the author's own cultural background. However, knowledge of ‘what works’ across geographic borders has major implications for both practitioners and researchers.

Further studies in this area should continue to identify those characteristics of IT use and practice that are different across countries and frame them within the context of perceived systems effectiveness. Specific approaches to planning, policies for IS control, systems development, and patterns in technology implementation and diffusion are only a few of the many factors that may vary among cultures and contribute to levels of perceived systems effectiveness. Clearly, a full understanding of this new IS domain cannot be garnered in a few studies. Rather, research must accumulate in order to define the dimensions of culture and their associated impact with IS practice and success. Only then can a deeper knowledge of ‘what works’ across nations be framed within the context of human imperatives that impact resulting organizational and technical structures.
