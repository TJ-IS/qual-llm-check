# Government regulation through voluntary cooperation: the strategic impact of information technology

Trevor A Williams

School of Management, Human Resources and Industrial Relations, Queensland University of Technology, GPO Box 2434, Brisbane Q4001, Australia

In public regulation there are substantial shifts towards strategies which emphasize voluntary rather than mandatory relations between regulators and those regulated. Information technology (IT) systems can encourage or discourage voluntary cooperation. From a study of electronic tax return lodgement in Australia it is suggested that IT has strengthened voluntary relations at the level of transactions, but that asymmetry in the basic regulatory relationship may be an impediment to full voluntary cooperation.

Keywords: government regulation information technology

New information technology (IT) can have important effects on relations between organizations. However, discussion of these effects has been largely restricted to describing systems applications, with insufficient attention given to the networks of interorganizational relations (IORs) in which IT is introduced. Often such networks are complex, dynamic and contain significant uncertainty. Failure to take sufficient account of essential features of IORs in the design and implementation of IT systems can have unanticipated and unwanted consequences, as was exemplified by the role now attributed to machine trading during the 1987 international share market crisis. Conversely, new IT systems can assist the development of cooperative relations and collaborative strategies between organizations if the systems are planned and implemented with adequate knowledge of the IORs.

IT systems play an important role in the development of collaborative, strategic linkages between private enterprise organizations (Rockart and Short, 1989). There is also growing use of IT systems for strategic purposes by public sector organizations. The costs and ineffectiveness of public regulation by conventional bureaucratic methods are prompting governments and their agencies to pursue regulatory aims through alternative approaches, such as ‘privatization’ and ‘deregulation’, which rely on voluntary cooperation and compliance by the regulated. The shift from regulation through externally imposed control to regulation through voluntary cooperation entails important changes in relations between regulatory agencies and their target organizations or populations. In assessing the contribution of IT systems to strategic aims of public regulation, an important question is how such systems affect the prospects of achieving regulation through voluntary cooperation.

This paper examines IORs between the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) and tax agents and identifies issues that affect the ATO's strategic goal of achieving maximum voluntary taxpayer compliance. The paper then assesses the introduction of electronic tax return lodgement in terms of its effects on IORs and its contribution to the voluntary compliance strategy. The findings are both encouraging and cautionary. The positive effects of IT systems on regulatory relationships reduce transactions costs and increase efficiency. They also provide some scope for the regulated to exercise greater control over transactions and to reduce uncertainty in their dealings with regulatory agencies. However, while government agencies may realistically seek greater mutual cooperation in their dealings with the public, the basic asymmetry which often underpins public regulation may set limits to the attainment of voluntary cooperation.

## Background

## Public regulation through interorganizational relations

O'Toole (1988) suggests that changes from conventional bureaucratic regulation to approaches based on voluntary cooperation and compliance require regulatory agencies to rethink the ‘implementation structures’ of public policy, regarding them less as monolithic command and control structures and more in terms of interorganizational networks in which voluntary relations play an important part.

Oliver (1990) identifies mandatory relations established by formal legal-political prescriptions and five main types of voluntary relations between organizations:

(1) Asymmetry, where relations are influenced by the desire to control other organizations and by reluctance to relinquish autonomy.

(2) Stability, where organizations seek to reduce uncertainty and increase predictability in their transactions with other organizations.

(3) Efficiency, where organizations establish relations that economize on the costs of transactions between them.

(4) Legitimacy, where relations are established in response to institutional pressures on organizations to justify their activities and outputs.

(5) Reciprocity, or the pursuit of common or mutually beneficial goals and interests.

Whereas traditional regulatory approaches emphasize mandatory relations between government agencies and the targets of regulation, the effectiveness of voluntary approaches depends, in varying degrees, on how successfully they appeal to target organizations on the dimensions of asymmetry, stability, efficiency, legitimacy and reciprocity.

The effectiveness of regulatory strategies is also influenced by how strongly agencies are placed relative to their target organizations. The interorganizational conditions most favourable to regulatory agencies include (Zald, 1978):

(1) Clear and consistent public policy mandates.

(2) A single regulatory agency responsible for implementing the mandate, or strong consensus and coordination between agencies where more than one is involved.

(3) High visibility and permanence of the behaviour to be regulated.

(4) Sufficient agency capacity to reward compliance and punish noncompliance.

(5) Sufficient willingness and ability of target organizations to comply or, conversely, low target organization capacity to avoid or resist regulation.

Even in extreme cases, however, mandated regulatory control is never absolute but rather is interdependent with the voluntary purposes and actions of those over whom control is exercised (Lammers, 1988). Furthermore, the interdependence between mandatory and voluntary IORs is best understood, not in static terms, but as a dynamic and evolving process which is affected by developments outside of as well as within the interorganizational network (McGuire, 1976; Stern, 1979).

## Interorganizational dynamics in the Australian Tax System

The Income Tax Assessment Act identifies the Australian Taxation Office as the principal control agent in the Australian Tax System, and assigns it wide formal powers in the operation of tax laws and its dealings with taxpayers and their agents. Tax agents play an important role as intermediaries between the ATO and some two-thirds of taxpayers. Agents perform functions which are essential to the administration of the tax system, such as ensuring a timely flow of tax returns. They are also in a strategic position to influence taxpayer attitudes towards paying tax and, hence, the level of taxpayer compliance with tax laws, the amount of revenue collected and the costs of collection (Tomasic and Pentony, 1990).

Relations between the ATO and tax agents have changed significantly in the past 10 years. In the 1970s tax avoidance was widespread. Loopholes and ‘grey areas’ in tax laws provided considerable scope for tax avoidance, or the use of legal means to escape paying tax. The attitudes and decisions of the courts tended to be sympathetic to tax avoidance because they emphasized strict interpretation of the letter of the law. If the arrangements which taxpayers adopted to minimize tax payable did not contradict what the law stated, disputes between taxpayers and the ATO were likely to be decided in favour of taxpayers. The attitudes of the courts reflected norms regarding acceptable tax conduct which probably prevailed in the wider society. While it was accepted that governments could not function without revenue, individuals generally pay tax grudgingly and do not like to pay more than they have to. Tax avoidance was socially acceptable because it was legal, the more ingenious avoidance schemes were admired and those which proved effective were emulated (Tomasic and Pentony, 1990).

Much of this changed dramatically following the election of the Hawke Labour Government in 1983. Legislation introduced new taxes which targeted areas where avoidance was prevalent and strengthened income reporting controls. Under the self assessment policy introduced in the mid-1980s, all Australian taxpayers became responsible for paying the correct amount of tax whether they knew the law or not. Penalties for non-compliance were made more severe and the determination and imposition of penalties was entrusted to the ATO. The courts' attitude also changed from strict interpretation of the letter of the law to emphasizing the law's intent, and close scrutiny of tax arrangements to establish their commercial credibility (Tomasic and Pentony, 1990).

The ATO's position as a regulatory agency has been strengthened in the following ways:

(1) Overall norms and standards of tax compliance have been made clearer and more consistent by government policies and the attitudes of the courts.

(2) The ATO has emerged more clearly as the principal regulatory agency, whereas previously its authority was more vulnerable to judicial contradiction.

(3) Important areas of tax avoidance have been made more visible.

(4) The ATO's capacity to punish non-compliance by taxpayers and their agents has increased through improved surveillance and more severe penalties.

(5) The capacity of taxpayers and their agents to resist paying the correct amount of tax has been reduced.

The ATO has gained the upper hand in the regulatory process, and the focus of tax agents has shifted from tax minimization to ensuring that their clients comply with tax laws at minimal cost. However, the ATO still depends on agents for efficient and orderly tax administration and for compliance by the majority of taxpayers. Hence, it is important to consider how the changes affected voluntary relations between agents and the ATO.

Voluntary IORs appear to have been affected in the following ways:

(1) Asymmetry has shifted in favour of the ATO because, while agents still have power to the extent that the ATO depends on them, they have lost autonomy and have been forced into closer interdependence with the ATO where they and their clients have less discretion in the question of whether to comply.

(2) Stability and predictability have increased but the changes have also introduced new sources of uncertainty. Tomasic and Pentony (1990) found that there was a real risk to taxpayers and agents of inadvertent non-compliance due to the complexity of tax laws and the detailed and frequent changes in them.

(3) Transactions costs have risen because, although it is less expensive for taxpayers to comply than not comply, compliance is more expensive than the old means of tax avoidance.

(4) The ATO rather than (or with the support of) the courts has become the main judge of legitimate tax conduct, which affects agents' livelihoods and the credibility with which they represent their clients.

(5) Reciprocity has been affected to the extent that taxpayers' interests generally are now best served by compliance at minimum cost, which is more compatible with the ATO's goals than tax avoidance was.

Agents may have accepted and even welcomed the replacement of tax avoidance by compliance as the focus of their relations with the ATO, but there also appear to be tensions in these relations. The study by Tomasic and Pentony (1990) and a survey of tax agents conducted for the ATO by Donovan Research (1991) suggest that, in the relationships which emerged from the changes:

(1) Most agents are satisfied in their role of minimizing the amount of tax their clients are required to pay, giving independent advice to clients, trying to ensure full compliance, and acting as intermediaries between taxpayers and the ATO.

(2) Many agents resent doing work which they think should be done by the ATO and which makes them feel like agents of the ATO.

(3) Many agents feel that they bear too much of the responsibility for taxpayer compliance and too much of the risk of penalty for non-compliance as a result of self-assessment.

(4) Agents want more input to the formulation of tax policy.

## The Electronic Lodgement Service (ELS) in Australia

The Electronic Lodgement Service (ELS) is an important element in the ATO's strategy of maximizing voluntary taxpayer compliance with tax laws. The strategy is to reduce both the resources tied up in routine checking and processing of tax returns and the time lapse between lodgement of returns and the issue of assessment notices and refunds. The resources released from returns processing are intended to be used to improve taxpayer services. The ATO plans to balance the increased emphasis on voluntary cooperation with a strengthening of its auditing capability so that, while the goal is ‘improved service to honest taxpayers’, there will be greater risk of detection for taxpayers who use illegitimate means to avoid paying tax.

ELS is a national computer-based system which allows authorized tax agents to transmit income tax returns electronically. ELS accepts returns prepared according to specification and processes the data to the point of issuing an assessment. Most agents use personal computers and modems to transmit tax return data files to the ATO. Some prepare returns on floppy disks which are delivered to ATO branch offices. Access is through Telecom Australia's Austpac network to Data Takeon Machines (DTOMs) located in the Adelaide, Brisbane, Melbourne and Sydney branch offices.

After an agent transmits a group of tax return files to ELS, the DTOM tells the agent which returns have been received without transmission errors and which have been accepted for processing. The accepted returns are physically loaded onto branch office processing machines for automatic processing. There are two types of returns. 'Paper returns' are lodged both electronically and on paper, and electronic processing cannot begin until the paper versions are matched to the electronic versions by keying in the Tax File Numbers. 'Paperless returns' do not need to be matched to begin the processing. These returns are lodged electronically and agents retain the paper copies. Paper returns are seen by the ATO as an interim measure, with full electronic transmission and processing becoming the standard mode.

If there are no transmission or lodgement errors, if the information on the return is complete and arithmetically correct, and if there is no request for a tax ruling on any item in the return, the return is processed automatically through to the issue of an assessment notice. If a tax ruling is required or if the return is for a non-resident of Australia, the return can be sent electronically to an off-line area for attention. If the return contains errors or omissions, it is corrected by returns processing staff and put back into ELS for completion of processing.

Following trials in Adelaide in 1988–89 and Melbourne in 1989–90, ELS was implemented nationally for individual income tax returns in the 1990–91 tax year. By April 1991 some 4800 agents had enrolled in ELS and lodged 2.9 million or 55 per cent of the total expected individual returns. The ATO's objective was to turn around 80 per cent of ELS returns within 14 days, compared with 6 to 8 weeks previously, and all branch offices achieved this target. For the 1991–92 year the ATO aimed to have 70 per cent of agents enrolled in ELS and to turn around 98 per cent of returns within 14 days.

ELS is not simply the automation of a procedure. The ATO considers that it brings substantial benefits. For taxpayers the main benefits are faster issue of assessment notices and refunds and quicker response to requests for amendments to returns. For agents, ELS offers: a better service to clients, prompt acknowledgement that the ATO has received returns, a substantial reduction in processing errors by ATO staff because agents enter the data themselves, 24-hour electronic communication with the ATO, improved cash flow, an extension of the time allowed them to lodge returns as an inducement to use ELS, and improved efficiency in their business operations through the use of computers. The main benefit that the ATO claims for itself is that it can reduce the resources spent on returns processing and use these in areas that directly contribute to its goal of maximizing voluntary taxpayer compliance.

Venkatraman and Kambil (1991) argue that electronic lodgement in the USA has further important effects on the market for return preparation and lodgement services. Their view is that return preparation will become commercially less important as software enables preparation to be automated and more easily done by taxpayers themselves. Electronic filing or lodgement services are expected to be offered independently of return preparation and by a wider range of providers such as banks and supermarkets. They see the potential of electronic data interchange being exploited more fully through the integration of services offered by tax agents, financial organizations and merchandising firms to create a new range of products and services which includes lending in anticipation of tax refunds, catalogue purchasing, and sophisticated financial planning. Venkatraman and Kambil (1991) see the entire basis of competition changing as new sources of competitive advantage bring forth new players, change the roles of established players, add new relationships, and transform existing relationships.

Stern (1981) has argued that target organizations are only likely to cooperate willingly with public regulation if incentives are offered that stabilize their environments and permit them to grow. ELS offers tax agents benefits in their dealings with the ATO and there is the further possibility, suggested by Venkatraman and Kambil (1991), that electronic lodgement will introduce new growth opportunities in agents' business environments. Prior to the introduction of ELS, tax changes in Australia had stabilized relations with agents, but they reduced agent autonomy, created new areas of uncertainty and risk for agents, increased the costs of compliance, and strengthened institutional pressures on agents to conform to regulatory requirements. While many agents may have been relieved to see the end of the tax avoidance era, the changes up to that point appear to have cost the agents and benefited the ATO. Given the ATO's aim of improving voluntary cooperative relations in tax compliance, it is important to understand how ELS actually affects voluntary IORs between agents and the ATO and the prospects for voluntary cooperation.

## The study

During June and July 1992 interviews were held with a random sample of 200 registered tax agents enrolled on ELS. The research objective was to obtain opinions on ELS from a comprehensive range of agents. The agents, chosen by a stratified random sampling procedure, dealt with the ATO through seven branch offices which represented the range of ATO branch locations: capital city central business districts, greater metropolitan areas, major regional centres, and country towns. Selection of the branch offices also took into account differences in levels of agent satisfaction with ATO service identified in an earlier survey conducted by Donovan Research (1991). The number of agents selected through each branch office was approximately in proportion to the total number of agents registered with that branch. Table 1 shows the total numbers of agents registered with each branch office and the numbers of agents interviewed.

The interviews obtained background data on the client size and primary client segment (individual taxpayer, small business, corporate) of each agent, the proportion of individual returns lodged through ELS, the frequency of agent contact with the ATO during the main lodgement period, the ATO sections which agents had most contact with, the range of tax and other financial services which agents offered, and whether tax services were integrated with other services. The interviews then sought agents' views on how ELS affected their dealings and relations with the ATO on the dimensions of asymmetry, stability, efficiency, legitimacy and reciprocity. Finally, agents were asked how they saw the level of cooperation between themselves and the ATO, whether this had improved as a result of ELS, and whether there had been other developments in relations with the ATO which were more important than ELS. ## Effects of ELS on asymmetry

With ELS, agents perform the data entry tasks previously carried out by ATO staff. This gives the agents greater control over a part of their transactions which had caused them frustration when processing delays occurred due to ATO keying errors. It also gives the agents greater autonomy because the tax return data which they enter will be accepted without ATO intervention unless there are errors or omissions in the data. Thus, around three-quarters (74 per cent) of the respondents reported that ELS had given them more control over their transactions with the ATO and over processing errors (77 per cent). They also thought that they had more control over their own planning and organization (73 per cent), which they attributed mainly to the longer time period ELS agents are allowed in which to lodge returns. Most agents (73 per cent) thought that ELS gave the ATO enhanced surveillance capacity but evidently were not worried, with 52 per cent taking the view that this was being used neither to penalize nor help agents, while 36 per cent thought that the ATO was using its greater monitoring ability to advise and assist them. However, the Donovan Research survey (1991) identified a desire by agents for more input to the formulation of tax policy and, for 83 per cent of respondents, ELS has not led to greater agent participation in regulatory decision-making.

## Effects of ELS on stability and predictability

Both the Tomasic and Pentony (1990) and the Donovan (1991) studies found the issue of uncertainty and risk of penalty for inadvertent non-compliance to be a source of dissatisfaction for agents in their relations with the ATO. ELS appears to have reduced uncertainty for agents in the initial lodgement, processing and assessment of returns by making the time period shorter and more predictable. Almost two-thirds (62 per cent) of respondents said that they had more knowledge and confidence about how the ATO would treat the returns they lodged through ELS. A similar proportion (62 per cent) thought that faster processing of returns and issue of assessment notices without intervention by ATO staff gave them greater certainty of outcomes. For one-third (34 per cent) of the agents, quicker assessments and refunds and more predictable timing meant that they knew earlier when clients would be paid or would have to pay, which enabled them to assist clients to plan for tax. Computer software and information enabled more accurate predictions and easier forward planning, and the extended lodgement period gave agents more time to plan with clients. Over one-third (39 per cent) of respondents thought that the quicker refunds, fewer processing errors and delays, and the more certain time frame made their clients less anxious about their returns.

ELS evidently has had less effect on uncertainty about eventual outcomes. One-third of respondents (32 per cent) did not have more knowledge and confidence about how the ATO would treat their ELS returns, and disagreed that faster processing and assessment of returns gave them greater certainty of outcomes. They said that certainty of outcomes came from confidence in their own return preparation, that ATO staff had not intervened since the introduction of self assessment before ELS, and that the ATO could still decide to audit returns later. Most respondents thought that selection of taxpayers for audit was random or that it had been affected by self assessment rather than ELS. One-third (32 per cent) thought that clients' prospects of being audited actually increased with ELS due to greater information systems capability and the release of ATO staff from routine processing work. Only 22 per cent of respondents thought that ELS gave them faster access to tax rulings, which Tomasic and Pentony (1990) had suggested offered a means of reducing taxpayer and agent uncertainty. Similarly, while Venkatraman and Kambil (1991) suggest that agents could exploit information systems to provide clients with more sophisticated and integrated financial planning services, 58 per cent of respondents thought that ELS had not affected their ability to help clients to plan for tax, because it did not resolve what could and could not be claimed and clients could be audited later. Even more respondents (82 per cent) denied that ELS helped them to provide other financial services and integrate these with their tax advice to clients. Thus, while a significant proportion of respondents reported that the shorter and more predictable time frame for initial processing and assessment made their clients less anxious, more respondents had found that their clients became more anxious and sensitive to delays, or said that their clients were always anxious and that ELS had not affected this.

## Effects of ELS on efficiency

The majority of agents (71 per cent) reported that earlier refunds resulted in them being paid more quickly by clients which improved their cash flow. Opinions were divided on whether it was more or less costly to prepare returns. While 45 per cent of respondents said that ELS made it simpler and less time consuming and reduced their paper and postage costs, 48 per cent saw no difference in costs; particularly agents who lodged returns on floppy disks (rather then via modem) which were not returned to them by the ATO. Almost two-thirds (63 per cent) thought that ELS reduced the time they spent making enquiries about returns to the ATO but 34 per cent reported no change.

A third of the agents (33 per cent) were attracting more clients due to ELS and the prospect of obtaining faster refunds, but 62 per cent thought ELS was no longer a competitive advantage and agents who concentrated on corporate clients had not sought more individual taxpayers as clients. On the question of whether ELS created new business opportunities, again one-third agreed that it did. Most agents equated new business with attracting more clients to their existing services rather than exploring new possibilities such as those suggested by Venkatraman and Kambil (1991). The majority (85 per cent) did not think that ELS threatened their business in any way, which also indicates that ELS was not regarded as a technological development which could substantially change the competitive basis of the tax industry.

ELS appears to have benefited most agents by improving their cash flow and reducing some costs in their transactions with the ATO. It is not seen by most as a source of competitive change or of new business opportunities and growth.

## Effects of ELS on legitimacy

The ATO emerged from the changes of the 1980s as the principal judge of legitimate tax conduct. Being enrolled in ELS may enhance an agent's image with the ATO, thereby appearing to be operating within the law and willing to cooperate with the ATO. Being well regarded by the ATO may be important to agents' efforts to represent their clients and to their professional standing. Being on ELS may also affect agents' images with taxpayers.

Opinions on how ELS affected agents' standing with the ATO varied, with 45 per cent believing that the ATO had a more favourable attitude towards agents on ELS and 39 per cent expressing the view that the ATO's attitude had not changed. Those who thought that the ATO's attitude to ELS agents was more favourable had a general impression gained from the ATO's marketing campaign, but also reported that ATO staff were more helpful to them with ELS problems and pointed to the longer lodgement periods which agents on ELS were allowed. Among agents who saw no difference in the ATO's attitude, most simply said that they were unaware of any change, some thought that it had been more favourable at first but was no longer so, and others replied that they had always had good relations with the ATO. The majority of respondents thought that ELS had improved their images with clients.

## Effects of ELS on reciprocity

The changes since the 1980s may have produced a greater coincidence of interests between tax agents and the ATO in the matter of compliance but, according to previous studies (Tomasic and Pentony, 1990; Donovan Research, 1991), they also created tensions with agents believing that they were used too much as agents of the ATO and that they bore too much of the responsibility and risk in transactions with the ATO.

More than half (57 per cent) of the respondents thought that ELS resulted in them doing more work for the ATO because they now performed the data entry, and also were responsible for keeping tax records which the ATO no longer kept and for providing statistical information to the government. Conversely, 39 per cent replied that they did not do any more work for the ATO and many of these saw ELS as being of mutual benefit. Over one-third (38 per cent) thought that the work they did for the ATO was more than offset by the gains to them while 28 per cent disagreed. One-third (36 per cent) reported that the ATO did more for them with ELS but 58 per cent disagreed.

As a result of ELS, more than one-third (38 per cent) of respondents thought that they had more in common with the ATO and that there were more mutual concerns. Their reasons included both parties having a common stake in the system, improved relations and, to a lesser extent, agents acquiring similar concerns to the ATO as a result of taking over some of its functions. However, 58 per cent of respondents said that they had no more in common with the ATO, with some emphasizing that they still acted for their clients whose interests may conflict with those of the ATO. Nevertheless, 52 per cent of agents thought that ELS had reduced or eliminated tensions and conflicts with the ATO through the reduced processing time, fewer errors and delays, quicker refunds, less need to make enquiries, and the longer lodgement period. More than a third did not think that ELS had reduced tension and conflict. While some of these agents pointed to persistent problems, others said that there had not been tensions and conflicts before or attributed improvements in their relations with the ATO to more general change than ELS.

## Effects of ELS on overall cooperation

The ATO's strategy is directed towards maximizing voluntary compliance with tax laws and the cooperation of the tax agents is important to this strategy. Hence, an important overall measure of the effects of ELS is whether it has contributed to a higher level of cooperation between agents and the ATO.

According to 62 per cent of the agents interviewed there has been improvement in cooperation which they attribute to ELS. Their reasons include faster lodgement and assessment of returns, more cooperation and help from ATO staff, better feedback and access to information, fewer processing errors, quicker refunds and other responses from the ATO, and having more time to lodge returns. Of the 32 per cent who reported no change, half said that relations had been good before. A small number of agents (4 per cent) reported more cooperative relations for reasons of ATO policy and staff attitudes rather than ELS. Only 3 per cent said that relations had become less cooperative. For half of the agents ELS is the single most important development in their dealings and relations with the ATO, followed by the establishment of the tax agents liaison service centres and improved communication contact and the introduction of self assessment.

Respondents' views of ELS are most consistent with respect to its effects on the asymmetry dimension of their relations with the ATO. For most respondents, ELS has increased their control over transactions. It has had no effect on their participation in ATO policy and decisions and it has increased the ATO's ability to monitor agents, although most respondents are not concerned that this enhanced capability will be used punitively against them. On the other voluntary IORs, visual inspection of Table 3 suggests that there is greater variation in respondents' views. From a strategic perspective, an important question is whether these differences between respondents are systematically related to the ATO's goal of voluntary compliance. Given that 62 per cent of respondents thought that the level of cooperation in their relations with the ATO had improved with ELS and 31.5 per cent disagreed, it is possible that these two respondent groups are different in other respects.

## Discriminant analysis of respondent differences

Discriminant analysis was carried out to identify the main sources of variance in agents' attitudes towards ELS. Discriminant analysis facilitated study of differences between those who felt cooperation had improved (62 per cent) and those who did not (32 per cent) with respect to all variables simultaneously, and was also appropriate because responses on the variables were dichotomous (Klecka, 1980). One discriminant function was extracted from the data which combined the response characteristics of the two groups and allowed examination of the relationships between the individual variables and the function. Structure coefficients measured how closely each variable was related to the function (independently of its relationships with other variables), or how much each variable contributed to discrimination between the groups along the discriminant function. A Wilks' lambda was obtained to estimate the magnitude of the group differences and, converted to a chi-squared distribution, the probability of the observed differences being present in the tax agent population.

Stepwise inclusion of variables (Klecka, 1980) identified five variables which made significant unique contributions to the differences between the two groups. The structure coefficients, in descending order of association between each variable and the discriminant function, are shown in Table 4. The Wilks' lambda of 0.774 indicates that the function reveals some discrimination between the groups beyond the ability of the variables themselves to discriminate among respondents. The chi-squared of 46.515 is significant at 0.000, suggesting that the differences between the two respondent groups also exist in the tax agent population. The procedure correctly assigned 77 per cent of respondents to the two groups, compared with a prior probability of 50 per cent, which suggests that the function is a reasonably accurate discriminator and, indirectly, confirms that the differences between the two groups are not spurious (Klecka, 1980).

As already noted from Table 3, the majority of respondents were in agreement about the positive effects of ELS on their control over transactions with the ATO. Hence, the asymmetry variables would not be expected to play much part in discriminating between the two groups of respondents.

For respondents who reported improved cooperation in their relations with the ATO, the attraction of ELS appears to lie in a sense of greater reciprocity (less tension and conflict with the ATO, and the ATO doing more for agents on ELS) and in getting more clients (efficiency).

Only one of the respondents' background characteristics features prominently in the discriminant function. Differences in size of clientele, main types of clients, frequency of contact with the ATO via ELS, and mix of tax and other financial services, do not appear to have affected respondents' reactions to ELS. Case identification made it possible to examine the membership of the two respondent groups defined by the discriminant function, and this revealed that the groups did not differ according to the branch offices which agents dealt with.

The background variable that does feature is surprising. Tax agents liaison and service centres were established to provide information and assistance to agents. They do have enforcement-related tasks, such as reminding agents of their obligations to lodge returns according to schedules, but much of their contact with agents is intended to be cooperative. They have also played an increasing role in assisting agents to lodge returns through ELS. However, at least in some branch offices, the tax agents liaison and service centres were not directly and actively involved in ELS when it was first introduced. This caused some adverse comment from other sections, such as returns processing, which were encountering unexpectedly high error rates in agents' electronic lodgements and thought that the liaison and service centres should be responsible for helping the agents. In its own right, contact with the centres is reported favourably by respondents overall but, at least at the time of interviewing, there may still have been problems with this contact from the agents' viewpoint.

## Conclusions

IORs are becoming increasingly important in the strategies which organizations pursue in their environments and are a major domain for IT systems applications. In public regulation, there have been substantial shifts towards strategies involving voluntary as well as mandatory IORs. The increasing application of IT systems can support this change of approach if the systems offer inducements to voluntary cooperation by meeting the needs of the interdependent parties.

The study reported here is exploratory. The interview questions were open-ended to allow exploration and the responses to most questions could only be coded dichotomously. A survey of a larger sample of tax agents using scaled items would be expected to produce more reliable results. Nevertheless, assessments of ELS by the agents who were interviewed suggest that it is contributing to the ATO's strategy by strengthening voluntary IORs, mainly through giving agents greater control and certainty with respect to lodgement and processing of returns and enabling them to economize on the costs they incur in these transactions. Respondents who reported that cooperation had improved in their relations with the ATO appear to have been more sensitive to some of these effects and to have perceived greater reciprocity with the ATO as a result.

However, the case of ELS suggests that a constraint on strategies to achieve the aims of public regulation through voluntary cooperation may lie in the regulatory relationship itself which, historically, has been based on the exercise of bureaucratic authority by the regulators over the regulated and which often spawned mutual mistrust and antagonism. Thus, the great majority of respondents reported no increase in their participation in regulatory decision-making; ELS has not reduced uncertainty about eventual outcomes for a significant proportion of agents; and the effects of ELS in promoting reciprocity in relations between agents and the ATO appear to have been limited. At least in this case, asymmetry has two aspects, control over transactions processing and control over regulatory policy and decision-making. ELS has reduced the first aspect but not the second and, in regulatory relationships which retain strong elements of asymmetry, there may be corresponding limits to the extent to which mutual confidence can be strengthened as a basis for fully voluntary cooperation.

Nevertheless, there is scope to move further towards voluntary cooperative regulation through the practical help that the regulatory agency offers the regulated and finding ways to reduce uncertainty, for example by simplifying tax laws and introducing IT enhancements which improve information access and aid decision-making. ELS may well come under more pressure in these respects as it is extended to more complex tax returns. Beyond this, whether public regulation can become fully voluntary and cooperative appears to be a question of the regulated having an effective say in what they will comply with.

The importance and tractability of the different aspects of IORs will vary according to the case in question. Within the field of public regulation, regulatory strategies have been shown to range from universalistic rulebook enforcement through detached command and control (where the agency relies heavily on its formal mandate) to non-enforcement and the fostering of cooperative self-regulation, where the agency attempts to develop voluntary relations by assisting target organizations in their particular circumstances (Braithwaite et al., 1987). At least in part, the appropriateness and feasibility of a strategy will depend on the nature of the regulatory function. Some functions, such as conciliation, provide considerably more scope for agencies to assist target organizations and establish voluntary cooperative relations than do other functions. Taxation probably is one of the more difficult areas in which to shift the strategic orientation from mandatory to voluntary compliance. Nevertheless, such a change in regulatory strategies is central to public sector reforms in many countries. The case examined here suggests that, as IT becomes more widely applied to public sector operations, the new systems can make a positive contribution to strategic change if they are informed by an adequate understanding of the IORs which are involved.
