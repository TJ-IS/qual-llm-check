# Information technology and information systems perspectives on business strategies

M H Atkins

Centre for Management Studies, University of Aberdeen, Edward Wright Building, Dunbar Street, Old Aberdeen AB92TY, UK

This paper investigates the nature of the business strategies firms pursue and considers how firms that are pursuing different business strategies differ in the information technology and information systems (IT/IS) strategies adopted. The empirical results arising from survey research conducted in the UK show that most businesses appear to be competing through new products in existing markets and the most popular information system is one which increases market share. For those firms whose strategy is to sell existing goods in existing markets, the results reveal that the most important contribution of IT/IS is considered to be enabling the reduction of costs. Whilst firms do use IT/IS in a strategic way, this is frequently undertaken for defensive reasons to maintain the firm's position in the industry. However, IT/IS strategies are also seen to support the general business strategy of product or market innovation. Recommendations for further research are also made, which focus on the potential bias introduced into this type of research where only IS managers are surveyed and business managers' views are not taken into account. This study has looked at only two models of business strategy and there is a need to extend the analysis to look at other models of business strategy, particularly Porter's.

Keywords: information technology, information systems, business strategy, market penetration, diversification, defender, first mover

This paper presents the results of a survey of information systems (IS) managers' perspectives on the business strategies pursued by their organizations. There is an emphasis in the current strategic information systems literature on analysis of the way technology and business strategies are linked or aligned, either through joint determination or an iterative procedure to achieve congruency. In this paper the primary emphasis is on the business strategy; in particular it is about the IS managers' perceptions of what these business strategies are and ways in which information technology and systems (IT/IS) may be used to deliver those strategies. In essence, therefore, the paper seeks to investigate how firms that are pursuing different business strategies differ in their IT/IS.

The nature of strategic business decisions was well defined by Ansoff (1987, p. 24) who described such decisions as ‘primarily concerned with external, rather than internal, problems of the firm and specifically with the selection of the product-mix which the firm will produce and the markets to which it will sell’. This means that strategy is about the environmental context of the organization and not just ‘important’ issues.

A definition of strategic information systems is not immediately apparent, however. One definition reported and adopted by Reich and Huff (1991) was proposed by Wiseman (1985): (they are) 'information systems used to support or shape the competitive strategy of the organization' (p. 7). This definition may now be regarded by some as too broad on the basis that IT/IS need to be integrated or aligned with the business strategy — a mere support function is seen as inappropriate. Nevertheless, the definition is a useful starting point for the exploration of strategic information systems which span the interface between business strategy and IS.

## Previous research

The evidence for a link between IT and business strategy is equivocal. A UK survey (Wilson, 1989) found that over three-quarters of financial-sector and Times 500 companies claimed to have an IS strategy. This needs to be treated with caution because, in the prevailing climate, the preparation of an IS strategy is obviously a prime task of the IS manager, but the fact that a company has an IS strategy does not guarantee that it achieves competitive advantage thereby or uses IT in a strategic way. In addition, of course, the strategy may well be internally focused and devoid of any external focus expected of a strategic information system. Indeed, many organizations' IT/IS 'strategy' statements more resemble 'shopping-lists' of thing to buy or do than reflect the way that business strategy can be delivered through technology.

Galliers (1987a) reported evidence (p. 238) that ‘supports the contention that consideration of competitiveness tends not to be incorporated into information planning in practice’. The evidence cited is that of Kearney (1984) who found that in only 30 per cent of British firms surveyed did IT make a contribution to market share and in nearly half of the companies, IT failed to reduce costs. Of course, Kearney’s survey was undertaken comparatively early in the 1980s when the strategic impact of IT was almost certainly less widely recognized (at least in the UK) than it is today.

The paper by Galliers (1987a) drew upon earlier work of Earl (1986) and identified the ‘changing nature and focus of information systems (IS) planning over time’ (emphasis added) (p. 229). However, the way in which organizations compete is unlikely to be a function of time. There is no evidence that new and emerging organizations compete in ways that are different to the ways in which older, more mature, organizations compete. Galliers outlined a changing focus of IS planning, suggesting that the tasks, objective, direction and involvement, methodological emphasis and administrative context of IS planning would all change with increased organizational maturity. The model was purely normative and unsubstantiated, however.

Nevertheless, IS can deliver strategic impacts and a number of the more important and/or more common means of achieving competitive advantage through IT and IS were enumerated (Galliers, 1987a, p. 237):

(1) establishing entry barriers (McFarlan, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985);

(2) introducing or changing switching costs (McFarlan, 1984);

(3) differentiating products or services (Parsons, 1983; Harvey, 1986);

(4) concentrating on a particular market or product niche (Parsons, 1983);

(5) limiting access to distribution channels (Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Meiklejohn, 1986);

(6) ensuring competitive pricing (Breath and Ives, 1986; Harvey, 1986);

(7) reducing supply costs and easing supply (Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Meiklejohn, 1986; Oliff and Marchand, 1991);

(8) increasing cost effectiveness and efficiency (Parsons, 1983; Collins, 1986; Meiklejohn, 1986);

(9) using one's information resource as a product in itself (Meiklejohn, 1986; Galliers, 1987b).

It is important to consider whether, in fact, numbers 7 and 8 represent strategies. Porter (1985) emphasizes that all firms should produce at the lowest possible cost given their chosen competitive strategy. Therefore ‘reducing supply costs and easing supply’ can only be of strategic importance (in the sense of the relationship with the external environment) insofar as this allows the individual firm to do significantly better than its competitors, thereby enabling a cost leadership strategy. A similar argument applies to ‘increasing cost effectiveness and efficiency’. Accordingly it seems appropriate to concatenate these two strategies into one (‘easing supply’) and to restrict the list of potential strategic uses of IT/IS to eight.

In the main most of those questioned for previous research have been IS managers or those working within the IS domain and those asking the questions have themselves frequently come from a similar background.\* This area of research can therefore be complicated by IS managers' lack of experience outside their own functional area (Atkins and Galliers, 1991a,b).

## Models of strategic business behaviour

It is clear that organizations do compete in different ways but differences in the strategic approach of IT or IS when firms pursue these different business strategies has not yet been the subject of empirical analysis. It is possible to explore various models of competitive strategy that have been espoused from time to time.

The recent paper by Chan and Huff (1992) identified 10 alternative strategy typologies; for present purposes two are considered in detail. In practice it would not be possible to identify, differentiate or verify all models in a single piece of research. Some, for example Porter's (1980, 1985) model, have been excluded because of the difficult semantics involved: these were considered likely to lead to difficulty in establishing valid responses in a questionnaire survey. Those selected are both enduring models where techniques for analysis are already well established.

The first model considered was proposed by Ansoff (1965) and is still used as a primary modelling tool by strategy students and practitioners. Elegant in its simplicity, the model distinguishes products and markets. Firms may continue selling their existing products or introduce new ones and they may continue to operate in their existing markets or move into new ones. The model is frequently represented as a two-dimensional grid (Table 1) from which four alternative strategies emerge. Of course, individual companies may attempt different strategies for each strategic business unit (SBU)\* and, in theory at least, there is little to prevent a company from choosing more than one of these strategies for any SBU. In practice, most firms will pursue only one strategy for each SBU, although in businesses with numerous SBUs, the strategy for the entire business enterprise may appear confused with elements of each strategy appearing.

The second generic model of strategic orientation selected was that of Miles and Snow (1978). They suggested that competitors in any industry would fall into one of four distinct categories, each one of which would imply different responses to the strategic initiatives of others. The four categories suggested were: defenders, prospectors, analysers and reactors. These categories are explained more fully in the following section.

## IT/IS contributions to business strategy

First, consider the ways in which IT/IS may be linked to the business strategies suggested by the Ansoff model. What are the appropriate IT and IS strategies for companies following market penetration strategies?

It is likely that measures undertaken to reduce costs or lock in customers will play a significant part in such strategies. For certain types of firms (most likely those in manufacturing rather than service industries), it is possible for IT to play a key role in a strategy of product development. For example, computer aided design or manufacturing systems (CAD/CAM) may help to reduce product lead times or enhance product quality. Another example is the use by oil exploration companies of sophisticated software (frequently developed in-house) used in the location and assessment of new oil finds. Some oil companies believe that their proprietary software gives them a strategic advantage over their competitors. The strategic use of IT for market development is likely to require a different set of IT and IS skills; perhaps high-level management or executive information systems (EIS) will be required to enable potential new markets to be located and serviced quickly.

The link between IT and diversification is less easily identified, particularly since so much diversification arises through the acquisition of existing businesses rather than through the organic growth of firms. In fact, IT may act as a brake on successful diversification, particularly if businesses are locked into monolithic systems incompatible with those of newly acquired businesses. Indeed, it has recently been suggested that this is one reason some companies are increasingly looking to outsource their IS (Gupta and Gupta, 1992).\* Thus the imperative to correctly ensure congruency between the IT/IS strategy and the business strategy; an IT/IS strategy not congruent with the business strategy may prevent the achievement of that business strategy.\*\*

What are the IT/IS strategies that might be appropriate for firms following the business strategies suggested by the Miles and Snow model? Defenders focus their effort on existing operations most commonly by emphasizing cost reduction. An appropriate metaphor for this type of competitor might be ‘we maintain our position’ (in existing markets and products, it might be added).\*\*\* Thus defenders are similar to those who seek a market penetration strategy as in the Ansoff model and so the most likely IT/IS strategies will be those associated with cost reductions or locking in customers.

By contrast, prospectors (sometimes known as ‘first movers’) focus on product innovation and developing market opportunities. They thereby display aspects of both the product development types and the market development types described by Ansoff. A suitable metaphor for prospectors could be ‘we aim to be first’ (to offer new products or services that are different). The appropriate IT/IS strategies are likely to be those such as CAD/CAM or EIS that give the desired product or market shift.

Analysers (sometimes known as ‘late movers’) may frequently operate in two or more related markets, carefully selecting their strategic response according to the precise needs of the individual markets, some of which may require a defensive response and others which may require a more proactive response. Their competitive style is likely to be generally reactive and therefore an appropriate metaphor would be ‘we aim to follow quickly’ (new products or markets created by others). Thus the IT/IS strategies in such firms might be based on a planning system that Earl (1986a) called ‘inside-out’ when describing the IS planning function. To align and integrate with the chosen business strategy and potentially gain competitive advantage from IT, the IT and IS would need to support such ‘inside-out’ intelligence functions.

Finally the reactors: these lack a coherent and consistent strategy and are often ineffective. It is probable that these competitors are very slow to react and therefore an appropriate metaphor might be 'we make changes after fairly strong threats' (to our basic business). It is hard to perceive of a positive role for IT and IS amongst reactors. However, it is possible that the presence of existing IT or IS, coupled with a reluctance to change, may be seen as an excuse for inaction in these firms. In all probability the phrase 'the computer (system) won't allow us . . .' is used all too frequently by reactors.\* This would be a case of dysfunction between the IT/IS and business strategies and might be interpreted as a situation where the IT/IS strategy is allowed to determine (or is the excuse for) the business strategy.

## Empirical results

In order to test the validity of the above analysis and to establish the nature of competitive behaviour among users of large computer systems, a postal questionnaire survey\*\* was sent to members of the UK IBM Computer Users Association whose cooperation in this exercise is acknowledged. Members owning or using only personal computers were excluded from the sampling frame and out of approximately 350 questionnaires sent out, 185 were returned. Thirty-seven per cent of the responses were from businesses in manufacturing or process industries, with services (including transport), retail distribution and banking accounting for 13.7, 14.2 and 15.8 per cent respectively. The mean number of employees in the responding organizations was 6026. The sample therefore covered some of the very largest users of IS in the UK and for this reason the results should be considered highly representative.

The questionnaire was sent to the IS manager in each organization. While this follows earlier practice by other researchers, as suggested above, it could possibly bias the results. The alternative of a large-scale case-study approach was rejected on the basis of time and cost. Analysis showed that the majority of respondents were very close to the centre of decision-making and it is reasonable to suppose that they were familiar with the competitive strategies of their organizations. Not only were many of the businesses sampled large in terms of number of employees, the respondents held very senior positions. Twenty-eight per cent of respondents reported directly to the chief executive officer (CEO) and in less than 15 per cent of cases was there more than one executive between the respondent and the CEO.

The survey aimed to consider the relevance of alternative deliverable IS to the business strategies using the Ansoff and the Miles and Snow frameworks described above. Background questions were asked about the organization, perceived priorities for IS in the 1990s, and details of experience and training received by the respondents. Additional questions were explicitly designed to identify the nature of competitive behaviour. In order to identify firms' positions in McFarlan's (1984) strategic grid, respondents were asked to assess the impact of both their existing and their new or developing IS on their general business strategy using a four-point differential scale in each case. The results of this are tabulated in Table 2.

Out of the total of 185 respondents, 18 could be identified in the ‘support’ or ‘factory’ quadrants of the grid, 70 (38 per cent) in the ‘turnaround’ quadrant and 97 (52 per cent) in the ‘strategic’ quadrant in which IT has greatest strategic impact. This result may seem to identify a surprisingly large number of organizations in the ‘strategic’ quadrant. Whether the result is a true reflection of respondents’ beliefs or whether it is more a reflection of a desire on their part to identify their organization in the ‘strategic’ quadrant is an open issue. The fact that indirect rather than direct questions were used to investigate this aspect of strategy would suggest that this result is more likely to be a reflection of the true position than one which reflects respondents' knowledge of the literature or where they would like to be located.

## The Ansoff model

Two separate approaches to empirical analysis of the Ansoff model were used. First, respondents were asked to identify which one category of business activity best fitted their situation (for example, concentrating on existing products in existing markets or new products in new markets). The results from this question are shown in Table 3. The most common business strategy was competing through new products in existing markets (product development). There is a greater managerial effort required to move into new markets which provide fresh uncertainties compared with those in existing markets which are already familiar and understood. By contrast, only 18 firms (less than 10 per cent of the sample) said that they pursued a business strategy of selling new products in new markets (diversification).

A second and less direct approach was to ask respondents to rank (on a scale of 1 = highest and 4 = lowest) how they thought their chief executive would view investment in four alternative systems development proposals, each with the same payback period. These ranks were converted into scores for purposes of analysis as in Table 4, which combines these data with those from the earlier analysis. Amongst all firms, the most popular IS was one which increased market share. This confirms that market penetration is seen as a key business strategy. One explanation for IS managers responding in this way, despite the fact that higher market shares are not necessarily associated with higher profits, might be the 'visibility' of market share — there is a simplicity and immediacy about information on market share that can rapidly be interpreted as performance. By contrast, systems that enabled costs to be monitored or share to be maintained obtained low rankings.

In those firms seeing themselves as competing through market penetration, the most preferred IS was one that would reduce costs. Thus the expectations expressed above (i.e. that IT/IS could contribute significantly to a market penetration strategy by helping to reduce costs) have been confirmed by those IS managers who participated.

The pattern of responses amongst those firms pursuing market development or product development strategies was similar to that for all firms, partly because of the dominance of firms pursuing these strategies. The results for the diversifiers have to be qualified by the fact that they represented less than one-tenth of the sample. Nevertheless, the results reveal very strong preferences among diversifiers for IS that can increase market share and a stronger preference than average for maintaining market share. Reducing or monitoring costs are seen as less important than the average. Again, it appears that market share is seen as an indicator of success even by those thrusting firms which are exploring new markets.

Analysis was undertaken to test for a relationship between the firms' position in the strategic grid and the business strategy employed. The discussion above suggests only limited potential strategic applications of IT/IS for product and market developers and even less for diversifiers (where it was noted that existing IT/IS systems and structures may impede the diversification process). ## The Miles and Snow model

It will be recalled that this model identifies four types of competitive behaviour and for each type a metaphor briefly describing that behaviour was proposed. Firms' responses to the metaphors were tested by obtaining a measure of strength of agreement on a scale 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Using this method it is not possible uniquely to identify a single strategic thrust for each respondent; a good number of respondents agreed with each of the four metaphors. Table 6 shows the basic data for each of the metaphors together with the overall scores obtained by multiplying the percentages in each group by the scale value. Briefly, this shows the following results: defenders, 395; prospectors, 355; followers, 309; reactors, 269.

The ‘defender’ metaphor received the highest intensity of reaction with nearly three-quarters of the firms stating ‘agreement’ (denoted by a response of 4 or 5). The next most preferred strategy was that of a prospector with just over half of the sample registering agreement. Only 37 per cent agreed with the follower metaphor and less than 30 per cent agreed with the reactor metaphor.

The results from the Ansoff model showed that IS managers believe that their organizations are mainly interested in maintaining market share and would wish to concentrate efforts on market penetration strategies. This finding is reinforced by the evidence from the Miles and Snow analysis which shows that, overall, the defender metaphor is most preferred.

It is interesting to consider these data in the light of the information about position in the strategic grid. A high proportion of the respondents were classified in the 'strategic' quadrant which is normally associated with proactive use of IT/IS to gain competitive advantage. Yet the most preferred metaphor was that of defender. The prospector metaphor came only second. At least some consistency can be observed in that the reactor metaphor was placed last! Table 7 shows the data relating to the defender metaphor. Remarkably, the majority of ‘support’ firms were not in agreement with the defender metaphor but over three-quarters of both ‘turnaround’ and ‘strategic’ firms are in agreement with this metaphor.

Association between position in the strategic grid and belief in the defender metaphor was tested using the chi-squared test. The experimental value of chi-squared obtained was 20.04 at 12 degrees of freedom which has a probability of 0.066. There is therefore limited statistical evidence at the 10 per cent level to reject the null hypothesis that there is no relationship between position in the strategic grid and belief in the defender metaphor. The most reasonable inference from these data is that firms frequently use IT/IS as a strategic defence to maintain the firm's position in the industry. A case-by-case analysis would be necessary to verify this.

Analysis of the relationship between strategic position and response to the prospector metaphor is given in Table 8. Half of the ‘support’ firms ‘disagreed’ with the prospector metaphor (denoted by ratings of 1 or 2) and increasing proportions of firms in each part of the grid expressed agreement (28, 33, 53 and 66 per cent in ‘support’, ‘factory’, ‘turnaround’ and ‘strategic’ groups respectively).

Association between position in the strategic grid and the strength of agreement with the prospector metaphor was tested using the chi-squared test. The experimental value obtained was 25.35 with a probability of 0.01. The null hypothesis of no association between position in the strategic grid and belief in the prospector metaphor can be rejected at the 1 per cent level. The result is therefore highly significant and the conclusion is drawn that firms in the strategic quadrant are more likely than others to be prospectors. In other words, the IT/IS strategies are perceived to be part of the general business strategy of product or market innovation through being a ‘first mover’ in the market.

## Conclusions and further research

Questions posed to identify how firms fitted in the context of the Ansoff model revealed that the most common business strategy amongst the UK firms selected was product development. The majority of these respondents' IS managers saw themselves under pressure to increase market share. For those firms seeing themselves as competing through market penetration, the most preferred IS was one that would reduce costs. This confirms expectations that the most important contribution of IT/IS to a market penetration strategy would be by reducing costs. The results reveal very strong preferences among diversifiers for IS that can increase market share and a stronger preference than average for those that maintain market share.

In terms of the Miles and Snow model, the questions posed were unable to identify any unique competitive stance. The defender metaphor received the highest intensity of reaction, with nearly three-quarters of the firms stating agreement which raises questions about the relationship of these responses to firms' position in the strategic grid since many of them could be identified in the 'strategic' quadrant. The expectation was that there are greater opportunities for IT/IS strategic applications for defenders than for prospectors so at first sight the evidence would seem to contradict this. If the responses are accepted as accurate reflections of the business strategies actually employed, then it would seem that firms' primary aim is defensive and that their secondary aim is aggressive. It would seem a natural reaction first to protect what is there and then to set out to build upon it. It is interesting to speculate whether this is a typically British stance, or whether similar results would pertain elsewhere.

A number of additional issues suggest the need for further analysis. First is the question of self-identification of business strategies by IT personnel. This study, like others before it, may be reporting evidence that is biased by the respondents' lack of background in wider business issues. Broadening the scope of the respondents, perhaps on a case-by-case basis, to evaluate the nature of the business strategies pursued would clearly be of benefit. A second extension of the study should also be in terms of evaluating other models of business strategy and the role of the identified IT strategies within them. Analysis of the Miles and Snow model has not permitted unique identification of strategies modes and a more refined instrument, possibly along the lines reported by Parnell and Wright (1993), might be appropriate.

Linking or aligning business and IT/IS strategies remains a major challenge for many businesses. The research that this paper reports shows that UK IS managers appear to have a clear appreciation of the business strategies pursued by their organizations and that the purpose of the IS used is largely congruent with the revealed business strategies. The actual process by which this has come about, however, was not an issue considered and would require further research.
