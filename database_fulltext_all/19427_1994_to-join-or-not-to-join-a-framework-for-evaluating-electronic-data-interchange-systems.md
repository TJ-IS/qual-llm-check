# To join or not to join: a framework for evaluating electronic data interchange systems

Sten Nygaard-Andersen\* and Niels Bjørn-Andersen
Copenhagen Business School, Department of Informatics and Management Accounting, Howitzvej 60, 2000 Frederiksberg, Denmark

The article proposes a normative contingency-based framework for assessing the implications of an electronic data interchange (EDI) system focusing on technical usability, business contribution and implementation costs. The framework is aimed at users contemplating joining an already existing EDI system. With this perspective it should also be of significant interest to present and future potential EDI sponsors.

Keywords: electronic data interchange (EDI) systems, implementation, implication, framework benefit, diffusion

The use of electronic data interchange (EDI) is growing rapidly, and is expected to be the dominant form of business communication between companies in several markets during this decade. [See e.g. Communications World (1991) no. 5, (1992) nos 2 and 49; Computerworld (1992) nos 15 and 43; Anderson (1990); Pedersen (1990); Price Waterhouse/IKO (1992); Bjørn-Andersen and Nygaard-Andersen (1993)].

However, the success of an EDI system depends on, more than anything else, whether users can be enticed to join the network and in this way contribute to forming the critical mass making EDI an advantage for everybody. So far the diffusion of EDI in general has not progressed according to expectations; there are many examples of EDI applications failing to achieve a satisfactory adoption level and level of usage if adopted.

The majority of the existing literature regarding EDI systems applies a strategic perspective. That addresses primarily the problems faced by the EDI sponsor (owner). This literature begins with the early American examples of inter-organizational systems (IOS), e.g. the reservation systems SABRE and APOLLO (Copeland and McKenney, 1988; also Hopper, 1990), McKesson's order entry system ECONOMOST (Clemons and Row, 1988), and American Hospital Supply's ASAP-system (Cash et al., 1988). It also contains normative models for analysis, planning, design and implementation of EDI systems in order to achieve strategic advantages e.g. Konsynski and Warbelow (1989), Porter and Millar (1985), Johnston and Vitale (1988), Emmelhainz (1990), and Cash et al. (1988).

In this literature, the business partners of the EDI-sponsoring organizations typically occupy a passive role, and they are given only cursory attention in the strategic literature. However, the strategic advantages of EDI are only achieved if the users (business partners) 'buy in', as Johnston and Vitale (1988) note:

The problem remains that, although EDI presents many opportunities to achieve competitive advantages, many organizations install it under pressure from their large customers, thereby questioning the very existence of a strategic advantage.

Concern for the advantages of the (smaller) business partners of the EDI sponsoring organizations was also explicitly voiced during the EU EDI conference in the spring of 1993 [Trade EDI Systems Program Conference, Brussels, 20–21 April], where the question was raised if there was any business potential at all for small and medium-sized companies in joining an EDI network unless one was forced to do it.

The purpose of this article is not to answer this question directly. Instead we will provide a framework enabling companies to identify the opportunities and threats involved in joining or not joining a particular EDI system given their own specific situation.

This perspective is in contrast to the strategic models and frameworks of analysis which are predominantly concerned with the problems of the EDI sponsor regarding design, implementation and potential uptake by business partners. Such a sponsor perspective may not be adequate to the situation of the EDI-using organization. Our framework adopts a business partner perspective, attempting to provide guidance to companies confronting an adoption and internal implementation decision, while they have had little or no influence on the technical and business related decisions determining the shape of the system.

The framework has been developed primarily based on in-depth case studies of two Danish EDI systems. For each of these, we studied one of the sponsors and two of the users/business partners. We used written material, company reports and extensive interviews with key actors. For one of the EDI systems we also used an extensive survey of all business partners (Larsen and Stærmose, 1992).

The objective of the case studies was to identify the relationship between background characteristics of adopting business partners and the subsequent integration of the EDI system. The goal of this analysis was to define a contingency framework for the adoption behaviour of business partners.

The graphical presentation of the framework may suggest a sequential evaluation process. This is not the case. Rather, scenarios are developed for each element in the model to form a (relatively) complete description of the reaction of the prime competitors, the reaction of the main business partners, and the reaction of the evaluating organization itself given the actions from competitors and business partners.

## Environment of the EDI arena

In many instances, the EDI system will cut across traditional divisions into markets and industries, and will follow the exchange of goods and services. Accordingly we have found it useful to name the collective of organizations relevant to a particular EDI system, the EDI arena. Companies developing, using, or contemplating joining a particular EDI system are all part of this EDI arena.

There are certain conditions in an EDI arena which influence the way in which EDI is adopted (Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Suomi, 1992). It is argued that in order to understand the increased use of EDI one has to look at economic, organizational as well as technological developments. Our studies confirm this argument and suggests that five different elements in the environment are taken into consideration in order to understand the diffusion of a particular EDI system. The environmental elements, as shown in Figure 1, are ‘inter-organizational dependence’, ‘increased competition’, ‘value added network services’, ‘communications needs’, and the ‘technology’ itself. The general state of these elements may be interpreted as the EDI maturity of an arena.

## Increased competition

EDI systems are often introduced in order to reduce costs in highly competitive areas. The more apparent the cost-benefit, the higher the diffusion rate. In other words, increased competition functions as a catalyst for the diffusion of EDI. This type of crisis has a legitimizing effect in the sense that EDI becomes a necessary innovation in order to survive.

## Inter-organizational dependency

The introduction of EDI typically increases the level of interorganizational cooperation between the participants. The closer linking of business partners has often been mentioned as one of the competitive advantages for the EDI sponsor. It may be tempting to conclude that this linking is to the disadvantage of smaller participants who find only one or a few of their business partners using the EDI system. However, we have found that EDI is primarily established between partners who already have a very high level of cooperation. The importance of the already established business links as a precondition to do EDI may play an explanatory part in the limited success of ‘open-EDI systems’. In Scandinavia the most successful open EDI systems are sponsored by the government (e.g. tax and customs systems) or intermediaries (e.g. transportation industry) that are found attractive primarily by larger organizations.

In most cases the EDI sponsor is the gate-keeper to the system determining who can join. In contradiction to most open EDI systems, the tendency in closed systems (proprietary systems) is that the sponsor makes a greater and more powerful marketing effort towards business partners. We believe the number and the strength of business ties in a given EDI arena has substantial implications for adoption and use.

## Communication needs

Another fundamental prerequisite for the effective use of an EDI system is the need to exchange relatively large volumes of structured data between already existing business partners. Volume, sensitivity, urgency and integrity of data are all factors which influence the uptake of EDI. These kinds of information requirements may be found in areas where external communication is fundamental to business processes, i.e. businesses which are dependent on external logistics operations.

## Technology

Under the heading of technology we include information systems, standards and knowledge about the technology. EDI technology is not particularly complex and in general it is considered to be mature, although the linking of different information technology (IT) systems is complex and often requires substantial effort. However, it is often the case that the companies in a particular EDI arena will be at very different IT maturity stages. Accordingly one has to consider the technical capability of the different potential EDI partners for coping with the challenges of linking their own internal information systems to an EDI solution.

A fundamental requirement for any EDI system is the availability of standards and a communication procedure for identification of goods and services. On the general level it is often the EDIFACT standard guiding the development of EDI systems. More specifically, however, our studies show that it is the coding of goods and services which presents the largest obstacle to the diffusion of EDI, i.e. a uniform identification of what is to be communicated. We find support for this argument in examples from highly standardized industries like the transportation industry, the consumer goods industry and the public sector. These highly standardized industries all have business procedures and element identification. Accordingly they are able to set up highly standardized EDI networks.

## Value added network service provider

Only few IT user organizations have sufficient detailed knowledge regarding standards to be able to drive their own EDI development. Early diffusion of EDI in a given arena is therefore dependent on whether there are other organizations available to provide technical competence.

Value added network service (VANS) companies are established to perform part of the linking role by receiving, storing and transmitting EDI documents. On top of this they offer services like e-mail, linking to other networks, document conversion, money-transfer, database facilities, security systems, etc.

The Danish VANS companies have played a major role in the diffusion of EDI in Denmark by developing, maintaining and implementing EDI systems across the board in a number of potential EDI arenas. They have done this via large scale marketing by offering a set of almost pre-packaged services to customers at both ends of an EDI network, and by minimizing the technical obstacles involved in the exchange of EDI documents.

## EDI maturity

The state of these five factors determine the EDI maturity of a particular arena and the potential of obtaining a critical mass of users and use. An analysis will enable an organization to estimate whether it has fallen behind its competitors and business partners or whether there is time for a proactive strategic move. The suggested analysis of the EDI arena may be summarized by determining whether the business environment is characterized by:

\- increased competition;

• stronger inter-organizational business relations;

\- availability of business specific EDI applications and services;

\- increased need for communication (e.g. just-in time (JIT) manufacturing);

\- high levels of IT maturity among business partners and competitors.

The answers to these questions will enable a scenario analysis of the competitive position of the organization regarding EDI.

## EDI-user organizations

In our analysis of EDI cases, it became clear that the most decisive internal organizational factors influencing the decision whether or not to adopt EDI, were the business objectives ('rationale' for doing EDI) and the general IT competence ('IT maturity') in the organization.

## Rationale for implementing EDI

There can be multiple reasons for organizations to implement EDI. In most cases arguments in favour will refer to the possible achievement of business objectives. At the operational level, EDI will often be used to cut costs and speed up internal information flow with the aim of attaining short term positive effects on the bottom line. We shall refer to this as a bottom line rationale. At the strategic level, EDI could be used to achieve strategic objectives like reduced lead time, higher flexibility, and the linking of business partners. We shall refer to this as a competitive advantage rationale.

## IT maturity\*

IT maturity is the term used to describe how well an organization has adopted and integrated IT solutions to achieve business objectives. We use it here to distinguish the two extremes defined as either high or low IT maturity.

Low IT maturity characterizes the organization with limited IT experience. Typical systems will be traditional systems like accounting, invoicing and word-processing. Applications are not integrated in a network, and data are not shared across departments and functions. Systems architecture is fragmented and consists of isolated information islands. With this level of IT maturity it is not likely that the organization can introduce EDI without external assistance, and any integration of EDI with existing systems is likely to be difficult. In such organizations we found several examples of data being manually re-entered into the EDI system from existing IT applications and vice versa.

High IT maturity characterizes the organization which to a large extent supports its primary business functions by the use of IT solutions. Data is shared among functions, end-user programming is widespread, and the systems are perceived as a totally integrated part of the daily routines and business processes. It is in this kind of organization one would expect to find executive information systems, decision support systems, groupware, e-mail, external communication systems, etc.

## Classification of organizations

By combining the two dimensions of ‘IT maturity’ and ‘rationale for EDI’ we obtain the matrix shown in Figure 2 where four archetypes of EDI users are defined, the ‘rationalist’, the ‘functionalist’, the ‘opportunist’, and the ‘strategist’ with each having their own distinctive behaviour.

The rationalist is characterized by having a low level of IT maturity and a focus on utilizing EDI for achieving cost reductions. Several examples of this type were found among the user organizations in our study. It was interesting to see that there were even examples of companies re-entering the data from the EDI system in order to avoid large scale modifications to existing systems.

The functionalist has a high level of IT maturity, but its internal IT expertise is used only from an efficiency perspective, typically to reduce labour costs in handling paper documents. Companies who are uncertain about the long range potential of EDI, and who wish to gain some experience, will typically be characterized as ‘functionalists’.

The opportunist has the same low level of IT maturity as the rationalist, but contrary to the latter, the opportunist is keen to achieve competitive advantages through the use of EDI. The opportunist does not have the IT competence which is a requirement to really exploit EDI, and is clearly following a high risk strategy without having the necessary prerequisites. However, the opportunist is convinced that the rewards in the form of long range EDI-based links to business partners will be more important than a positive cost-benefit in the short run. The opportunist is trying to take advantage of the new business opportunities and worries about the costs of catching up technologically later.

The strategist has long range strategic visions for the exploitation of EDI not just for short term cost savings but also for achieving competitive advantages. EDI sponsoring organizations obviously belong to this category, but we also identified a number of their business partners in this category. It is a common feature of strategists that the IT strategy is part of the business strategy. It is to be expected that the strategists will act in accordance with one of the four objectives described by Keen (1988) in his analysis of the possibilities of utilizing telecommunications for competitive advantage:

\- operational necessity in order to sustain the level of service;

\- defensive necessity to keep present market shares;

In assessing the value of the user classification it should be remembered that the four types represent extremes. \- relative position and strategy of business partners to EDI;

\- relative position and strategy of competitors to EDI;

\- probability of a change in position and strategy by competitors and business partners to EDI;

\- the need for a change in their own strategy regarding EDI.

## Assessment of the EDI system

Having dealt with the environment and the classification of user organizations, we now discuss the third part of the contingency based framework shown in Figure 1, the assessment of the EDI system itself. As can be seen here, the centre of the framework focuses on technical usability, business contribution, implementation costs, and trade offs. Each of these will be dealt with below.

## Technical usability

In spite of the obvious relationship between technical usability and business contribution, the former is often ignored when evaluating EDI systems. This is especially prevalent in opportunist and rationalist companies, who have little IT maturity. Compared with internal systems, user organizations have little or no possibility of adjusting or modifying the EDI system. All modifications have to be dealt with locally, and it is far from certain that this is easily done when the IT maturity level is low.

We shall deal with the technical usability under three headings: reliability, organizational impact and user friendliness.

Reliability. The purpose of an EDI system is typically to support routine tasks related to day-to-day operations. These routines are often of high importance for external relations, but they are frequently not critical for the survival of the company in the short run. They fulfil a coordination role for operations between the company and its business partners (e.g., as seen in external logistics operations). The general reliance on EDI as a means of coordination therefore depends on the reliability of the system, and on whether it is used. An unreliable system will not be used as a medium for critical information. This does not mean that the system has to be 100 per cent perfect, but it is vital that the company can cope with the level of unreliability of the EDI system.

Analysis of the EDI systems in our cases shows that opportunists and rationalists with a low level of IT maturity are more vulnerable to unreliable systems than strategists and functionalist. We found for instance several opportunist and rationalist companies who had dropped using the system ‘because it never worked’ or was ‘inappropriate’. At the same time, strategist and functionalist users found the same systems ‘very effective’. Strategist and functionalist were also much more relaxed regarding systems break-downs and other irregularities than were the opportunists and rationalists. The reason is that the IT mature organizations are used to handling advanced IT applications. They understand what goes wrong and how to fix it. They have the necessary insight into functionality and know the recovery procedures.

Organizational impact. A reduction in staff is typically one of the benefits associated with an introduction of an EDI system. The mention of staff reduction or change of work routines in many organizations will lead to unpleasant and quite often unanticipated organizational consequences. The severity of the impact is highly dependent on the implementation and integration strategy and will inevitably have consequences for the efficiency of the system once implemented.

A superficial integration of EDI with existing work procedures is likely to result in inappropriate work routines and extra work-load, e.g. if previous work procedures are used in parallel with the EDI system, posing a need for two different ways of working. The lack of integration of the EDI system with existing IT systems is again typically characteristic of the rationalists and opportunists, who do not have the skill or capacity to reengineer their organization. It is also among opportunist and rationalist users that we have found most reports on negative organizational impact.

Strategists and functionalists on the other hand frequently handle the introduction of EDI from a vantage point. We found several examples where the EDI system eases the work-load, provides more autonomy, and reduces the monotony in the job. The high level of IT maturity of strategists and functionalists in this way leads to a better implementation procedure and to a better understanding and usefulness of the system seen from the perspective of the end-users.

User friendliness. Lack of user friendliness is notorious for having a negative effect on the use of an information system. And the effects of this are the more significant when the end-user has little experience as we found in rationalist and opportunist organizations. Experienced end-users have more realistic expectations, and they know how to compensate for most trivial errors or flaws in the system. Furthermore, one could hypothesize that end-users who are used to working with different systems have a more accurate mental model of the EDI system and its functionality than somebody who is relatively new to the situation.

Technical usability and IT maturity. We can conclude this section about ‘technical usability’ by summarizing that the usefulness of an EDI system is strongly dependent on the IT maturity of the user organization. Accordingly, any assessment of the reliability of an EDI system should take IT maturity as a starting point rather than any generic assessment of quality. In general our analysis shows that implementation will be much easier and cost-benefits will be much more substantial in strategic and functionalist organizations with a high level of IT maturity.

This is illustrated in Figure 3, where the four user types are grouped in pairs. The vertical bars illustrate the 'IT maturity', the 'technical usability', and the 'business potential'. If we take an EDI system with a given level of 'technical usability' (a fixed point on the middle bar), the two long arrows illustrate the relative business potential achieved for the two groups of user organizations.

But the IT maturity of an organization is not fixed once and for all, and it can be accelerated. Proper utilization of consultancy, training and staffing will undoubtedly contribute to shifting the full arrow further down as indicated with the curved arrow. It is also likely that the confrontation with the request regarding joining an EDI system will force top management in rationalist or opportunist organizations to radically upgrade their level of IT maturity. In this way, the EDI system can have a positive effect on the business strategy. It is important to take these changes into account when one is assessing the potential of competitors and business partners.

The crucial questions regarding technical usability of an EDI system concern the user organization's ability, given its present IT maturity level, to implement, maintain and utilize the EDI system. Pertinent questions to ask in this respect would be whether:

\- the system is useful at its present technical level;

\- it is possible to enhance the usefulness of the system through internal training;

\- the EDI sponsor is prepared to improve the system;

\- the costs of improving the system might be acceptable;

\- the competitors and other business partners are likely to be able to utilize the system at its present technical level.

## Business support

The primary benefit associated with the profitability of an EDI system is whether it supports essential business or administrative processes which are worthwhile automating.

This is the second major area which is necessary to assess in order to decide whether or not to join an EDI scheme. We shall discuss business support in the three categories of functional coverage, product line coverage, and business partner coverage.

Functional coverage. A business transaction can be characterized by a sequence of messages between business partners. A typical example is a buying transaction initiated by a request for information about products available. The following sequence of messages could consist of offer, order, order confirmation, invoice and payment. We define functional coverage as the number of different messages it is possible to handle in the system.

A partial coverage means that communication with business partners will consist of a mixture of manual and automated process. Full coverage means that all transactions are handled by the system. Full coverage makes it much more attractive to integrate the EDI system with existing systems.

Product line coverage. Product line coverage is defined as the extent to which the system supports buying, selling or other exchanges of goods, services and information between business partners. For the assessment process it is especially important how large a share of the full product range is supported by EDI. This again influences the possibility of eliminating manual functions and introduces true EDI; i.e. application to application exchange of data. Many companies in our sample were interested in reducing the number of products and suppliers in an effort to avoid keeping stock of unprofitable products. EDI has turned out to become an important factor in strengthening the business ties between the buyers and vendors.

Business partner coverage. Product coverage presupposes the existence of relevant business partners. To the rationalist and the functionalist it will be enough to have just one business partner, if this relationship represents enough transaction volume. However, to the opportunist and the strategist the business partner coverage is typically a question of creating and re-enforcing ties to already existing critical business partners, with whom it is possible to create strategic alliances. Furthermore, it is likely that the strategist will have a wider strategic objective associated with a widespread diffusion of the EDI system.

Business support and coverage. The coverage of functions, of goods/services, and of users on a given EDI system in itself is a good measure of success of an EDI system, provided that a reasonable level of transactions are processed via the system. Activity and volume signal effectiveness and will attract new companies. Accordingly, EDI systems with a high transaction volume will also have a high business potential even though at a particular moment in time the different types of coverage might not be satisfactory. However, it is only opportunists and strategists that will exhibit such a level of long range planning that they are prepared to sacrifice the demand for short term ‘return on investment’ in favour of longer term gains. Accordingly, the business potential associated with a particular EDI system will look differently depending on the rationale for EDI.

Companies who apply a bottom line rationale will demand that at the time of investment in the EDI system, it is possible to come up with a positive cost-benefit calculation. In contrast, the opportunist and the strategist will be prepared to include uncertain future business potential in the calculation. Accordingly, one would expect to find more opportunists and strategists using the EDI systems with partial coverage of functions, products, and business partners.

In all three areas the crucial question is the attainment of a critical mass. Even though it is possible to make an overall assessment of an EDI system, this will not necessarily apply to the individual organization. One will have to look at specific functions and products in relation to competitors and business partners in order to assess the specific cost-benefit.

The problems involved in assessing the business support potential may be addressed through the following questions:

\- is it possible to improve the business support potential by actively marketing the EDI system to other existing partners?

\- are there plans from the sponsor to improve the coverage of the system potentially making use more extensive?

\- how may we expect the competitors and business partners to react to the business support potential of the system?

## Implementation costs

The importance of careful organizational implementation is well known from research and literature on internal information systems (IS) (Bjørn-Andersen, 1986). As could be expected the same rules apply to the implementation of EDI systems, not least when these are integrated with internal IS and work processes. From the study of EDI in 140 Canadian organizations, Bergeron et al. (1992) conclude that

The more successful firms . . . have provided more organizational support for EDI in terms of top management involvement, training for all individuals concerned, structuring of the EDI function, and insuring the collaboration of all departments and commercial partners involved.

However, intensified implementation activities result in increased costs which necessarily must be included in the cost-benefit equation. In general we find that companies with a high level of IT maturity will need lower implementation costs as will be explained below. We propose that the implementation costs for assessment purposes can be divided into three broad categories, costs of integration, training and procurement.

Costs of integration. Ideally, EDI systems should be integrated with the internal information systems in a way totally seamless to the end-user (application to application EDI) to obtain optimal efficiency. However, on a technical level this may not be so easy to achieve if existing systems are not easily modifiable. We expect that the strategist and functionalist will be able to achieve a high level of integration due to their high IT maturity, since employees are familiar with systems solutions as a part of their daily work.

Furthermore, management in these organizations are familiar with the implications following organizational development/change due to systems introductions.

But for the opportunist and the rationalist, the technical integration will often require substantial investments in new interfaces, modifications in database structure, new security and update procedures, changes in work procedures, etc. if this is to be achieved. If the true costs of these investments are measured, the opportunists and rationalists will often find a negative cost-benefit of an EDI investment in the short run.

Costs of training. Rationalist and opportunist companies will typically have end-users with a low level of experience with on-line transactions. This will add extra costs for a general training in IT topics over and above the training in the procedures necessary for handling the new EDI system. This training should not be underestimated, and must be costed accordingly.

Costs of procurement. The costs of procurement are directly related to the level of integration of the new EDI system with internal systems. The typically low cost solution for small- to medium-sized companies within the opportunist and rationalist categories is to acquire a personal computer without any electronic integration with internal systems. This low cost/low benefit solution can be fine in the short run, but might be an impediment to further long term development, since it might be difficult to migrate from that. A second risk is that a simple solution creates a false security in the conviction of having gained EDI experience without considering the fact that it is on a rudimentary level. This might be fatal at a later stage, where a market requirement could necessitate a full integration, for example in order to directly impact the production control system of a small supplier.

Consolidation of implementation costs. Rationalist and functionalist companies applying an efficiency (bottom line) perspective will typically base their assessment on financial grounds. But they will do it from their own angle.

The rationalist will do the sums based solely on the directly measurable investment need and benefits achieved in the form of direct savings or increased income. The cost-benefit assessment will be in the ‘rational tradition’, taking into account the present level of IT maturity and internal IT competence. Rationalists will implement and utilize EDI to the extent they expect to achieve a quantifiable positive cost-benefit.

Functionalists will use the same procedure, but will have a vantage point from their high IT maturity level. However, by focusing only on the bottom line, the functionalist will not take advantage of the full potential. The main reason for this is the level in the organization where the decision is made. This is consistent with the widely held belief that the largest obstacle to a strategic use of IS is in the application of formal cost-benefit analyses (Bjørn-Andersen and Turner, forthcoming). However, competitors applying this ‘sub-optimal’ implementation strategy should not be underestimated. Due to the high level of IT maturity in a relatively short span of time, functionalist companies will be able to implement the necessary changes in order to utilize EDI aggressively in order to achieve competitive advantage.

The strategists assessing EDI from a competitive advantage perspective will confront fairly extensive implementation costs. But strategists will come from the vantage point of having a high level of IT maturity to make more predictable judgements. Extended cost-benefit assessments taking into account the less quantifiable aspects are likely to be applied. It is in this group that we shall find many EDI sponsors and early adopters of EDI systems.

The opportunist is likely to reject the explicit cost-benefit assessments, which are likely to show that a positive cost-benefit cannot be achieved from an investment at the present point in time. But the opportunist holds the opinion that linking up to the EDI system is a competitive necessity in order to maintain or further develop business relationships. The balancing of certain costs against uncertain potential future gains means that fundamentally the decision is not rational, and the opportunist is in a vulnerable situation, which is often a result of a pressure from business partners. But if the result is successful, the opportunist has often gained a differentiation advantage.

The importance of implementation costs may be summarized in the following questions:

\- which level of integration is optimal given the present level of IT maturity?

\- is it worthwhile to improve the level of IT maturity?

\- what are the long range costs and risks associated with a minimum solution for implementing EDI?

\- what are the costs of postponing further integration?

\- what might be the reaction of competitors and business partners to the introduction and use of EDI?

\- to what extent is it necessary to revise the earlier chosen implementation strategy in the light of the answers to the above questions?

## Inter-organizational 'trade offs'

Earlier sections suggested that some organizations were in a better position to assess and take advantage of EDI-based opportunities than others. Furthermore, it was assumed that the presence and distribution of this ability among organizations in the EDI arena was likely to influence the diffusion and frequency of use of an EDI system. However, one of the most influential parameters concerning the overall use of an EDI system is inter-organizational inducements, i.e. an organization trying to persuade others to join or to refrain from joining an EDI system by offering tangible or intangible trade offs. All of the other issues discussed earlier in this article may be neutralized by the attempt of organizations to influence the EDI uptake decision of their trading partners. Despite the important role of the inter-organizational influence it often appears to be the least visible parameter at work when it comes to predicting or explaining the diffusion of an EDI system.

The term ‘negotiation power’ suggests the use of Porter’s (1980, 1985) strategic frameworks. However, inter-organizational influence is not just a question of bargaining power, but is often based on other and more refined means for influencing the behaviour of others.

The most powerful means for negotiation are found where one organization's survival is directly dependent on the business relationship of just one trading partner, which in turn use its commercial position to influence the EDI uptake decision of the other. However, observations from the case studies suggest that balanced commercial interdependency, traditions of trade, and even personal relations are used also as means for negotiation in the attempt to convince trading partners of a specific choice. It seems that even recommendations and declarations of intentions from trustworthy trading partners alone, are enough to influence the decision of an uncertain decision maker.

## Coercion

The use of coercion is typically exercised by large, commercially important organizations proclaiming that after a certain date, the use of EDI is a precondition for a continued business relationship. This approach has been applied extensively, e.g. in the automotive industries and consumer goods industries, where large corporations have taken advantage of their commercial position with suppliers. However, coercion may also be seen in situations where the product is a scarce resource. Thus, where the supplier is in a position to require that the distribution channel uses EDI, coercion is often used to secure a fast diffusion of EDI. However, a forced diffusion will often lead to the implementation of a minimum solution among the coerced users, as the only incentive for joining is that of not being ‘punished’. The use of coercion is most likely to be exercised by ‘strategists’ and ‘functionalists’ not by ‘rationalists’ or ‘opportunists’.

## Compensation

The use of compensation as a means for negotiation is based on the notion of shared benefits. This approach is most likely to be used where one organization is subsidizing other organizations' use of EDI, i.e. the subsidizing organization perceives the opportunities of having EDI partners to be of such a magnitude that it is considered worthwhile to share some of the benefits in order to secure diffusion and use of the system. Thus, it may be expected that some organizations are willing and able to share expected benefits with trading partners even before these are actually accrued. In many cases such behaviour is regarded as a strategic investment in the future and will only appear among 'strategists'. The compensation may be realized by offering discounts, refund of the costs of initiation, reductions of network costs etc. The use of compensation has a direct influence on the cost/benefit calculation of the receiving organizations. Securing EDI diffusion and use through the use of compensation is most likely to be applied against organizations lacking the ability to take advantage of the EDI system and whose decision process rely directly on the use of quantitative cost/benefit estimates, i.e. especially the 'rationalist'.

## Partnership

The term ‘partnership’ refers to the offer from one organization to share the risks and opportunities of joining a specific EDI system. To suggest ‘partnership’ as a means for negotiation may not seem to offer much power of persuasion towards an otherwise reluctant trading partner. However, observations from the cases show that this means of negotiation has substantial impact on the diffusion of an EDI system, not least when the offer comes from a reliable business partner with a good reputation. One of the reasons may be found in the fact that two or more trading partners may reduce or eliminate the risk of not having critical mass by making a coordinated decision. Thus, it may be expected that groups of organizations may coordinate their decision, whether this decision is to join or refrain from joining the system in question.

## Consequences of inter-organizational influence

The attempt to influence the decision of other organizations' decisions is often termed as a typical sponsor activity. However, as an EDI system is adopted, this phenomenon seems to gain widespread use among the common users as well. Observations from the case studies suggests that most organizations that find a system beneficial will actively take the role of a systems promoter in that they will attempt to secure their investment by supporting the diffusion and thereby the general usability of the system. Furthermore, it appears that the attempt to influence trading partners does not depend on the existing commercial power relations or position in the value chain.

The use of inter-organizational ‘trade offs’ makes it extremely difficult to make estimates concerning who will join the system and to what extent they will use it. Predicting the possible consequences of inter-organizational negotiations on the development in the EDI arena, requires deep insight into the hidden interdependencies of the system stakeholders.

Independent organizations rarely have a chance to influence the future development of an EDI system's functionality or rules of usage. However, there are examples where a group of influential organizations has succeeded in negotiating changes with the system's sponsor. Such changes are most likely to aim at enhanced usability to the influencing organizations, as illustrated in Figure 4. But enhanced usability to one organization may induce a reduction of competitive advantage to others. This situation may, for example, occur if a closed system is opened allowing formerly excluded competitors to use the system. The consideration of such future developments ought to be included in the evaluation of the EDI system.

This section discussed how inter-organizational influence may radically change the otherwise predictable pattern of behaviour among the stakeholders of the EDI arena. The consequences of external influence to the decision making of individual organizations may for example be addressed by the following questions:

\- what are the organizational and financial costs of giving in to external influence?

\- what are the organizational and financial costs of not giving in to such influence?

\- what are the risks and opportunity costs of trying to influence the decision making of trading partners?

## Evaluation

Earlier sections of this article described a number of issues affecting the possible diffusion, use and consequences of an EDI system. This section provides a suggestion for how to put the result of the analysis into practical use. Each of the former sections addresses a number of issues found to be of influence in achieving business advantages through the use of EDI. Many of these issues mutually influence each other and may create synergistic effects to the advantage as well as to the disadvantage to the analysing organization. One of the problems in the analysis of EDI systems is the complexity of the mutual influence of the earlier addressed elements. Traditional cost-benefit analysis often tries to reduce this complexity by decomposing the different issues into quantifiable measures. In the worst case, all elements that cannot be quantified into financial measures are excluded from the evaluation process. The downside of such decomposition of mutually influencing parameters is that those issues generating synergy effects are separated from each other and the context which gives them meaning, whereby these interrelated issues no longer appear to be obvious. This is not least the case where interrelationships involve matters of a qualitative nature.

To avoid discarding important information by applying rigid and overly formalized frameworks of analysis, it is suggested that scenarios are used as a basis for decision making. An analysis of the EDI arena concerning environmental issues, the potential and current users, and the system-related issues, as suggested in earlier sections, should provide the analyst with a number of qualified assumptions concerning the development in the EDI arena. These assumptions are the suggested input to the scenario building process and the subsequent decision making as illustrated in Figure 5.

According to Clemons (1991) decision makers tend to fall into two common traps in their evaluation of IT solutions. First, a negative net present value on an investment does not mean that this cannot be the best solution, i.e. if ‘not investing’ leads to an even lower net present value. Such a perspective may remind one of ‘zero-based budgeting’ in that Clemons implicitly recommends that the IT solution is compared with the costs of continuing the existing routines. Second, Clemons points out that the choice to continue as usual is no guarantee for the status quo. Other organizations may change the rules in the competitive arena and thereby disturb the basic assumptions of the analysing organization.

In order to avoid mistakes of this kind it is suggested that an organization carry out in-depth analysis of the earlier addressed issues from the perspective of ‘using EDI’ as well as ‘not using EDI’, e.g. the consequences of not using a specific EDI system if the competitors and/or critical trading partners choose to do so. These two scenarios may then serve as the basis for further scenario building under the use of a ‘what if’ approach to likely reactions of key players in the EDI arena. The most likely scenarios could be evaluated in a comparative analysis for example by using a ‘strength–weakness–opportunity–threat’ approach.

The EDI system itself is discussed under four main topics concerning technical usability, business support, cost of implementation and inter-organizational ‘trade offs’. The consequences of using EDI are radically different from those invoked by the use of intra-organizational IS. Consequently, the model for evaluation suggests that the results from analysis are used to create a number of likely scenarios, thereby creating alternatives presenting holistic descriptions of the outcome of different decisions. The recommendation of a scenario-based decision-making process is based on the assumption that this is the only approach which is flexible enough to include all the information required to uncover the complex and qualitative synergy effects which may be expected to influence the opportunities of using EDI.

Rational analytical models are often met with a certain amount of suspicion both from academics and practitioners. The criticism is often focused on the fact that models only to a limited extent provide a true picture of the situation they model. A second criticism is that decision makers do not use rational and analytical approaches to trouble-shooting and decision making.

However, the overall purpose of this model for analysis and evaluation is not to suggest a true picture of the EDI uptake situation. Case studies and contact with practitioners have revealed a concrete and latent requirement for neutrally developed critical descriptions, analysis, and models concerned with the opportunities and consequences of EDI. This need has especially been expressed by small to medium-sized organizations with a limited level IT maturity, who were faced with the decision of whether or not to join a specific EDI system. Thus, the primary purpose of this model for evaluation is to provide a framework for analysis, taking the perspective of the many common EDI users (non-sponsors). The model attempts to fulfil these needs by addressing issues which have already been shown to influence the diffusion, use, and the opportunities a common user may expect from an already established EDI system. Thus, it is our hope that this model for evaluation of EDI systems may be regarded as a first step in fulfilling the needs of practitioners.

Research on EDI in Europe is still limited, especially from the perspective of the common user organization. This research suggests that there is still much insight to be gained regarding topics like means for negotiation, rationales for implementing EDI, and the relationship between depth of integration, costs, and benefits. The business-oriented framework for evaluation of EDI presented here has not yet been fully tested. However, we have plans to do so in the near future.
