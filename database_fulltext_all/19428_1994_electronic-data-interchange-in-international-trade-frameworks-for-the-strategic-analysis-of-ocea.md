# Electronic data interchange in international trade: frameworks for the strategic analysis of ocean port communities

Clive D Wrigley

Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Canada, and Erasmus University Research Institute for Decision and Information Systems, Rotterdam, The Netherlands

Rene W Wagenaar

Faculty of Business Administration, Erasmus University, Rotterdam, The Netherlands

Roger A Clarke

Department of Commerce, The Australian National University, Canberra, Australia

As informational aspects of international trade become more important, the strategic positions of ocean port communities become increasingly dependent on the quality and availability of telecommunication and processing infrastructures. This paper develops two frameworks to assess the level and nature of electronic trade integration within the context of a port community enterprise model. Port communities throughout the world are applying electronic data interchange (EDI) to trade functions because it enables open communications among many partners, provides acceptably quick communications for relatively low cost, and offers the prospect of significant savings in time and cost through reduced data capture volume and error-rates. EDI enables not only internal efficiencies but also the creation of new trade services. The first framework specifies the information systems and technology needed to enable EDI in international trade. The framework comprises two levels of infrastructure: communications and documentary; both supporting a third superstructure of business processes. Port communities are evolving from providing not only goods-handling facilities but offering advanced data-handling and processing systems as well. As competition between ports increases, information systems become key elements in their strategic positions. A second framework provides the structure for understanding physical and informational strategies of ports and port communities, and to examine their importance. Port communities may follow several models of infrastructure integration in moving towards their strategic objectives. However, realizing this potential requires research to determine the specific configurations that will fit into each trade environment. The contributions of this research to governments and corporations are primarily to provide models of successful application of EDI to international trade, and insight into technology transfer and diffusion, corporate competitiveness and policy formulation.

Keywords: international trade, EDI

The term ‘international trade’ refers to the purchase and movement of goods and services across national boundaries. International trade has been important for millennia as the means by which an economic region exploits a comparative advantage in order to accumulate wealth. The volume and monetary value involved have increased in recent decades, as shown in Figure 1.

Most advanced countries actively encourage international trade. They place special emphasis on exports as a means of stimulating their country's domestic economies, job markets and earning foreign currency. Many countries retain or periodically renew barriers to imports, but the most economically successful countries have generally found it necessary to permit a moderate degree of import freedom. A few small countries, notably Singapore, have adopted a strategy of specialization, focusing on specific secondary and tertiary industries in which they have, or believe they can develop, comparative advantage and establishing symbiotic relationships with other countries in order to ensure the supply of industrial raw materials and even food.

A number of factors are affecting international trade during the 1990s. In Europe, recent events which are likely to bring about change in the patterns of trade include: the economic integration of the former Eastern Bloc countries; the combination of the European Union (EU) and European Free Trade Association (EFTA) into the European Economic Area (EEA); the likely entry of a number of additional countries into the EU; and the connection of the Danube with the extensive inland waterway system of Western Europe, achieved through the opening of the Main–Danube Waterway. Additionally, the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) between Canada, the USA and Mexico creates a large trading block which appears likely to shift the use of labour and manufacturing facilities over the coming years.

Important aspects of international trade are in transition. Although the volume and value of air cargo has increased rapidly in recent years, the world still conducts the largest proportion of trade by sea cargo. This transition is most marked at the interface between the ocean and inland transport segments — the ocean ports. Historically, the effectiveness and efficiency of an ocean port's physical goods movement determined its ability to attract new business away from slower, less efficient ports of entry. In many ports, however, the informational aspects of trade are becoming a distinguishing feature. The provision of streamlined clearance mechanisms and real time cargo tracking reduce the cost of conducting trade, and increase its speed and reliability. These are attracting importers and exporters to be selective in choosing their port of entry or exit.

At the centre of this transition is electronic data interchange (EDI). EDI offers the prospect of easy and cheap communication of structured information throughout the corporate community, and is capable of facilitating much closer integration among hitherto remote organizations. It underlies a range of important business systems, including just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing and logistics systems, and quick-response retail systems. In addition, EDI is finding wider acceptance in the trade of domestic goods and services. Purchase orders, delivery notices of various kinds, invoices, payment instructions, remittance advices and other common documents, make up a large proportion of existing EDI traffic.

With the establishment of publicly available standards for many trade documents (UNSM EDIFACT), considerable penetration of EDI has occurred, and widespread adoption by the international trade community appears inevitable. The volume of documents which are potentially convertible to EDI format creates economies of scale which will drive the conversion process. Because documentation requirements add several percentages to the cost of trade, the potential economic efficiency gains amount to billions of dollars per year; for example, EDI message volumes to and from the customs authorities will easily exceed 100 million per year by the end of the century. Canadian Customs alone will transmit over 5 million customs declaration forms in 1992 without paper based documentation (IDEA, 1991).

In addition to the efficiency aspects of EDI, the capture, transmission and processing of documents offers new opportunities for providing informational services to the trade community. These services vary widely from port to port, but generally centre around the ability to obtain asset status, provide flexibility in logistics, and facilitate integration with other sectors of the economy.

There has been little discussion of these matters in the information systems literature to date. This paper presents analyses of a number of key areas where the application of EDI may achieve economic improvement in ocean port communities. The first section presents an overview of international trade where we discuss trade phases, trade flow and present an enterprise model of an international trade system. The second section provides a brief definition of EDI and its use in international trade. The third section develops a framework for the information systems and technology needed to enable EDI in international trade. The framework comprises two levels of infrastructure: communications and documentary; both supporting a third superstructure of business processes. This framework is key to understanding where EDI initiatives will have the greatest impact on trade procedures and services. The following section develops the concept of port communities. Port communities are evolving from providing only goods-handling facilities to offering advanced data-handling and processing systems as well. Competition between ports is increasing, and information systems are key elements in the strategic positioning of port communities. The second framework presents a grid on which port communities may place their physical and information system services to gain an understanding of their strategic importance. Next, we examine several models of infrastructure integration which port communities may follow in moving towards their strategic objectives. Realizing the potential of electronic commerce requires a substantial amount of research to determine the specific configurations that will fit into each trade environment. Therefore, we next discuss research methods for conducting comparative studies. Finally, we conclude our paper with a discussion on the implications of such research for governments and corporations.

## International trade

## Trade phases

International trade, like domestic trade, divides approximately into four phases.

(1) Pre-contractual, including such activities as the specification of the purchaser's requirements, the discovery of potential suppliers, and the solicitation, receipt and evaluation of offers, and negotiation regarding the conditions of sale, culminating in the effecting of a contract.

(2) Contractual, including such activities as the issue of purchase orders under a general contract, and the negotiation of variations to the conditions of sale.

(3) Logistics, including the activities involved in physically transporting goods after agreement is reached. Many different information flows are necessary to support these activities, such as delivery date inquiries, delivery schedules, notices of despatch, notices of receipt and non-receipt.

(4) Post-delivery, including such activities as invoicing, settlement and statistical reporting to, for example, industry associations and government statistical agencies.

International trade differs in a number of respects from trade within a single nation-state. First, transport over physical and legal distance involves greater risks than domestic trade. Second, it involves transfer of information, goods and money across international boundaries, and therefore attracts the attention of a variety of government agencies, in at least the exporting and importing countries, and in any others whose territory they cross. Third, it involves activities in two or more legal jurisdictions that are often significantly different, not only in regard to the specific laws applicable to the transaction, but also in terms of the philosophy and precepts underlying trade. Other factors which add to the complexity of international trade are differences in languages and time-zones, and often the lack of direct personal contact between buyer and seller.

## Trade flow

International trade involves vast tonnages of materials, high monetary values and large volumes of complex data. Some of the dimensions along which activities vary include:

(1) The direction of flow, in particular:

\- import trade into a country or geographical region;

\- export trade out of a country or geographical region; and

\- transit traffic into one country's port for the express purpose of transshipment on to another country or region.

The latter is a significant activity in a number of locations such as Singapore (for smaller south-east Asian ports), Montreal (which transships 70 per cent of its volume into the USA), Hong Kong (as an entry or exit point to trade with Southern China), and ports such as Rotterdam and Hamburg which perform the same function for the landlocked countries of Switzerland, Austria, Czech Republic, Slovakia and Hungary.

(2) The modality of the flow, in particular:

\- sea traffic, that subdivides into:

— tankers, e.g. for liquid petroleum, petroleum products and chemicals;

— bulk cargo vessels for dry materials such as iron ore, coal and wheat;

— container vessels, that carry cargo in standardized containers; and

— ‘general cargo’ vessels, that include not only conventional open-hold ships, but also roll-on-roll-off (RORO) and lighter-alongside-ship (LASH) vessels;

\- air traffic;

\- inland waterway traffic;

\- rail traffic; and

\- road traffic.

There is a significant volume of multi-modal traffic, involving containers designed to move from one mode to another; for example the lifting of containers between train-bogies, truck-trailers and ships; and, the driving of trucks directly onto trains for transport through alpine tunnels in countries which are not prepared to accept the road congestion and air pollution to which large lorries give rise.

In the case of containers, it is necessary to distinguish between full container load (FCL) cargo, in which a consignment comprises one or more containers, and less-than-container-load (LCL) cargo, in which the consignment requires less space than a full container, sharing the space with other consignments. LCL cargo usually requires an additional trade role to undertake freight consolidation.

Effective, efficient and adaptive communications are an important factor in international trade. One reason is the significant direct costs involved in the transmission of data. More importantly, however, are the negative impacts of slow or unreliable communications. These impacts include the costs of problem investigation and error correction, delays in the delivery of goods, increased investment in buffer stocks, disruptions in manufacturing processes, and lowered customer loyalty.

Communications are critical among the large central sea ports, air ports and smaller remote locations. In both the exporting and the importing zones, cargo is generally handled several times in quick succession. For example, cargo may move from the original point of loading, into a dockside park, onto a small vessel in a feeder port, and then off the small vessel onto a large ship in a major port.

The major phases, EX0 . . . IM3, correspond as closely as possible to physical goods movement constraints. For example, the EX0 data exchanges constrains goods moving from the Exporter to the Terminal until complete. Within each phase of goods movement, each flow has a sequence number that corresponds approximately to the order of occurrence in real time. However, it is not a strict ordering as each role instantiation may operate differently according to internal structure and efficiency. Finally, the physical system may proceed in the absence of required documentation, but this is the exception rather than the norm. This model assumes the physical system is constrained until all required documentation is in place. This avoids introducing an ambiguous 'trust' component.

This is because the two are sufficiently different that a significantly more complex model would be needed to deal with both kinds of trade. In order for it to apply to customized and custom-built goods, some additional complexity in both the model and the language used would result. Many of the documents vary in name, content and legal significance between industry sectors and between countries. Hence such a general model requires specialization in order to reflect the practices in any particular industry and legal jurisdiction.

Prior studies have shown that international trade involves at least 20 types of enterprises exchanging over 100 formal documents, supplemented by nearly 40 less formalized communications (PMA, 1990). Several parties, including financiers, insurers, and a wide range of government agencies require the nature and value of the cargo, being primarily concerned with:

● revenue collection: customs duty applied to imports, and approval fees applied to exports;

\- industry protection: controls over import of goods which are prohibited, or are subject to quotas or punitive levels of duty, to protect local businesses;

\- safety protection: controls over the transport of goods which are flammable, toxic or otherwise dangerous;

\- environmental protection: controls over the import of goods which do or may contain biologically active material such as bacteria, viruses or seeds and that are therefore either banned or subject to quarantine; and controls over export of such goods where international undertakings or government policy require it;

\- social protection: controls over the import or export of goods deemed in that

country to be socially harmful, such as drugs of addiction and books; and

\- animal protection: controls over the import or export of animals or of animal products, deemed in that country to be threatening to the survival of species, such as live birds, pelts, fur coats and elephant tusks.

These requirements place processing overhead on the trade activity, often duplicating the same data onto similar but different forms. Parties involved in trade have processed and communicated the vast amount of information flow with the aid of many different technologies, including, manual and computer based systems, computer-based data capture, printing, telephone, telex and fax. Generating different documents from the underlying data is one area where EDI will have significant impact.

In addition to the amount of data involved in international trade, a number of factors complicate the document flows considerably. These include factors such as: the venerable history of international trade and accumulated traditions; the vast array of countries, cultures and languages involved in the activity; and, the continual changes that international trade and trade related data flows undergo. As a result, the handling of a single consignment involves large numbers of documents, whose contents vary depending on the nature of the cargo, the carriers, the sending country, the transit country (or countries), and the receiving country. If analysed independently, without an appreciation of the history and the contingencies which may arise, document flows may appear highly redundant and wasteful.

We now turn to the main focus of the paper to address how emerging information technologies will affect international trade in the coming years. To do so, we first define EDI, then describe how international trade uses it.

## Electronic data interchange

Electronic data interchange (EDI) in this paper refers to the exchange of documents in standardized electronic form, in an automated manner, directly from an application supporting one organization to an application supporting another (Swatman and Clarke, 1991). Some key features of the term are:

\- the use of an electronic transmission medium (normally a value-added network) rather than the despatch of physical storage media, such as magnetic tapes and disks;

\- the use of structured, formatted messages based upon agreed standards (such that messages can be translated, interpreted and checked for compliance with an explicit set of rules);

\- relatively fast delivery of electronic documents from sender to receiver (generally implying receipt within hours, or even minutes); and

\- direct processing by application software, generally resulting in a response to the sending organization.

EDI's direct impacts are to reduce the amount of data capture and transcription and the delay between despatch and receipt of messages. These generally result in a decreased incidence of errors, less time spent on exception-handling, and fewer data caused delays in the business process. Benefits can be attained in: inventory management; transport and distribution; administration; and cash management. EDI offers the prospect of easy and cheap communication of structured information throughout the corporate community, and is capable of facilitating much closer integration among hitherto remote organizations (Sokol, 1989;

Emmelhainz, 1990; Gifkins and Hitchcock, 1990; Kimberley, 1991).

Until the late 1980s, the strategic information systems literature tended to regard electronic linkage as a means whereby information technology (IT) could be harnessed as a source of competitive advantage (Benjamin et al., 1984; Ives and Learmonth, 1984; Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Clemons, 1986; Johnston and Vitale, 1988). Since then, however, perhaps resulting from national and international efforts at standardization, far more significant benefits arise from the specific form of electronic linkage, EDI, where it is applied in a collaborative manner. These collaborative efforts typically involve whole industry sectors (Rockart and Short, 1989; Benjamin et al., 1990; Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990; Swatman and Swatman, 1991). Also, collaboration can extend beyond the national to the international domain (Konsynski and King, 1990; Kuula, 1990; Clarke et al., 1991; Swatman and Clarke, 1991).

## International trade EDI

Many types of business are localized, largely because they deal in heavy, low-value materials (e.g. sand-quarrying), involve personal services (e.g. customized computer systems), or are created and nurtured by local laws (e.g. accounting and tax advice publications). Industry and business groups designed and implemented early EDI systems within national boundaries primarily because of the large volumes of within-country commercial activity and the significantly greater complexity of international trade. To large corporations, however, national borders are historical relics that create barriers to business. It is more natural to think of worldwide industry sectors for aluminium and wool, for example, than to think of a large number of national aluminium and wool sectors linked through international trade. There is therefore a strong economic and business imperative toward greater efficiency, effectiveness and adaptiveness in the international arena also.

EDI can be applied in various ways to international trade (Malhotra, 1988; van Maaren, 1988; McCubbrey and Gricar, 1989; Swatman and Clarke, 1991). It may simply automate existing flows by replacing paper with cleaner, less error-prone, faster electronic flows. It may, however, stimulate the rationalization of data-flows, to simplify, consolidate or just omit the use of some of the many documents (Wrigley, 1992). In addition, modification to the procedures associated with the documents, redesign of the organizational structures within which the procedures are carried out, and even changes in roles and responsibilities within relevant industries, can benefit all concerned. Conversely, EDI may also be used to defend existing industry structures and relationships (Purton, 1988; Clarke, 1992; Wagenaar, 1992).

## International trade EDI framework

A pre-condition for the implementation of EDI-supported trade is the existence of IT facilities. These include a high level of domestic computing capability to enable the efficient capture and processing of import and export related data in electronic form, and communication networks to enable the efficient and reliable transmission of data to and from the exporting or importing countries.

Beyond the mere existence of IT, it is necessary to have standards which define documentary structures and protocols to enable the exchange of commercial data among business processing units. Finally, the commercial data and business processes must combine into an architecture which is coherent and provides a comprehensive set of services to support the handling of international trade data. ## Communications infrastructure

The OSI/RM (Open Systems Interconnection Reference Model) addresses the basic telecommunications services and the various standards which conform to that model. Of especial interest to EDI are the message enveloping standards (X.400-1984, X.400-1988 and X.435-1991 PEDI) and the X.500 directory standards. Some additional standards, such as Message Authentication Code (MAC), continue to prove valuable, at least in the short term.

Much of the discussion around electronic commerce presently focuses on the services offered by value added networks. These third-party organizations provide services such as store-and-forward message delivery and message tracking. However important the communication infrastructure may be, it is only the first step in supporting electronic data interchange.

## Document standards infrastructure

It became apparent some years ago that progress in international trade EDI would be dependent not only on underlying telecommunications standards, but also on standards which defined documentary structures and protocols. Estimates of the aggregate cost of the data flows involved in international trade vary between 4 and 7 per cent of the goods' value (UNCID, 1990). Considering the total value of goods transported each year, small improvements in the effectiveness and efficiency of the data flows would result in substantial economic gain.

The logical data model representing these documents contains many entities, including contracted shipments; consignments; specific elements within consignments; container-loads; containers; ships, aircraft, trains and trucks; and voyages or journeys. There have been and continue to be attempts at rationalization or simplification of trade documentation, variously within individual countries, among trading partners, and internationally.

The United Nations plays a central role in the clarification and rationalization of international trade. It has established such standards as: the United Nations Standard Messages (UNSM); the Trade Data Elements Directory (TDED); and, the Uniform Rules of Conduct for Interchange of Trade Data by Teletransmission (UNCID). Many of the world's users of EDI are already committed to the use of this family of standards. ## Business support superstructure

Considerable progress has been made in establishing communications and document standards infrastructure. Additional, and substantial, work is required to establish the rules for usage of these defined documents, so that value-added services may be built around the standard messages. At present, even though the data structure definitions exist, the various trade communities must collaborate extensively to correctly interpret the data elements and structures. For example, the International Framework for Transport Message (IFTM), specifies the messages to use and the interpretation of the data elements. The defined messages in Figure 4 are of a sufficiently general nature that they can support trade in many different kinds of goods. Each industry or cargo type, however, has specific needs, and these may require very specific usage of particular messages, segments or data-items, the use of specialized segments within messages or data-items within segments, and even purpose-specific messages.

Due to the plethora of logistic chains in each economy and the particular needs and practices which have come about, the standard setting bodies have designed messages to possess a great deal of flexibility to ensure that these messaging needs can be expressed. The effect of this flexibility is to increase the amount of investment required by each set of trading partners to enable mutual electronic understanding. Message complexity may actually be one of the key inhibitors, or at least delay factors, in EDI diffusion. For this reason, the EDI councils of each trading nation (e.g. EDIA, EDICC, EDICA) and the working parties they support, perform critical roles in coordinating the sectoral message implementation process and developing the business support and superstructure. One output of this activity is the publication of sectoral specific implementation guidelines which recommend segment subsets and message layouts (see e.g. Li, 1992).

As the communications and document standards become available, it is possible to consider the suite of business support systems which use the underlying infrastructure. These value-added information services can be categorized into two general classes:

\- the creation, processing, delivery and acknowledgement of trade communications; and

\- the establishment and maintenance of on-line databases which provide access and information on:

— movement schedules;

— cargo tracking and notification of cargo arrival;

— customs clearance;

— cargo tagging and notification of hazardous or controlled goods;

— financial services, including documentary letters of credit and insurance;

— resource availability, including containers, and space in vessels, ports and depots;

— traffic statistics; and

— news items relevant to trade and the trade community.

## Port communities

In this section we introduce the concept of port communities and analyse various alternative strategies employed to attain EDI integration. Port communities are seldom identifiable, incorporated entities, but can be conceived as the set of companies, individuals and government bodies whose collaboration enables economic activity within a geographic region surrounding a port. Port communities are composed of a number of organizations, both public and private. These organizations vary widely in function and include: the port authority, customs, shippers, forwarders, importers, exporters, plus a number of other roles, all involved in conducting trade.

The notion of ‘port community’ is complex. A port may be a single set of wharves supported by a common physical infrastructure, or may extend across a wider area. A ‘port community’ is an alliance of organizations which together perform logistical and related functions in a particular port, and thereby provide vital enabling services for economic activities within the local and nearby regions. Such alliances generally revolve around a port authority, which is in many cases a body established by some level of government.

Port communities are primarily a set of interdependent organizations. Moreover, the primary responsibilities of organizations in such an alliance relate mostly to that port (especially the port authority, but also local companies), and are usually associated with a level higher than the port (e.g customs, quarantine and health authorities). Additionally, many organizations have associations with multiple ports (e.g. shipping companies, freight forwarders and customs agents). Despite their amorphous existence, port communities are important economic hubs. Assessment of their activities and their strategic application of EDI requires extensive research.

## The roles of collaboration and competition

The framework presented in Figure 3 refers to the Communications and Document Standards layers of the International Trade EDI Framework as ‘infrastructure’. There are potentially serious disadvantages in permitting the market to determine the form and characteristics of that infrastructure. Rather, collaborative processes have been essential to the establishment of standards. On the other hand, the business support superstructure is an arena in wich competitive forces among individual companies, ports and whole port communities can be harnessed, to ensure that a diverse range of alternative products are available to clients of the international trade sector which are compliant with those standards. Port communities must strike a balance between the need to collaborate and need to compete.

## Port community strategy

Ports are in competition with each other, through the effectiveness, efficiency and adaptiveness of their systems for supporting physical goods flow, data processing and information services. They aim to sustain the loyalty of existing clients and hence attract repeat business, and to attract new business from existing and from new clients. Being an alliance and not a legal entity, it is not easy to ascribe a strategy to them. Individual entities within the alliance may have strategies, and many of them will significantly involve other members of the alliance.

In relation to some forms of trade, most ports enjoy some degree of natural cost advantage over their competitors, based on their physical proximity to, or transport connections with a hinterland. A significant proportion of traffic is, however, discretionary, in that two or more ports can offer similar cost and quality of service. For example, containers bound for western USA from East Asia, can enter at either Seattle or Los Angeles; and cargoes for north-eastern North America have a wide variety of ports available to them, including Montreal, New York and Baltimore. In these cases, shipping agents, freight forwarders and carriers have some flexibility in choosing the port of entry.

Some ports have developed a 'hub' strategy, offering efficient transshipment of goods. This strategy involves cargo movement: to and from large ocean-going vessels which need suitable docking facilities; and, to and from smaller vessels which can offer cost-effective delivery into smaller ports. Singapore has long been a hub for South-east Asia, and has extended its long-standing hub strategy into the air and telecommunications areas. Rotterdam, Antwerp and Hamburg also operate as hubs for land-locked countries of Europe which are reachable through the highly developed inland waterway systems.

Shippers base decisions as to which port to use for particular export and import consignments, on a wide range of attributes, including port charges, speed of entry, supporting infrastructure, etc. The value added services a port offers, and particularly the extent and nature of its electronic support for trade, are becoming increasingly important factors.

## Port community comparative variables

The underlying motivation of port communities is to increase or decrease the values of a number of critical success variables. Three measurable variables which differentiate port communities are:

\- time;

• resource consumption; and

\- control.

The time variable refers to the period that goods are constrained from moving either because of physical or documentary processing delays. It is important to trade because it determines the quality of service to port clients. In the case of long-haul trade, the data flow (or data aspect system — DAS), may not act as a constraint upon the goods flow (or physical aspect system — PAS). In short-haul trade, however, delays in the data flows may constrain the flow of goods. For example, if goods are ready to load onto a ship but must wait for completion of the necessary paperwork, then the DAS constrains the PAS. If port communities target EDI to overcome a competitive disadvantage, or to build a competitive advantage, then they must consider time as a critical variable on which to focus.

The second variable, resource consumption, relates to the costs including labour effort, required to effect trade. These costs arise from the routine processing of trade documentation, and in many cases from regrettably high levels of exception handling. The objective for EDI applications is to reduce these transaction costs, through improving the efficiency of routine work and of exception-handling, but also through reducing the incidence of exceptions. At the wharf, for example, advanced knowledge of truck arrival at the terminal gate enables the terminal operator to minimize the number of intermediate stacking locations required, and better scheduling reduces the time drivers and trucks sit idle.

The third variable, control, refers to the ability to provide flexibility in cargo movement and surveillance of the goods flows; for example, if a port is full then it may be possible to redirect cargo to an alternate port of entry or exit, along with the supporting documentation. Additionally, knowledge of the physical location of goods and their status is valuable for both importers and exporters in keeping their clients informed, and appearing to them to be serving their interests to the full.

## Alternative port community strategies

Traditionally, revenues to port communities came from moving cargo through the ports. EDI now creates opportunities for port communities to provide value added services to their trading partners. The growth in relative market share between different ports will be dependent on a number of factors. The main determinant will be the level of services which the port community is able to offer to the trading partners. The primary outcome variable of interest is the percentage of 'GNP' which trade related services generate. It is possible that revenues generated from these value added services may match or even surpass revenues generated from moving physical goods.

Figure 5 presents a general framework for understanding alternative strategies which port communities may consider when evaluating the implementation of electronically supported trade. In this figure the data aspect systems (DAS) refer to the collecting, processing, and dissemination of data necessary to carry out trade. The physical, or goods, aspect systems (PAS) refer to the actual movement, handling, and perhaps transformation of traded cargo.

A port community may choose to offer only the traditional physical transit services (segment 1). It may leverage on its location, by offering additional physical services such as repair, testing and customized assembly (segment 2). It may move beyond this by attaining high standards in quality and cost, thereby attracting transshipment and intermediate processing of goods which would otherwise pass through other ports closer to the points of origin and ultimate consumption (segment 3).

All of these three strategies assume some minimum level of support for data flows, but the primary focus is on the goods. A port community may choose to place greater emphasis on its support for data flows. This may be simply at the level of passing data reliably, accurately, quickly and cheaply (segment 4). It may, on the other hand, offer value-added services in the forms of support for document flow, management of databases, and end-to-end support from consignee to consignor (segment 5). It may make these offerings so attractive to clients that it may handle data which relates to goods passing through other ports. Additionally, a port may win goods related business on the basis of superior data related services (segment 6). Such superior and integrated physical and data services are likely to attract globally operating corporations.

Port communities may also align themselves with non-competing ports in other continents. In much the same way that airlines form alliances with other airlines in relation to feeder traffic and frequent flyer programmes, ports may also form overseas alliances. To be effective, electronic linkages must support these alliances. Each may promote the other port as the main gateway or entrance facility to the respective continents. For example, Rotterdam and Singapore are currently testing an X.400 based EDI link for this purpose. The effectiveness of these alliances will be measurable by tracking traffic value and volume over time.

## Models of infrastructure integration

In order to conduct strategic analysis of the impact of international trade EDI, general forms of port communities become useful models for comparison. The simplest useful model involves a port, embedded within some legal jurisdiction, and associated with a community of cooperating organizations. The organizations within a port community may operate only within that port community, some organizations may provide services in two or more ports within the same legal jurisdiction, while others may be active in ports in multiple legal jurisdictions. It is common for regulatory activities to be specific to national jurisdictions; in particular customs and quarantine. There may also be some services provided by government, such as pilots for ships entering the port.

The simple model depicted in Figure 6a may be usefully expanded in several ways. Figure 6b represents the pattern whereby multiple ports within a single legal jurisdiction co-operate by providing links among themselves. This has occurred in many countries, although in many cases only in restricted fashion. Figure 6c, d and e present three different levels of international integration. One is by way of multi-national organizations which are active in ports at both ends of a trading partnership. The next refers to circumstances in which particular ports establish links; for example, Singapore and Rotterdam have such an arrangement in place. Finally, the governments of different countries may actively facilitate trading activities; for example, the Customs Services of Australia and New Zealand have established linkages intended to improve the efficiency of trans-Tasman trade.

## Models of scenario formation

In addition to the forms which infrastructure integration may take, the economic and political mechanisms whereby various scenarios come into being are also of interest. This section identifies several alternative processes.

## Multiple initiatives by multiple parties within a port community

Scenarios may be implemented which address particular segments, such as the link between forwarders and shipping agents; or which support a particular function, such as applications for approval to export. Such scenarios frequently are intended to deliver a combination of both infrastructure and superstructure.

This approach harnesses the energy of competition and addresses a limited-scope, relatively simple objective. Such projects are therefore unlikely to fail for reasons of technical complexity or political difficulty. On the other hand, they risk the emergence of a fragmented set of mutually incompatible services. Nonetheless, some port communities may achieve a great deal through this path. It is the dominant model which has been evident in North America.

## Integrated initiative by a major player in the port community

A port authority, or some other dominant entity within a port community alliance may motivate and drive projects. In some cases, a network services provider may provide the nucleus around which the scenario forms. A regional or national government may provide its imprimatur to the project, and may go further and provide encouragement, co-ordination and financial support. Such approaches tend to result in hierarchically structured systems intended to progressively address all aspects of international trade and logistics. Such scenarios tend to concentrate on infrastructure, but not to the complete exclusion of superstructure.

## Collaborative initiative within a port community

In some cases, an entire port community may collaborate in the development of a shared communications and document standards infrastructure. This provides a technical, organizational and political consensus as a basis for the offering of particular superstructure services on a competitive basis. In addition, explicit agreement must be reached on document standards, mechanisms for negotiating trading partner agreements, collaboration on the development of new documents and the rationalization of wasteful procedures.

Once again, this approach involves very considerable difficulties in bringing together traditionally distant stakeholders and industry associations; and once again, some port communities may be successful through such collaborative approaches. Although the Canadian company TDNI grew out of a national study, and provides services to the trade and transportation sector throughout North America and in Europe, it also operates as an international trade community network services provider for the port of Vancouver.

## Collaborative initiative among port communities

In a few cases, collaboration may occur at a level higher than the individual port; for example among several ports located near to one another and with many common members, or among all the ports of a single country. The challenges are in principle even greater, but several instances exist. These include Singapore Network Services (SNS), which provides an infrastructure for the two ocean-ports of the small island state of Singapore; and Tradegate which provides a common infrastructure for the many ports scattered around the much larger island of Australia. In the case of SNS, both infrastructure and superstructure are provided by the community company, whereas Tradegate is conceived as the facilitator of a common infrastructure, with value-added superstructure provided by any company on a competitive basis.

Some countries have profited from the imagination shown by their customs agencies. Traditionally these government agencies, charged with the collection of revenue and the application of laws restricting the flow of goods, have been conservative, slow and even obstructive. Customs agencies in some countries have, however, recognized the importance of their role to the domestic economy, both in terms of facilitating export earnings and of avoiding delays in the delivery of important import goods to their destinations (CCC, 1989a,b). In some cases, customs agencies have ‘encouraged’ companies to connect to an EDI network by ensuring additional costs and delays for those organizations not using EDI. Australian Customs Service is particularly advanced in its application of EDI to export control and to import control, in both the air and sea cargo segments. In the case of sea cargo in particular, it has been instrumental in the emergence of an integrated solution to electronic documentation, rather than restricting the scenario to documents expressly related to customs matters.

## Research directions

Several studies of trade EDI systems of a single port or country have recently emerged (Konsynski and King, 1990; PMA, 1990; Sol et al., 1990; Wagenaar, 1991). Multi-port or multi-country studies conducted have been largely descriptive (Malhotra, 1988; PMA, 1990) indicating the need for more analytical approaches and more rigorous methods (Clarke et al., 1991; Wrigley, 1991).

International comparative research on trade EDI systems is called for. In particular, research should address the following general topics: (1) history and functionality of systems currently in use; and, (2) models of trade procedures and quantitative analysis of efficiency. Research should describe and analyse the technical and organizational features of international trade EDI systems, the priorities placed in different systems on particular features, the processes whereby the systems have come into being, and the directions in which the systems are developing. Attention is to be paid to the systems' economic, political and cultural environments.

Research projects may be developed using three different, but complementary, methods, each with their respective contributions to knowledge. These methods are:

1. Descriptive studies.

2. Surveys.

3. Models of trade.

## Descriptive case studies

As there are differences in the manner and extent of EDI application to international trade between different locations (ports, regions or countries), detailed case studies which collect data on a number of important variables, will provide deeper understanding of the EDI integration process. Some important factors include:

\- the features of EDI implementations;

\- the nature and volume of the trade which the location handles, and of the modes (bulk, containerized FCL, LCL, bunkering) and the types of flows (import, export, transit);

\- the history and the existing power relationships within the international trade community (including individual corporations, business associations, port authorities, government agencies and labour unions), and the extent to which power is exercised;

\- the general IT infrastructure and experience within the enterprises, port and country;

\- barriers to the diffusion process such as: lack of awareness, low IT literacy and skills, inadequate regional and corporate IT infrastructure, fragmentation arising from competition, lack of a community culture and co-ordinative mechanisms, lack of critical mass and poor industrial relations;

\- the origins, stimulus and politics of the adoption process including: competitive cf. collaborative use of IT, contribution to waterfront reform, and actions by competitor and partner ports and regions;

\- promotional and stimulatory measures adopted such as: none, community-financed education, or active government policy measures;

\- the arrangements whereby EDI services are offered such as: open competition, competitive supply within a common infrastructure, or communal services;

\- positive factors in the diffusion process including: promotion by ‘champions’, publication of early adoption experience and government policy.

## Surveys

Case studies should provide insight into the most important factors for comparative measurement across ports. Survey research at this point will provide comparative statistics on the above variables, plus allow specific focus into several other areas. For example, the availability of support services will likely affect investment by global shipping organizations. Surveys of selected decision makers would reveal the specific attributes of ports which they consider the most important criteria in selecting location dependent investment.

## Modelling

While the above two research approaches provide positive comparisons, they do not address issues of design. To move towards normative statements about how a trade system ought to function, electronically, it is necessary to model and simulate complex trade scenarios, considering that:

\- a variety of circumstances exist in which the critical path for international trade is documentary rather than logistical, particularly in short-haul trade such as that on road, rail and inland waterways within Europe, and inter-continental air-freight (Sheombar, 1991);

\- documentary flows can be expedited by applying EDI, such that the documentary flows (as distinct from human decision-making processes and actions) under no circumstances hinder the logistics; and

\- alternative enterprise definitions, document flow designs and procedures are capable of being conceived, which would improve the efficiency of international trade without sacrificing or materially altering the functions performed (Wrigley, 1992).

\- integration with, and application of EDI to, the internal data processing of the participants may fundamentally change organization structures.

## Discussion: implications of research for governments and corporations

There is considerable scope for the application of EDI to support international trade. However, there are many inhibitors to progress. First, it is important that a layered architecture be adopted, differentiating: communications infrastructure, documentary infrastructure, and business process superstructure. It is vital that the standards underlying the infrastructures be developed in a collaborative manner. Competitive market forces can be harnessed in the supply of standards-compliant products and services for both the infrastructures and superstructure. We have examined multiple development paths whereby these structures can be brought into existence, and alternative strategies which ports and port communities may adopt. The factors which determine the development and features of international trade EDI systems are currently poorly understood. Given that the quality of a nation's international EDI capabilities is an increasingly important factor in its economic wellbeing, directed research in this area will give rise to benefits in all countries which seek to take advantage of EDI opportunities. Research is needed in a variety of areas in order to assist port communities to realize the potential of EDI in international trade. Particular areas where research may contribute knowledge to both public and private sectors are:

Technology transfer and diffusion. Different locations and different modes of transit have achieved different degrees of progress in their use of EDI. Synergy among inter-dependent trading partners will be enhanced if they each have access to pre-digested information about exemplars of good practice. This will contribute to improved quality in individual systems, and improved co-ordination among the systems of trading partners.

Corporate competitiveness. Trade is increasingly internationalized and competitive. Companies require information in order to discover how IT can be used to address structural and procedural inefficiencies, create or enhance competitive advantage, and identify and counter competitive threats. Further, research will provide companies with more reliable data on the potential benefits of EDI in international trade and of the barriers to achieving them. The information will be relevant not only to organizations directly active in the industry, but also to those which are dependent on the efficiency of export and import. It will also be of significance to companies providing services related to the industry, particularly IT suppliers.

Policy formulation. Governments have vital policy roles to play in relation to the stimulation of collaborative projects including: the improvement of national infrastructure; general stimulation of EDI usage; the removal of barriers, particularly of a legal and government institutional nature; and, depending on the government's philosophy, direct provision and/or facilitation of funding for aspects of EDI scenarios. Research will demonstrate the extent to which government agency involvement with, and intervention in, these processes, has been critical, helpful, neutral or harmful. It will guide the policy process by identifying factors which require evaluation in each regional and national context. This will assist in achieving the potential benefits from each region's and country's international trade.

The appropriateness of government policy is critical to achieving and sustaining national competitive position. The availability of authoritative studies will assist in the formulation of policies designed to counter factors inhibiting performance and to stimulate efficiency, effectiveness and adaptiveness. This applies at the level of individual ports, at the national level, among supra-national groupings such as Europe's Single Unified Market, and at the international level.

## Summary

The application of EDI to the support of international trade promises to have profound impact on trade procedures and services in the coming years. The frameworks presented in this paper provide useful vehicles for analysing the strategic position of ocean port communities. The enterprise model of international trade shows that trade phases and trade flow may be structured sufficiently to apply EDI support. The first framework places the information systems and technology needed to enable EDI in international trade in perspective. The framework comprises two levels of infrastructure: communications and documentary; both supporting a third superstructure of business processes. This first framework is key to understanding where EDI initiatives will have the greatest impact on trade procedures and services. The concept of port communities reveals the complex environment into which EDI initiatives must operate. While port communities are evolving from providing only goods-handling facilities to offering advanced data-handling and processing systems, competition among ports is increasing. This environment places information systems as key elements in the strategic positioning of port communities. Therefore, the second framework presents a grid on which port communities may place their physical and information system services to gain an understanding of their strategic importance. We also examine several models of infrastructure integration which port communities may follow in moving towards their strategic objectives. However, realizing the potential of electronic commerce requires substantial research to determine the specific configurations that will fit into each trade environment. The contributions of this research to governments and corporations are primarily to provide models of successful application of EDI to international trade and to highlight similarities that may provide insight to technology transfer and diffusion, corporate competitiveness and policy formulation.
