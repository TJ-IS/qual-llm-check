# Computer support for strategic organizational decision-making

F A Wilson

The Information Technology Institute, University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, UK

While information systems continue to be promoted within organizations as tools to support strategic decision-making, there is growing concern over the ability of such systems to model the social and political complexity of the situations to which they are being applied. This paper examines the nature of organizational decision-making and the use of computer-based systems to support this activity. The debate queries the extent to which such artifacts should be allowed to become enmeshed and embedded within the strategic decision-making activities of organizations which operate within increasingly complex environments.

Keywords: managerial actions, decision-making, computer-based systems

As advanced societies develop from an industrial to an information and service-based economy they are becoming dominated by an interlocking network of institutions that are supported by the rapid development of computer systems. Proponents of these systems argue for a linkage between this technology and effective decision-making. Pressure upon organizations to utilize their information technology (IT) systems at a strategic level is apparent from the increasing number of systems aimed at senior management, described generically as decision-support systems (DSS) or executive information systems (EIS). These products are designed to assist managers in those actions which are categorized as being within the unstructured qualitative framework of strategic activity.

This paper considers the actual and potential symbiosis of new information technology with the concepts of qualitative decision-making within an organizational environment, and examines some of the recent developments of systems intended to support this function. It highlights the continued thrust of vendors and technicians to develop methodologies and implement systems capable of interfacing with unstructured managerial tasks and to provide support for activities at the apex of the managerial hierarchy. These developments are contrasted with research into the key area of strategic managerial decision-making. Thus it is suggested that there is a lack of critical understanding within the paradigms of research which examine the role of computer usage in managerial decision-making, and that this is based upon a prevailing rationalistic orientation to systems design. Finally, the possibilities for the true extension of the role of computer-based systems within managerial decision-making activities are explored.

## Computer systems and managerial work

Over the past 40 years, developments in organizational computing have resulted in increased involvement of this technology in the business processes of most areas of enterprise. In the 1980s, concern has centred upon the provision and communication of information for management coupled with the use of computer and communication technology to achieve competitive advantage and business process transformation (Scott-Morton, 1991). The 1990s promises to be a decade characterized by a combination of interorganizational information systems, communication networks, supported by open architectures, and heterogeneous database systems which may create a comprehensive information infrastructure within which the business of the organization will be enacted (Wilson and Wilson, 1992). The aim of this technology is to provide a powerful means of gaining new insight into and control over business functions and to assist directly in decision-making in all organizational areas. The ultimate goal is the integration of information processing in order to create a decision-making environment in which organizational members can regard and understand the organization in new ways. It is suggested that the new data infrastructure will permit radically new business process activities and allow the organizational membership to create new value-added, information-based products and services (Keen, 1991).

## Philosophy of the computer system/decision-support paradigm

The recent developments of computer and communication technology for management applications are being promoted as part of a paradigmatic break with the more traditional forms of management information systems (Carter et al, 1992). However, it is argued here that much of this development, often promoted by academics and vendors as tools for decision-making ‘empowerment’ among the organizational membership, may on detailed examination merely reflect the continued extension of the rationalistic objectivist philosophy with its tradition traceable to Weber’s (1922/1968) concepts of bureaucratization. This is, in part, probably a reflection of the roots of systems design in the automation of ‘well understood’ business activities. Well-defined processes and a well-defined problem space are at least implicitly assumed. Typically, computer-based applications to management decision-making and professional judgement follow the work of Simon (1960) and presume a heuristic search through a problem or possibility space undertaken by a socially isolated individual. Newell and Simon (1972) further objectify knowledge and qualitative reasoning by claiming that computational manipulation of symbolic representations is representative of human thought, understanding and choice. This suggests a model of expert human judgement and qualitative reasoning that can be transferred from the human ‘expert’ and implemented onto a computer. This process reflects the concept of single-loop learning where the assumptions underlying one view of organizational reality are embodied in the system which provides, for example, ‘variance and exception reports’ highlighting critical deviations from target performance. Many of the tools that computer systems provide seem to be those that support the monitoring and regulating functions of the single loop orientation.

## Decision-making within organizations

In following the evolution of theory surrounding the concept of organizational decision-making it may be useful to consider two main variables. First, the extent to which the aims or objectives of an organization are shared throughout an organization. This will range between the (ideal) types of ‘shared’ and ‘conflicting’. Second, the extent to which the capability to make or influence a decision has been diffused throughout the organizational membership. This will range between the (ideal) types of ‘undiffused’ and ‘diffused’. Four different mechanisms through which organizations may exhibit the particular forms of decision-making are identified in Figure 1.

The explication of what is meant by bureaucratic/rational processes of organizational decision-making may be traced to the work of Barnard (1938). The rational model presumes decision events are ‘purposive choices of consistent actors’ (Allison, 1971, p 11). Under the rational choice model a criteria of rationality is defined through a (shared) consistent set of goals or objectives while the capability to influence the decision outcome is diffused throughout the organization. The rational model presumes that there is a unified purpose or set of preferences characterizing the entity taking the action. Until Simon (1957) introduced the concept of satisficing, it was assumed that search was costless and that large numbers of alternatives would be considered. The satisficing concept suggested a bureaucratic model where decision-making occurred according to rules and regulations which had been adaptive and effective in the past and where ‘most of the behaviour is determined by previously established procedures’ (Allison, 1971, p 67). The bureaucratic model suggests there is a much less extensive information search than the rational model and consequently less (undiffused) participation of the organizational membership in the decision-making process. Through these ideas theory was able to progress, particularly through the work of March and Simon (1958), and Cyert and March (1963), and to develop the image of an organization as a satisficing coalition of acting participants. Underlying this image was a model of man as intentionally rational but the extent of this rationality was ‘bounded’ by the complexity of the actual situation of the decision-makers.

The political/decision process models of decision-making promote the view that organizations should not be conceptualized as the phenomenal expression of some essential inner principle such as economic rationality. Instead, they must be treated as consisting of varying systems of thought and action which reflect the underlying and irrevocable ambiguity, uncertainty and capriciousness of organizational life. The decision process model departs from the bureaucratic-rational model by removing the assumption of consistent (shared) aims and objectives. Influence upon decision-making is diffused since there are no overwhelmingly powerful actors with defined preferences who possess resources which enable them to achieve these preferences (Cohen et al, 1972; Stava, 1976). The political model of decision and action may be seen as an indeterminate outcome of substantive struggles between people who deploy differing (undiffused) influence and resources and who seek to control and decide the nature of organizational activity. Organizational behaviour may then be regarded as a consequence of decisions to act in certain ways by purposive agents. These decisions and actions are shaped by struggles around competing substantive objectives or diverse ‘modes of rationality’ (Clegg, 1989, p 238). The concept of the ‘boundedly rational individual’ of the bureaucratic–rational models obscures the power relationships of the political–decision process models. Furthermore the bureaucratic–rational models’ preoccupation with ‘the decision’ involves the risk of imputing a direct relationship between the abstraction of mental intention at the individual or small group level and the concreteness of realized action at the organizational level. An associated problem, within an organizational context, is that it also reinforces an undifferentiated, mechanistic image of one or a small number of central decision-makers who are in control, thereby diverting attention away from the fact that organizational actions do not always correspond directly to leadership intentions. Focusing on the decision can also mask the ways in which general commitments are reshaped, elaborated and redefined over time through complex processes within and outside organizations. Mintzberg and Waters (1990, p 4) support this in suggesting that in many situations an organization may be perceived to have “‘acted’ though the management never “decided””. This upholds the view of Douglas (1987) where she argues that the really important decisions are not taken by persons, but by the institutional framework. Indeed, some researchers have abandoned the concept of the link between management and decision-making, preferring to look for actions within organizations (Mintzberg and Waters, 1985) since these are the only traces actually left behind in those organizations.

In conclusion, the initial theoretical reasoning concerning individual and organizational rationality (Von Neumann and Morgernstern, 1947; Simon, 1957) has changed as the reality of organizational decision-making was revealed to be a process dominated by cognitive, ideological and political practices that distort concepts of pristine objectivity, neutrality, rationality and control. One of the results of this theoretical evolution is the discovery of contexts in which the very notion of ‘rational decision’ dissolves into ambiguities and so loses the meaning ascribed to it in other contexts. This supports Weick’s (1985, p 110) argument that rationality within organizational action may be viewed as ‘a set of prescriptions that change as issues change, as a facade created to attract resources and legitimacy, and as a post-action process used retrospectively to invent reasons for action.’ As such, the fundamental assumption, that formal organization structures and decision-making processes are necessarily designed with operational efficiency and long-term goal achievement as their dominant priorities, dissolves into a plethora of subjective interpretations and political evaluations. The essential character of organizations becomes one of component parts (human beings) who can attribute meaning to their situations and can therefore impute to organizations whatever purposes they wish, and make of organizations whatever they will. Thus organizational action cannot be the expression of some essential rationalistic principle since claims to such principles as prime movers necessarily neglect the actual complex and contingent conditions under which organizational action occurs. More relevant at the strategic level of decision-making is the political process of problem formulation and the way in which contextually relevant possibilities are identified and justified.

## Some observations on the decision-support paradigm

The previous discussion suggests that the concept of ‘the decision’, like so many other concepts in computing theory, may sometimes prove to be an artificial construct. The key decisions made by a manager, particularly at a strategic level, are generally poorly defined, or understood, and cannot be fully represented within a pre-specified problem space. ‘Facts’, as they are entered into a computer system, are represented symbolically and are not objective truths gleaned from the physical world. The environmental contingencies that led to the emergence of a problem are inclined to change, and there are no guarantees that the problem was adequately perceived in the first place. Data entered into a system may be based on assertions and interpretations by an individual within a context emanating from unavoidable, and sometimes unrecognized predispositions. The manager may be prevented from responding creatively, because of the limitations imposed by a certain system conceptualization of what constitutes a problem. In many cases the bias of the system developer towards numerical representation of a problem may lead to a forced description of problem (a restricted language set), a pattern of reasoning which may be inappropriate to the situation. As a consequence, tensions may arise within the organization that are created by the very use of the computerized system. Several of the difficulties that may arise from the normative forms of system design are discussed in subsequent sections.

## Computer systems and purposeful behaviour

The difficulty in defining a system's objective, in terms of the extent to which it is capable of criticizing its own data, may lead to the problem of obscuring the need for managerial interpretation of the objectives of the system. Ulrich (1981) describes this with reference to the concepts of purposiveness and purposefulness within computer systems. He describes purposiveness as referring to the efficiency of the components of a system to fulfil the basic performance definitions. He describes purposefulness as the critical awareness by self-reflective humans of the implications of a system with regard to its ends or purposes. Normative system design, through its own epistemological and methodological standards, has little flexibility for dealing with human purposefulness within its theoretical frameworks. As Westrup (1993) has observed, system designers tend to claim that their discipline enables them to explain and design goal-directed systems without recourse to teleological concepts such as purposeful behaviour. The semantic and pragmatic aspects which are mandatory for grasping motivation and the complexity of the managerial (social) environment come to be regarded as extrinsic and irrational categories.

## The semantic consideration

A further difficulty arises in the use of language both as a means of designing and developing a system and in the interaction between the system and user once implementation has been completed. The system development cycle is generally a linguistic interchange between the system designer and an expert. This critical role of the context, within which observations are made and within which conversations take place, exposes a potential impediment to computer systems design for decision-support, ie the danger of developing a system with an emphasis upon the syntactic, at the expense of the semantic and pragmatic levels of communication (Ulrich, 1981).

This focus upon the syntactic level may often distort discussions concerning the principles of complexity control which underlie a computer system's development. Ashby's (1964) Law of Requisite Variety states, that to control a complex system the controlling system must be able to generate at least as much variety as the system being controlled. The actual mechanism of control consists of establishing and maintaining the controlled system in states dictated by the controller or, more specifically, adjusting the values of designated variables of the controlled system to those desired by the controller, and maintaining those values. In terms of designing a computer system to assist in managerial decision-making, an approach that may exclude relevant variety from consideration (in order to reduce the controlling computer system's own internal complexity and maintain its syntactic coherence) may fail to generate the relevant kind of variety required for its set task of complexity management in social systems.

As computer systems become linked to decision-making within an organization and reliance upon them increases, the ability of the user to question the validity of the system's output may be severely eroded. A danger exists that the logical truth of a computer system's internal structure will develop a belief in the user of the quasi-objectivity of the syntactic truth nets within the computer resulting in a failure in the user's ability to question the underlying assumptions of the system.

## The decision-maker/computer system relationship

As computers become incorporated into the mode of strategic organizational decision-making, their importance as a means of information reduction and analysis increases. Beer (1975) argues that a computer is wasted if deployed merely for more and more data production and data storage. He suggests that the function of the computer is 'to recognise what is important and to present only that very little information' (Beer, 1975, p 431). In cybernetic terms the computer's role is the filtration of information so as to discriminate between significant and insignificant data. The syntactic truth nets that result may tend to replace the unreliable judgement of human users by absolute control of some crucial variables. More precisely, the possibility of wayward decision-making on the part of the users may be 'homeostatically' restrained within certain modest limits of human error by the system designer. This may not be seen to be problematic to a rationalist designer because, given the manager's own need for certainty, no 'rational' manager will ever wish to ignore the results of computer-based analysis. Relating to an artificial CBS model of the environment is in part at least, relating to one's own previous actions and reactions, reflected through the environment via the information system. The pressure on strategic decision-makers is to choose courses of action and CBS models present the means to do this rationally. To the extent that the concept of 'rational decision' enjoys prestige with the wielders of organizational power, it must apply in the context of 'problem solving'. To solve a problem from the point of view of the power wielder is to answer the traditional question: 'How can I get the most for the least?' This is the fundamental question of technology and of competitive business. The posing of the problem turns attention to the aspects of the situation which make concrete formulations possible. Efficacy of action can be measured if it is expressed in fiscal form, cost-benefit ratios, returns on investment, etc. The use of computer support may seem to illuminate the problem as it is posed by the power wielders because such calculations clarify and operationalize the meaning of 'efficacy'. Consequently in a culture in which science is practically identified with technology, success with virtue, and security with power, there is unrelenting pressure to translate vaguely stated socio-cultural (strategic) problems into clearly stated business (operational) ones (cf the rise of the 'total quality' movement). Supporting this perspective, Fromm (1974) has argued that the manager's need for certainty makes it difficult for him to refuse a method of planning that is 'free from the arbitrariness of the human will and entrusted to a computer system' (p 50). This is not to say that the manager may not be well aware of the uncertainty and potential fallibility in such computer-based decisions, due to the heuristic gap between syntactic truth and pragmatic meaning; 'but if disaster does happen after the decisions are made on the basis of unquestionable "facts", it is like an act of God, which one must accept, since man cannot do more than make the best decision he knows how to make' (Fromm, 1974, p 51). Thus to the extent that rational methods are so employed to solve the problems so translated, they are developed in a systems framework which presupposes the bureaucratic-rational models of decision-making. Furthermore, as the managerial process becomes more automated, opportunities for professional discourse are reduced, with the resulting potential for lower quality professional judgements. This is not surprising, since only in these rigid contexts can a 'rational decision' be unambiguously defined. Beyond these contexts is an intellectual barrier which cannot be breached without abandoning the notion that every decision problem has a rational solution in the sense of an optimal course of action to be chosen by measurement of 'utility' value. However, as Marcuse (1964, p 190) states 'the rational is not irrational, and the difference between an exact recognition and analysis of the facts, and a vague and emotional speculation is as essential as ever before'. The problem may be that computer systems are not rational enough. They remain problematical in the extent to which they are isolated from the social reality which makes facts and determines their function and importance. This reality will remain larger than the attenuated form of decision-making which may result from the over-reliance upon computer systems. The dangers in this mode of decision-making have been observed at the clerical level of many organizations by Fearfull (1992), and at higher managerial levels by Sewell and Wilkinson (1992). Situations in which policies and operating standards are challenged may tend to be exceptional rather than the rule. Thus a situation of 'invisible' control, that is, the operation of control systems which are not readily perceived by the organizational membership, may become embedded in the rules and systems of the organization and its technologies (Zuboff, 1988). The result may be a managerial 'worldview' based upon the perception of the world favoured by the system designer. Alternative viewpoints and courses of action may become fewer as these information systems develop and their users come to rely on them as their main sources of apparently unequivocal information.

## The need to be reflexive

Many advanced societies are moving from an industrial, to an information and service-based economy. Tertiary development involves many structural changes, enabled by the rapid penetration of computer systems into the workplace. As Hirschheim (1985) has argued, it is a mistake to view computer systems in purely technical terms. Rather they should be seen as social systems and more participative approaches should be used in their design. In order for this to take place without severe organizational and societal disruption there is a need for individuals who ‘bring something new to the perception of the world, cut through the accustomed categories (and) break down rigidities' (Becker, 1980, p 165). In supporting this the means must be found to provide individuals with the heuristic support (the conceptual framework) needed to reflect upon the normative implications of computer systems design and their implementation.

This analysis suggests the development of a variety of tools and approaches which move away from the traditional paradigm of systems design and development. The emergence of participative design methodologies such as Checkland's Soft Systems Methodology (1981) provide a foundation for structuring complexity, uncertainty and conflict among multiple participants in managerial activities. These methods are designed to move the organizational discourse away from 'single-loop', or non-reflective learning to 'double-loop', or reflexive learning where claims to objectivity and system 'purposefulness' are questioned and verified through discourse (Argyris and Schon, 1978). In single-loop learning, organizations attempt to fulfil pre-determined objectives, and monitor performance in relation to those objectives. Double-loop learning requires that the organizational objectives, and the assumptions underlying them, be evaluated themselves in relation to changes perceived in the environment. By reinforcing single-loop learning, a computer system may foster an objective, value-free orientation. As technical advancements are driven by economic demands, all other considerations are rendered secondary or are lost. A highly sophisticated single-loop learning system may actually serve to keep the organization on the wrong course, since people are unable or not prepared to challenge underlying assumptions. The existence of single-loop learning systems, especially when used as controls over employees, may thus prevent double-loop learning from occurring.

Further potential for empowering organizational actors with the means to examine the assumptions and values of computer system development may be available in the development of the ‘critical systems heuristics’ approach (Ulrich, 1983). This approach aims to encourage computer designers to make themselves and others aware of the normative content of designs. Ideally all proposed designs would be submitted to critical inspection and not presented ‘objectively’ as the only scientific possibility. This development would specify that all designs must be subject to a debate in which all those affected by a design, but not involved in it, are encouraged to participate. Such a debate presupposes the enhancement of the communicative competence of the participants through the creation of an ‘ideal speech situation’ (Habermas, 1970), where genuine debate and undistorted communication facilitates the move towards a truly rational consensus about both the means and ends of the computer design system. In such a situation a need exists for the views of ‘as many different individuals as possible so as to have as varied a view of reality as possible, for only in this way can we get a rich approximation of it’ (Becker, 1980, p 165).

However, as Morgan (1986) suggests, the ability to achieve critical awareness in most organizations may prove elusive. While some organizations have been successful in institutionalizing systems that review and challenge basic norms, policies and operating procedures in relation to changes occurring in their environment, eg by encouraging ongoing debate and innovation, many fail to do so. This failure is especially true of bureaucratic organizations, since their fundamental organizing principles often operate in a way that actually obstructs the learning process. This is compounded by the complexity of the link between an organization and its use of technology. Because of the increasing scope of technological requirements, as well as their extensive influence over organizational activity, the costs of large-scale technologies ‘create immense demands that dramatically narrow the room for democratic decision-making' (Strasser, 1984, p 162). Success in the use of critical systems thinking concerns understanding the strengths and weaknesses and the theoretical underpinnings of available systems' methods, techniques and methodologies. Considerable effort may be required to develop new organizational structures which will devolve this form of knowledge to all those affected by the system.

## Conclusion

The use of computer systems within the strategic managerial decision-making process provides access to complex communication and modelling capabilities. A danger has been identified in computer systems development based upon a set of validity claims which cannot be discursively questioned by the user who is not cognizant of the system's logic. Decision structures may be imposed through the computer system which may then result in an organizational culture wherein, if the justification of norms is precluded and individual identity is disregarded, absolute conformity is demanded and cultivated. Systems which support 'objective' views of organizational reality are useful, and probably even essential, for supporting many managerial efficiency, quality and perhaps training activities. However, in functions devoted to strategy formation, where the concern is mainly with the interpretation of the organizational situation, an over-use of computer systems during the early stages of strategy formulation may marginalize more hazardous, spontaneous and intuitive forms of sense-making, leading to a more unified 'world view' but at the same time exacerbate the problems associated with routinization. The reinforcement by the computer systems, and acceptance by the strategic actors, of a uniform and monolithic 'symbolic universe' perceived as necessary to explain and understand what the organization is all about may disable innovative change and adaptation. This may have disastrous consequences on managerial action since such a system may entail the development of a pervasive managerial ideology designed to prevent any disturbance to the 'new' system-based organizational reality. The potential result may be the organizational version of the Oliver Sacks (1985) report of the case of Dr P who, due to the gradual advance of his disease (a degenerative process in the visual parts of the brain), showed the inability to make cognitive judgements in terms of interpreting objects as themselves (visual agnosia). Despite the affliction, Dr P continued with his work as a music teacher and remained 'prolific in the production of cognitive hypotheses'. Sacks's reports of Dr P's 'absurd abstractness of attitude — absurd because unleavened with anything else — which rendered him incapable of perceiving identity, or particulars and rendered him incapable of judgement'. The case may be an illustration of what may happen if information systems are developed which eschew the judgemental, the particular and the personal, and become entirely abstract and computational. The success of managerial action involves not only classifying and categorizing (the computing function), but also the continual use of human judgement and intuition. If managers lose touch with these dimensions, as they solely engage in instrumental action through the medium of computer-based systems, the normative and subjective dimensions will be lost, leading to distorted understandings and ultimately to a distortion of actions within the organization.
