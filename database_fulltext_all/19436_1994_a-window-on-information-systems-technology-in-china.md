# A window on information systems technology in China

Michael D Ishman, C Carl Pegels and G Lawrence Sanders
School of Management, Jacobs Management Center, SUNY at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY 14260, USA

The status of information technology in China is important to Western firms contemplating cooperative commercial arrangements with Chinese firms. This paper discusses the characteristics of the information technology environment in China. It presents the results of an open-ended questionnaire administered in 1986, and two structured questionnaires administered in 1990 and 1992, to a group of Chinese managers about the introduction and diffusion of computer-based management information systems in their respective organizations. The study presents managers' perspectives across multiple layers of organizational structures. The particular managers who shared their views can be best described as ambitious fast-track individuals who are open to new ideas. They are perhaps representative of the future industrial leaders of China. Of more immediate importance, they typify the type of manager currently involved in China's 'open market' sector. These managers are critical employees of import/export divisions, members of joint venture teams and spokespersons for branch offices of foreign firms. In other words, they represent the type of Chinese manager with whom Western firms currently deal, and will be dealing with in the future. Other implications for firms contemplating joint venture relationships with Chinese firms are also given in light of the authors' experience and the results of the survey.

Keywords: information technology, China, management information systems

China is frequently referred to as the awakening giant. Indeed, the changes taking place have important implications for the West and for stability in the world (Pegels, 1987). Its one billion plus people are hard working with a high degree of literacy. Although the percentage of people engaged in agriculture is over 80 per cent in many provinces, China also has developed a strong industrial base. The industrial base has been and is going through a modernization phase. China's four modernization programmes — agriculture, defence, industry, and science and technology — have been compared with the industrial revolution in their ambition and scope (Kosenko and Coskum, 1985). As part of this modernization phase information technology (IT) is gradually being introduced in China. The Chinese perceive the acquisition and development of information processing technology as a primary vehicle for modernization. This emphasis may be well-placed because it has been argued that developing countries can achieve higher growth rates by becoming information oriented economies (Sweeney, 1981; Karunaratne, 1982).

Estimates for the installed base of computer hardware in China at the end of 1986 were 8000 minicomputers and mainframes and 200 000 microcomputers (Tate and Maier, 1987). An article in China Daily (1986) presents somewhat lower figures of 5000 mainframes and 130 000 microcomputers. A more recent article in China Daily (Kan, 1990) puts the figure at 400 000 personal computers and 8000 large and medium-sized computers. About that time, in the USA, there were over 20 million personal computers in the work environment alone (US Department of Commerce, 1991). China's computing power in MIPS (million instructions per second), as indexed against the USA (set at 100), is 0.2 (Juliussen et al., 1987). It has also been reported that much of the hardware in China is heavily underutilized (Shea, 1988). Reasons for this low level of utilization, as revealed in the research results reported in this paper, can be traced mainly to inadequate planning and managerial ability.

During the past five years only a few papers have been published on the development of information system technology in the People's Republic of China. Those papers have dealt with library information systems (Lin, 1988; Shi and Larson, 1990; Wang, 1990), science and technology information systems (Taihong and Derong, 1987), information centres (Chien Lin, 1988) and hardware/software issues of China's computer industry (Tate and Maier, 1987; Shea, 1988; Kan, 1990; Tai, 1990; Wu and White, 1990). Several other articles have addressed the problem of the Chinese character set as a major obstacle to the diffusion of IT (Boyd, 1988; Cross, 1988; Lin, 1988; Dambrot, 1989; Meyer, 1989; Mori and Kawada, 1990; Shi and Larson, 1990; Wu and White, 1990). However, because this is an issue that is important to all those countries where variations on the Chinese character set are used (China, Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Korea and Singapore), not all of those articles have been specifically about China.

Of all the papers reviewed, only one considered the issue of user participation and satisfaction in the introduction of information systems (Ming-Te et al., 1988). The topic coverage was limited in its scope and the information provided is somewhat dated. Consequently, there is a paucity of current information on managerial and end-user perspectives on information systems technology. It is the objective of this paper to fill this information gap by reporting on: the results of an open-ended survey of Chinese managers in 1986; the results of a structured questionnaire administered in 1990 and 1992; and field observations of the authors gathered over a 10-year period.

## Open-ended survey results of Chinese managers, 1986

In 1986, a group of 39 Chinese middle managers with four to 15 years of management experience were asked the question: 'What are the major challenges you will be facing in modernizing the information processing functions of your organizations and the country in general?' The 39 managers were participating in an English language Executive MBA programme, held at Dalian University, Dalian, China, and they came from many of the major industrially developed areas of China. While their views cannot be generalized to the whole of China, they can be viewed as a representative sample of progressive Chinese managers who are open to the introduction of new ideas and technology.

The question asked was somewhat open-ended but the managers had been exposed extensively to computers and information systems and they had one week to reflect on their answer. The question was one that they certainly had contemplated, either explicitly or implicitly, during their classroom hours or while doing their assignments for the courses in the programme. We initially classified the open-ended responses as shown in Table 1.

As can be seen from this early survey, the major problem confronting these Chinese managers was a lack of understanding of computer potential. A secondary but still significant problem was the fear of unemployment and fear of the unknown impact the computer would cause. Lesser problems as viewed by the respondents were: (i) shortage of qualified personnel; (ii) insufficient resources to acquire the computer-based technology; (iii) lack of business management training by potential adopters; and (iv) the Chinese character set.

Several of the issues noted in the 1986 open-survey have been reported elsewhere. Shea (1988) has noted that many of the computers imported into China during the mid-1980s have been underutilized or left idle due to a shortage of trained personnel. Both Wang (1990) and Lin (1988) have reported that there are too few knowledgeable people in information retrieval. Lockett (1988) notes that there is generally a low level of technical and professional skills among Chinese managers. As previously mentioned, problems associated with the computer processing of the Chinese character set have been extensively addressed in the literature and the low capabilities in foreign languages for information interchange have been reported by Taihong and Derong (1987).

## Structured questionnaire results, 1990 and 1992

The results from the open-ended survey served as the framework for developing a structured questionnaire for measuring perceptions regarding the barriers or obstacles to using computers in the People's Republic of China. Other resources were then used to expand the questionnaire. For example, some of the key information systems issues as expressed by corporate general managers in the Brancheau and Wetherbe (1987) survey were included in the questionnaire. In addition the questionnaire was scrutinized by several individuals with knowledge and experience on the state of information systems in China.

In 1990, 122 graduates and students of the English language Executive MBA programme of the State University of New York at Buffalo, held at Dalian University, Dalian, China, and supported by the US Department of Commerce and the Chinese government, completed the questionnaire. In the summer of 1992, the questionnaire was again sent out to all 208 previous graduates of the English language Executive MBA programme at Dalian University. Seventy-seven managers responded to the survey. Of those 77, 50 had also responded to the 1990 survey. If one were to generalize the characteristics of all of the respondents in the survey it would be one of managers open to new ideas, in possession of high levels of ambition, and well-educated in Western management concepts, tools and practices.

## Field observations on computer technology in China

During their China experiences, while teaching and consulting in China over a 10-year period, two of the authors had the opportunity to interview Chinese managers and observe at first hand what is happening in the context of computer applications to technical, administrative and management problems. Below we summarize some of those field observations. A condensation of the summary is shown in Table 3.

While the Chinese managers surveyed in 1986 only viewed the Chinese character set as a moderate challenge to information processing, we observed that it could be a major obstacle to the diffusion of IT. For example, the latest technological developments are not available until they are adapted to the Chinese character set. Because of this, software development languages (database, spreadsheet, BASIC, etc) are one or two releases behind their Western counterparts. Even after software has been modified to store and print Chinese characters, keyboard input using the Kanji character still presents a challenge. Notwithstanding the problems related to using Western software in China, many Chinese firms are opting to use Western software because it meets their requirements.

In some Chinese firms there is considerable resistance to using existing applications software (payroll, inventory, etc). The reasons are a combination of pride, specialized requirements of Chinese enterprises, and technical difficulties related to the Chinese character set. Although Japanese Kanji is similar to the Chinese character set, we saw very few instances where they were using Japanese software. This may be linked to historical and cultural differences between the two countries.

A recurring common problem, noted in the surveys and field observations, is the shortage of managerial personnel with skills in management and IT. Engineers in design departments are the most prominent computer users since they can readily take advantage of the number-crunching prowess of the computer. Understandably, FORTRAN became the lingua franca of computing in China because it is amenable to solving highly structured scientific problems. Only recently have other programming languages become more widely used.

This engineering orientation often carries over to the acquisition of IT. Computers are frequently purchased by management the way one buys a truck or a piece of manufacturing equipment; that is, the hardware is purchased with little thought given to the software, not fully realizing that hardware without software is useless. Management assumes that their engineers and computer centre personnel can make it work.

Some of the resistance to IT can be traced to political and social mores. For example, position in Chinese hierarchy is usually determined by the number of people supervised. There is a general attitude in China, which was supported by the open-ended survey, that IT will replace the hordes of pencil pushers. One computer centre manager reported that he had obtained an accounting software system ready to be installed in his organization. However, the chief accountant, anticipating work force reduction, refused to cooperate and as a result the accounting system was never installed. The good news is that the respondents to the structured questionnaire did not consider this a significant issue.

Resistance to change often borders on dogmatic resistance. Change in any form is difficult to implement because innovativeness and progressiveness have not been cultivated in the Chinese managerial system (Davidson, 1987). In one organization every office has a microcomputer, yet pen and pencil continue to be used for record keeping.

Data integrity is a major concern in any information system in China. During the cultural revolution China discovered that data could be created through ‘brainstorming’. In other words data is generated by imagination rather than by fact. It was found that data, if selected properly, can make people feel prosperous and can satisfy superiors who are under pressure to report that production output goals have been reached or exceeded. Leftovers of the ‘phoney’ data generation practice are still around. Recognizing the severity of the problem, the Chinese government took drastic measures and passed a law in its attempt to curb the generation of false data.

Lockett (1988) found that information used by Chinese managers in decision-making is subject to a filtering process. Chinese organizations are highly differentiated activities with predominantly hierarchical organizational structures. Decision-making must pass up and down through those multilayers of the organization and information is subject to various degrees of filtering.

The development of integrated distributed communication networks is essential for China's long-term quest for modernization. Indeed, respondents to the structured questionnaire considered the data transmission channels as the third greatest impediment to using computers in China. As has been noted earlier, there is considerable sentiment that less developed countries need to become information-oriented economies. The infrastructure for such an economy is based on telecommunications networks that integrate not only national resources in industrial and rural environments but are also a critical link to international markets (Sweeney, 1981). Note also that rural telecommunication links should not be neglected, as studies have found positive spill-over effects in developing countries when telecommunication facilities have been introduced in stagnant rural hinterlands (Karunaratne, 1982).

China's geographic dispersion and variety of terrain coupled with the demand from foreign investors for broad band communications channels necessitates renovation. The current system is by any standards inadequate. There is evidence that China is attempting to correct the situation by installing fibre optic and coaxial cables, microwave towers, communications satellites and computerized data switching. High quality channels are available between most cities; however, the demand is high and availability is not guaranteed. One interesting alternative pursued by some Chinese organizations is leasing a channel from the military.

The technical barriers to distributed processing are only part of the story. The organizational and cultural barriers loom as an even larger presence than technical considerations.

By Western standards Chinese business units within the same corporation are alarmingly autonomous. The result is that there is limited sharing of data and systems between units. Each business unit in the same corporation has its own computer development staff and develops its computer systems independent of what is happening at other locations. We have observed, for example, two ball bearing manufacturing plants in Beijing and Shanghai belonging to the same corporation with totally different financial, inventory and scheduling systems.

As noted in the open-ended survey the Chinese are wary of the impact of IT. Some of these fears will undoubtedly be reduced with education; however, deeply rooted cultural and social barriers may prove to be too monumental to overcome.

First of all, the Chinese are endeared to their informal communications networks. Exchanging information within organizations involves a greater degree of social interaction than is found in the West. Any actual or perceived formalization of communication channels, or jobs, will undoubtedly meet with resistance. Secondly, exchanging information between organizations is frustrated by cultural, historical and political forces. Remnants of feudalism, the cultural revolution, and the Communist Party are forces still pervasive in Chinese enterprises. As a result, there are real barriers (walls around plants, security guards, etc) and social barriers (be wary of outsiders) with which to contend.

Historically, most managerial moves in China have been vertical, not horizontal, from one company to another. There are, however, indications that some Chinese managers are making significant career changes outside the 'normal' organizational paths. As can be observed in Tables 4 and 5, the types and sizes of organizations represented in the surveys span a wide range of business activities. Between 1990 and 1992 there is a slight shift in the type and in the size of business activities in which these Chinese managers are involved. When the responses to both surveys are examined, it is found that a higher than expected number of specifically identified managers have moved to smaller organizations. In some cases the move is entrepreneurial in nature, in others the move is to a branch office of a foreign firm. The numbers are relatively small, but nevertheless surprising. The major change is a move away from education/research (typically low pay) to import/export organizations. Import/export organizations can reap many benefits from the Western management skills these managers acquired from their MBA degree.

Finally, access to books, technical documents and literature in general is limited. It has been estimated that there are not enough knowledge books of any kind in China to give one book to each person in the country. Coupled with the language differences between the West and China, this presents a real challenge to the education process.

## Major barriers to information systems adoption in China

Based on the responses to the surveys, and the field observations, IT in China is still in a state of transition. Information systems are beginning to appear, but the rate of diffusion and level of absorption from the perspective of Chinese policy makers and firms contemplating joint ventures, is low. The major barriers to the adoption of IT are:

(1) China's social, political and cultural environment.

(2) Questionable attitudes towards computing technology.

(3) A weakness in functional area knowledge as it applies to product, process and managerial areas.

(4) A low level of information systems ability; including product, process and managerial areas.

It is not likely that China's social and cultural environment will change rapidly, even if political change occurs during the next few years. China's social and cultural values have been established over a much longer period than in the Western countries. As a result change will come more slowly and will be less pervasive. An added ‘brake’ on rapid change is China's enormous rural population, amounting to 80 per cent of the total population. This population needs to be absorbed into China's evolving future economy. At present the urban and rural populations are kept apart. Integration will come in the future, as has occurred in all developed countries. This integration process is one of China's biggest challenges; it will undoubtedly slow the pace of cultural and political change.

Ambivalent attitudes towards computing technology are very much part of the social and cultural aspects of Chinese organizational culture. In the Chinese organization, information is power. Organizational communication is largely by word of mouth, taking place at the numerous meetings and face-to-face contacts that are the core of the Chinese culture and social structure. Memos, reports and paper trails are not abundant in China, and technology is viewed as a potential destroyer of the present status quo. It is rightly perceived by many Chinese managers that IT will diminish some of their power and these managers present a tremendous barrier to the successful utilization of information technology in China.

The education and training obstacles category in Table 2 reflects a lack of basic understanding and appreciation of information processing and how information processing can be of assistance in improving the management and operations of corporations and government agencies. Not only does this category reflect a lack of knowledge of IT, but also a lack of general management training. Utilization of IT requires the presence of trained managers in functional areas such as accounting, finance, marketing, sales, engineering, personnel management, production and other operations. Without trained functional area management, the viability of IT will be at risk and by Western standards the typical Chinese manager is not well trained in functional area management skills. In fact, in many instances appointment to managerial positions is a form of political compensation for past loyalty to the Communist party. Chinese organizations are not totally void of management expertise, but functional and general management needs education, training and development. This is a monumental task which China is just now beginning to take seriously. This clearly is an enormous task and will take many years. Only following this management development period can we expect to see widespread diffusion of IT in China.

Related, though not independent, to the above is the low level of business knowledge possessed by information systems professionals. The new movement to functional area management training benefits information systems personnel as they will become better-trained managers. It is the functional area managers who must understand and realize the benefits of IT if China is to witness the diffusion of computing technology in government, business and industrial enterprises.

## Conclusion

China is a large complex country with a population of over one billion people. No single study can hope to capture all of the diversity in even one portion of this vast population. However, this report does give the reader a small window on IT systems in China. The study presents managers' perspectives across multiple layers of organizational structures. The particular managers who shared their views can be best described as ambitious fast-track individuals who are open to new ideas. They are perhaps representative of the future industrial leaders of China. Of more immediate importance, they typify the type of manager currently involved in China's ‘open market’ sector. These managers are critical employees of import/export divisions, members of joint venture teams and spokespersons for branch offices of foreign firms. In other words, they represent the type of Chinese manager with whom Western firms currently deal, and will be dealing with in the future.

Considering that the responses to the questionnaire survey are quite recent (1992), the information processing industry in China is still a long way behind the developed world. However, catching up is feasible and can be done quite quickly provided resources are available and allocated to the information processing activities within corporations and to the development of infrastructure. Evidence indicates that both capital and training commitment will be done on a selective basis to serve those industrial and government sectors that are critical to China's exports, internal security and defence.

As part of the Western world's window on China, the issues raised by the managers surveyed are important to any foreign firm venturing into this vast marketplace. Such firms must plan to deal with the major barriers reported. Some of the barriers to information systems adoption are familiar to any organization that has attempted to implement technological change. Those types of barriers call for approaches that have previously worked in the West. Other issues are unique to China's social, political and cultural environment. They will require innovative ideas that need to be cultivated from within China's management ranks. Such cultivation requires education and training. Fundamental education for large numbers of future managers is a responsibility of Chinese policy makers. Other governments and Western universities can, however, offer implementation models and participate in joint programmes that serve as conduits for knowledge sharing.

Western firms also must initially be prepared to spend time and money on efforts to assimilate their methodologies with the uniqueness of the Chinese business environment. This environment has evolved over a much longer period than in the West. What impact IT will have on societal structures in China remains to be seen. In the West, when computers were implemented, organizational structures changed (Whisler, 1970), but the expected dramatic impact of computing on the work environment has not yet been found (Danziger and Kraemer, 1986). Some jobs are enriched, some are made more routine (Robey, 1987), some jobs are eliminated and some new ones created (Attewell and Rule, 1984). To the Chinese, unemployment is a dominating concern. Some are sceptical of the benefits of IT, particularly since underemployment is already a national problem. Will the Chinese make the ‘great leap forward into the information technology age’ or will they be willing to follow gradually the IT practices of the developed world?

## Appendix: 1990 and 1992 questionnaire structure and sample demographics

In 1990, a questionnaire was sent to 102 graduates of the English language Executive MBA programme of the State University of New York at Buffalo, held at Dalian University, Dalian, China, and supported by the US Department of Commerce and the Chinese government. These graduates were from the MBA classes of 1986, 1987 and 1988. Thus they all had had time to reflect and see first-hand the growth of computerization in China. Fifty-two graduates responded to the survey. This is a very good response rate considering the sensitive nature of some of the questions from the Chinese government perspective, the Chinese mail system and a current address for the graduates.
