# Career orientations of information systems employees in South Africa

Magid Igbaria

Programs in Information Science, The Claremont Graduate School, 130 East Ninth Street, Claremont, CA 91711, USA

Guy Meredith

Viadata, 15 Shirley Road, Claremont 7700, South Africa

Derek C Smith

Department of Information Systems, University of Cape Town, Private Bag Rondebosch 7700, South Africa

Managing the careers of information systems (IS) personnel is important to the strategic use of information in business. Finding appropriate mechanisms for motivating IS personnel will likely re-emerge as a critical success factor for IS managers. This study examines dimensions and levels of career orientations and their correlations with individual and work-related outcome variables among 112 IS employees in South Africa. The findings reveal a rich diversity of career orientations held by IS employees. The data strongly suggest that IS employees are very service- and security-oriented. IS personnel in South Africa are also lifestyle and managerially oriented. However, IS personnel scored low on technical orientation and entrepreneurship. This paper suggests that the dual career ladder is not an effective device for managing IS personnel. Organizations must be careful to provide career paths that retain and motivate workers and more importantly find a match between the organizational needs and the individual needs and restructure jobs accordingly. Suggestions for future research are offered and implications for management are identified.

Keywords: career orientations, career anchors, information systems, career management, career paths, South Africa

Information systems (IS) are recognized as a means of creating and sustaining competitive advantage in the global marketplace. Many organizations invest in IS in an effort to remain competitive and to maintain or even enhance their performance (BusinessWeek, 1993; Fortune, 1994). As an organization becomes more dependent on the use of information systems and technology, the importance of having competent IS professionals and the effective utilization or management of IS professionals in the organization increases. An important element of effective management of IS people is the design of a career management system in order to satisfy employees' career values and aspirations and to assure the organization that it has the right mix of people, with the right mix of skills, at the appropriate organizational levels (Greenhaus and Callanan, 1994).

IS employees are critical to ensure the successful implementation and use of IS in organizations (Baroudi, 1985; Niederman et al, 1991). Outlining the critical issues facing the IS profession in this decade, several researchers identified management of IS people as one of the areas requiring immediate attention (Igbaria and Greenhaus, 1992; Niederman et al, 1991). Butler Cox (1991) concur, suggesting that the real value of investment in IS will derive not so much from the technology but from the people employed to perform the IS function. Computerworld (1995) reported that 'demand for IS professionals skilled in today's booming technologies far outstrips supply. As of December 1994, the job market increased by a massive $44\%$ over 1993. Compared with 1992, the number of available IS jobs has increased by $64\%$ ’ (p 1). The imbalance in the demand and supply guarantees that recruiting and retaining IS personnel is going to be a critical success factor facing IS management for many years.

The concept of career as a link between individuals and — through their work — the organization within which work is performed has been viewed from both an external and an internal perspective. Prior research on management of IS people has tended to concentrate on the ‘external career’ of employees; that is, on the sequence of jobs and positions through which employees progress (Greenhaus and Callanan, 1994; Van Maanen and Schein, 1977). Career paths are examples of organizationally defined careers and, as such, are referred to as external careers. Little attention has been devoted to examining aspects of the ‘internal career’ of IS employees; that is employees’ career aspirations, values, perceptions, and effective reactions to job experiences — which can have important implications for their satisfaction, commitment, and retention within the organization (Igbaria et al., 1991). Ginzberg and Baroudi (1988) call for an examination of issues pertaining to the internal career of IS employees, focusing on their self-concept and career values.

An important element of employees' internal career is career orientation or career anchor, that is one's career aspiration, and in sense, defines one's self-concept in terms of one's career (Schein, 1987). Derr and Laurent (1987) state that the core of this dimension is described as 'What do I want from work given my perception of who I am and what is possible?' (p 456). The career anchor or career orientation is significant because it influences career choices, affects decisions to move from one job to another, shapes what is being looked for in life, determines the individual's views of the future, influences the selection of specific occupations and work settings, and affects employees' reactions to their work experiences (Schein, 1975). A measure of career orientation enables an organization to find a match between the organizational and the individual needs and to restructure jobs accordingly. It also serves as a useful information base for individuals contemplating career change, and for organizations seeking to help individuals plan their careers (DeLong, 1982; Igbaria et al, 1991). Career orientation, therefore, provides a focus or direction to channel employees' efforts and determines what may be done to achieve their career goals and aspirations. These studies demonstrate the importance of career orientations in managing IS employees and suggest a need for further work in this area. Furthermore, prior research on career orientations (Baroudi, 1988; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1988, 1992; Crook et al, 1991; Igbaria et al, 1991; Crepeau et al, 1992; Igbaria and Baroudi, 1993) was confined to North American subjects. The present study extends the prior research by testing the career orientations of IS employees and examining the generalizability of previous findings to a non-North American country that can be expected to have at least a partially different culture than that in the USA.

## Research background

This section explains the research background. First, the career orientation concept and the relationship with career paths are discussed. The second part of the section discusses the significance of the cultural context and external validity. Recognizing the North American bias of existing empirical IS research, it argues the need for more cross-cultural research and introduces the cultural context of the present work.

## Career orientations

Career orientations of IS employees are thought to be of major importance in understanding reactions of IS employees to various career development opportunities (Crepeau et al, 1992; Igbaria et al, 1991). These studies reinforce the importance of understanding variations in career aspirations and patterns. Schein (1975) suggested that employees may hold different values and pursue different types of careers. In fact, there is considerable diversity among IS employees' career orientations (Igbaria et al, 1991; Crepeau et al, 1992; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1992). While some employees value advancement or freedom above all else, others primarily value the intrinsic excitement of work, and still others place the most significance on security and balance in their lives. Research has shown that compatibility between an individual's desires and aspirations, and job setting, produces high levels of job satisfaction, career satisfaction, and organizational commitment, and a low intention to leave the organization (Igbaria et al, 1991). Conversely, incompatibility can result in dissatisfaction and a corresponding desire to leave a particular work setting.

Empirical work exploring dimensions of career orientations has been guided by the work of Ginzberg and Baroudi (1988). They noted that building a dual career ladder was the most commonly recommended IS management solution in both the practitioner and research literature. The IS career literature discusses the inadequacies of the traditional technical to managerial career path which most entrants to the IS profession face (Chesebrough and Davis, 1983; Baroudi, 1988; Carlyle, 1989; Ferratt and Starke, 1989; Pierce, 1991); offers advice to technicians on overcoming the problems they face in moving into managerial positions (Ruhl, 1990; Lawton, 1991; Ward, 1991); relates the problems encountered in remaining in a technical career path (such as technical obsolescence) and how these may be overcome (Youst and Lipsett, 1990; Currid, 1991; Hooten, 1991); and, advocates the use of the dual career path (Butler Cox, 1991). The dual ladder requires organizations to provide viable career paths for technically inclined as well as managerially inclined personnel. IS employees with a strong managerial career orientation are mainly concerned with movement into positions of managerial responsibility and seek recognition primarily from their organizational superiors. In contrast, IS employees with a strong technical career orientation, are primarily concerned with making career choices that enhance the professional content of their work and seeking recognition from their professional peers outside and inside their organizations. The dual career ladder recognizes that IS employees may have differing career needs and attempts to provide incentives that are consistent with employees' underlying career values. The organization may benefit from this approach by motivating employees and reducing turnover. For the individual, this system may offer flexibility, freedom, challenge, personal growth and career advancement opportunities. The dual career ladder, however, may not deal effectively with the various career options desired by IS employees as it assumes two options are sufficient (Igbaria et al, 1991; Crepeau et al, 1992; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1992). These studies suggested that the dual career ladder concept does not adequately describe the career needs of individuals and organizations.

Schein (1987) found that individuals hold a wide variety of career interests, which he labelled career anchors or orientations. The career orientation or anchor is a concept developed from a longitudinal study of 44 alumni from the Sloan School of Management in Massachusetts. The study took place over a period of 10 to 12 years, during which information was elicited regarding the alumnae's values and attitudes, the choices and changes they had made in their careers, and the reasons for, and feelings experiences with, each change. While the individual career histories varied considerably, the reasons for the changes, and the emotions experienced with each change, exhibited high regularity. The increasingly distinct and stable patterns revealed a 'growing sense of self — "this is me, and that is not me" (Schein, 1987, p 159), which appeared to be derived from the experience of early years in the career. This self-concept was termed the career orientation or anchor. It is defined by Schein (1985, p 28) as 'that set of self-perceptions pertaining to your (1) motives and needs; (2) talents and skills; and (3) personal values that you would not give up if you were forced to make a choice'. It is important to realize that this self-concept is not present on first entry into the workplace, but is developed through occupational experience, from which employees learn what their talents, motives and abilities really are. Schein (1987) has identified eight career anchors that guide employee's career decisions: (1) technical/functional competence; (2) managerial competence; (3) autonomy/independence; (4) security/stability; (5) sense of service/dedication; (6) pure challenge; (7) life-style integration, and (8) entrepreneurial creativity.

Technical/functional competence. The primary concern of individuals with this anchor is the actual content of the work. Employees who hold this career orientation typically want to remain within their technical/functional area. Such individuals identify strongly with their area of expertise, and normally have a strong aptitude and motivation for a certain type of work. They are stimulated through the application of their technical or functional skills and the knowledge that they are experts in their field. They also eschew the administrative and managerial tasks of the generalist, which they believe to be painful though necessary, and seek to be specialists in their field. Some do become functional managers, but only if doing so permits them to remain in their specialized area.

Managerial competence. Individuals with this anchor discover as their careers progress that they wish to become general managers rather than grow in a particular functional area within the organization. Management interests them more than specialization, although this group usually understands the need to have a solid grounding in a specific discipline before becoming a generalist. Such individuals display the ambition to rise to high positions in the organization hierarchy and are motivated by opportunities for additional responsibility, contribution to the success of the organization and high earnings. They require a combination of talents in key areas in order to succeed in general management: analytical competence, interpersonal and intergroup competence, and emotional competence. They differ from other groups in that the combination of the above three skills is key to success, whereas other groups rely on the development of one skill element.

Autonomy/independence. Individuals with this orientation have an overriding need to exercise independence, in the way they work, the rate at which they work, and in the standards they apply to their efforts. Such people have an innate dislike of being restricted by other's rules and regulations, whether these involve working hours, dress codes or any other organizational policy. They are likely to refuse promotion which would elevate them to positions of greater responsibility, but of lesser autonomy.

Security/stability. Security-oriented people have a prevailing concern for a career which is stable and secure, in which future developments are predictable, and which allows them to feel that they have ‘arrived’. This concern manifests itself in either job or geographic security. The need for security may be satisfied by remaining in the same organization, the same industry, or the same geographical location. People with this orientation generally prefer stable and predictable work.

Service/dedication. Individuals anchored to a sense of service are primarily concerned with a cause which they feel strongly about and typically seek to improve the world in some way. Such individuals are interested in improving the lives of others, perhaps by working in a ‘helping’ occupation. Professions characteristic of this orientation are teaching, the ministry and medicine, though not all individuals in these careers possess this anchor and personnel with this anchor can also be found in business and organizational careers.

Pure challenge. People who hold the challenge anchor derive their satisfaction from facing and overcoming increasingly difficult problems and obstacles. This group, unlike the technically anchored, is not restricted to being challenged by a certain type of problem, such as a technical one, but may enjoy technical, managerial or even athletic challenges. To this group, competing and winning out over others is of primary importance. These individuals seek novelty, variety and challenge in their work. Managing these individual presents, in itself, a formidable challenge. While they may be highly motivated to perform, and remain loyal to an organization for as long as they are given the opportunity for self-test, they are also very determined and have little time for those around them who do not share their ambitions. Without a constant source of challenge, these individuals become demotivated and an irritation to themselves and those around them.

Lifestyle. The dominant theme of individuals with this orientation is achieving balance in all major sectors of their lives. Specifically, individuals with this orientation would want harmonious integration of family and career activities. It is believed that this orientation is likely to become more common as it is a reflection of social trends in society and an unavoidable effect of dual-career families.

Entrepreneurship. This group has a predominant need to create an enterprise of their own, through the creation of a new product, service or business which will be recognized as theirs and identified as existing entirely through their efforts. The spur which drives these individuals should not be confused with the creative urge experienced by certain researchers and designers. Entrepreneurs are driven to create not just something new, but something with which they can be identified and which will produce significant earnings by which their success can be measured. Individuals with this orientation want the freedom to build and operate their own organization in their own way. It is important to recognize that a career orientation does not imply limited career growth or change. Instead, it indicates that career movement and advancement occurs within a specific, bounded area.

## Cultural context and external validity

Hofstede (1980, 1991) recognizes that many popular management and motivation theories such as Herzberg's two-factor theory (Herzberg, 1966), Maslow's hierarchy of needs (Maslow, 1970) and McGregor's theories of $X$ and $Y$ (McGregor, 1960) reflect the North American culture and argues that their applicability in other cultures is questionable. Hofstede argues in several contexts of his analysis that popular motivation theories are culture-bound. Additionally, Derr and Laurent (1987) and Schein (1987) both argue that career concepts developed in the USA may not be transferable to other countries. It is suggested that countries as cultural settings would vary in the degree of prestige they assign to different career paths, which, in turn, would lead to variations in how employees in different cultural units perceive and feel about their careers. On the one hand, it might be expected that few differences in levels of career orientations among South Africa and the USA IS professionals should exist since both countries share similar employee attitudes (Rosen and Shenkar, 1988). They share English as a common language, facilitating communications and easing transfer among countries and between countries. On the other hand, there is evidence to suggest that the South African IS industry is four to five years behind the USA in terms of implementation of modern management practices (Picton, 1989). In Hofstede's (1991) analysis of 50 countries, he found that South Africa and the USA differ in individualism (the USA and South Africa were ranked 1 and 16, respectively).

## Relationship between career orientations and individual and work-related outcome variables

Career orientations of IS professionals are important due to the notion that these orientations could be significantly related to work-related outcome variables such as satisfaction and commitment. To date, however, there has been little empirical work on the association between career orientations and work-related variables as well as individual variables for IS professionals. Crook et al (1991), Ginzberg and Baroudi (1992) and Igbaria et al (1991) posit that various individual variables relate significantly to career orientations. Previous empirical research suggested the examination of gender, age, job type, organizational tenure, job tenure, and organizational level as potential correlates of career orientations. Recent studies (Crook et al, 1991; Igbaria et al, 1991) report gender differences in career orientations, with women found to be more lifestyle oriented than men. Men, on the other hand, are more technically and managerially oriented than women. In a similar vein, organizational level has been found to be associated with career orientation. Employees in professional positions have been found to be more technically or security oriented than those in managerial positions, while employees in managerial positions are more managerially oriented than professionals. Similarly, employees who hold managerial positions (management) are more managerially oriented than people who hold technical positions (programmers). Employees who hold technical positions are more technically and autonomy oriented than non-technical people. It is also expected that age may be related to career orientations, with older employees being more security oriented than younger employees. Thus, in the present study, the relationships between individual variables and career orientations were examined.

Baroudi (1988) examined the relationship between career orientations and career satisfaction. He reported that the correlations vary substantially. For five out of the nine career orientations no relationships were found to exist with career satisfaction. The other four correlations (managerial, security-job, service, and identity) were all significant but the strength of the relationships varied from one to another. Thus, the relationship between career orientations and satisfaction were also examined in this study. Finally, Igbaria et al (1991) also examined the relationships between career outcomes and the fit between career orientations and job type. They examined only two career orientations (managerial and technical). Their results show that individuals who are technically oriented reported higher levels of job satisfaction and career satisfaction, but lower levels of commitment and intention to leave. Therefore, it is suggested that career orientations and career outcome variables may be correlated. IS researchers have looked into the career orientation of IS employees only in the USA (Crook et al, 1991; Igbaria et al, 1991; Ginzberg and Baroudi, 1992). This study is the first to examine the career orientations of IS employees outside the USA. It attempted to investigate the network of relationships of career orientations with selected personal variables — gender, marital status, age, tenure, job type, organizational level, and education — and key career variables — job characteristics, job and career satisfaction, organizational commitment and turnover intentions.

## Method

## Sample and procedure

A sample was desired which could be considered broadly representative of the IS industry. It was decided that a sample from the Computing Society of South Africa (CSSA) be selected, as its members represent a wide variety of organizations, positions and industries (CSSA, 1990). In addition, as this study intended to measure the career orientations of individuals who are likely to have made a firm decision to follow a career in IS, the CSSA was considered an appropriate body for sampling. The CSSA is a body whose members are actively employed in, or associated with, the IS field. Strict rules govern full membership, and these stipulate that an applicant should have a computer-related qualification (ranging from a certificate in computing to a Master's Degree) and a minimum number of years experience (from three to eight, depending on qualification). Individuals may apply for Associate Membership provided they are employed or studying on a full time basis in the computer field.

The sample was further limited to members between the ages of 30 and 44 inclusive. Firstly, a career orientation is developed through one's interaction with the work environment, and may take 10 years or more to manifest itself clearly (Schein, 1985). It is probable, therefore, that individuals under the age of 30 are in an exploratory stage of career development, while those over 30 have developed a clear and stable career orientation. Secondly, limiting the sample to those below the age of 45 was expected to reduce the likelihood of extraneous variance caused by the 'midcareer crisis' (Schein, 1987; Burke, 1989; Stout et al, 1988), a period during which individuals undergo self-confrontation and can be expected to reassess their attainments, goals, values, initial career choice and future. Finally, the choice of sample is supported by demographic trends taking place in the labour force. Naisbitt (1985) reports that the number of entry-level workers (ages 18–24) has dropped substantially in the 1980s, with the figure expected to drop even further in the 1990s. As a result, the average worker of the 1990s will be between 35 and 44 years old (Luthans and Thomas, 1989). As this study sought to examine the average IS professional, the age limit imposed seemed appropriate. Indeed, this decision is borne out by the membership figures of the CSSA, which suggest that the majority (54 per cent) are in the age group 30–44 years.

## Mailing procedure and respondent characteristics

In similar research, conducted in the USA and South Africa respectively, Igbaria et al (1991) had a response rate of 20.3 per cent, while Kaplan's (1990) was 44 per cent. It was assumed that a response rate between these rates was appropriate. A response rate of 33 per cent was assumed, which implied that the sample size chosen had to be three times greater than that dictated by statistical considerations (Kaplan, 1990). Thus, 300 members of the Western Cape Chapter of the CSSA, aged between 30 and 44 years old, were randomly extracted from the full membership list.

Each of the 300 subjects was sent a questionnaire and an addressed and postage paid return envelope. Of the 300 questionnaires sent out, three were returned unopened due to incorrect addresses. It is assumed that 297 actually reached the addresses. Of these, 112 usable questionnaires were returned by the deadline set for inclusion in the analysis, resulting in a return rate of 37.7 per cent. The demographic characteristics of the respondents are presented in Table 1. The comparative percentages provided under the heading CSSA are representative of the members of the Western Cape Chapter of the CSSA aged between 30 and 44 years (n = 499). These data provide substantial evidence that the demographic characteristics of our sample are quite similar to those of the total CSSA population.

## Measures

Gender was assessed with a fixed-response item (1 = male; 2 = female). Similarly, marital status was coded to create a dichotomous variable (1 = not married; 2 = married). Respondents indicated their job title in an open-ended item. Education consisted of six levels from (1) standard IX or less (some high school) to (6) postgraduate degree. Salary consisted of six tiers from (1) below R40000 to (6) R120000 or above. Age was measured in years. Tenure in the current organization was measured by the number of years individuals had been employed in their organizations. Participants were also asked to indicate how many years they had been in the IS field. Organizational level consisted of two levels: (1) professionals, and (2) supervisors and managers. Positions were classified as managerial when the job title suggested managerial, supervisory or coordinating responsibility, or when the hierarchical level indicated manager status, such as project leaders and IS managers. Positions were classified professional in nature when the job title suggested technical responsibilities and the hierarchical level indicated professional status. The IS professionals included systems analysts and designers, and programmers. The demographic items were included in the background information section of the survey.

## Analysis and results

In order to examine the prevalence of the career orientations among IS employees in South Africa, the average score of each of the nine career orientations was calculated. The data are presented graphically in Figure 1. The average scores for each of the nine career orientations range from 2.71 for technical orientation to 3.70 for service. The data show that three career orientations: service (mean = 3.70), job security (mean = 3.65) and geographic security (mean = 3.63), are ranked high. At the other extreme, technical orientation (mean = 2.71) and entrepreneurship (mean = 2.96) are ranked low. Other career orientations represented in this sample include lifestyle (mean = 3.56), managerial competence (mean = 3.47), autonomy (mean = 3.32), and pure challenge (mean = 3.23). While these results are consistent with Ginzberg and Baroudi's (1992) findings, they are contrary to those of Igbaria et al (1991). Note that Igbaria et al (1991) analysed only the dominant career orientations and not the average scores.

The relationships between career orientations and some of the demographic variables (gender, marital status and organizational level) were also examined. Analyses of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine the relationships between these demographic variables and the average scores of career orientations. Figures 2, 3 and 4 present the average scores of the nine career orientations by gender, marital status, and organizational level, respectively. Figure 2 shows that gender is related to managerial competence (F = 3.74, p ≤ 0.05) and job security (F = 5.06, p ≤ 0.05).

Figure 3 presents the average scores of the career orientations by marital status (unmarried vs married). Results show that no significant relationships were found.

Figure 4 presents the average scores of career orientations by organizational level (professional vs management). The data show that IS employees in managerial positions scored higher on managerial competence (F = 16.74, $p \leqslant 0.001$ ) and job security (F = 3.69, $p \leqslant 0.05$ ), but they scored lower on life style (F = 4.04, $p \leqslant 0.05$ ) than IS employees who are professionals.

## Discussion and implications

The results of this study reinforce the importance of understanding variations in career aspirations and patterns. The findings reveal a rich diversity of career orientations held by IS employees in South Africa. IS employees were widely spread across various orientations. This study suggests that IS personnel cannot be classified as either managerial or technical in their career aspirations and orientations. It also identifies the dual career ladder as not providing a complete model to accommodate the internal career needs of IS personnel. The dual career ladder provides options only suited to the managerial competence and technical competence orientations. However, the data show that IS employees in this sample scored the lowest on the technical competence orientation. On the other hand, service and security — job tenure and geographic — were the highest orientations, followed by lifestyle. Job-tenure, security-oriented IS employees placed little importance on challenge and entrepreneurship, but more importance on technical and managerial competence as well as on geographic security. Geographic-security oriented IS employees tended to be relatively unconcerned with entrepreneurship. The results of this study may be unique to the geographical area of the Western Cape and similar research conducted in other parts of the country would be of interest to establish whether these results are relevant to other areas or not.

The study shows that managerially oriented IS employees are less concerned with technical competence, lifestyle and geographic security but more concerned with job security and entrepreneurship. Additionally, autonomy-oriented IS employees scored highly on pure challenge, lifestyle and entrepreneurship. It is quite possible that the high scores on geographical security and lifestyle orientations may be limited to the Western Cape in South Africa, as it can be presumed that many individuals move to the Cape because of the lifestyle they are able to pursue, and because of the beauty of their surroundings. However, a further explanation for the predominance of this orientation may be due to the changes taking place in the work force. Toffler (1981) reports that, while job mobility was resisted by 10 per cent of employees in the 1970s, between 33 and 50 per cent of employees now resist such moves, no matter how much better a salary is offered with the transfer.

The predominance of the lifestyle orientation may not only be restricted to the Western Cape. Toffler (1981, p 396) reports that ‘the balance has shifted away from saluting the company … towards a greater emphasis on family and lifestyle’. A new work ethic described by Naisbitt (1985, p 92) which states that the workforce today has ‘a widespread expectation … that work should be fun’ may be contributing towards this shift in emphasis and, consequently, to the focus on lifestyle. Sonnenberg (1991, p 41) supports this view, stating that ‘(those) … who slept, ate and breathed work, now … are taking weekends off and muttering about personal fulfillment and quality of life’. Therefore, organizations should provide flexible leave and working hours, and paternity and maternity leave, which would be of value to the lifestyle oriented.

The findings also show the distribution of career orientations by demographic variables. Females are less marginally and job security oriented than males. Results also show that IS employees who held managerial positions were more managerially oriented, but less technically and lifestyle oriented than those who hold technical positions. Similarly, IS professionals were less managerially oriented but more job security and lifestyle oriented than IS management. Results also indicate that while educated IS employees scored higher on autonomy and managerial competence, they scored lower on service. Their professional training and education has made them totally self-reliant and responsible and they are, therefore, more autonomy, managerially and challenge oriented (Schein, 1987). This is somewhat consistent with our expectations. More educated people would rather advance to managerial positions and need more autonomy and challenge than less educated IS employees. Additionally, older and more tenured IS employees scored high on job security and geographic security. This suggests that these individuals are more security oriented than newcomers. Organizations should find a way to keep them in one geographical area as they may strongly resist a move to a different location.

There was a clear tendency for employees with different career orientations to gravitate toward different positions in the IS function. As expected, individuals in technical positions held technical orientations, and those in managerial positions, managerial orientations. These findings are consistent with the view that career orientations influence employees' career decisions to pursue specific jobs and career paths (Schein, 1975). Since IS personnel are driven by a diverse set of career values and goals, rewards must be valued by the employees in order for them to have incentive value (Vroom, 1964), and since employees holding different career orientations value different type of activities and accomplishments (Schein, 1987), somewhat different rewards may be necessary to motivate different types of employees. It should be noted that the meaning of achievement, accomplishment, and success rests on the individual's particular career orientations. Rewarding a lifestyle oriented employee with a better job that requires a geographical transfer, or a technically oriented employee with a lucrative but constraining promotion may well detract from motivation and performance. At the very least, organizations need to understand and consider their employees' career orientations in the development of their reward systems. Employees should discuss their needs, values, and career orientations with their supervisors on an ongoing basis, so that realistic feedback is provided, challenging assignments are given, and meaningful career goals are established.

The career orientations concept offers some ideas of ways to restructure jobs to respond to employees needs. Employers can add to the employee's sense of security as well as appeal to those to whom lifestyle and community service are important with unconventional working hours. Organizations should have flexible programmes and change the job content to accommodate different employees. Management should be aware of the various career orientations of their employees and should be clear about what they require from each individual. Jobs should be analysed with a view to establishing exactly what kind of work is performed in the job, so that employees might better select jobs suited to their orientation. As Toffler (1981) points out, organizations should not attempt to seek one set of incentives with which to motivate the entire workforce. Indeed, it is likely that a simple readjustment of current career paths and incentive schemes with a view to offering a 'menu' of options, such as medical benefits, flexible working hours, optional leave, pensions and insurance, and rewards in the form of bonuses, merit awards, sabbaticals, and share options which individuals could select according to the requirements of their orientations (Toffler, 1981; Schein, 1985) would suffice. Additionally, management should permit lateral career movement of staff, particularly with entry-level employees. Indeed a variety of experiences and maximum feedback from each is likely to assist individuals in gaining a clear and stable self-concept far quicker than would result from few jobs or minimal feedback (Schein, 1985). The company will also benefit, in that they will be able to ascertain the strengths and weaknesses of employees sooner and will thus be more knowledgeable about the positions for which they are best suited (Butler Cox, 1991).

The relationships between career variables and career orientations show that both rewards and activities (ie task-based and organizational-based rewards) are positively correlated with service and that organizational-based rewards are positively correlated with managerial competence. This shows that managerially oriented employees tend to pursue jobs that provide them with advancement opportunities as well as recognition within the company. The data also show that managerial orientation is positively correlated with organizational commitment. This may be attributed to the feeling of equity among this group. Additionally, job and career satisfaction are positively correlated with geographic security and negatively with autonomy and entrepreneurship. Employees with these latter orientations are more likely to leave the organization. This may be due to the fact that most organizational reward systems are not at all geared to deal with autonomy oriented people (Schein, 1987), and therefore they can be expected to be more dissatisfied with their jobs and careers. The results are also consistent with our expectations concerning the relationship between geographic security and career attitudes. Schein (1987) also found that individuals with geographic security orientation are more satisfied with their jobs and careers and are less likely to leave the organization. These individuals are primarily concerned with the need to stay in a chosen location, so the best strategy with such individuals is not to offer them a high-paying transfer to some other geographical area. These findings may have implications for the human resource strategies used to manage IS personnel. As career orientation defines the career goals or aspirations of IS personnel, it may be necessary for IS and human resources departments to develop career management systems that are congruent with the career aspirations of IS personnel. An organization should find a match between the organizational and the individual needs and restructure jobs accordingly. Compatibility between career orientations and job settings produces high levels of satisfaction and organizational commitment, as well as a low intention to leave the organization (Igbaria et al, 1991). Conversely, incompatibility can result in dissatisfaction and a corresponding desire to leave a particular work setting.

Finally, IS employees who scored very high on entrepreneurship are obsessed with the need to create, and they get easily bored and dissatisfied (Schein, 1987). They get heavily involved in their job in order to make it happen. Those individuals with entrepreneurship orientations, who are considered difficult to retain (Schein, 1985) may yet be offered suitable careers. Atterhed (1985) suggests that entrepreneurs no longer have to leave companies in order to satisfy their creative desires. The concept of ‘intrapreneurship’, which combines the role of employee and entrepreneur, has arisen in recent years; a concept which provides the company with an opportunity to capitalize on the imagination and ideas of the entrepreneurs, and the entrepreneurs to exercise their creative talent without the risk they would normally face on their own. Kanter (1989) suggests that intrapreneurs can be offered a reward system structured around the performance of their product, which allows them to satisfy their need to make money, whilst showing the world what they have achieved on their own (Schein, 1985).

It is hoped that the insights gained from this study will improve our understanding of career management of IS personnel and some of the differences between the career orientations of IS personnel in South Africa and the USA. However, the study reported here has a number of limitations. First, the nature of this study precludes any notion of causality. At best, career orientation and perceptions of reward activities and work-related outcome variables should be interpreted as correlated instead of causes of career strategy. Future research using a longitudinal methodology may be able to address the issue of causality. Second, in view of the diversity of career orientations and the ineffectiveness of the dual career ladder, future research may need to examine the techniques used to motivate IS personnel with different career orientations.

Third, several studies have suggested that career issues and problems vary by career stage (Dalton et al, 1977; Greenhaus and Callanan, 1994). Future studies using a longitudinal methodology may need to examine the circumstances under which rewards are used and by whom at the different career stages. Fourth, Igbaria et al (1991) examined the fit and misfit between career orientations (technical and managerial) and job types. This needs to be re-examined by examining the fit between all the career orientations, and the rewards and the activities an individual performs. Fifth, further research should examine other facets of the work situation, to ensure that the difference in career outcomes between matched and mismatched individuals is not attributable to other factors. Considerable work has been performed on other issues pertaining to career satisfaction, and questions regarding role conflict and ambiguity (Miles and Perrault, 1976; Baroudi, 1985); satisfaction with supervisor support (Greenhaus et al, 1990), stress (Parasuraman, 1982); likelihood of additional promotion (Veiga, 1981); and career stages (Dalton et al, 1977; Mount, 1984); might be added to future surveys to ensure that other important factors that may influence job and career satisfaction are not overlooked.

Lastly, the results of this study may be unique to the geographical area in which the study was conducted, and similar research conducted in other parts of the country would be of interest to establish whether these results are relevant to other areas or not. In addition, the sample chosen for this study comprised members of one association, the CSSA. As the CSSA is not a body which IS professionals are required to join, it is possible that the sample is biased towards those who voluntarily join a professional organization, for reasons unknown to this study. However, at the time of this research, the CSSA was believed to be the most representative organization of IS professionals in South Africa. Notwithstanding this fact, further empirical research which examines individuals from other organizations would be useful. Also, while this study had specific reasons for limiting the age of its sample, further research may wish to extend the age limit so that those over the age of 44 are included. This may indicate whether the prevalence of the lifestyle orientation is a result of the new work ethic and changing workforce, as suggested, and is, therefore, less extensive among older employees.

## Conclusions

The results of this study provided interesting insights into the career management of IS employees. Peter Keen (1991) states ‘the pace and effectiveness of business innovation through IT depends on people rather than technology’ (p119). Therefore, it is imperative that firms have strategies for managing the human side as well as the technical side of IT. Organizations need to take certain actions to facilitate the employee’s career development and to recognize that employees have different forms of orientation towards work. These orientations are the basis for career choices, since people are likely to make job and organizational selections that are consistent with their own orientations.

This paper supports the notion that the dual career ladder is limited and is not an effective device for managing IS personnel. The majority of IS personnel in the study did not score highly on the technical competence orientation. The findings of this paper reveal a rich diversity of career orientations held by IS employees. Security, services and lifestyle orientations are the most prevalent career orientation among IS employees. The results also demonstrated that gender is related to managerial competence and job security and IS employees in managerial positions scored higher on managerial competence and job security, but they scored lower on lifestyle than those in professional positions. In addition, significant correlations between some of the career orientations and individual and work-related outcome variables were identified.

Organizations should be responsive to the career development needs of all employees. A variety of approaches is being used to retain the most qualified IS employees. Companies need to provide a balance between employees' professional and personal lives. Computerworld (1995) reported that some companies need to provide on-site day care, exercise facilities or a fully equipped home office in order to keep and sign up the best. For example, Barnett Technologies provides their employees with easy access to YMCA, a company-owned outdoor jogging track, and an on-site health care facility. Other companies, such as Microsoft, also helps employees to create a balance between work and family obligations.
