# Information flows amongst executives: their implications for systems development

Frederic Adam and Ciaran Murphy

Executive Systems Research Centre, University College Cork, Western Road, Cork, Ireland

Senior managers have tended to resist the incursion into their personal domain of computer systems meant for their use. Their main criticism is that technical solutions are being imposed on them without an adequate analysis of the problems at hand. This suggests that the way in which executives obtain and exchange information may not be adequately understood. With the help of a framework designed to identify top executives' networks of information flows, the study reported in this paper analysed the information practices of 16 executives from four organizations. The findings of the research indicated that executives use a combination of communication flows and information flows in a proportion which varies depending upon the context of their different activities. It also revealed that executives initiated information and communication flows of a different nature depending upon the role they play and the level of those with whom they deal within the organization. The results of the study suggest that very specific approaches are needed when identifying executives' needs in terms of developing systems aimed at supporting top managers' strategic activities.

Keywords: information flows, communication, decision making, executive support systems, strategic information

Over the past two decades, the emphasis in information systems design has shifted from systems that merely process vast amounts of data and shed some light on narrow areas of the organization towards systems capable of providing managers with the strategic information they really require (DeLong and Rockart, 1984; Galliers, 1987; Rockart and DeLong, 1988a, 1988b; Business Intelligence, 1990). But, information systems for managers raise specific problems about the nature of managerial work itself, as they are intended to tackle the needs of senior users whose most important role is to create ‘a vision of the future of the company and to lead the company towards it’ (King, 1985). Despite many years of research and many attempts to develop systems for executive use, little is known about the way computers could, and should, support such unstructured tasks (Ackoff, 1967; Bjorn-Andersen et al, 1986; Eisenhardt, 1990; Economic Intelligence Unit, 1991; Carlsson, 1993).

So far, senior managers have tended to resist the incursion into their personal domain of computer systems meant for their use. Their main criticism is that technical solutions are being imposed on them without an adequate analysis of the problems at hand. Computer systems are perceived as a source of information overload or, at least, as being unable to increase the capacity and efficiency of managers' information processing (King, 1985; Rockart and DeLong, 1988a; Earl, 1989). This is important because, unlike many other computer users, managers frequently have a choice over their dependence on and use of the computer. If the system does not provide them with timely, accurate and relevant information to help their strategic decision making, then they will use alternative sources or even make decisions without detailed information (Bjorn-Andersen et al., 1986). This suggests that the technological determinism represented by the simple introduction of such systems as decision support systems and executive information systems is doomed to failure unless we develop a richer and more comprehensive understanding of the role of information and communication in the work of executives and the rules or criteria which they use to build up their information procurement and transmission strategies.

Several management researchers have called for a more extensive study of the role of information in the work of executives and the circulation of this information between them and the rest of the organization (Mintzberg, 1976; Kiesler and Sproull, 1982; Galliers, 1987; Jones et al., 1988). These researchers have suggested that executives' work is busy, very fragmented and based more on intuition than on rationality. The ideal vision presented by the classical theory of management, of the planners in their offices reflecting on the future of their company behind the protection of closed doors, has been abandoned (Carlson, 1951; Stewart, 1967; Mintzberg, 1973, 1975, 1976). Carlson, Stewart and Mintzberg have shown how important information is to the work of executives. Mintzberg used the metaphor of 'the executive as an information processor' to represent managers' contribution to their organization's information processing (Mintzberg, 1973).

The studies of Swanson or Feldman and March also show that the role of information in the work of top management is more complex than originally described (Swanson, 1978, 1987, 1992; Feldman and March, 1981). They claim that information must be regarded as a vector of power and influence and that there is a hidden cost in any transmission of information which is borne by the information providers of the organization (Weick, 1983). Very complex relationships exist between the managers who provide information and the managers who need that information, each executive informing and being informed at different times.

Drucker distinguished between communication — where perception and sharing have primacy — and information which is specific and based on the principle of economy: the fewer the data needed, the better the information (Drucker, 1970). He indicated that ‘the more it [the information] can be freed of the human components . . . the more valid and reliable does it become. Indeed, it becomes increasingly informative’, while ‘the most perfect communications may be purely shared experiences, without any logic whatever’ (Drucker, 1970). Drucker argues that information presupposes communication and that the effectiveness of the information process depends on our ability to communicate. He stated that, ‘in the absence of effective communication, the information revolution cannot really produce information. All it can produce is data' (Drucker, 1970). This distinction between information and communication must be linked back to the different roles these two types of interpersonal interaction play in the work of executives and must be reflected in the channels offered by an information system for executives for the transfer and the exchange of information.

A number of studies have already been carried out on the nature of the communication practices of executives. One observer of executives, Hensey (1986), claimed that such communication behaviour as the consultation of colleagues, superiors and others is the mark of successful managers (as opposed to other behaviours such as the reliance on reports, graphs and printouts). Swanson hypothesized that information accessibility would increase with (1) increased quality of the sources and the channels used, (2) increased ease of access for the user of the information to these sources and channels and (3) greater degree of familiarity of the user with the sources of information (Swanson, 1992).

Kefalas (1973), Keegan (1974), Daft et al (1988) and Jones et al (1988) carried out extensive studies of environment scanning by executives and on the information media and sources used by them. These studies indicate that executives' usage of communication channels is linked to such situational factors as the equivocality or uncertainty of the problems they are dealing with (Daft and Lengel, 1984), their hierarchical level (Jones et al, 1988), the performance of their organization (Daft et al, 1988) and the competitiveness of the environment in which their organizations operate (Kefalas, 1973). McLeod, Jones and Poitevent concluded that managers spend their entire career trying to construct and maintain an overall information system — 'the key to personal, and firm, success' (McLeod et al, 1984). Eisenhardt carried out further research into this issue and identified that successful managers frequently rely on the advice of one or two experienced counsellors, who have extensive knowledge of their industry or of their area and can give advice on strategic matters (Eisenhardt, 1990).

These findings indicate that the issue of information transfer and communication among executives is difficult for the managers themselves and may be out of reach for the designers of information systems for executives at this point in time. The precise characteristics of the information flows that executives maintain with the structure they belong to, both as information consumers and information providers, need to be understood. A way must be found to represent and to analyse some ‘living examples’ of executives’ information networks as described by management researchers (Kefalas, 1973; Mintzberg, 1973; Daft and Lengel, 1984, 1986; McLeod et al, 1984; Daft et al, 1988). The relationships which executives have with the other members of their organization must be categorized and a theoretical background for information networks analysis must be built up to enable researchers to draw guidelines for the development of systems for executives. Part of this research will involve identifying the specific missions that such systems could have and which would respect and support executives’ information habits. In a recent paper, Carlsson attempted to describe how communication and information technologies (CIT) can be used to design and build systems to support executive teams (Carlsson, 1993), but he restricted his research to small medium enterprises (SMEs) in high velocity environments. We try to provide generic guidelines that will be useful for the development of systems that support the work of top managers in all organizations. This can only be achieved if the context of the tasks which the system has been assigned to tackle has been understood and that the ways to support and improve executives’ information practices have been conceptualized, which this research attempts to do.

## The study

This research, therefore, was aimed at investigating the characteristics of the information flows that link top executives to their environment both inside and outside their organization. To achieve this goal, the major information and communication flows used by executives involved in the development of their organization's strategy had to be identified rigorously and analysed with the help of a methodology which would allow the collection and analysis of relevant data.

In particular, we wanted to differentiate between information flows, whereby one provider informs a recipient, and communication flows, whereby executives share information on an equal basis. Our underlying objective was to identify those aspects of executives' informational habits which should be considered when trying to help them acquire their information and share their understanding of the world with their colleagues.

This research goal was broken down into several operational objectives:

\- identification of the major information and communication flows used by the executives, either as providers or recipients of information, and the channels used for these transfers;

\- the development of metrics to enable the quantitative analysis of the patterns of information and communication flows used by executives;

\- an assessment of the overall quality of the relationships developed between senior executives, to try to define a number of information roles which these executives played in their organization;

\- the drawing of implications for the design of systems for executives.

These objectives required the researchers to create a link with the living objects of the study, ie the executives. This was accomplished by targeting four companies and conducting in-depth interviews with four executives in each of them. Thirteen of the executives were executive directors of their companies and all were heads of functions. As such, they were involved in the strategic decision making of their organizations. Because of the need to explore with the interviewees some issues that they had not articulated previously, it was necessary to hold long interviews (2.3 hours on average). We coined the term ‘Deep Survey’ to reflect that our study was more concerned with the depth of the analysis than with the width of the sample. This may limit the generalizability of our findings but it enabled us to create much tighter relationships with the executives interviewed and to learn more about their information habits — a factor essential for our study.

In conducting the interviews, use was made of an indicative questionnaire. This served primarily to ensure that the same questions were asked of all interviewees. All issues were raised in an open ended way, allowing the researchers to obtain amplification or clarification from the respondents. During the interviews, use was also made of a specially developed map of the environment of each executive, to focus on the information flows between the executives studied and their subordinates, peers and superiors (Figure 1). These maps sought to determine the number and the nature of information and communication flows that executives dealt with and the channels used for these flows. The distinction between the categories of flows was made by the researchers on the basis of the account given by the interviewees and their description of the interactions they used.

## Findings of the research

## Characteristics of the flows amongst executives

The exploration of the maps describing the networks of relations obtained during the interviews provided data on the pattern of linkages that executives establish with their environment. Based on the links drawn on the maps and the explanation given by the interviewees, it was possible to quantify the extent of the transfer of information between executives and to formulate a judgement on the organizational roles played by executives in these information relationships. Most of the links could be matched in pairs and revealed two-way relationships between executives. However, executives were found to make use of a small number of one-way links (approximately 20 flows out of the 186 flows used by all the executives interviewed; or 11 per cent) and these were primarily concerned with external information gathered through such media as newsletters, journals or books. The executives explained that scanning the environment via non-personal sources (ie books, journals, newsletters etc) was a necessity for their jobs (Kefalas, 1973). Eisenhardt (1990) also identified this characteristic of successful executives who collect and exchange a lot of real-time external information.

## Quantitative analysis of the relationships — information in-flows, out-flows and communication flows

The study measured the number of information in-flows (where the executive is the receiver of information), the number of information out-flows (where the executive is the transmitter of information) and the number of communication flows (where two or more executives are sharing their understanding and it is not meaningful to attempt to determine who originates the exchange) that executives were using with their superiors, peers and subordinates. The results are presented in Figure 2.

These show that the three directions in which executives can spread their interaction networks are developed to similar degrees — ie 34 per cent of the flows used by executives link them with their superiors, 28 per cent of the flows link them with their peers and 38 per cent link them with their subordinates. Prima facie, this suggests the existence of an ‘Information Equilibrium’ where executives use a similar proportion of their flows to correspond with their superiors, peers and subordinates. However, a closer analysis of the results indicates that the distribution of the flows between in-flows, out-flows and communications flows is different depending upon the direction of the link. In particular, the ratio of communication flows to information flows (in-flows and out-flows) varies from 0.29 for links with subordinates to 0.48 for links with superiors and 0.57 for links with peers. This variation can be seen clearly when the flows are presented in graphical format, as shown in Figure 2. This highlights the fact that the relationships between executives at different levels are of a different nature and accordingly require different modes of information exchange — eg communication in one instance versus information in other instances.

Additional observations can be made on the nature of the relationships established between the different managerial levels. As can be observed in Figure 3a and b, the pattern of relations which executives have with their superiors in other departments is similar to their pattern of relations with subordinates in those departments (Figure 3a: diagonal flows). This pattern shows that downward flows represent only a small proportion of all flows (20 per cent), whereas information flows going upwards — mainly formal reporting — account for nearly 60 per cent. In addition, the proportion of communication flows is also small — approximately 20 per cent. In summary, the ‘diagonal’ relationships in the companies studied are characterized by a lot of formal reporting but little interaction taking place. This empirical evidence of the lack of interaction, coupled with a high degree of mechanistic reporting, may help to explain the claims of previous researchers that senior executives suffer from information overload as subordinates keep sending reports upwards almost ritualistically, without having the opportunity to verify how useful these reports are or how they could be improved.

By comparison, the pattern of relations between executives and their direct superiors (within the same department), on the one hand, and between them and their direct subordinates, on the other hand, are very dissimilar (Figure 3b: vertical flows). While the number of upward information flows is almost the same for both kinds of interaction, the number of communication flows and downward information flows is very different. The senior executives interviewed in the study view themselves as communicating more with their superiors than with their subordinates and send three times as much information to their subordinates as they receive from their superiors (as highlighted in the areas circled on Figure 3b).

These results quantify the common assertion of previous researchers — that executives use more communication the higher they are in the hierarchy of an organization (Mintzberg, 1975; Daft et al, 1987). Significantly, this study shows that this only applies within a given functional area of an organization or at a peer to peer level. This does not apply when executives are dealing with other executives, be they superiors or subordinates, in other departments.

This difference in pattern, whereby communication flows seem to replace one-way flows, is partially explained by an analysis of the organizational context in which executives share information. The executives interviewed appeared to have a preference for communication when the context of their query was not well understood, when there was no clear agreement on or vision of an organizational problem or, more often, when the situation was uncertain. One company operated in a market where volumes could vary by over 50 per cent from one week to another and planning required very frequent brain-storming to establish ‘educated guesses’ as to what the trend was going to be like. Conversely, they seemed to prefer information flows when dealing with more clearly understood and better documented problems — ie when routine (known and obtainable) information sufficed. Another company operated in an environment where a limited number of large competitive contracts was offered every year, of which they could expect to receive 30 per cent. Contracts were known about well in advance. Thus, the transfer of information in this organization involved highly standardized forms and status reports which were sent automatically according to specified rules. This finding is in agreement with our previous finding regarding the importance of communication between executives and their superiors at the highest levels of organizations. The higher one goes within functional areas, the greater the extent to which executives need communication to deal with the complexity involved in building up common interpretations and in elaborating their company’s strategy. At the top of organizations, executives rely much more on communication in reaching their final decisions. In her study of strategic decision making in fast changing environments, Eisenhardt (1990) found that executives develop close advisory relationships with more experienced colleagues. These relationships increased executives' confidence in making difficult choices, a finding confirmed in our study.

## Quality of the relationships — information roles played by executives

The nature of the ‘two-way information relationships’ described by the executives interviewed could vary considerably, depending upon the organizational role played by the executive. For example, ‘information providers’ generated a flow of prepared documents and then received feed-back or additional queries from their ‘client’ (eg the Financial Controller to the Finance Director). However, in quantitative terms, the structure of in-flows and out-flows seemed to be similar for both executives, each of them being source and target within the relationship. In qualitative terms, one executive played the role of information provider while the other was an information user and they did not share information on an equal basis. This type of relationship, when identified in the study, was labelled as an ‘asymmetrical’ relationship. The relationship where executives were exchanging information on an equal basis was labelled ‘symmetrical’.

Based on this dual classification of information transfer, the 15 executives (one executive could not be classified due to lack of data) were classified into one of three categories, depending upon the type of relationship they maintained with all the players in their organization (their subordinates, their peers and their superiors). These categories, (1) top level decision maker (five executives), (2) specialized decision maker (five executives) and (3) expert (five executives) reflected the informational role which each executive played in the management processes of his company as explained by our Window of Information Relationships of Executives (see Figure 4).

The ‘top level decision makers’ were the four Finance Directors and the Purchasing Director of Company C. The role of these executives was to take part directly in the strategic decision making process at the highest level of the organization. They all maintained symmetrical relationships with their peers and their chief executive officer (CEO), mainly based on communication either formalized (board meetings) or informal (unscheduled meetings). By contrast, these executives maintained asymmetrical relationships with their subordinates, whom they primarily used as information providers to help build up their knowledge of the business in a variety of ways so as to enrich their contribution to the discussions about the general strategy for the firm.

Figure 4 Window of Information Relationships of Executives

The ‘specialized decision makers’ were the two Sales Directors, the Human Resources Director, the Production Manager and the Technical Specialist of Company D. They maintained asymmetrical relationships with their CEO, to whom they reported directly in a formalized and compulsory manner — the sign of an asymmetrical relationship. Their contribution to the company’s operations was more like the contribution of a specialist who has unique knowledge and understanding of a sector, even though the scope of their responsibilities was broader than that of a mere specialist. As a result, they engaged in the same amount of boundary-spanning relationships with their peers and communicated with them like the executives in the first group. They also maintained asymmetrical relationships with their subordinates. These specialized decision makers were the ‘counsellors’ identified by Eisenhardt in her study of strategic decision makers (Eisenhardt, 1990). The very specific demographic profile of the ‘counsellors’ revealed in her study was not totally confirmed here (two of the executives in this group were quite young), but some of the specialized decision makers we identified would have fitted Eisenhardt’s ‘best manager in the team’ label.

The ‘experts’ were involved solely in asymmetrical relationships; they were the two IT Directors, the two Financial Controllers and the Training Officer. They maintained asymmetrical relationships with all the players in their organization and provided information to the other directors, either in a spontaneous fashion or on request. They were the technical specialists who contributed to their company mainly by keeping their colleagues abreast of what was happening externally and internally, either in their field of responsibility (Training Officer, IT Directors) or at the level of the whole organization (Finance Controllers), with the view of keeping the organization up to date from a technological or organizational point of view. They advised the most senior executives on the important decisions facing their organization. This was their way of integrating their expertise into the organization (Webb and Cleary, 1994).

## Institutionalization and emergent links

Our study also found that executives, when confronted with situations involving varying degrees of complexity, made use of two major classes of links:

1. Institutionalized links — which executives used to obtain routine information relating to the better understood aspects of their roles. In this case, they relied mostly on formal channels because these adequately supported the transfer of such information. Special committees, scheduled reporting and other compulsory mechanisms were examples of these links.

2. Emergent or evolving links — which were links that executives used for a particular project or problem. While there was no doubt that these links were appropriate in the context of the current queries, executives needed to establish whether they would be useful for future projects and problems and whether they should be maintained.

Monge and Eisenberg identified that all organizations are based on both formal and emergent networks, which coexist and can be best understood in the context of each other. They explained that the degree to which one type of network (formal or emergent) dominates is a function of the position of an organization in its evolutionary life cycle (Monge and Eisenberg, 1987).

In our study, executives were involved in much more institutionalized links (74 per cent) than emergent links (26 per cent). However, this overall figure masks a fundamental difference. The relationships between executives and their superiors and subordinates were dominated by institutionalized links (82 per cent), whereas, in the relationships between the executives and their peers, emergent links (50 per cent) were as numerous as institutionalized links (50 per cent). Managers had no difficulty in giving examples of processes that had moved from being emergent to being institutionalized. This suggests that processes within functional areas in organizations have an evolutionary life cycle, which takes them from starting as emergent processes to becoming formal established procedures.

Interestingly, while horizontal links were as likely to be emergent as institutionalized, all of the executives interviewed perceived the horizontal linkages as being particularly important. The Production Director of Company A was the most specific on this point. He stated: 'Horizontal communication is an important factor of performance for a company because, if vertical communication allows greater control of the operations, it is the horizontal communication that speeds up the decision-making process. In today's very competitive markets, this speed makes the difference between the very successful companies and others'. This was identified by Daft and Lengel as very important because: 'organisational information processing is more than what occurs by individuals', the difference being that while individuals merely try to interpret data in response to a problem, organizations try to work out a common understanding and 'converge toward a similar interpretation' (Daft and Lengel, 1986). In addition, this type of 'balanced' power structure, whereby people have the opportunities to work as teams and share ideas and opinions, is the key to the empowerment of the management team (Eisenhardt and Bourgeois, 1990; Carlsson, 1993).

Furthermore, executives in two of the four companies acknowledged that the lack of this type of horizontal exchange amongst them constituted a potential problem for their organizations, which management attempted to solve by focusing the attention of all executives on the importance of maintaining open and efficient communication channels. In this context, it was significant that horizontal relationships were based more on emergent links (50 per cent) than any other type of relationships. It is worth noting that this percentage is achieved despite the reliance of nearly all executives on one form of institutionalized horizontal communication: board meetings.

## Implication for the design of information systems

The results of the study have consequences for the features and functionalities of information systems for executives. The first finding is that executives are not a homogeneous grouping in terms of their information and communication needs. Some always receive and send messages of the same nature in information content and form and require highly customized procedures to serve their needs, whereas other executives require multi-purpose systems to accommodate the sending and receiving of different types of messages and information flows. Some are primarily involved in communication relationships, others specialize in informing their colleagues, while most of them, at least at board level, are involved in both activities to various degrees.

This has far-reaching implications in terms of capabilities that need to be integrated into systems designed for executives. Some executives will require efficient presentation tools to allow them to build compound documents (that they can print as well as simply display on screen). Others will need the capability to search the corporate database seamlessly and integrate a variety of indicators into the documents they create. All of them need their communication to be supported and enhanced. This suggests that information systems for executives should not

simply be ‘information providers’, but ‘information transmitters’, which could be used as links between the categories of executives described above. This approach represents a shift in emphasis from the traditional literature on executive information systems, which has often regarded the fact that an information system contains new, unknown information as its most important attribute (Daft and Lengel, 1986; Rockart and DeLong, 1988b). The provision of an information transmission tool catering for the exchange of different types of information would facilitate the acceleration and consolidation of the overall circulation of information within an organization. The requests for information and the resulting reports would come through the same medium, reaching more than one information provider as well as more than just the executive who made the initial request. The other advantage of such systems would be that their technical management could be left to the people who are best at it — the IT department, whereas the information content would be directly in the hands of the people interested — the executives.

Our findings concerning the two types of links (emergent and institutionalized) which executives use require a study of the potential missions of an executive system. Emergent links, where executives rely on communication and creativity, cannot be approached in the same manner as institutionalized links where executives rely more on existing formal channels. Analysts and managers, who are involved in the development of systems meant to link executives with each other, must be aware of the different types of co-operation which exist between executives and must be capable of identifying the types of interdependence amongst them.

Having analysed the different situations which we encountered in our study, we developed a Window of Missions for Executive Systems (defined as systems aimed to meet the information and communication needs of executives) which describes the approach which should be taken when contemplating the development of information systems for executives. This window is presented in Figure 5.

In the first quadrant, the goal of the system is merely to stabilize the information network and improve the speed and ease of use of the existing links. Institutionalized links, because they are well documented, and existing reports, because they are easily available, are automated and computerized so as to increase the reliability of these vital transfers. The system is likely to help managers relay internal and external real time information to each other, thereby speeding up decision making (Eisenhardt, 1990).

Figure 5 Window of Missions for Executives' Systems

In the second quadrant, the institutionalized network must be reengineered to change the manner in which business is done. This requires a much higher degree of co-operation from executives and involves experimentation with new technologies, new communication channels and new media.

In the third quadrant, the information systems are used to formalize the links and information flows which have developed in a spontaneous fashion. Emergent management practices are well documented, often for the first time, and an attempt is made to organize and schedule some form of reporting.

In the fourth quadrant, emergent relationships are taken one step further, by a strong emphasis on developing communication with new nodes in the organization and developing new links amongst the nodes. Experimentation is also used to suggest new ways to transfer information. For example, managers have the opportunity to interact with new experts they never used before.

The study overall suggests that a personal analysis of the needs of each executive might be necessary, as opposed to a general definition of requirements in any organization, when developing information systems for top executives. This confirms the need for the availability (and the design) of different sets of features for such systems and also additional ‘personal’ functions, to suit the different roles which are played by executives. Thus, developers of computer systems for executives should consider the following features:

\- each executive's dashboard, or any pro-forma report that he or she uses to control the activities relevant to his or her job in the company, should be automated and integrated into their systems. This was also put forward by Carlsson (1993) as helping managers to resolve the first paradox of strategic decision making: ‘a quick yet rational strategic decision process’ (Bourgeois and Eisenhardt, 1988);

\- a facility, that allows each executive to initiate debates on the issues linked to the company's operations that currently require attention, or will require attention, by forwarding any type of material to any colleague in the organization, should be available to the groups of managers who need to co-operate on certain issues;

\- a specially designed interface, where a system would be more of an ‘information transmitter’, should allow executives to replicate the information flows they have with the rest of the company (symmetrical or asymmetrical);

\- an all-purpose email facility would help managers to maintain a direct link with those in the organization who do not have easy access to them (eg the workers). It would also reduce the flow of unwanted messages within the specific discussion spaces reserved for certain topics. According to Carlsson (1993), this would be very useful to improve co-ordination and communication in managerial terms by speeding up communication and the execution of decision.

Hiltz and Turoff noted that it might be possible to identify a limited number of common roles within an organization and to use them to structure the flows of communication and information within a system, each executive having the possibility to play all or a set of the roles as a user of that system (Hiltz and Turoff, 1985). Our classification of executives as top-level decision makers, specialized decision makers or experts can provide a basis for such an analysis of common organizational roles. This, in turn, could identify specific sets of functionalities for different types of executives and, as suggested by Hitz and Turoff, may help to route communication into topic-oriented and role-specific discussion spaces to avoid overload and ‘facilitate the emergence of on-line social groups with strong norms’ (Hiltz and Turoff, 1985), ie institutionalize the usage of communication and information exchange systems amongst executives.

## Conclusion

Our research has demonstrated that it was both meaningful and useful to study the information flows that executives use in the context of their organizations to exchange information with their subordinates, peers and superiors and to co-ordinate their actions with those of their colleagues. Our mapping technique proved successful in highlighting the most important aspects of the relationships which exist amongst executives and the different roles which executives play in the information processing of their organizations.

As was shown in Figure 3a and b, the patterns of diagonal flow appear to be consistent upwards and downwards, whereas the analysis of the vertical flows — which exist within departments — indicates that the balance between in-flows, out-flows and communication flows changes with the hierarchical level. This requires further research to attempt to generalize this finding and to provide explanations as to why executives only increase their usage of communication within functional areas and with their peers and use mostly simple information flows with executives in other departments.

The study also enabled the identification of the different roles which executives play in the information processing of their organizations. Executives were found to be either experts who are involved mostly in asymmetrical relationships and are net information providers, specialized decision makers whose contribution to the company's operations is based on their understanding of a sector of the organization, or top-level decision makers who build up their knowledge in a wide variety of areas with the support of other managers in their organization to develop the strategy for the organization. This classification of executives can provide a basis for the analysis of the common organizational roles of executives and hence give a better insight into the role of communication and the transfer of information within an organization.

In terms of information systems designed to tackle the issues dealt with in this study, efforts have so far exclusively been made to meet the needs of executives by developing information systems per se — ie systems that concentrate on the asymmetrical dissemination of information. Our study has shown that this represented only one-third of the relationships used by executives involved in the strategic decision making at the highest level in their organizations. This results in most of the needs of senior managers being ignored by information systems specialists and may explain the high failure rate to date of systems such as executive information systems (Ackoff, 1967; King, 1985; Business Intelligence, 1990; Economic Intelligence Unit, 1991).

In the light of the above findings, there is a need for further research in the area of executive support. The framework developed in this study has proved useful in analysing the role of information and communication in the work of executives. Future research should concentrate on further unpacking the role of information transfer and communication amongst executives. This, in turn, will help developers of executive systems to address more helpfully the needs of their executives.
