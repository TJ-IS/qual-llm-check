# Reconsidering reengineering

Keynote Talk at the IFIP TC-8 Joint Meeting, Bond University, Gold Coast, Australia, May 1994\*

J L King

Center for Research on Information Technology and Organization (CRITO), University of California, Irvine, California 92717, USA

My purpose is ostensibly to consider the role of government and changing public–private interactions in the realm of technical and social change, all within the context of business process reengineering, or BPR. In the process I'll suggest that the current roster of IFIP TC-8 interest groups itself represents a retrograde concept of information technology (IT) and organizations that will soon collapse, especially if fundamental changes to organization and institutional life such as the retreat of hierarchical coordination in favour of market coordination come to pass. Others have touched on the basic IT issues, and on some organizational concerns. There are similar generic issues facing government organizations. I won't focus on either. Instead, I will focus on the question of direction finding in shaping our future. The idea of BPR is fundamentally about shaping the future, so there is in my talk a connection to the conference, whatever you might think.

The fall of Marxism is the best thing that ever happened to Karl Marx. Those of us who wish to understand the relation between technical and social change must start with Marx because he alone among 19th century socio-economists recognized the centrality of technical change to economic growth. Let me begin my talk with a quote from Marx: 'Men make their future, but they do not make it exactly as they please'. The challenge before us if we embrace BPR is the choice between two starkly different points of view inherent in Marx's aphorism. One is that we make our future. This is the obvious point of BPR that bears repeating. The choices we make matter, and we probably will do better if we think through what we want before we act. This is the good side of the BPR fad — it mobilizes thoughtful action. The other is the mythology of many BPR accounts that we can make the future exactly as we please. This pernicious idea is shown in the very language of the BPR noun clause: reengineering implies prior engineering. Were our existing organizations and institutions engineered? If so, by whom, and on whose authority?

Mats Lundberg has argued that we do not engineer, but rather that we shape the world. This is a process view that fits Marx's first message. It's a good step, and makes us ask who needs BPR? But it doesn't solve the problem of how we want to shape our world, and what happens when the actual shape we make is not exactly what we had in mind. Two specific concerns arise when we wish to ask what we want the world to be: scope and control. Most of what I have to say in my remaining time concerns these two words.

If we are going to do as Mats says, and expand our purpose to a larger context, we need to ask, how large do we go? The minute we do so we are in danger of being pulled into the black hole of General System Theory. Everything is connected to everything else. You cannot grasp everything. Where do you draw the line? We have to make the action space bigger, but not too big. We assume we can do this, and the previous speakers have attempted to give us suggestions as to how we might do so. Mats says we should expand IS developments to ‘big processes’ and concentrate on the overall ‘pay off’ Niels Bjørn-Andersen says we have to look beyond the organization to interorganizational activities. Ralph Sprague says we must concern ourselves with the growing importance of electronic documents. Doug Vogel says we must anticipate the effects of electronic meetings. But regardless of where we settle, we face the crucial question of how we deal with this expanded space.

The lunatic fringe of BPR think that this space can be understood on our terms. Not so: we have to understand this space on its terms. To do so, we have to expand our intellectual tool base beyond system building as we currently know it. We must embrace the exploratory and analytical tools pioneered by our colleagues in sociology, economics, political science, anthropology and psychology — a message well known to many information systems researchers. The challenge goes well beyond this, however. The new space we must occupy is not a static space. Regardless of what methods we use, the world we investigate through this space is changing even as we study it. And as important, the whole idea behind our embracing the broader space is that we will change it. If we don’t change it, why bother with BPR? Thus, we face the dilemma that the study of the future forces us into the facts of the present, but these very facts can be expected to ‘change’ as the future unfolds, and with them, all our alternative futures. Drawing a boundary around the space we should explore can seldom be more than a hit-or-miss proposition.

If we are going to follow the lead demanded by BPR and suggested by my colleagues, we have to change not only what we do, but what we are, specifically with respect to our point of view. This is not as easy as it sounds because of the problem of control. BPR in what I call its lunatic form embraces a fascist illusion that the power of will can produce a society by design. If we have learned anything this century, it is that over-emphasis on control leads inevitably to catastrophic consequences. I believe that key elements of the BPR ideology run the risk of over-emphasizing control. The risk shows up in the two terms ‘integration’ and ‘sustainable competitive advantage’, both of which have been used by previous speakers. These terms are not problematic in themselves — both of them articulate understandable preferences for organizations. But without understanding the inherent contradictions that arise when these preferences encounter the world as it is, the bias towards control gets out of hand. Let me explain what I mean in both

instances.

Integration depends on recognizing the components of process that involve numerous actors and tasks. The key to integration as it is used in BPR is taking action to enforce and control the inter-dependencies among the components. The goal is to drive out variance and chance. But in a world that itself is full of variance and chance, this strategy is self-defeating. Integration makes each part of a process so dependent on the processes upstream and downstream that failures in any of the integration controls brings the entire process down. Engineered processes depend on the logic of their design. They fail two ways: when components fail (an easy problem to fix), and when the logic of design no longer fits the context in which the process must function.

We have heard about BPR being used to reduce long product lead times. Long product lead times are themselves the result of process integration. When we hear that the solution to this problem is more integration, we have to wonder what is going on. In fact, if you look carefully at what organizations have been doing recently to solve this problem, you see that the solution is dis-integration! By breaking down those interdependencies to facilitate greater flexibility within the components of production, and placing greater reliance on locally negotiated coordination by the components where they interconnect, the problems of long product lead times can be shortened. This kind of dis-integration is behind just-in-time (JIT), concurrent engineering, total quality management (TQM), and most of the other BPR strategies that actually seem to help. In contrast, the salient feature of the one major BPR strategy that has generally failed — manufacturing resource planning — is that it really tries to integrate business processes in the traditional sense of term, and in the way that most BPR pundits imply.

At this point I want to tell you an instructive story. For many years, Ken Kraemer and I did research on computerization in city governments. We were particularly interested in experiments with so-called ‘integrated municipal information systems’. Most people find studies of local government pretty boring, and our studies were no exception. In fact, the only people who were ever really interested in our work were the people in GOSPLAN — the Soviet central economics planning agency. Their world view was, quite simply, business process engineering gone insane. They knew their system was failing, and they badly wanted to ‘reengineer’ it. They hoped that the discoveries in the US experiments with integrated systems would help them succeed at their impossible task. When the Soviet Union collapsed, Ken and I lost our only truly dedicated audience. But of course, in the spirit of the conservative politics, we now say that it was our part in the Cold War, and that just like Star Wars, our job was to pretend this hare-brained integrated management information systems (IMIS) scheme would work and thereby force the Soviets to renew their commitment to a failing course of action.

Now, you might think that I am using ‘integration’ in the old-fashioned narrow sense of the term, when what is meant is the new, liberated meaning in which business processes become, not integrated, but integrated-ish. To that I say, if we don’t pay attention to the meaning of the words we use, we really aren’t going to be able to sort out the pieces of this expanded action space that everyone insists we must explore. Integration is an ongoing problematic for organizations, and organizations drift from one side of the integration spectrum to the other as conditions change. Integration is not the goal — integration is a valve that we open or close to reach specific goals in specific contexts.

Let me turn to sustainable competitive advantage. This is a meaningful preference for individual companies, but it is profoundly troublesome if we want to see our organizations in the context of the world, as we must, for example, if we think interorganizational systems are important. What's wrong with sustainable competitive advantage? It's an oxymoron under the assumptions of democratic capitalism. You probably know that Karl Marx thought capitalism would fail. What many of you probably don't know is that Adam Smith thought so, too. So did Schumpeter, although he probably came closer than any economist this century in explaining why capitalism has been so successful for so long.

The dilemma beneath these dismal views of capitalism is the inherent logic of the business cycle — a problem not yet sorted out. The detailed explanation of this is too intricate to handle here, but let me illustrate one piece of it, viz sustainable competitive advantage. The world of competition exists in the turbulent channel between two treacherous shores. One is the catastrophe of perfect competition, which under micro-economic theory produces a state of zero profits. Zero profits destroys competition by destroying incentive. The other shore is monopoly or its common cousin, oligopoly, which destroys incentive by destroying competition. If we build systems to produce sustainable competitive advantage, towards which shore are we turning? I guarantee you that sustainable competitive advantage is not a strategy that will steer a course towards the centre of the channel.

This is the central problematic of BPR's key ideologies, if taken to the extremes we often see articulated. We do make our world and we don't make it exactly as we please. The question before us is, what should we pursue since the whole idea is to do something? I think we have to move from engineering to vision, by which I mean a shift from ‘how’ to ‘what’. We can now begin to see some glimmer of a world we can build. The question is what world we ought to create. The how remains important, of course, and no matter how hard we try we won't create exactly what we decide we ought to have.

This brings me, at long last, to my ostensible duty to talk about government. No matter how brainwashed we've been that the private sector is the important sector, and that government is just the unhappy consequence of market failure, the fact remains that government is usually the residual claimant of the complex social questions concerning the world we 'ought' to create. Only government has claim to the two keys assets required for peaceful resolution of such questions: constitutional processes that allow people with fundamentally different points of views to come into agreement; and the monopoly on the use of force without which society itself disintegrates. But the old notions of government are pretty quickly disappearing, and the once hard lines between the private and public sectors are blurring. As I note in the written essay, the deconstruction of the Cold War, which was certainly a blurry affair involving public and private sectors, is producing a new form of nationalistic economic competition in which multinational firms serve as 'champions' of their respective countries. The lines become further blurred. In the process, the world we 'ought' to create becomes more difficult to define because our sense of what we can create is changing so fast. The notion that IFIP should have a 'special' interest group that deals with computerization in government is already as archaic as the Fordist 'stovepipe' operation. The future, to the extent we can see it at all, does not seem to recognize anything very 'special' about the line that divides the public and private sectors. Maybe we should start thinking about the alternatives to a world thus divided.

The choices before us when we dream of ‘reengineering’ the world should inspire caution in we information systems people, who’ll still be here trying to make things work when all the BPR consultants have moved on to the next fashion. After all, someone will have to try to pick up the pieces of the shattered expectations sure to be left in the wake of the BPR movement. More important, however, we need to think about the very thought that we can or should even aspire to ‘reengineer’ the world. The worst case scenario is not, as the BPR clan seems to think, that we might fail in the reengineering. Rather, it is that we might succeed with the creation of something profoundly disturbing. Those of us trusted to envision the world as it could be recreated are bound to ask ourselves, what will we suffer if our dreams come true?
