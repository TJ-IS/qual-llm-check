# Organizational innovativeness, competitive strategy and investment success

Amy W Gatian

University of Tennessee, USA

Robert M Brown and James O Hicks, Jr

Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University, Blacksburg, VA 24061, USA

Case studies in the information systems literature provide evidence that strategic information systems (SIS) have been used to gain a competitive advantage. In the organizational behaviour and strategic management literature, links have been shown to exist between environmental factors, organizational variables, strategic choice, and firm innovation. This study combines and extends these areas of research by examining relations among the innovative climate of organizations and the SIS strategies implemented, the perceived success of investments made in SIS, and general end-user involvement. The analysis reveals that the innovative thrust of an organization significantly affects the aggressiveness of the firm's strategy for investment in information technology, the perceived success of investments in SIS, and the involvement of end-users in application development. Additionally, there are significant relations between investment strategies selected and perceived success.

Keywords: strategic information systems, competitive advantage, organizational innovativeness, competitive strategy, investment success

Case study results provide evidence that strategic information systems (SIS) may be used in organizations to create a competitive advantage. Subsequently, researchers have developed investment frameworks around findings from these studies which suggest that any company may obtain a competitive advantage by using certain investment strategies outlined in the frameworks. This study follows the next logical step in this area of research by describing if and how a wide cross-section of firms are generally investing in SIS, and the perceived success of such investments. We also extend this area of research by examining the relations among SIS investment practices and environmental factors such as the innovative thrust of organizational personnel.

Reports on case study findings at companies such as Merrill Lynch, American

Hospital Supply (now Baxter International), McKesson Drug, and Rosenbluth Travel led several researchers to propose frameworks for categorizing and systematically exploiting strategic investment opportunities (Porter, 1980, 1985; McFarlan, 1984; Clemons and Row, 1987; Wiseman, 1988). Yet beyond the frequently cited case studies and theoretical frameworks, there is still little empirical research which identifies general investment practices across companies and industries. For example, to what extent are firms investing in innovative technology or SIS in attempts to gain a competitive advantage? Also, can actual investment practices in a broad range of companies be categorized by one of the existing theoretical frameworks? This research is motivated by the need to answer some of these questions concerning general investment practices in companies.

A second motivation for this study is to help answer questions raised in previous case study analyses about the effects of the innovative thrust of organizational personnel and an organization's overall receptiveness to innovation on investment practices. For example, in a study of strategic information system practices at GTE, Rackoff et al (1985) found that executive management was generally unaware of the potential impact of innovative technology on the firm's strategic planning process until an external consultant explained the potential benefits. Is this true in other companies? Is executive management of some of the most competitive companies in the world generally supportive of investment in innovative technology (IT) as a part of the organization's competitive strategy, or are information systems (IS) still viewed as primarily a support function in most organizations? If executive management does support investment in IT, how involved are the IS managers in the strategic planning process? The general relationship between innovative climate and investment in IT across firms and industries is still unclear.

In this study, surveys were completed by the chief information officers (CIOs) of 251 large, competitive firms concerning actual IT investment strategies employed, innovative climate, success of investments made to date, and descriptive data on end-user computing practices. A summary of responses to the preceding questions is reported in this paper, and the results provide some interesting answers to the practical questions outlined above. Further, a model is proposed for synthesizing the findings from this research with previously developed theory. This empirically derived information is relevant to both practitioners and researchers. Findings from this study can help CIOs in planning IS investment strategies because it provides information about strategic differences among firms and the characteristics of firms that pursue different strategies. The findings from this study also provide researchers with a framework useful for planning future research efforts, since it identifies variables important to IS investment strategies.

## Conceptual development

Many senior executives feel they are not getting their money's worth from IT. They feel as if they have been led to believe that positive results may be achieved by simply throwing IT at problems (Vincent, 1990). Indeed, a great deal has been written about the role of IT in the development of organizational competitive strategies (McFarlan, 1981; Porter and Millar, 1985; Wiseman, 1988). However, successful implementation of innovative IT requires more than the purchase of sophisticated hardware and software. The climate within an organization must be conducive to innovation.

## Innovative climate

Damanpour (1987) found organizational variables to be particularly important in predicting technological innovation and innovations which produce changes in products and/or services. In addition, receptiveness of organizations to change has been found to be a significant factor for achieving success in technical innovations (Zmud, 1984). Receptiveness to change, like many other organizational variables, is in large part a product of corporate culture, where corporate culture has been defined as the ‘assumptions and beliefs shared by members of an organization’ (Johnson, 1992, p 29). Strategy professors and chief executive officers (CEOs) seem to be in agreement that the effect of organizational culture on strategy success is an area that needs further investigation (Zahra and Pearce, 1992). In this study, we only examine one aspect of corporate culture which we call the innovative climate of an organization. More innovative firms are more receptive to change, and are thus more likely to be successful in using IT to achieve a competitive advantage.

Organizational leaders' values, roles and personalities play key roles in the adoption of technological innovations (Damanpour, 1987). Also, leaders often shape the organizational characteristics by, for example, being able to modify the organizational structure. The literature is replete with the call for management involvement in IS development if firms are to attain a competitive advantage through IT (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1991). The call for management involvement has often been construed as hands-on involvement, when perhaps what should be encouraged is the fostering of a climate that invites innovation. Ettlie and O'Keefe point out the existence of 'widespread evidence in the innovative literature that the attitudes and values of organizational members are significantly related to innovative behavior' (Ettlie and O'Keefe, 1982, p 164). Jarpenpaa and Ives (1991) show that executive involvement defined in a psychological sense rather than a participative sense is more strongly associated with successful employment of IT, ie the innovative climate that is fostered by management and the organization, in general, may be the most important construct to achieving and sustaining a competitive advantage through IT.

The relationship between a company's competitive environment and its organizational strategy is cited as an important area for further study by strategy professors and CEOs (Zahra and Pearce, 1992). The environment provides top management with information that it uses to modify the organizational structure and strategy-making processes (Li and Litschert, 1993). In turn, the structural differences among firms influence how IT may be used for securing and sustaining a competitive advantage (Clemons and Row, 1987, 1988). Clemons and Row (1991a) suggest that the answer to obtaining a competitive advantage lies in whether IT can be used to lever differences in strategic resources. Identification and leverage of unique resources represents a much more aggressive management style than, for example, focusing on cost-cutting measures.

Miles and Snow (1978) identify four strategic types of organizations: prospectors, defenders, analysers, and reactors. Tavakolian (1989) uses Miles and Snow's typology to examine relations between IT structure and organizational competitive strategy. Prospectors are considered to be firms with aggressive competitive strategies, ie pioneers in development, while defenders are firms with the most conservative competitive strategies, focusing primarily on cost savings (Tavakolian, 1989). Analysers make fewer and slower changes than prospectors and have moderate investment practices, while reactors do not engage in strategic planning — they simply react. From an investment in IT perspective, prospector and defender firms are perhaps the most interesting to study, since they represent polar perspectives on strategic planning.

Joyce and Slocum (1990) argue that organizational climates cannot be separated from the strategic context in which a firm operates, and that the management of firms with the most innovative climates (prospector firms) have a good deal of discretion in selecting strategies for achieving competitive advantages. Thus, the innovative climate of an organization directly affects the investment strategies selected. A direct relation should also exist between the innovative climate and success at being innovative. These hypothesized relations are depicted by the arrows from the climate construct to success and from climate to the strategy construct in Figure 1.

## End-user involvement

Ives and Olson developed a descriptive model of user involvement based on a meta-analysis of the literature addressing the relation between user involvement and MIS success (Ives and Olson, 1984). In their model, they show that organizational climate affects user involvement in any given situation. In innovative or prospector firms, little formal control is exercised over organizational members, allowing decision makers (end-users) more freedom in selecting solutions to problems (Joyce and Slocum, 1990). Thus, in an IS context, firms with more innovative climates should have a greater degree of end-user involvement in system development. This relationship is depicted by the arrow from climate to user-involvement in Figure 1. Additionally, if more aggressive (prospector) organizations tend to shift more responsibility to user departments, as research by Tavakolian seems to suggest, then strategy selection should impact end-user involvement, as depicted by the arrow from strategy to end-user involvement in Figure 1.

In summary, an organization's innovative climate should directly affect strategy selection, end-user involvement in system development, and success at using IT to attain a competitive advantage. Further, more aggressive strategic investment practices in IT require more coordination with the functional areas of a business and thus there should be more user involvement in systems development. The remaining relationship depicted in Figure 1 between SIS investment strategy and the perceived successfulness of those investments is discussed in the following section of this paper.

## Competitive advantage

The first studies on obtaining a competitive advantage consisted primarily of case studies of individual companies that had invested in IT and had attained a competitive advantage. From these initial studies, several researchers became intrigued with finding ways to systematically exploit opportunities for gaining a competitive advantage. Theoretical frameworks for identifying these opportunities have been developed by many authors including McFarlan (1981), Porter and Millar (1985), Clemons and Kimbrough (1986) and Wiseman (1988).

Porter's (1980, 1985) work on strategic management for competitive advantage is the foundation of much subsequent research on investment in SIS for competitive advantage. He identified five competitive forces confronting any firm including: the threat of new entrants, the intensity of rivalry among existing competitors, pressure from substitute products, the bargaining power of buyers, and the bargaining power of suppliers. He further developed three generic strategies to combat these forces, including: overall cost leadership (being the low cost leader), differentiation (of product or service) and focus (finding a specialized niche in a market).

McFarlan (1981) was one of the first to draw upon the work of Porter to identify existing opportunities for strategic applications of IT. He proposed five questions as aids to assessing investment opportunities:

1. Can technology be used as a barrier against market entry?

2. Can technology change the basis of competition?

3. Can technology be used to generate new products?

4. Can technology be used to build in switching costs?

5. Can technology change the balance of power in supplier relationships?

Clemons and Kimbrough (1986) suggest that SIS share three characteristics: such systems will reduce costs or add value for customers or benefits to users; users incur significant switching costs when changing to systems offered by a competitor; and adoption time is short relative to the time a competitor needs to copy the system, providing early developers with an opportunity to develop their initial base of users.

Porter and Millar (1985) developed an information intensity matrix for identifying IT investment opportunities in relation to the use of technology along the value chain. The intent is to help companies identify strategic investment opportunities in the offering of products, as well as in the development of products. The matrix may be used to identify cross-sectional opportunities for investment based on the information content of the products and the information intensity of the value chain.

Wiseman also expanded upon previous works and developed five specific strategies, called strategic thrusts, to combat competitive forces. These strategic thrusts focus on: product/process differentiation, product/process cost control, product/process change and/or introduction, growth support, and alliance formation. Wiseman's framework was chosen to categorize investment strategies in this study for several reasons. First, this framework of strategic thrusts integrates findings from the most extensive review of case studies to date. Secondly, Wiseman proposes that a firm may successfully seek strategic advantage by employing one or more of the strategies outlined in his framework. Thus, if firm managers believe that they have successfully invested in innovative technology for a competitive advantage, it would be useful to determine if actual investments can be classified according to a prescriptive framework such as Wiseman's.

Beyond being one of the most well known and frequently employed frameworks, Wiseman's framework was selected based on evidence that his methodology is one of the most effective for implementation of competitive strategies. Bergeron et al (1991) found that the types of innovation opportunities identified by Wiseman were more attractive to managers when it came to actual implementation of the strategies than the types of opportunities outlined in Porter's framework. Additionally, Lee and Adams (1990) note that Wiseman's framework is one of the few that has been successfully tested as a means of identifying strategic opportunities available through information systems.

The five strategic thrusts identified by Wiseman (1988) were used to assess aggressiveness of firm strategies. Within Wiseman's framework, the strategies of cost reduction and growth support are descriptive of more traditional roles ascribed to IS and may be more attractive to organizations with more moderate management styles. On the other hand, product differentiation, process transformations and alliance formations are more likely to require exploitation of a company's unique resources and, therefore, are expected to be descriptive of a more aggressive use of SIS.

The purpose of this study is not to test whether CIOs are familiar with and use any of the theoretical frameworks which appear in the academic literature. However, there is ample evidence that firms are investing in SIS in attempts to attain a competitive advantage. Based on findings by Bergeron et al and Lee and Adams it was anticipated that if firms are in fact investing in SIS, the framework developed by Wiseman would be the most useful for helping CIOs categorize existing SIS investment efforts. This study also differs from previous research in that it involves empirical testing of a framework in a cross-sectional examination of competitive firms.

All of the theoretical frameworks developed for application of SIS by McFarlan (1984), Porter and Millar (1985), Wiseman (1988), Clemons and Row (1991b) and others suggest that the innovativeness or aggressiveness of SIS investment strategies selected by companies should impact firm success in attaining a competitive advantage. This hypothesized relationship is explicitly tested in this study and is depicted by the arrow from strategy to success in Figure 1.

## Research methodology

The research methodology is presented in four subsections. The first section formally states the hypotheses tested for the model shown in Figure 1. The following two sections describe the sample and the survey used. The final section describes the statistical analyses used to analyse survey responses.

## Hypotheses

The formally stated hypotheses focus on the relations among the following variables, as depicted in Figure 1: innovative climate, investment strategy, perceived success of investment and end-user involvement. These hypotheses are presented below:

H1: The overall innovative climate of an organization significantly affects the aggressiveness of a company's strategy for investment in innovative technology.

H2: The overall innovative climate of an organization significantly affects the perceived success of investments in innovative technology.

H3: The overall innovative climate of an organization significantly affects the involvement of end-users in application development.

H4: There is a significant relationship between the aggressiveness of the investment strategy and investment success when investing in innovative technology.

H5: There is a significant relationship between the aggressiveness of strategy selection and end-user involvement.

## Sample selection and participants

In this study, a sample of 600 companies that trade their shares over the New York Stock Exchange were randomly selected as possible sample units. Large, public firms were chosen, based on evidence that firms likely to be successful at obtaining a competitive advantage with information technology are firms which are already profitable (Lee and Adams, 1990). Questionnaires were sent to the chief information officer or the vice-president of information systems of these companies, depending on the structure of the organization. Most questions were based on a seven-point (0–6) Likert-type scale. Of the 600 companies surveyed, 251 usable responses were collected, for a response rate of 42 per cent.

As discussed earlier, anecdotal evidence suggests that during the 1980s there was a shift from the view of IT as a support function to IT as a competitive weapon. One piece of evidence of this shift is the creation of the first executive level positions (CIOs) in the IS departments of many organizations during the 1980s. Typically, the CIO is responsible for, among other things, communication with the other top executives in the organization of the potential strategic role of the IS department (Stephens et al, 1992). Strong CIO/CEO partnerships are crucially important to the success of major systems projects in an organization. Among the many benefits from a strong relationship is evidence of exceptional firm accomplishments with IT relative to competitors (Feeny et al, 1992).

Another way of viewing the CIO is as the most powerful technology champion in the organization. Several recent articles address the importance of a technology champion in organizations. Information technology champions have been described as ‘managers who actively and vigorously promote their personal vision for using information technology, pushing the project over or around approval and implementation hurdles’ (Beath, 1991). Schon characterizes the technology champion as someone with the power and prestige necessary to ‘cut across the special interests essential to the product’s or process’s development’ (Schon, 1963). Beatty and Gordon (1991) point out the fact that implementation of new technology is much more likely to be successful where there is enthusiastic involvement of technology champions. The increasing interest in the role of CIOs and technology champions in organizations provides evidence that feedback from CIOs concerning their assessment of the IS department’s involvement in the strategic planning process is one important gauge of organizational success in making the transition from using IT as a support function to using it as a strategic weapon.

## Survey instrument

Before mailing questionnaires to the 600 firms in the primary study, the survey instrument was pilot tested on 60 randomly selected companies that also trade their shares on the NYSE. Twenty-six questionnaires were returned for a response rate of 43.3 per cent. Suggestions made during the pretest for improving the understandability, and thus the content validity, of the questionnaire were incorporated into the questionnaire before the primary mailing.

Cronbach's alpha test for the overall internal reliability was computed on the Likert scale questions in the instrument pertaining to climate, strategic thrust and success from investment, resulting in a score of 0.84. Generally, Cronbach's alpha represents the amount of error in the measurement, where scores at or above the 0.80 level are considered acceptable for basic research.

Climate measurement. The survey instrument, Appendix A, includes three questions to assess CIO beliefs about the innovativeness of the organizational climate. These questions required responses with regard to the overall innovativeness of the organization, innovativeness of executive management, and innovativeness of the IS department. A fourth question in this section of the questionnaire asked CIOs to assess the level of involvement the IS department has had in the organizational strategic planning process.

Strategic thrust measurement. The five strategic thrusts identified by Wiseman were used in the questionnaire to assess the aggressiveness of firm strategies. Within Wiseman's framework, cost reduction and growth support may be viewed as descriptive of more traditional roles ascribed to information systems. This view is supported by the fact that while these strategies have been employed in some situations to obtain a competitive advantage, they are also typically necessary strategies for maintaining the current level of business health. Product differentiation, innovation in business conduct and alliance formation are descriptive of more aggressive uses of SIS, because they typically go beyond the necessary strategic planning efforts for maintaining business health. It is anticipated that the more innovative the climate of the firm, the more likely the firm is to employ more aggressive investment strategies.

Success measurements. Information systems' success or effectiveness is a complex concept. Approaches which have been advocated for measuring IS effectiveness include management information systems (MIS) usage estimation, user satisfaction, incremental performance in decision-making effectiveness, cost-benefit analysis, information economics, utility analysis, the analytic hierarchy approach and information attribute examination (Srinivasan, 1985). In a round table discussion at the 1985 International Conference on Information Systems, researchers agreed that measures of user-perceived effectiveness were of growing importance.

In this study, simple measures of user-perceived effectiveness are used. Specifically, the CIOs were asked to what extent their company has invested in IT to gain a competitive advantage (Appendix A). They were also asked to assess how successful the firm has been in attaining a strategic competitive advantage from those instruments. The response to this question was used to ascertain success. However, to explicate response differences between prospector and defender strategic types, CIOs were also asked to respond to questions concerning investments made due to competitive necessity. In addition, one question on the survey asked respondents to report the percentages of company investments in IT over the past 10 years made for competitive advantage and competitive necessity, respectively. Intercorrelations among reported investment percentages and Likert scale responses revealed that respondents were consistent in their answers.

End-user involvement. Based on research by Ives and Olson (1984) and Tavakolian (1989), a secondary interest in this paper is to examine the impact of the organizational climate and competitive strategies on end-user involvement in application development. End-user involvement was assessed by asking two questions. One question asked how involved users are with in-house application development and the other question asked how involved users are with developing their own programs using fourth generation languages. These represent very general measures of the end-users' involvement in the development of systems applications overall. A relation between success and end-user computing was not hypothesized because success, as operationalized in this paper, represents success with SIS, where end-user computing represents a measure of involvement in the overall systems applications in the organizations. It was not the intention of the authors to make inferences about end-user involvement in the development of SIS. As such, it would have been inappropriate to attempt to draw inferences about the relations between perceived success of SIS applications and general end-user computing.

## Analytic procedures

LISREL VII, a statistical package designed to analyse latent variable models, was used to provide information pertaining to the overall fit of the hypothesized model as represented in Figure 1 and to estimate the strength and nature of the structural relationships in the model. Three variables, (1) innovative climate of the organization, (2) innovative thrust of executive management, and (3) innovative thrust of the IS department, provide the measures used to construct the latent variable organizational climate. These variables are denoted by the boxes in Figure 2. The unobserved or latent variable, climate, is denoted by the oval in Figure 2.

End-user involvement was constructed from responses to the two questions which relate to how involved users are with the systems development. These relations are also depicted in Figure 2 by the boxes.

The latent variable strategy was constructed from the measured responses to questions from the Wiseman framework concerning the importance of investment in IT for purposes of product/process differentiation, product/process cost control, product/process change/introduction, growth support, and alliance formation. The success latent variable represents the CIOs' rating of organizational success in investing in IT to gain a competitive advantage.

## Results

The results are presented in two subsections. The first provides summary statistics of the survey responses. The second presents the LISREL results from testing the formally stated hypotheses.

## Questionnaire summary statistics

Summary results from CIO responses are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Predictably, CIOs ranked the IS department as the most innovative, as shown in Table 1. There is also strong evidence that CIOs feel the IS department is not very highly involved in the strategic planning process.

When asked to rate how important investment in IT is for each of the five major investment decisions, CIOs gave cost reduction the highest average score as evidenced by Table 2. This is intuitively appealing, since cost control should be a primary consideration for all companies under all circumstances.

To further investigate what types of investment strategies drive investment decisions, the extent of investment to gain a competitive advantage was regressed over the five competitive strategies in a step-wise fashion. With investment to gain a competitive advantage as the dependent variable, product/process innovation entered the model first with $R^{2} = 0.2169$ and p value = 0.0001. Product/process differentiation entered the model second with partial $R^{2} = 0.0854$ , model $R^{2} = 0.3023$ and p value = 0.0001. No other variables were significant. Therefore, it appears that innovations such as the introduction of new products or process changes are most closely related to investment in IT to gain a competitive advantage. Distinguishing products and services from those of competitors and/or reducing the differentiation advantage of competitors also appears to be related to investment for CA.

## Model testing

The test results from the LISREL analysis of the relationships described in Figure 1 provide substantial evidence to support the first four hypotheses (Figure 2). Hypothesis 5 is not supported by the data. This hypothesized relationship was not significant at alpha = 0.05 and was not retained in the model shown in Figure 2. This was done in order to provide the best fit of the model to the data. The resulting fit was quite good, as indicated by a goodness-of-fit index (GFI) of 0.942, an adjusted goodness-of-fit (AGFI) of 0.907, and a root mean square residual (RMSR) of 0.09. (See the Appendix B for further discussion of these terms.) Intercorrelations of the latent variables are presented in Table 3. The highest correlation between latent variables occurs between climate and strategy, 0.580. The next highest correlation occurs between climate and success, 0.450.

The parameter estimates included in the model may be interpreted as beta weights. Analysis of the parameter estimates in conjunction with the intercorrelations provides a more complete picture of the relationships among the latent variables. All of the parameter estimates are significant at alpha = 0.05. t values range from 1.844 for the relationship between the latent variables climate and end-user involvement to 7.154 for the relationship between the latent variables climate and strategy. No further revisions to the model were necessary because all relationships were positive and significant at the alpha = 0.05 level.

## Discussion

One of the most important findings from this study is that large, competitive firms do, in fact, actively invest in SIS for the express purpose of improving their relative competitive positions within their respective industries. Further, it appears that CIOs from some of the sample firms believe their organizations' investments in SIS have resulted in some form of competitive advantage. Additionally, the Wiseman framework is confirmed in this study as an appropriate vehicle through which CIOs are comfortable categorizing investment strategies. These findings represent a significant contribution to the literature. Most of the research in this area has been either case-based research or theoretical in nature, whereas the findings in this study are empirically derived and represent a broad cross-section of large competitive firms. In general, previously developed theoretical arguments concerning investment in SIS are supported in this study.

The LISREL results support the assertion that the innovative climate is positively related to the strategic choice of investment in innovative IS technology. The value of the parameter estimate in the LISREL model between climate and strategy indicates that the higher the innovative climate of the organization, the more likely the firm is to employ an aggressive IT investment strategy. A closer analysis of the latent variable constructs clarifies these relationships. While the responses to all three innovative climate questions are significantly related to the (innovative) climate latent variable, the weights assigned to the innovative climate of the company and the innovative thrust of executive management are more than twice that assigned to the innovative thrust of the IS department. Thus, even though the CIOs rated the innovative thrust of the IS department higher than the innovative thrust of executive management or the innovative climate of the company (see Table 1), it appears that the innovative thrust of the IS department is less important to the overall innovative climate of the company than either of the other two. This finding also underscores the importance of management support as discussed by Ettlie and O'Keefe (1982), Zmud (1984) and Jarvenpaa and Ives (1991). These results, along with the finding that CIOs collectively believe IS involvement in the strategic planning process is relatively low (Table 1), parallel the findings by Rackoff et al (1985) that executives are still not fully aware of the potential strategic uses of IT.

Aggressiveness of investment strategy was measured by the questions concerning investment in IT for purposes of product/process differentiation, product/process cost control, product/process change/introduction, support of growth, and formation of alliances. While all measures may be descriptive of aggressive investment policies under certain conditions, investment for purposes of product/process differentiation, product/process change/introduction, and alliance formation would seem to represent more assertive uses of IT. These three variables all had higher parameter estimates with strategy than did cost control and support of growth. In fact, the parameter estimates for these variables were approximately four times the parameter estimate associated with cost control, the one measure more typically aligned with the traditional role of IT. The high mean scores on product/process cost control across all company categories as shown in Table 2 confirms this variable's role as a common need across all company categories. The more aggressive the investment strategies, the more likely the firm was to successfully obtain a competitive advantage.

Innovative climate is also significantly related to end-user involvement in system development. It appears that the more innovative the climate of the organization, the more end-users are involved in in-house development and in designing applications using fourth generation languages. This result supports findings by Tavakolian (1989) and Joyce and Slocum (1990). However, strategy was not significantly related to end-user involvement as had been expected. Perhaps no relation was detected because most end-user projects are related to more traditional types of systems applications.

Overall, results of this study indicate that the most important variable to consider in deciding to invest in IT for competitive advantage is the innovative thrust of executive management. However, the most important factor to achieving success is the innovative climate of the organization. Psychological and financial support from the CEO and other top executives have been shown to be critical success factors for traditional information systems. It appears, from the results of this study, that CEO and top management support is also the most crucial determinant of success of SIS. The significant, positive relation between the climate and success latent variables also indicates that companies with more innovative climates are more likely to achieve success in employing IS for competitive advantage. This finding supports theoretical arguments from the strategic management literature which were previously discussed in this paper.

These results have important implications for the CIOs and CEOs of competitive firms. In order for investment in SIS to become an integral part of a company's competitive strategy, CIOs feel they need to be more actively involved in planning. The addition of the CIO position in many companies is a positive step towards integration of systems strategy and organizational strategy, but apparently there are still communications gaps that need to be filled before the strategic potential of SIS can be fully exploited.

## Summary description of measures employed in LISREL analysis

LATENT VARIABLES — Latent variables are unobservable constructs, typically psychological or behavioural constructs, whose ‘reality’ is inferred from observable variables [0].

PARAMETER ESTIMATES — Generally, parameter estimates are analogous to path coefficients or beta weights. Considered in conjunction with the intercorrelations among variables, the reader can interpret individual relationships within the model in much the same way as one would analyse individual relationships among variables in multiple regression analysis.

STANDARDIZED SOLUTION — Parameter estimates may be reported in many ways in LISREL analysis. Here, the standardized solution (SS) is reported, in which the latent variables are scaled to have variances equal to one (range from -1.0 to +1.0) and the observed variables are still in the original metric [0].

GOODNESS OF FIT INDEX (GFI) — The GFI is a measure of the relative amount of variance and covariance jointly accounted for by the model. Generally a GFI >0.90 is strong evidence that the overall model being tested fits the data very well.

ADJUSTED GFI (AGFI) — GFI is independent of sample size. AGGI represents the GFI adjusted for degrees of freedom. Chi-squared is also reported sometimes, but its magnitude is a function of sample size and with large N, as is the case in this study, chi-squared will almost certainly be significant, and thus meaningless ([0], p 603).

ROOT MEAN SQUARE RESIDUAL (RMSR) — RMSR is a measure of the average of the fitted residuals and can only be interpreted in relation to the sizes of the observed variances and covariances (GFI). In general, the smaller the residuals, the better the fit. RMSR = <0.10 or 0.11 indicate a good fit ([0], p 603).
