# Cooperative supply chain management: the impact of interorganizational information systems

Christopher P Holland

Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Booth Street West, Manchester M15 6PB, UK

The impact of interorganizational information systems on the structure and management of a supply chain in the textile industry is analysed from a managerial perspective. Case data from detailed, partial longitudinal studies of manufacturer and retail organizations are presented. The competitive strategies of organizations in the supply chain are described and their associated patterns of communication are analysed. It is shown that companies are moving towards cooperative relationships in an effort to make the supply chain as a whole more competitive. The resulting market structure is an electronic hierarchy in which business processes are integrated across organizational boundaries using interorganizational information systems. The strategies of the individual firms are evolving as new opportunities arise and different problems present themselves. The results are compared with current theories on market structure and competition in an electronic trading environment and future trends are outlined.

Keywords: interorganizational information systems, supply chain management, textile industry, market structure, market competition

Interorganizational information systems are being implemented in all types of industries to establish electronic links between separately owned organizations. These may be simple electronic data interchange (EDI) systems for exchanging data such as purchase orders, advice of delivery notes and invoices or may involve more complex transactions such as integrated cash management systems or shared technical databases. The phenomenon of shared information systems is having a profound effect on business relationships, market structure and competition (Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990; Hart and Estrin, 1991; Holland et al, 1992). There is considerable debate about the effects of IOSs on competition and different conclusions have been reached on the possible outcomes, including the polar forms of market structure, electronic markets and electronic hierarchies. In this paper the problem of assessing the impact of IOSs on competition is considered from a managerial perspective. The research method is to analyse a special type of market network, a product market supply chain, and explore the interactions between IOS strategy and market structure.

The study of market networks is a major research theme which runs through different management subjects, for example marketing (Cunningham and Culligan, 1989), organizational behaviour (Aldrich and Whetten, 1981), strategy (Jarillo, 1988) and information systems (IS) (Malone et al., 1987, 1989; Malone and Rockart, 1992). One special type of market network is a supply chain. Supply chains are composed of organizations which perform successive stages in the marketing of a product/service group. For example a supply chain in the automotive industry might consist of the extraction of raw materials, component manufacture, vehicle assembly, distribution and retail. Separate stages in the supply chain are connected together by business processes. For example the management of logistics and finance processes is concerned with the flows of products and cash respectively.

In this paper a supply chain in the textile industry is analysed. In common with supply chains in other types of industries the textile supply chain is attempting to improve its competitiveness and a series of innovative business strategies and IOS implementations are being considered. A supply chain consisting of prominent companies was chosen in order to capture the leading edge practice in this area. The textile industry illustrates many issues; for example, quick response to changes in market demand, rapid launch of new designs, continuous improvement strategies in product quality and cost reductions. These themes are also of direct relevance to management thinking in other industries.

## Problem statement

The general research problem is concerned with the evolution and management of different types of market structure in an electronic trading environment. The main theoretical approach to the problem of predicting likely changes to market structures as IOSs become the norm has been to use transaction cost economics (eg Malone et al, 1987; 1989; Clemons and Row, 1992a; Ebers, 1993). This has provided valuable insights into the relative costs of different market structures although the divergence of opinion between the authors indicates that the respective theories are in an early stage of development. Another hindrance to evaluating the competing theories is the relative scarcity of data to test them (Wrigley, 1991; Bakos and Brynjolfsson, 1993). The two principal theories are electronic markets (Malone et al, 1987; 1989; Malone and Rockart, 1992) and electronic hierarchies (Johnston and Vitale, 1988; Johnston and Lawrence, 1988; Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990). Electronic markets theory predicts that as electronic trading becomes widespread then market style structures, characterized by open access to market data by all organizations and fair and open competition will become more efficient than other forms of market structure, in particular electronic hierarchies. Electronic hierarchies are characterized by privileged access to market data and are formed by small groups of vertically arranged companies that develop very close relationships between themselves, forming a ‘virtual hierarchy’. Electronic markets theory is based on the premise that electronic trading will shift the balance in most industries from hierarchy to market style structures because IOSs will reduce the coordination costs of searching and evaluating competing product offerings and will allow companies to work with large numbers of suppliers. Companies will therefore gain the advantages of increased market incentive which leads to lower production costs. The opposing view of electronic hierarchies is that IOSs will be used to tie-in trading partners and lock out competitors which leads to ‘virtual hierarchies’ — vertical integration but without ownership. In this scenario access to market data is privileged and the principal aim is to achieve sustainable competitive advantage from such systems (Johnston and Lawrence, 1988; Johnston and Vitale, 1988; Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990; Holland et al, 1992). A direct observable result from the electronic hierarchies hypothesis is a reduction in the number of suppliers to individual firms (Bakos and Brynjolfsson, 1993). In addition to IOSs there are other forces driving the reduction of supplier bases such as total quality management (TQM) and reduced time to market business strategies which are often implemented in tandem with IOSs. Clemons and Row (1992a) predict a move to the middle, that is, to a market structure between electronic markets and electronic hierarchies.

The approach adopted here is to consider two organizations that constitute a major part of a textile supply chain. An intensive case research methodology has been adopted in order to uncover the causal mechanisms relating the strategies of individual firms to the formation of business relationships and market networks. The general problem of analysing the impact of IOSs on market structure is a complex one but it can be simplified by considering a partial market network (Aldrich and Whetten, 1981). A product market supply chain is an appropriate focus of study because it captures the whole set of competitive behaviours displayed by separate organizations in the marketing of a product group from raw materials extraction to retail. Examining product supply chains as a focus of research into IOSs has also been suggested by Cunningham and Tynan (1990) and has been implicitly assumed by Hart and Estrin (1991) in their study of IOSs in the semiconductor industry.

A product supply chain can be defined as the group of organizations involved in the design, manufacture, distribution and retail of a specific product group. Most organizations will therefore be involved in more than one supply chain and may be involved in a large number of them, this being dependent on the size and diversity of their business, the nature of the supply chain(s) and their position(s) whether this be manufacturer, distributor, retailer etc. For example a truck dealer with a single franchise agreement would be in just one supply chain involving component suppliers, a truck assembler, itself and its customers. A component supplier in this particular automotive supply chain might also supply other motor manufacturers and source its raw materials from more than one supplier. It would therefore be a member of several supply chains. Logistics and financial processes connect together the component suppliers, truck assembler, truck dealer and customers in the management of product and cash flows throughout the supply chain.

It is clear that a product market supply chain focus captures the salient characteristics of interaction between organizations and on how competition will likely develop as electronic trading is introduced. It is also a concept which is intuitively understood by managers who can therefore articulate their thoughts in terms of their understanding of their own supply chains much better than the more abstract notion of a market network.

The research question can be stated in terms of a product market supply chain:

What is the impact of IOSs on the management and structure of a product-market supply chain?

The electronic markets and electronic hierarchies theories are taken as a starting point in trying to answer this question. A schematic diagram of the interaction patterns for both of these theories is shown in Figure 1.

In addition to these characteristic patterns, both theories make predictions on the type of business relationship which would evolve between organizations. In an electronic market each individual organization would manage a large number of loose and unstable trading relationships. In an electronic hierarchy one would expect a small number of very stable relationships which would change very slowly over a long period of time. Gradual change would be accompanied by significant inter-dependencies across business processes such as logistics, finance and design and more generally for knowledge and expertise, product technology and possibly IS. These relationships would be closely integrated and consequently difficult to sever. Following from these predictions a corollary is that it is also possible to infer specific market structures by analysing business relationships. This is the approach that is taken here. In the next section the research methodology for analysing a supply chain and in particular for exploring the nature of business relationships between its member organizations is discussed.

## Research methodology

The general research problem concerning market structure in an electronic trading environment has been broken down into the analysis of a special type of partial market network, a supply chain. This reduced problem has several characteristics which are important in determining the appropriate research methodology. The phenomenon of IOSs is difficult to separate out from its organizational and market contexts. For example cost reduction and quality improvement strategies are often cited as significant reasons for justifying the implementation of an IOS which is a simple explanation of business strategy driving IS development. But there is also the case of the IS influencing the choice of business strategy. Once a simple electronic exchange of purchase orders and invoices has been implemented between two trading partners they may then decide to exchange other types of data such as sales histories and back order status on products. Similarly at the market level of analysis, IOSs can be said to be driving market structure but it could also be argued that specific types of market structure would have evolved anyway. This is an intractable problem and it is probable that the implementation of IOSs drives and is driven by organizational and market variables, thereby creating a causal feedback loop.

In order to start to understand the interaction between IOSs and market structure the research method must capture sufficient detail in order to get close to the problem. Another major influence on the choice of research methodology is the level of theory on this topic. Although there is an extensive literature on market structure and competition the research on the effects of IOSs is at a rudimentary level and is characterized by very little direct research (Wrigley, 1991). There is also considerable disagreement on the long term outcome and also on the mechanisms of change.

The difficulty of separating out the phenomenon from its context and the conflicting theory suggest a detailed case study approach as the most suitable research strategy (Yin, 1981). The framework proposed by Eisenhardt (1989) for building theory from case research has been adopted as a basis for organizing the design of the research which has been divided into distinct stages:

1. Development of research questions.

2. A priori theoretical constructs.

3. Data collection and analysis.

4. Model building.

5. Comparison with literature.

Each of these stages is described in turn.

## A priori constructs

The case data are organized around the a priori constructs of IOS application, business strategy, information technology (IT) link, information flows and market context. These are themes which have been distilled from the literature and refined and developed throughout the data collection process. Each of them is described in turn.

## IOS application

The IOS application construct is the business process which is being supported or enabled by the IOS. For example this could be a simple trade data exchange system or a more complex cash management one. This construct allows all types of IOSs to be classified according to their business application. The remaining constructs can then be used to define an IOS with reference to its strategic, technological, information and market structure components.

## Business strategy

The business strategy construct captures the strategic dimension of an IOS — it explains why it has been implemented. This could be as part of a stated plan to reduce costs, improve quality and build better relationships with trading partners or it might be as a result of being forced into an electronic based relationship by a larger customer or supplier. It is an important construct because it could partly explain and predict the future course of such systems based on the current intentions and historical actions of organizations and managers.

## Information technology link

The IT link is concerned with the technical nature of the electronic link. At the moment the electronic mail box type connection to share data is predominant to other more advanced types of links such as direct links which enable software to be shared.

## Information flows

Plotting information flows is a method of analysing a business relationship. The source, type, purpose and destination of each identifiable information flow between organizations is drawn. The objectives are to uncover the mechanisms of coordination, identify the scale of organizational integration and establish where the control over decision making lies. For example in logistics management a simple exchange of data such as purchase orders, invoices, advice of deliveries etc might replicate simply the information flows in a manual ‘classical’ trading relationship. However, a more novel approach would be to share production data so as to give a long term outlook in addition to short term requirements. Control over product flows could rest with either the supplier or the customer. This is therefore a potentially powerful construct but there are situations such as shared software solutions (ie two or more companies using a common IOS application) where it is difficult to apply in practice.

## Market context

The market context represents the unknown factor of principal interest from the research question, namely what will the structure of markets be as electronic trading becomes the norm. The two polar forms already identified are electronic markets and electronic hierarchies but there are other intermediate forms such as a biased market (Feldman, 1988), hybrid markets (Williamson, 1991) and quasi-integration (Blois, 1972). It is therefore more accurate to conceptualize market structure as a continuum (Mahoney, 1992). This construct represents the crux of the research question and will be analysed in detail in the study of a supply chain described below.

## A case study of a textile supply chain

The textile supply chain studied consisted of mills which supplied the raw materials, a large textile manufacturing company — ‘Textiles’ — and a leading UK retailing organization — ‘Chain Store’. The company names used are fictitious but the details are correct in every other respect. Chain Store cites its strong relationships with suppliers as being of critical importance to its success in maintaining growth and profits. One of its most important suppliers for fashion garments is Textiles which is a worldwide organization manufacturing thread, clothing, homewares, fashion retail, yarns and fabric. Chain Store and Textiles are important and powerful companies in the garment industry and between them are capable of controlling and influencing the development of the whole of the supply chain from raw materials to retail sales. Both companies are heavily interdependent with each other and have chosen to cooperate in the development of IOSs. Given that they are also able to influence other companies in the garment industry because of their size and market power this study therefore illustrates the possibilities of applying IOSs to the whole of a supply chain.

Case data were collected from senior managers in both companies over a period of approximately 12 months. IOSs are discussed in the context of Chain Store's business vision of total supply chain management from the retailer to the producer of raw materials. It would be artificial to separate the strategies of the supplier and the retailer and instead the strategies, IT links and information flows of the IOSs are given which represent the activities of a network of organizations rather than the autonomous activities of individual firms.

## Business strategy

Increases in annual stock turn of products have been attributed to the logistics IOS but other strategic reasons are driving the development of IOSs in general. In the past, hard-nosed negotiation in the context of an adversarial relationship took place between Chain Store and its suppliers and the organizations were kept at arms length from each other. Now the culture has changed to joint management of the supply chain. The IOS manager from Chain Store remarked on the new forms of relationships with suppliers:

There is now an open and honest sharing of information. Buyers still negotiate on merchandise but agree the management of the supply chain in partnership with suppliers. This change has taken some time to achieve and there may be a gradual parting with those suppliers which cannot adapt.

Over the past five years Chain Store has established an internal IT infrastructure connecting stores, warehouses and head office which supports a modern logistics operation. From initial ideas from commercial managers a business systems strategy was developed on the basis of what could be done with IT. The business vision and the information systems have been related in a two-way process, each influencing the other. IOSs are now a condition of trading with Chain Store and it has little difficulty in persuading suppliers to conform to its own methods.

The organizational boundaries of Chain Store are effectively being extended to include suppliers and perhaps more significantly other stages of the supply chain. The aim is to control information flows and decision making in an IOS encompassing several hierarchically arranged organizations. This view is reflected by a Textiles technical manager with overall responsibility for the technical part of EDI implementation:

EDI was initiated by Chain Store and suppliers were initially reluctant. However, there are now many joint projects and Textiles has become a proactive force for EDI in the industry.

The IOS that supports logistics management between Chain Store and all of its suppliers, including Textiles, is now fully operational. However the manufacturing and distribution only takes a maximum of six weeks out of the total of over 60 weeks for the whole product life-cycle. The strategy is therefore changing to focus on other parts of the product life-cycle. A summary of the main stages in the total life-cycle for fashion garments is described before detailing the strategies for other IOS applications.

## New product launch

The development of the Spring 1993 fashion offering (in Chain Store shops) is led by the fashion shows held at the end of 1991. Chain Store defines the concepts but leaves the design up to its suppliers. Typically each supplier is invited to tender for a particular product group but may also wish to submit a design for other products. A product offering at this stage is a package which consists of a mill, its cloths, the outline of a product and a price. Specific designs or styles are not owned by the suppliers but are chosen and possibly re-allocated to another manufacturer to produce. A product may be produced by a different manufacturer to the one which designed it. Chain Store therefore sets the quality standards, approves certain suppliers and strongly influences the choice of mills. It also effectively controls an internal market. A limited amount of competition takes place to control prices and encourage innovation but the main objective is the attainment of high quality in garment design and manufacture. The design stage, from choosing the colours to passing the pilot designs for full scale manufacture, takes a total of six months. Buying the cloth and cutting the garments takes a further four to five months. The last stage, manufacturing, storage and distribution of the finished garments, currently takes just six weeks out of a total of 12 months. The goods are called off by Chain Store and shipped to multi-user warehouses where there is a minimum stock held to satisfy forward sales flow cover.

It can be seen that there is a huge commitment to a new product launch. The process is composed of a series of sequentially interdependent stages which leave little room for parallel operations. It is no longer appropriate to consider the individual strategies of the separate organizations in the supply chain because the nature of the interdependencies between them means that coordinated changes are needed to achieve quick response systems which work. For example there is serial dependence between the mills, Textiles and Chain Store for the flow of goods, that is product manufacture is followed by distribution and retail in a sequential process. However in production planning and retail sales forecasting there are reciprocal dependencies between Textiles and Chain Store because there is an on-going exchange of information such as initial retail sales estimate to drive the production plan, initial retail sales data, revised production plans and finished stock data and so on. Similarly for new product design the process is reciprocal with a complex exchange of ideas concerning choices of style, colour and fabric.

## New product design and logistics processes

For a reduction in time for the design phase Textiles has implemented an internal computer aided design (CAD) system. As well as assisting in the design process itself it also allows new garment designs to be printed for visual inspection in order to communicate ideas and colour concepts to potential customers. Separate samples of cloth are used to convey texture information. Previously a mill would take five to six weeks to weave a new design. By using the CAD process new design concepts and colours can be shared in less than 24 hours. There is a lot of potential to extend CAD concepts to an interorganizational basis. Buyers could look at new designs in three-dimensional models on a high resolution monitor and choose specific styles to be printed and made into a pilot garment within hours. This could of course be done in the retailing organization as long as it had the technology. Similarly the mills could use a common CAD forum to promote new cloth designs and once a pilot design was passed for production the mills would have immediate access to the new design.

Logistics management has been driven by Chain Store and has helped it to monitor suppliers' production performances against targets, reduce stock levels and increase the accuracy of orders and deliveries. Initiatives in shared CAD systems and exchange of dye information are being implemented by companies lower down the supply chain and potentially have more strategic benefits in terms of reducing the overall cycle time for a new product launch. Textiles is a leading company in this area and has two IOS applications under development. These are the exchange of dye information between Textiles, Dye Company and another manufacturer and the sharing of CAD designs between Textiles and its trading partners.

For colour flexibility the current system is to send the garments and a printed document together from a knitwear factory to the dye factory and there is a one to two week cycle time from sending the plain garments to receiving the finished coloured product. A new standard has been developed to send dye information electronically. This has now been achieved between Textiles Knitwear (England) and its dye company. Other manufacturers such as Sock Company are now being added to the system. The main benefit from this arrangement is the postponement of the colouring decision until the last possible moment. Textiles receives sales data from Chain Store by facsimile but Chain Store could easily send the colouring information directly to the dye company. Another possibility is for Textiles to have access to retail sales data and use that to make colouring decisions. These possibilities are only now being explored and the outcome will arise from organizational choice rather than any predetermined impact of the IOS.

## Cooperative strategies to support supply chain business processes

The need for cooperative behaviour has been recognized and is reflected in the setting up of special initiatives that examine ways of enabling Chain Store, its suppliers including Textiles and the mills to work together better. The processes of new product design encompass all activities that take place, from the conception of a new design idea to its full scale production. Logistics management processes is composed of the activities that take place between the factory gate and retail which are principally stock management and distribution. Up until now the focus has been to improve the management of product flows between Textiles and Chain Store and several innovations have been implemented. The first of these which is already implemented is access to manufacturer's stock data by Chain Store so that it is able to monitor suppliers' stock positions and request stock directly without having to liaise with a sales department. In addition to stock data becoming more transparent from the manufacturer to the retailer, Chain Store also shares plans and retail sales analyses reports with Textiles in an effort to re-balance production in a dynamic response to changing retail market conditions.

In other retailing companies, for example General Electric and Eastman Kodak, sharing of data between the retailer and the manufacturer has been extended to the sharing of software to allow more flexibility and closer organizational integration based on common IS. Chain Store and Textiles have the potential to evolve their shared data systems to more integrated IS. The trend towards shared IS between companies to support cooperative strategies is also evident in other industries such as automotive, computing and banking (Holland and Lockett, 1993; Holland et al, 1994), travel (Clemons and Row, 1992a, 1992b) and health care (Short and Venkatraman, 1992).

The strategy of the supply chain is to move towards a more flexible structure which both reduces the overall cycle time to produce a new style of garment and introduces the possibility of changing production volumes mid-season from Chain Store's original forecast to actual customer demand. However there are inherent constraints to more flexibility. Once a design is agreed it cannot be changed midway through a season. However, within a season sales data can potentially be used to change production in terms of volumes and colours of garments. The CAD developments are therefore aimed at reducing the time to market a new product while logistics and dye information exchange applications are aimed at improving the volume and colour flexibility within a fashion season. Each of these IOS application strategies and their benefits are considered in turn.

There are three main IOS applications. These are logistics management throughout the supply chain, exchange of dye information between Textiles, other garment manufacturers and Dye Company, and a CAD system developed by Textiles which has data links with Chain Store. These applications are considered as a group because they have many common elements such as a strategic philosophy of quick response, similar IT links and all of them are a part of the overall changes which are occurring in business relationships and market context.

## IOS applications (quick response systems)

Chain Store has implemented electronic links for logistics management with all of its suppliers. Logistics management is therefore well established and is now entering into its second stage with improvements to Chain Store's internal IS which are having a knock-on effect with their suppliers. The exchange of dye information between Textiles, its dying company and other manufacturers is now fully operational and is the preferred way of doing business. A CAD system developed by Textiles is now being extended to include Chain Store, initially by data exchange links between Textiles and Chain Store and the possibilities are only now being explored. In this paper the term CAD is used to mean all computer applications which aid the design of a new product from its initial concept to its manufacturing design. In an interorganizational context this could include for example, exchange of sketches between designers and buyers, visual modelling and pattern cutting algorithms. The definition of the term CAD is given to make its meaning and use in this paper explicit in order to overcome the lack of agreed nomenclature between managers in different parts of the textile supply chain, managers from different industries and academics. Therefore the definition may be different to those used by individual managers in the textile industry.

Information flows and loci of decision making. The IOS for logistics connects Chain Store with immediate suppliers to gain access to their stock position and for placing orders (see Figure 2). This has changed the purchasing process from a vague art — 'We would like some of this product, some of that colour' to a precise arrangement. Orders now specify product code, colour, quantity, delivery time and location. Products are pulled through the supply chain with Chain Store controlling decision making concerning stock movement based on open access to suppliers' stock data. Suppliers receive plans and retail sales analyses from Chain Store's buyer groups.

For the exchange of dye information the information flows are shown in Figure 3. In Phase 1 the manual printout of dye information referring to and accompanying a specific shipment is replaced by an electronic message containing the same information: product specification, quantity and colouring information. Although Knitwear (England) and Dye Company are both part of Textiles group, they operate autonomously for most purposes. This cannot therefore be described as true EDI (ie crossing organizational boundaries) but it is nonetheless a forerunner to a more comprehensive IOS incorporating customers, suppliers and competitors.

The second phase is more complex because of the addition of Knitwear (Scotland) which acts as the marketing interface to Chain Store and the inclusion of Sock Company which is another manufacturer that wishes to use the dye facility. Although the exchange of dye information is only at an early stage it can be seen that there are no technical barriers to Chain Store choosing colours for knitwear products on a daily basis. For example, it is conceivable that Chain Store could have a direct link to the dye company so that it could send colouring information directly to them once it was in a position to choose the mix of colours which it wanted. Another possibility is to use EDI links with dye suppliers for ordering new dye. This would make the exchange of information for the dye supply chain completely electronic.

The CAD system being developed by Textiles is an interactive one. It is therefore not possible to draw information flows in the same way as say for product ordering. However a schematic diagram of the proposed system is shown in Figure 4. This will be a truly shared and open system (although each organization will use the application for different purposes). For Textiles, CAD will assist their designers in developing new styles. Chain Store will be able to inspect new designs, request changes and have a sample garment made in a few hours for inspection purposes which would be accurate in every respect except the type of material. Similarly the mills would be able to assess the design of the cloth and commence its manufacture as soon as a pilot garment was passed for full scale production. CAD between Chain Store and Textiles incorporating all aspects of new product development including initial concepts, sketches, three-dimensional visualization of garments in full colour and specific cutting designs for manufacturing is potentially the most significant IOS application in reducing the product development time for a new garment.

Information technology link. Currently information is exchanged through an electronic mail-box connection supported by a third party network. The factors considered important in the choice of network provider were security, reliability, integrity and control in order to maintain the Chain Store's quality image to other companies in the supply chain. For shared CAD systems direct links which are capable of carrying video signal data are being planned. This would mean that although the CAD system may be based in Textiles' design centre it could also be used by buyers from Chain Store's offices.

## Market context

Chain Store is very ambitious with its plans and has already implemented IOSs for logistics management with almost all its suppliers. The control over stock management has moved distinctly towards Chain Store because it has access to suppliers' stock data. Products are 'pulled' through the supply chain. The plan to extend the pull concept to production of garments and even raw materials raises several questions. To what extent will small and medium-sized suppliers allow Chain Store control over their enterprises, in particular their production processes? For example, yarn producers often belong to a much larger organizational unit. Will the parent organization allow a part of itself to be controlled by a customer who is not that important except to a very small part of its business?

Chain Store is using IOSs to manage closer relationships with a very small number of suppliers. By accessing the stock position of finished goods suppliers they are integrating the IS with their own. This open sharing of data also makes it feasible to monitor supplier performance against annual contracts which specify proportions of different sizes, styles etc which was very difficult using a traditional trading relationship. In parallel with logistics the processes of design and dye information exchange are also being replaced by electronic links. Contrary to Malone's expectation that flexible manufacturing trends such as increased use of CAD would decrease asset specificity (Malone et al., 1987), in this case it is integrating further the whole of the supply chain for the purpose of design.

The study of Textiles and Chain Store is an example of the use of IOSs to support more integrative business relationships between organizations based on the enhanced capabilities of information exchange offered by the new technology. The emerging market structure is a network of tightly integrated organizations that cooperate together to form a supply chain entity. Chain Store is trying to control particular business activities of companies lower down the supply chain, eg yarn producers and dye manufacturers. Yarn production is often only a small part of a much larger organizational unit and therefore in the debate on electronic markets and hierarchies it is not sufficient to just consider organizational units, but suborganizational units as well. For example, a multi-product organization is likely to have different sub-units linked into different hierarchies or markets. In the garment industry it appears that manufacturers and mills will have to choose a small group of customer organizations and work very closely with them. Competition will then be conducted between product market supply chains rather than individual organizational units.

It is plausible to extend this concept of competition between supply chains to other industries because many of the product market characteristics and strategies of Chain Store and Textiles also apply to other types of markets and organizations. A significant implication for large multi-product organizations is that they are likely to attach different parts of their businesses to different supply chain groupings. More generally, specific product groups will be managed by integrated chains of organizations arranged hierarchically from raw material producers to retailers. The advantages of these ‘virtual organizations’ over market style arrangements are an ability to effect coordinated responses to external changes in the product market and the ability to control business processes throughout the supply chain which leads to improvements in quality and cost control.

The developments in market context outlined in this paper are being driven by the long term commitment of Chain Store's management to a key group of suppliers in an effort to attain superior quality to their competitors. This collaborative approach is deemed to be necessary in order to coordinate activities throughout the supply chain rather than rely on autonomous adaptation at each other's expense.

## Discussion

This study of a textile supply chain illustrates the evolution of an IOS network strategy, that is, a network of organizations cooperating to develop IOSs for a wide range of business processes to support close and integrative links between organizations at successive stages of the textile supply chain. The main advantages achieved from this arrangement are quicker responses to market changes both for product replenishment and launches of new styles, and preservation of high quality because quality can be planned and monitored closely throughout the supply chain and hence throughout the whole of the product life-cycle from initial sketch designs, fabric and garment design through to final retail sale. Innovative strategies involving shared CAD systems and fast response for changes in colour fashions demonstrate clearly the advantages of mills, manufacturers and a retailing organization working closely together. It is also apparent that the supply chain management strategy is still changing and that organizational choices over issues such as control and location of decision making for design, production and distribution will need to be addressed. Central to these issues is access to information. An obvious trend is towards increased transparency of information, for example Chain Store is benefiting from suppliers' stock data and Textiles receives Chain Store's plans and retail sales analyses. Similarly Textiles controls the CAD data and is planning to give access to trading partners. There are many possibilities and their appraisal has only just begun. However the overall direction is towards open and honest sharing of all IS, software and data, and a common core of IS to be used by all parts of the supply chain seems probable. In this scenario the importance of organizational boundaries diminishes and strategy is understood better by considering the behaviour of the supply chain in addition to the individual firm strategies. The critical role of IOSs in the implementation of cooperative strategies has also been demonstrated by Clemons and Row (1992b) in their study of an international alliance in the travel industry and by Short and Venkatraman (1992) in their study of the health care market and in particular Baxter International.

## Proposed model and comparison with literature

A significant part of the strategies employed by the companies in the study is to cooperate with each other. This also involves other companies in the supply chain such as mills which produce the cloth. It is the strategies of these firms to arrange themselves in a hierarchical structure. IOSs are being used to support these relationships which are characterized by mutal adaptation and which reduce competition in terms of open tendering. The composite effect of these individual strategies is to move the organizations closer together resulting in vertical integration but without ownership. They each 'own' a small part of each of the other organizations in the supply chain, not necessarily realizing what is happening to them. This ownership is not expressed in equity terms but by influence and shared responsibility. This pattern of behaviour falls into the category of hierarchical market structures. A causal feedback model is proposed that depicts the relationships between the retail market, cooperative strategies and the formation of a supply chain structure (see Figure 5).

The logic of the proposed model is that in business markets the interplay between business strategies and IOSs is leading to supply chains for specific product groups which are comprised of integrated business processes across organizational boundaries. Cooperation between organizations to manage logistical, financial, technical and design interdependencies means that a product market supply chain behaves as one unit — the competition between individual organizational units diminishes and is replaced by competition between supply chains. IOSs are being used to manage the interdependencies between organizations more effectively to make the supply chain as a whole more effective. This has been achieved through increased organizational and technical integration across organizational boundaries. Some of the benefits accrued from this arrangement are reduced total cycle time, coordinated responses to external changes (ie outside the product market supply chain), better quality and reduced costs. A major implication of this model is that competition will occur more between product market supply chains than between individual organizations. The resulting market structure from this model is electronic hierarchies in which organizations position themselves in product market supply chains which are vertically integrated but without ownership.

The proposed model and case data support the electronic hierarchies theory (Johnston and Lawrence, 1988; Johnston and Vitale, 1988; Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990; Holland et al, 1992) and contradicts the electronic markets theory of Malone et al (1987, 1989) and Malone and Rockart (1992). The individual organizations within the textile supply chain are implementing IOSs to support cooperative relationships with a small number of trading partners. The model extends the closely related concepts of vertical integration without ownership (Holland et al, 1992), value-added partnerships (Johnston and Lawrence, 1988) and information partnerships (Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990) by detailing a mechanism that explains how product market demand characteristics, business and IOS strategies, and supply chain structures are related to each other. This model also predicts the emergence of integrated supply chains for particular product groups. The strategic implications for organizations are that they should think beyond the boundaries of their own organizations to consider the potential of applying IOSs to design integrated business processes throughout the supply chain. Competitive advantages are more likely to accrue from improvements in supply chain performance rather than from innovations made by individual organizations.

## Conclusions

The case studies of two large organizations in a textile supply chain indicate the emergence of an integrated product market supply chain consisting of several hierarchically arranged organizations. It therefore supports the electronic hierarchies theory rather than the electronic markets theory. A causal feedback model is proposed that describes the causal mechanisms for the formation of an integrated supply chain. The retail market characteristics of rapid changes in demand owing to fashion changes and the requirement of high product quality mean that a coordinated response is needed from Chain Store, Textiles, other garment manufacturers supplying Chain Store and mills supplying the cloth. Electronic markets would therefore be inappropriate in this business market because autonomous adaptation by each individual firm would be sub-optimal for the performance of the supply chain as a whole and would also incur substantial maladaptation costs (Williamson, 1991). These organizations have therefore chosen to cooperate with each other in the management of business processes that run throughout the supply chain. Cooperative strategies are driving the implementation of interorganizational systems (IOSs) and this can be seen in several business strategies enabled by the innovative application of IS, eg quick response systems and the shared use of CAD to reduce the product life-cycle. The close linking of business processes between individual organizations based on shared IOSs leads to the emergence of an integrated supply chain that behaves as a single strategic entity and can effect a coordinated response to changes in the retail market. Competition between individual organizations is not taking place electronically but instead IOSs are being used to reduce the level of competition between suppliers and customers in a common supply chain. Competition is now taking place between separate supply chains rather than between individual organizations. Future research will be carried out on the structure of competing supply chains and on the dynamics of competition in electronic business markets.
