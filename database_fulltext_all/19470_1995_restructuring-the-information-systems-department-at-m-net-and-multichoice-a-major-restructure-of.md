# Restructuring the Information Systems Department at M-Net and Multichoice: a major restructure of an IS department

Frances Sutherland and Dan Remenyi

Graduate School of Information Systems, Department of Information Systems, University of the Witwatersrand, Johannesburg, South Africa. email: 035dans@mentor.edcm.wits.ac.za; 035fzss@mentor.edcm.wits.ac.za

This case study reports on how an Information Systems Department undertook a major restructuring of its activities in order to ensure that its processes came into line with a new client-oriented ethos. This was undertaken as a response to demands from the clients as well as in anticipation of a major restructuring of the organization itself. In other words the Information Systems Department felt it essential to get their own house in order before they could offer advice or participate in helping the overall organization reengineer.

Keywords: information systems, restructuring, entertainment network

In October 1986 M-Net launched the first subscription television network in the Southern Hemisphere. By February 1994, the organization boasted in an advertisement that it had nearly one million subscribers. Within just under eight years the service had expanded to become Southern Africa's premier entertainment network and one of the leading subscription-TV services outside North America. In the words of one of its senior IT executives, 'Growth at M-Net has been phenomenal'.

The main growth has been in the number of subscribers and this can clearly be seen in some of the key financial statistics which have been extracted from M-Net's public accounts and which are shown in Table 1. These financial statistics clearly demonstrate considerable success in increasing turnover each year. However profit after tax was not so consistent and after peaking in 1991 the earnings per share (EPS) declined and levelled out. Therefore from an investor point of view the phenomenal growth has not been accompanied by the same level of earnings or EPS.

## Problems faced by the management of the Information Systems Department

However the Information Systems (IS) Department was seriously concerned that it would not be capable of keeping pace with the growth of the organization. This concern first became apparent in 1991 when the M-Net acquired Information Trust Corporation (ITC) which is South Africa's largest supplier of business and credit information. The acquisition of ITC presented the IS Department with two major problems.

Firstly the IS Department's structure was ill equipped to handle diversification of the business. Started in 1986 as a small group, the traditional multi-level hierarchical structure of the IS Department was based on three internal departments: applications, technical, and operations. There were up to seven levels of control within each department. In the words of one senior IS executive: 'It grew in a haphazard way from three small environments to three large departmental empires which were not co-ordinated'.

Each department had its own manager and between 20 and 30 staff. However, 90 per cent of this staff complement was made up of contractors since the skills required were in short supply in South Africa at that time, skilled staff therefore gained higher salaries by contracting their services. Also the business grew so rapidly that the IS required to serve the business needs were frequently developed under conditions where time was critical. Only by employing a core group of experienced contract staff was the IS Department's manager able to meet business deadlines for new information systems. One of the most business-critical systems was the subscriber management system which recorded all M-Net subscribers and billed them for services. The General IS manager was informed of the IS Department's activity by the three departmental managers. This hierarchical structure is shown in Figure 1.

The reporting procedures which usually accompany such a hierarchical structure were strictly adhered to within this company. With the rapid rate of growth in IS services, this structure was not found to be conducive to satisfactory customer service and flexibility. The systems development times were too long, especially for a high-tech organization such as M-Net. As the company grew the need for new skills became critical. Apart from problems associated with the skills gap, the General IS Manager was working at a high strategic level with a gulf between him and the managers of the departments. This gap between the strategic and the operational levels within the IS Department became intolerable when new departments were added to the division in order to cope with diversification of the business.

The second problem which surfaced in 1991 was related to the focus of the IS Department. Since its inception the IS Department had been a cost centre, providing IS services within the organization. This approach had been effective whilst the major business growth was derived from increasing the subscription service. As the subscriber base grew the information system developed to manage business growth both in the numbers of transactions generated by a growing subscriber base, and in the geographical dispersion of subscribers which entailed collecting money from subscribers in neighbouring countries where different currencies and different exchange controls existed. As the company's billing system the subscription management system (SMS) was so crucial to the business that IS activities were scheduled to prioritize any support or development surrounding that system. The IS Department believed that their development and support of the

SMS was fundamental to the value they were able to provide to the business. Not only was the IS Department ill equipped to offer a good service to its new client, Information Trust Corporation, but that client was also a high-tech service provider and would demand exceptional service from the IS Department. The arrival of this external client required the IS Department to reconsider their role within the organization.

## Options open to the IS Department's management

At that time it would have been feasible for the IS Department to simply extend their existing systems development and support activities to include ITC. This could have been achieved either through creating a new leg on the hierarchical structure for ITC, or by hiring contract staff and effectively outsourcing the ITC workload. Alternatively since ITC had its own in-house IS Department, it would have been possible for M-Net's IS staff to ignore the impact of the new acquisition. The new company's IS Department could have continued to function as a separate entity with no interaction with M-Net beyond acknowledgement of their existence within the same group of companies.

The General IS Manager of IS felt that to either outsource ITC's information requirements to a third party, or to leave their ISD functioning apart would conflict with the corporate mission to provide an internally consistent information service to their customers. He thus decided to treat their first external client, ie ITC, as an opportunity to refocus the role of the IS Department towards customer service. The overwhelming criterion in deciding the ultimate course of action was the desire to make the IS Department incorporate the whole group.

## Restructuring of the IS Department

Two months earlier Ric Gordinho, the General IS Manager, had engaged a firm of IS Management Consultants to identify the most appropriate strategic direction for M-Net's IS Department. Brian Henry was the Senioro Consultant assigned to this task, he now accepted a full-time position with M-Net as the Senior Account Manager for IDC. His role was to manage the integration of the IDC account into M-Net's IS Department. However, Ric Gordinho and Brian Henry decided that a complete re-appraisal of the Information Systems Division was necessary. Their first concern was the potential for a conflict of interests which could create problems in terms of the business acceptance of the new IS Department. Whilst ITC represented their first third party customer, the subscription management system (SMS) was fundamental to the IS Department's existence within the firm. As Brian Henry commented: 'We immediately had two major clients who both had critical systems. We could not trade one off against the other. The conflict was ours'.

Clearly the IS Department had to become a profit centre and compete with outside organizations for the custom of user departments within the firm. Thus, the user departments did not need to utilize the services of the internal IS Department if they could obtain better services or prices elsewhere outside the organization. The general philosophy behind this move was that the IS Department had to become efficient, cost effective and customer focused. As a result of these three new objectives the IS Department has made many attempts to improve their effectiveness.

In October 1991 it was decided to perform a major reorganization of the

IS Department within M-Net. The corporate merger resulted in the two IS Departments of M-Net and ITC being combined which necessitated a large investment in IT:

After buying ITC we faced a relatively major IT equipment upgrade. We put the M-Net system and the system at ITC into one site on one mainframe which centralized the whole IS function. It was necessary since ITC had not upgraded for 10 years and were in a holding pattern whereas M-Net was leading edge technology. They were our first customer and they took our first years' growth in terms of capacity planning.

No sooner had the new Information Systems Division been set up than complaints were expressed about the service provided to ITC. At this early stage in the evolution of the new Information Systems Division it became clear that they would have to become more customer focused.

## The IS Department's vision and values

The re-appraisal process began towards the end of 1992 with the establishment of the Information Systems Division's Vision and Values. The Vision and Values were seen as the cornerstone on which the IS Department could establish its direction and build its strategy which had to function within the overall corporate strategy. In 1992 the corporate mission statement read:

M-Net's mission is to provide selected target markets with information and entertainment services by combining electronic technologies with quality customer care, and to turn each service into a profitable business within a reasonable time.

Clearly at that time the company used a broad definition of information, was expecting to develop as an information supplier, that could take the form of business-related information or entertainment. Certainly the management of ITC regarded this as the company's mission and thus felt that their credit services business could be enhanced through association with M-Net. The IS Department decided to take a far more holistic approach and prepare themselves to offer services to any type of customer with which the company might wish to engage, as their following vision statement shows:

VISION: M-Net's IS Department will be a leading provider of world-class IT-based solutions aimed at enabling our customers to provide excellent service and succeed within their markets.

The vision would be appropriate for any company to which the IS function was outsourced, and clearly re-focused the IS Department's perspective from being an in-house cost centre to becoming an externally oriented profit centre. In order to provide their staff with an understanding of the new ethos within the department the vision was expanded into a set of values which were clearly defined by the management team. The values and their definitions are shown in Table 2. However, the vision and values statements were created by the original IS Department's management team who worked on the strategy of the proposed new division for over a year. To some extent the values are autocratic in that the management team said, 'We have to change the culture, we represent it, we must decide what we want'. They decided to stop managing teams and start managing individuals.

## Technological statement of direction

The new structure consisted of new management areas whereby new managers were appointed and staff were re-arranged in order to flatten the structure. By identifying functional teams in the environment it became clear that there were five teams which could operate independently of each other. When the IS Department became a profit centre the finance and administration department became a sixth functional team.

A statement of technological direction was also prepared. This document states that ‘We will develop distributed applications encompassing Client/Server technology and Object Orientation’ and goes on to define the strategic components of the application platform technology, the network architecture, connectivity architecture, office systems architecture and application development architecture. This statement of direction is attached to all service level agreements which form the contractual basis for IS Department activity within the organization.

## Two new corporate strategies

In June 1993 M-Net was split into two companies, MultiChoice-Kaleidoscope and M-Net. Such a major organizational change could have presented serious problems for the role of the IS Department within the organization. The IS Department became part of Multichoice-Kaleidoscope whose mission is:

To establish the company as a global home entertainment, communication and subscriber management services provider. We will achieve this by combining electronic technologies with quality customer care.

Multichoice-Kaleidoscope has interests in six companies, including subscriber management services, communications technologies providing satellite technology to Africa and the Indian Ocean islands, cellular telephony investments and investments in entertainment technologies outside Africa.

M-Net is now focused purely on providing programming for the network, as their mission statement explains:

M-Net's mission is to develop into a leading provider of subscription television entertainment on the continent of Africa and its adjacent islands. We will achieve this by entertaining viewers in our target market with exclusive, innovative and provocative programming, using leading edge technology and creative marketing.

On the Johannesburg Stock Exchange, shares in the two companies will continue to be traded as a single linked share until the directors are satisfied that an arm's length relationship has been achieved, when they will be delinked.

## Management areas

The major restructuring of M-Net coincided with the IS Department becoming a profit centre. The fact that the IS Department's mission, vision and values were fully aligned with these two new corporate missions was due to the foresight of the IS Department in 1991. As a result the role of the IS Department was only marginally affected by the restructuring of the whole organization. The IS management team had to educate their staff to think competitively, and to work as members of a profit centre rather than as cost centre staff. This had been their fundamental objective since 1991 and the education of IS staff had been driven by the concept of ‘multi-squared’. Their objective is to provide IS services to multi-customers with multi-currencies and multi-needs, thus the term multi-squared, and re-organization of the IS Department was necessary to achieve this end.

The multi-squared concept was published in June 1993 and verbally communicated to a few senior people but it was not well received due to the uncertainty of what the impact on the IS Department would actually be. After some meetings to discuss the implementation of these ideas, it was decided that by eliminating a few levels of the hierarchy, and by reorganizing employees into empowered teams the improvements which the department needed would be achieved. This process began by creating teams which would work much more closely together and not necessarily have to comply with the previous strict hierarchy. Thus the IS Department grouped certain divisions together and eliminated others as depicted in Figure 2. To move from being a cost centre to a profit centre Service Level Agreements (SLAs) were introduced to record. The new structure consists of management areas (MA) under a general IS manager. The costing and contractual agreements between the IS Department and the rest of the organization. Together these divisions made up the newly restructured ID Department.

The terms ‘task group’, ‘project office’ and ‘value added office’ used in Figure 2 are explained in detail below in conjunction with their responsibility areas. In addition to the creation of teams the IS Department was reorganized on a matrix basis which allows individuals to be responsible to more than one manager at the same time. This created a much more flexible environment in the IS Department since experienced staff can now participate in development work whilst also assisting with the maintenance of production systems should the need arise.

## Problems experienced with the change

When undertaking a substantial reorganization project such as M-Net has done, the main problems arise not in deciding on what and who must change but in the actual process of how to effect such change. M-Net's plans for the IS Department meant breaking down old work patterns and then transforming them. Problems were experienced in both the breaking down and the building up. Brian Henry suggested that these problems fall into three distinct categories of communication issues, culture shock and changed behaviour due to profit centre requirements. These are discussed in detail below.

## Communication issues

Before embarking on the change, M-Net did a comprehensive study of who was talking to whom, both within the department and in terms of customer relationships. A chart was drawn up which showed the communication levels between IS staff. This study was invaluable to the senior managers in enabling them to identify people with good communication skills, people with strong relationships with the customers and thereby helped them in identifying the new divisions. For example, the Help desk had apparently been under the control of the technical manager but, after the relationship charting was completed it became clear that over 90 per cent of the customers gained help from two people in the system software section. Similarly it was discovered that a person in the LAN section gained understanding of his work from staff in the software division. By reviewing his position it was deemed that he was more important to the software section and his job description was changed.

Some of the findings revealed unhealthy groupings which did not work for the benefit of the whole. These were referred to as gangs. According to M-Net, 'A gang is a group of people who exist to the exclusion of everyone else'.

There is a lot of peer pressure within a gang. In order to achieve effective change there is a need to destroy all gangs since they resist change. They do not allow for flexibility and members tend to listen to gang leaders rather than authority outside the gang. Many of the problems associated with the change in the IS Department came from breaking down gangs and restructuring them into a group which is defined by M-Net as a group of people who complement each other in their work. Thus when working as a group, people do excellent work for the benefit of the company. Since restructuring the permanent staff the IS Department has reduced from over 90 to 78. Expansion is likely to be covered by contract staff.

## Culture shock

It is interesting to note that the main driving forces behind the IS strategy were experienced IS consultants. The importance of this was highlighted when one of the new senior managers stated

I come from a consultancy and I regard this as a project. The General IS Manager knows that I plan to be here for a maximum of three years to set up the new infrastructure. If that remains after I leave then he will know that I was successful.

This management approach has been called the ‘task culture’ by Handy (1989) who suggests that this attitude is highly relevant in businesses which are seeking reorganization since

Management is seen as the continuous and successful solution of problems . . . It draws resources from various parts of the organization in order to focus them on a particular knot, or problem. The organization is a network of loosely linked commando units, each unit being largely self-contained but with a specific responsibility within an overall strategy.

There is general agreement amongst the managers of the IS Department that they did not get their staff to fully buy into the vision and values generated two years before the restructuring occurred. This had led to situations close to mutiny. Possibly it was a case of task culture people working with staff who preferred a role culture. One example should suffice to illustrate this conflict.

We lost all our MIS statistics. Prior to June 1993 there were statistics on every operational activity, service level, every possible statistic you could think of was being generated — in fact there was an abundance of management information. After the restructuring was implemented that information just disappeared off the face of the earth. I am still trying to work out what happened. For 3 months we literally could not tell you what we were doing.

The management information technically existed but it required certain backup and manipulation procedures in order for the statistics to be extracted. The statistics included such vital information as service level agreement commitments in terms of downtime, uptime and use of communications, the quantity of requests for service performed and in which part of the business the IS Department's concentration was. The IS Department runs its business on requests for service (RFS) and there is a job card for every activity. In most entities or functional areas this process just disappeared except for one application where it was so well entrenched that it stayed. The senior managers suggest that the main problem was human nature.

If you are not happy with the process which is occurring then one way to make a comment is to stop the flow of information.

They believe that probably less than 2 per cent of their staff are consciously resisting the restructure, but at least 50 per cent are subconsciously resisting the change. They are the people who have been in the IS Department for five or more years and were extremely comfortable in the old structure. Most people in IS Department are aware of where the department is going and why, but they are not so sure of where they personally are going within that changing environment. The managers have not managed the gap and that is a critical management issue. They suggest that an inordinate amount of time needs to be spent simply listening to staff, hearing their concerns and then leading the staff towards the new goals. It is fundamentally an issue of providing good flows of communication within the division.

## Profit centre requirements

Another major problem was identified by the new manager of Finance and Administration who suggested that

For this IS Department the concept of being a profit centre is a bit of an anomaly since we need to make a profit whilst ensuring that our customers get the best service at the most economic price.

The complexity of evaluating the IS process within any business function is being handled by a qualified account who refuses to allow projects to go ahead until the business chief executive officer (CEO) concerned has signed the contract. One of the duties of the six account managers is to collect money from these CEOs. Clearly this is a new experience for staff, many of whom were employed as skilled IT technicians.

## Lessons which were learnt by M-Net management

1. Despite the high rate of technological change which characterizes their profession, IS staff are surprisingly resistant to modifying their working environment. The IS managers found this surprising as they believed that their staff, who were leading-edge IT professionals, should have been more comfortable with change. Their staff had regularly attended technical training courses and were used to learning and using new technologies. However this ability to adapt to new technologies did not extend to competence when coping with a redefinition of their role within the organization.

Therefore, like any other professional staff, special attention must be given to helping IT staff cope with change.

2. Reengineering is a process of change and change leads to more change, which means that there is no clear completion point. In this environment, staff were required to move from working within a highly controlled hierarchical structure to a transparent, fluid teamwork structure. The skills which this demanded of the staff had not been encouraged or developed by the previous reporting system. Teamwork requires a totally new attitude towards work whereby all staff are encouraged to interact with greater openness. Therefore restructuring projects such as the one described above need to be seen as a long term, continuous adaptation to the environment.

3. The changes in the working environment will be too much for certain members of staff to cope with or accept and thus some people will leave. Managers can contain the risk of losing key staff in this way by clearly identifying which people are in reality key to the functioning of the IS Department, and why that is so. It is suggested that managers should identify at least two groups of key personnel. One group is those staff who provide the organization with internal IS skills. They are key to maintaining operations and have become important often through long-term experience with the operating environment. Whilst the loss of their services will affect the IS Department, if sound documentation practices are adopted prior to changing the environment much of the in-house knowledge can be retained. The second key group is made up of those people who have shown a natural aptitude for networking with people both within the IS Department and with the users or clients. This group can be identified through tools such as netmapping which was used in this case, or other group dynamic methods which clearly pinpoint people with good communication skills across interorganizational boundaries. These people may provide a key to change management since their natural inter-relational skills will be valuable in developing transparent teams. They have the potential to lead the way by example.

4. Staff must buy-in to the new processes both from a technological and a cultural point of view. Much resistance can be avoided if staff are given opportunities to voice their concerns in a safe environment. The irony in this case is that the staff who currently feel threatened by the new environment fear that they may be replaced, whereas the management have fully recognized that they cannot easily hire new staff to replace the old since the new job specifications are not yet clearly understood by the organization.

## Conclusions

The concept of gangs versus groups is enlightening since it reveals the importance of procedures, rules and clearly prescribed roles which Handy (1989) refers to as the ‘role culture’ so frequently seen in organizations with over one hundred staff members. In the M-Net case although the number of people involved was much smaller, the gang approach to change emerged in the form of subversive behaviour. Senior managers who took responsibility for the change process recognized the potentially destructive nature of this and created groups to break down the gang mentality. This worked extremely effectively.

The senior managers of the IS Department are generally pleased with progress towards the adoption of their strategy. They feel that in terms of handling the adjustment of their staff they are two years into the problem and about halfway towards achieving their objectives. Although the process of encouraging honesty and commitment from their staff has been ‘painful’ they are confident that their

## strategy was correct. As one stated

The restructures of both the IS Department and the business were only linked because of the foresight of the original strategy. This multi-squared approach was envisioned and design works started three years before Multichoice was created or even considered. From a business planning perspective we were actually ahead of the game for the entire organization.

The IS Department of M-Net is a department which is continually growing and changing. As new opportunities for growth are identified, new task groups are assigned to explore the possibilities for economic development. Interestingly the ITC investment which triggered the restructuring of the IS Department has now been removed from the corporate strategy. During 1992, ITC felt that their corporate objectives were not aligned with those of M-Net, a perception which has been corroborated by the 1994 mission statement where the word information has been replaced with communication. Multichoice-Kaleidoscope is currently investing heavily in cellular telephony in competition with the Government-sponsored providers. ITC thus sought a purchaser and have now become part of a large US organization. Of their M-Net experiences one senior manager at ITC said:

We found out that outsourcing our IT was feasible. We now concentrate on data gathering and information distribution, leaving the technical details of IT to an external company.

The culture of the new IS Department at Multichoice is one of conscious evolution, a time will come when the current major reorganization plans are completed, yet change will still occur since it is fundamental to the new philosophy which drives the managers of this IS Department.
