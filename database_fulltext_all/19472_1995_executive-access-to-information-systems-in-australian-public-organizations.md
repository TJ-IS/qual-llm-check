# Executive access to information systems in Australian public organizations

Helen Hasan and Stephanie Lampitsi

Department of Business Systems, The University of Wollongong, Northfields Ave, Wollongong 2522, Australia

Senior executives in all organizations require information for strategic decision making and are becoming increasingly aware of the importance of the quality of their information systems. There is a continual increase in the numbers of executives who are directly accessing computerized information, however, when they do, there are significant consequences throughout the organization. Information systems personnel are often sceptical of the value of executive use of information technology and fear that increased managerial scrutiny may highlight flaws and deficiencies in ‘their’ systems. Managers of functional production and support units, who see themselves as ‘owners’ of data in systems under their control, are disturbed by the prospect that executives will have on-line access to organizational data. This paper reports the findings of a multiple case study of executive information systems, and executive computer use, in medium-sized Australian public organizations. As top management becomes more directly involved in the organization’s information systems, problems with the information flow around the organization are highlighted. At the same time the opportunity arises to improve the alignment of the organization’s information systems with strategic organizational goals. Management must then decide who should be responsible for the content and delivery of computer based information for strategic control and decision making.

Keywords: public organizations, management information, executive information systems (EIS), strategic goals

'The CEO goes on-line' (Rockart and Treacy, 1982) is a catch phrase that has echoed through the past decade and has evoked much speculation on the use of computers by executives. Attempts at implementing executive information systems (EIS) in organizations have met with both success and failure, with most progress being made in the private or corporate sector (Mohan et al, 1990). A more recent report (Kraemer et al, 1993) has shown that, although computer-based information (CBI) is extremely important for most public managers, there is little use of computers by the executives themselves. Access, analysis and reporting of CBI is left to intermediaries. Where this is the case, the managers' involvement with the information environment is relatively unchanged and has no specific impact on the organization.

However, where senior executives have become actively involved in their organization's information systems (IS) this is no longer so. Our research shows that, where executives have direct computer access to the organization's information, there are significant consequences throughout the organization, particularly for IS personnel and managers of functional production units, who see themselves as ‘owners’ of data in systems under their control.

Due to their position of power within the organization, executives are a special class of computer users and the unstructured nature of managerial work is often seen to be incompatible with traditional information systems. As active users, executives place new demands on the organization's IS in terms of the scope and integration of data from sub-systems across the organization. This provides a challenge to the IS staff. The literature shows that there is no clear definition of IS for managerial use, with the terms management information systems (MIS), decision support systems (DSS), executive support systems (ESS), executive information systems (EIS) and many others being used (see for example, Millet and Mawhinney, 1992; Whymark, 1991). Our research is concerned with any computerized systems that exist in an organization to serve the information needs of senior management and the term EIS will be used in this context.

This paper discusses the results of a study undertaken in four public organizations where a number of executives are beginning to make direct use of computers in their work. Structured interviews were conducted with representatives of three groups within the organizations: firstly the executives, secondly the IS staff and thirdly the managers of functional production and support units, hence forth referred to as ‘functional unit managers’ who own the organization’s operational systems (see Hasan and Cheung, 1993; Kraemer et al, 1993). The issues addressed were: the current and planned practices for information flow to executives, the attitudes of each group to executive use of computerized information systems, and the perceived consequences of this use.

## Methodology

This project has followed a methodology adapted from that of Gable (1992) as shown in Figure 1. Following the literature review, a single pilot case study was completed on EIS requirements in one organization. The protocol used for the pilot case study involved unstructured interviews with staff at all levels of the organization together with a study of company reports and other documents. The results have already been reported (Hasan, 1992; Hasan and Gould, 1994) and used to identify questions to be addressed in the subsequent multiple case study. These questions related to problems involving information flow to executives, attitudes to executive IS use and the consequences of executive IS use. They are expressed as follows:

\- At what level of management in organizations are computerized IS used by managers and what factors affect this?

\- What IS can be used at senior levels, how do they interact with other organizational IS and how do they relate to the strategic goals of the organization?

\- What are the attitudes and concerns of executives, IS staff and functional unit managers to the introduction of EIS?

The protocol adopted for the multiple case study used structured interviews, conducted in each of the four organizations, with representatives of the three groups of interest: top management (the executives), professional IS staff and functional unit managers. In all 36 such interviews were conducted, nine with executives, nine with IS staff and 18 with functional unit managers.

The data collected were:

\- The age, education and computer experience of all participants as well as their use of computers at work and their relationship with members of the other groups.

\- The IS staff were asked to supply details of the organization's information systems and their opinion of executive computer use and information needs.

\- Executives were asked to identify their information needs, their current sources of information, the perceived quality of the information and its relevance to organizational goals.

\- Functional unit managers were asked to state their views on giving executives direct access to their systems.

\- Information such as mission statements and organizational goals, was determined from annual reports.

The data recorded at the structured interviews were collated for each organization in order to establish information that would provide answers to the questions posed above. The quantity of information collected was substantial, as each interview had lasted from 60 to 90 minutes, and the information was rich in nature due to the interest evidenced by the participants, in the topics discussed. A summary and analysis of the information gathered on each topic is reported in the following sections of the paper, which concludes with a discussion of the most significant of these.

## Organizational and IS characteristics

The organizations targeted were four Australian public organizations representing the education, health, local government and energy supply industries. They were chosen because, apart from the industry, they had several common characteristics: all consisting of around 1000 employees and responsible to a state or federal government department with imposed reporting procedures and performance indicators. They all had relatively long experience of information technology usage at the operational level with a traditional portfolio of applications such as finance, personnel (human resources), customers (or equivalent) and so on. The systems used for these operational systems were mainframe based and current versions of these systems vary in age from 2 to 12 years. The organizations are all undergoing changes in structure including changes in the position of the IS Department. Furthermore they are each tending to decentralize provision of computing facilities and the adoption of microcomputers both stand-alone and networked. They were seen to be experiencing increasing pressure of a competitive environment where there was previously a relatively secure and relaxed public service approach. There was a reported increase in professionalism of the management of the organizations as they endeavoured to become more ‘business like’.

Areas where there were differences between the organizations are depicted in Table 1. There are currently either three or four levels of management from the CEO to the heads of Functional Production Units (which includes the IS Department). At the top level there is no current or desired use of computers by any of the four CEOs but there has been a recent (1992–1993) adoption of personal computers by second tier managers in three of the organizations. This is an interesting development in light of the results of the Kraemer study (Kraemer et al, 1993) which showed from a survey conducted in 1988, that most managers in public organizations in the USA preferred indirect use of CBI. This desire for computers by executives, shown in the 1990s, may coincide with the availability of the new technologies of easy-to-use Windows $^{TM}$ interfaces together with open systems and client server capabilities giving across platform data access.

## Current sources of executive information

In the 1990s the process of management has become information intensive (Broadbent et al, 1992). The executives interviewed recognized the concept of information as an organizational resource but stated that they had not given much thought to the practical ramifications of this. They had all recently participated in the determination of their organization's mission statement and in the setting of organizational goals but had given little consideration to the alignment of IS with these goals. Only two of the executives spoke of IS as a strategic weapon and both of these had responsibility for the IS Department in their respective organizations.

All of the executives interviewed gave approximately equal weight to four sources of information: computer systems, written reports, personal contact (including telephone and email) and meetings. They stated that information from the right people was the most believable, richest and most easily questioned, however they recognized that there were problems where one key person was the source of critical information, as was often the case. Several of the executives could identify situations where important information should be available on computerized systems and therefore available at any time. However, these 'bits of information' did not seem to fit into the traditional information systems of the organization. A broader definition of IS is needed and the organization information systems (OIS) concept as proposed by Van Gigch and Le Moigne (1990) could be adopted to 'imitate the information systems processing functions of human beings'.

The question arises as to who in an organization is best qualified to be responsible for management information. In organizations A and B of our study, the co-ordination and provision of management information was seen as the province of the IS Department in conjunction with individual Functional Production Units. In the other two organizations, C and D, separate Management Information Units had been set up for this purpose. Each of these units worked closely with the IS Department but were located in a different branch of the organization, one at the executive level and one located within a lower level Planning Department. Interviews conducted in all four organizations brought to light a number of factors which were related to these arrangements.

The principle factor that determined where the Management Information Unit was located, was the type of industry in which the organization was engaged. It was in the people-oriented industries of education and health care that separate Management Information Units had been set up. In the other two organizations, Local Government and Energy Supply, there were no such units. These two organizations are in the business of producing and managing physical products, such as public utilities, and most of their senior administrative staff have a more technical background, often as engineers. It may be that these more technical managers have more faith in their ability to get information from regular IS systems.

In all four organizations those who provided formal management information, both paper-based and computerized, looked to senior management to determine what information should be produced but as a rule made their own decision as to its form. The information itself was invariably based on internal, hard data and highly summarized for executive consumption. Of the two separate Management Information Units, the one in organization D was higher in the organizational structure and only produced information in paper-based form. The other, in organization C, was developing a computerized management information system to be used mainly by middle management. In organization D the information manager was a senior executive who took part in the determination of the organization's mission statement and strategic goals. It is therefore likely that their regular paper-based reports are satisfactorily aligned with strategic needs and this may explain why there are no immediate plans for computer use by executives in this organization.

In organizations A and B, where the IS Department supplied the management information, individual tasks seemed to be allocated to an individual staff member who was usually a systems analyst. For example, one IS person was responsible for helping the Personnel Department produce its weekly report. On the other hand the information co-ordinators in the separate Management Information Units tended work as a group with one or two members having the technical expertise to access the computer data stores.

## Current executive use of CBI

This study has been concerned with the direct use of computerized systems to provide information to those at the top of the organization involved in strategic decision making. The executives interviewed at the top and second tier management levels had very similar computer user characteristics despite having varied backgrounds. Although they had no formal computer training and classified themselves as novice users, they almost all had machines at home and were aware of 'Windows type' operating systems. They exhibited no computer phobia and were well aware of the potential advantages of CBI for managers.

Up to the present time there is no reported IS use by any of the four CEOs, however in organizations A and C this may soon change. In organization A the CEO has been connected to the EIS and in organization C the present CEO is retiring. A recent survey of all Australian universities (Jorgensen, 1994) found that only 12 per cent of information used for strategic planning and decision making comes directly from computerized systems but that 72 per cent of universities had new EIS initiatives underway.

In the four organizations of the study, computer use by second tier (executive) managers is only recent (1992–1993). The main operational system, used by one-third of those interviewed, was the finance system for setting budgets and tracking expenditure. However email was by far the most popular computer application used, followed by some word-processing and spreadsheets. Although email is not usually considered an ‘information system’ we would argue that it should be classed as a most appropriate tool for the type of unstructured information that is used by executives.

The facility most requested by the second tier managers interviewed, was for current status information, in line with either government imposed performance indicators or for comparison with other organizations competing with them for government grants. They did not express any desire for systems to do data analysis or modelling, or for systems to show projected trends as reported elsewhere (Pervan, 1992). This lack of desire for strategic analysis can be accredited to the following:

\- In the survey by Pervan, 73 per cent of the responses were from private companies where the atmosphere is competitive and profit driven.

\- Most of the respondents in the Pervan survey were not senior executives but IS managers and, as we observed, their perceptions of executive information needs may differ from those expressed by the executives themselves.

\- The managers interviewed interpreted the term information system to mean traditional quantitative reporting systems and they had a limited view of IS capabilities that may include analysis tools.

\- There was a common problem, reported by the executives in each organization, with the quality of information currently available in all forms both in its accuracy and timeliness. There was an urgent need for executives to have confidence in the quantitative data they received and to know at what time those figures were determined for useful comparative information.

As stated earlier management in these public organizations are reporting a trend towards increased professionalism, shifting from the traditional ‘public service’ non-profit orientation, to a market driven model, with increased accountability to both government and customers. Should this trend continue, they would adopt many of the characteristics of private companies in the use of quality managerial information systems, such as EIS, for strategic advantage. Several executives suggested that this may lead to the demand for more analytical and modelling facilities by senior managers. However, their current problems were with the quality and timeliness of the information they received and not with the analytical capabilities of the systems.

## Functional unit and IS managers' perceptions of executive IS needs

There was extensive use of computers, for access to organizational information by a majority of managers below the second tier. Managers at these levels in each organization were divided into roughly three equal groups: either very enthusiastic about the use of IS, not so enthusiastic but recognized the importance of CBI, or were quite sceptical of the advantages of computer systems. Apart from the IS staff, managers at these levels had no formal computer training except an occasional single subject studied at university and a few days training on specific applications.

All but one of the IS managers interviewed did not believe that executives would make serious use of computers in their work. There was one IS manager who felt that it was only a matter of time before executives were making extensive use of the organization's information systems as they became easy to use. In the three organizations where executives were now using personal computers, there was an attitude among some IS staff that the bosses could have their new toys because they had the power to demand this, but it would just be a passing fad or status symbol. This attitude may be a legacy of traditional systems analysis training that does not prepare IS staff for the flexible type of systems that are appropriate for managers.

Among the third group of interviewees, the functional unit managers, there was also doubt expressed on the appropriateness of computer systems for senior managerial work. However their main concerns were with the consequences to them of giving executives direct access to ‘their’ systems. These concerns included fears that executives would misinterpret the data, as well as what arrangements would be made for security of the information and also their jobs. There was obvious reluctance to change entrenched manual reporting procedures.

There was little evidence that IS staff and functional unit managers had an awareness of the relationship of their work to the strategic goals of the organization. This was reflected in a lack of concern for the flow of information from the operational systems to senior management and may be a consequence of insufficient involvement of senior management in IS planning and control.

## Future executive computer use

In the provision of EIS or other computerized support for executives, there appear to be factors that make the situation different in public organizations to those in the private sector. Low cost, top management commitment and an iterative development process have been suggested as particularly important for EIS in the public sector (Mohan et al, 1990). In the four public organizations of our study costs are certainly a prime concern however they have quite different approaches to the process of developing computerized systems for executives and have different levels of EIS commitment from executives.

The different approaches are as follows:

\- Organization A is setting up an EIS package under the sponsorship of one executive who has supplied all executives with networked PCs. This organization is introducing a clear policy of ownership of systems by the relevant department whose head chairs the team responsible for its development and management. At the same time the data belongs to the organization and can be used by senior management for strategic purposes. The IS Department is in turn responsible for the implementation and maintenance of the system as well as security aspects. This provides a sensible platform for EIS development but may cause some resentment from staff who have previously had a possessive attitude to the data in their systems.

\- Organization B has supplied Macintoshes to executives, at their request, but there is no specific EIS planned.

\- Organization C is proposing a grand scheme, in co-operation with other like organizations, to renew the whole OIS including an EIS. This is a joint management, IS staff, user group initiative. Currently second tier executives use Macintoshes only for email.

\- Organization D has its basic computer system imposed by the state department and has developed a myriad of small reporting and query systems for individual managers. There is little computer use by senior managers who only have access to terminals on the mainframe. One executive uses an EIS supplied by the State.

Most of the initiatives described here are very new or in the planning phase and are progressing cautiously. One reason for this is that there are concerns over costs and difficulties in estimating the benefits to justify costs. Little appears in the literature on objective measures of EIS benefits. It has been argued that subjective evaluation is the only way that benefits of such systems can be determined as they are in general so poorly understood (Gould and Hasan, 1994). One suggestion is that continued use is a good objective indicator of success, since use of IS by senior management is completely discretionary.

There are also political reasons for cautious progress. EIS projects involve staff from all levels of the organization and across many departments. Many staff members can foresee that an EIS has implications for their section of the organization and have concerns for its impact on their work.

## The consequences of executive use of IS

Although the executives in the four organizations of the study have to date had very little use of the organization's IS, the other staff highlighted a number of anticipated consequences which they perceived to be of major concern. They are listed here, as they raise a number of issues.

\- One immediate consequence is that the planning of systems for direct executive access to OIS data by executives brings to light any problems with the data that have long gone unnoticed. There are few large IS that do not have some badly structured data with inconsistencies, redundancies, inaccuracies and missing elements but most are able to function at the operational level. In organization A which is implementing an EIS these have inevitably surfaced and a considerable effort is required to fix these before EIS development can proceed.

\- Those responsible for the operational systems are the managers of the corresponding functional unit such as personnel, finance and so on. They are responsible for reports, containing information from their systems, which are forwarded to senior management. Most functional unit managers filter this information before it proceeds higher. They are concerned that, with direct access by executives to the raw data, the information may be misinterpreted. One such manager was adamant that an EIS must have a facility for textual annotation to explain exception aspects of the data.

\- The issue of data ownership is also one that is sensitive and must be addressed. An information policy, such as described above as used by organization A, makes clear the responsibilities for the various organizational information systems and data while recognizing the organization's position as the 'owner' of the information.

\- One CEO stated that ‘information is power’ and saw himself at the hub of the ‘information web’ of the organization. Other executives felt that, in most aspects, the flow of information around the organization was currently by-passing the executives, controlled by the technology and the technical IS staff. Several executives felt that they should get more involved in the technology to redress this. It is becoming apparent that the ‘information brokers’ in the organization should not just be the IS staff and should include high level management with a thorough knowledge of organizational strategic goals.

\- Many IS staff could see positive aspects to the increased involvement of executives with computers. They felt that this would increase executive support for the IS Department and consequently increase their share of resources. They also felt that executive computer use would set a good example for those staff at lower levels who had a resistance to new technology, particularly those the same age as most of the executives (over 50 years).

## Discussion

Our study has highlighted issues of concern whenever executives have access to any form of organizational IS. In these medium-sized Australian public organizations we have seen that, while none of the CEOs either has or desires direct use of CBI, many second tier executives have recently acquired ‘friendly’ personal computers that either have, or will have, direct access to organizational data.

No factors emerged from our study as definitive characteristics of managers who would readily adopt direct use of CBI. It appears that some executives come to a point where they realize potential of CBI to satisfy their needs and at the same time have confidence in their ability to use it. Many IS staff and lower level managers often wrongly assume certain characteristics of senior managers, in particular their ability and willingness to make use of CBI in their work. The development of an EIS may be a catalyst to improve the communication between executives and members of these two groups, leading to better alignment of IS with the organization's mission.

Of particular interest was that the type of organization appeared to be a significant factor in determining responsibility for management information. In the industries of energy supply and local government, where most of the managers had a more technical background as engineers or town planners, the IS Department had sole responsibility for the organization's IS. In the people oriented industries of health and education there were separate units for management information, run by non-IS personnel. Where the manager of this unit was at executive level, there was an obvious improvement in strategic information.

Where executive information requirements and IS needs are under consideration, there is an immediate impact on the rest of the organization. There is increased awareness of information as an organizational resource raising questions of data integrity, systems' integration and data ownership. At the time of the study, reservations on the future of EIS were expressed by IS personnel and managers of Functional Production Units, who traditionally saw themselves as the owners of the organization's information systems and data but there are more recent signs that this is changing. Executives, on the other hand, were very positive in their opinions on their future use of CBI. They felt that their greater involvement as users of IS would improve the availability of information throughout the organization and ensure that the IS were aligned with organizational goals.

The findings of this study suggest that the following are important components of a model of IS suitable for executives in medium-sized public organizations.

\- There should be a clear information policy in the organization, stipulating ownership and responsibility for systems and data (similar to that of organization A in our study). This will help to overcome resistance to executive use of CBI by those responsible for the data in the organization's IS.

\- There are benefits of having a unit separate from the IS Department set up within the organization to provide information for managerial decision making (as with organizations C and D). This unit should be located high in the organizational structure (as with organization D) and incorporate staff with computing expertise (as in organization C). The former will ensure the alignment of IS with organizational strategic goals and the latter should guarantee that the systems are technically sound.

\- If there are plans to completely rebuild all the organization's IS (as in the organization C), executive information requirements should be an integral component of the analysis and not considered as an after-thought. This should prevent problems with data that are often encountered integrating an EIS into existing IS.

\- Where resources are limited and operational IS is established or imposed on the organization, a gradual introduction of computers to executives (as in organization B) may be more beneficial than a formal EIS project.

\- As executives become more involved in the organization's IS it is necessary to be aware of, and plan for, the human impact of this at several levels of the organizations. Specifically, executives may be eager to demonstrate their use of the most recent technology to their own staff and to competitors whereas IS staff and user managers may be apprehensive of changes to their place in the organizational systems.

\- It is of fundamental importance that the CBI, used by executives, should be aligned with the strategic goals of the organization. It was clear that until recently there was little awareness of strategic objectives by those responsible for IS in the organizations of our study. We contend that a major contribution to this was the lack of involvement in, and use of, CBI by senior management. As a corollary to this it is hoped that as executives increasingly make more direct use of CBI, the alignment of IS with strategic goals will improve.
