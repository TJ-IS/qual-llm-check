# ITEM: a strategic approach to information systems in voluntary organizations

Gareth G Morgan

School of Applied Information Technology, Faculty of Computer Studies and Maths, University of the West of England, Frenchay Campus, Coldharbour Lane, Bristol BS16 1QY, UK

Voluntary and community organizations raise many special issues for the implementation of computer-based information systems, in terms of organizational culture, low budgets, use of volunteer personnel, diffuse objectives, and other factors. For these reasons, the development of information systems strategies in this sector is far from straightforward. Research is described which led to the development of a method known as ITEM, by which a small voluntary body can determine and implement an information systems strategy appropriate to its needs. The ITEM approach is outlined, and preliminary experience of its use is reported.

Keywords: voluntary organizations, churches, information systems, ITEM, ETHICS

The use of information technology (IT) in small voluntary organizations has increased greatly in the past 10 years with the advent of personal computers (PCs). But numerous authors report that the use of IT in this sector is often haphazard, poorly planned, and inadequately related to the needs of the organization (Marsh, 1985; Irving, 1986; Morgan, 1987).

A clear need thus exists for a methodology which could be used in the voluntary sector to assist the effective introduction of computer-based systems in a manner that will directly support the strategies of such organizations. Although methodologies to support the analysis, design, and implementation of information systems (IS) have formed a major research theme over the past 30 years, it will be argued in this paper that none of the existing well-known methodologies is particularly suited to the needs of small non-profit organizations.

Taxonomies of the available methods have been offered by authors such as Wood-Harper and Fitzgerald (1982), and Benyon and Skidmore (1987). Benyon and Skidmore comment, however, that no one method can meet the needs of the systems analyst at all stages, and this is certainly true. Soft systems methodology (SSM) (Checkland, 1981; Checkland and Scholes, 1990), for example, has been used extensively in analysing problem situations in non-profit organizations (see Le Saint (1987) for one of many examples of studies in this sector) but the originators of SSM never claimed that it could be used to plan the detailed implementation of IT. At the other end of the development cycle, Structured Systems Analysis and Design (eg Gane and Sarson, 1982) principally addresses large-scale development of new computer-based systems; it does not seek to address issues of organizational strategy, and its scale makes it relatively inappropriate for small personal computer (PC) based applications using pre-written software packages.

## Requirements of a methodology for this sector

For a methodology to support the implementation of strategic information systems in small voluntary organizations, it must meet a number of requirements.

In the first place it must cover the full range of issues from identifying organizational strategy, to information strategy, to IS planning, IT development, and IT implementation. It is simply not viable with very small systems to have to use a number of separate methodologies.

Second, it must be culturally acceptable in the voluntary sector. In dealing with organizational strategy it must be able to deal with the wide range of organizational goals that may be found, and cope with the lack of clear goals that is a common feature in this field (Butler and Wilson, 1990). Similarly, the types of IT solutions which are appropriate in large organizations are often quite unsuitable in small voluntary bodies that usually have no in-house IT professionals, and extremely low budgets. The technology that is appropriate will often be low-level rather than leading edge.

Thirdly, the actual process by which an IS methodology is applied must recognize the special nature of users in such organizations. The lack of IT professionals means that the users will normally themselves be the implementers of any new systems proposed. They must therefore be able to participate fully in the methodology used. But equally, some of the users will frequently be volunteers with no contractual obligation to the organization, and their time may be very limited, so for a methodology to be effective, it must be applicable with only a modest time commitment by those concerned.

Finally, an IS methodology for this sector must address longer term issues. IS failures are common even in commercial environments, where employees may be held directly accountable for their performance, but at least project management and reporting techniques are widely available to address such risks. In the non-profit sector such accountability is less frequently applicable, and even where IT solutions are successfully chosen and established initially, the fieldwork reported below found a whole range of reasons why systems can fall into disuse. These include changes of volunteer personnel, pressures of other commitments, failure to address practical issues such as data backups, and lack of user pressure to generate results. It follows that for an IS methodology to give strategic benefits in a voluntary organization, the methodology must itself ensure that the long term success of any new system is at the heart of the process.

## Characteristics of organizations addressed

The fieldwork used in the present research took local churches as the domain of investigation. However, the principles proposed here would in essence be applicable in many types of smaller non-profit organizations, such as small charities, community organizations, campaigning groups, etc.

The main characteristics of such organizations include few full-time employees, and a large part of the work of the organization is frequently carried out by volunteers. The organization may have only limited premises such as a small office or meeting hall, and volunteers may well work largely from their own homes. Decision making is likely to be by committees, rather than by individuals with significant authority, although many procedures and issues of authority may be diffuse and unclear. Finance is almost always very limited. The main sources of income are usually individual donations, membership subscriptions, and possibly local authority grants (though the latter are declining rapidly as a primary source of funds in the voluntary sector). The organizations concerned are likely to have charitable status and need to keep accounts to meet the requirements of the Charities Acts, though accounting is likely to be delegated to a treasurer without any formal accounting training.

There will usually be a clear sense of overall objective — such as to promote the Christian gospel, to raise money to assist medical work in particular fields, to promote specific environmental concerns, or to develop a sense of community in a particular locality. However, there is often confusion on the detailed strategy for fulfilling such objectives. The key individuals involved, whether volunteers or employees, are likely to have a deep personal commitment to the overall objective, but may well lack skills in running an organization. There will usually be a tension between the need to maintain the organization itself (for example through producing newsletters and circulating them to members) and the promotion of the primary objective (which may require work largely with those outside the organization).

## IT strategy for voluntary organizations

The notions of ‘IT strategy’ or of ‘the strategic use of IT’ arise frequently in discussion of information systems, but few authors offer precise definitions. In this paper, the term is used in the sense of applying IT in a way that will enable an organization more effectively to fulfil its organizational objectives. It thus follows that the issue of IT strategy can only be examined in relation to strategies of the organization as a whole.

Strictly speaking, IT strategy is only one aspect of a broader IS strategy, as the latter may well address issues unrelated to technology, although, as reported by Boaden and Lockett (1991) there is a great diversity of ways in which the terms are used. The focus of the present research was upon enabling churches and voluntary organizations to develop strategies to use IT effectively. However, any such strategy manifestly involves extensive consideration of organizational issues; it is not purely related to technology.

Earl (1988) sees the strategic view as emerging around 1980. He distinguishes a 1970s view of IT in terms of supporting business strategy (referred to in this paper as the administrative model) from a 1980s model that IT can itself provide strategic opportunities (the strategic model). The administrative model of IT is mainly concerned with improving efficiency, whereas the strategic model is concerned with new opportunities. Given that most churches and voluntary groups are not primarily administrative organizations, the benefits of IT on the former model are limited, but on the latter model, the potential benefits are huge.

Much of the material, however, tends to focus on aggressive images. Senn (1989)

discusses information systems as a ‘strategic weapon’, which he relates directly to issues of competitive advantage. He gives four ways in which this can arise: by locking in customers, by locking out competitors, by improving supplier arrangements, and by forming a basis for new products. Whilst the latter two areas may have some relevance to voluntary bodies, the paradigm of competitive advantage is inapplicable in this context, as very few voluntary organizations would support a competitive approach. Some of the churches in our case studies specifically sought better co-operation with other churches as one of the ways in which they wished to see improved communication.

More helpful images focus on strategic planning. Awad (1988), for example, explains IS strategic planning in terms of creating goals and strategies for information systems which are in line with those of the organization.

It follows that to develop an IT strategy for voluntary sector use, we need to understand not just the structure and organization of the church or charity, but specifically the concept of corporate strategy in such contexts. This is far from simple as the notion of strategy is not readily associated with charitable and voluntary organizations (Butler and Wilson, 1990, p 30). It is also the case that there is a wide diversity within the voluntary sector. Even in the specific case of local churches, there are at least five distinct models of church organization, each of which has different notions of organizational strategy (Rudge, 1968).

Nevertheless, preliminary work suggested that one major strategic issue for almost all voluntary organizations is communication — both within the organization (such as from the central officers to the membership as a whole) and from the organization as a whole to others it seeks to contact or influence. Investigations showed that this is an area in which even very small-scale use of IT can make a huge difference: for example, by facilitating better presentation and distribution of literature.

## Patterns of IT use

To those accustomed to more substantial uses of IT, the patterns of use in the voluntary sector can seem extremely primitive. In the case of local churches, very few user organizations possess more than a single PC, a printer, and some suitable software packages. Even within these, a good third may be dependent purely on machines with floppy disk drives, and only very few will possess laser printers (Martin, 1990). As well as general purpose applications such as word processing and spreadsheets, a good deal of software is now available specifically developed for church and voluntary organizations, to cover areas such as membership records, charitable accounting, and reclaiming tax on deeds of covenant (Berman and Dewhurst, 1987; Morgan, 1987). More specific software also exists for certain applications, such as welfare benefits calculations.

If the organization has a permanently staffed office, that will be the natural location for the hardware. However, as noted, much of the work of many voluntary organizations is carried out by volunteers, often in their evenings and weekends, and it may not be convenient or possible for them to have to go into an office for access to IT facilities. Thus it is not uncommon to find equipment purchased by the organization but located in the home of, say, the treasurer. The location of the hardware is a major issue in determining its effective use.

In addition to equipment owned by the organization, there may well be further hardware and software owned by individual officers of the organization (paid or voluntary) which they use, in some cases extensively, in relation to the work of the organization. For example, in a church, it is not uncommon to find that the minister has a personal word processor, and that the treasurer has access to a PC with a spreadsheet, even though neither is under the church's own control. In determining an IT strategy, careful account must be taken of such systems. Dependence on personally owned equipment can leave the organization very vulnerable if there are personnel changes, but ignoring it can lead to new computer systems being ineffective, if officers prefer to continue with their own personal facilities.

The majority of applications are used purely for administrative purposes — for tasks that would have been undertaken even in the absence of IT. Very little evidence was found of voluntary organizations that have gained strategic benefits from IT. Hence the task of an IT strategy in this sector is to find a means in which the overall objectives of the organization can be translated into appropriate uses of IT, but taking account of the very low level of IT investment that is usually possible. Despite the rapid growth in use of IT in this sector, there remain many organizations with no computer systems at all (at least not under the ownership of the organization itself). In many cases the strategy will therefore be concerned with the initial introduction of IT (where required).

## Research method

A three-stage programme of fieldwork was employed to investigate these issues. Some initial quantitative data on issues of IT in churches was available from the work of Hardcastle (1987), and the present requirements of devising an IS methodology for this sector demanded extensive consideration of cultural and attitudinal issues in the organizations concerned. A qualitative approach was thus adopted, based on detailed case studies with 10 churches at different stages of computerization.

In a first phase, three churches were selected with no church-owned IT at present, to act as a control against which attitudes in the other churches could be assessed. In the second phase, four were chosen which had taken explicit decisions to acquire computer systems at least 12 months previously. A final phase involved three further churches who were willing to consider the use or development of IT in the ensuing months, and who were prepared to take part in initial trials of the ITEM approach.

The churches were selected on a stratified basis to obtain a balance of denominations, of sociological settings, and (in the case of the second phase) of length of use of IT; further details are given in Morgan (1992a). A minimum size of around 100 members was set, to ensure the churches would have sufficient complexity to raise reasonable problems of internal communication.

In the first two phases, the investigation took an ethnographic model of investigation, based on short-term participant observation with multiple key informants. The researcher was fully immersed in each church for a three-day period and conducted in-depth focused interviews with an average of 15 persons in various positions in the church.

Arising from the first two phases, and drawing on existing literature, a preliminary version of the ITEM approach was proposed on paper. This was investigated on an action research basis with the three churches in the third phase of studies, and subsequently refined to give the method reported here.

## Philosophy of the ITEM approach

The term ITEM originated from the acronym Information Technology

Ecclesiastical Method in that it was proposed as a method for use with churches. As currently reported (Morgan, 1992b), information has been added indicating use in a wide range of community organizations, and the term is generalized to Information Technology Environmental Method in that it reflects the environment in which voluntary bodies operate.

ITEM is developed to meet the issues identified above under ‘Requirements of a methodology for this sector’. Its philosophy draws heavily on Mumford’s socio-technical method known as ETHICS (Mumford and Henshall, 1979; Mumford, 1983, 1984, 1993; Mumford and MacDonald, 1989). Nevertheless, ITEM is not an attempt to adapt ETHICS itself for use in a church context. Rather, it was found that of the main existing IS methodologies, ETHICS appears to come closest in its general philosophy to what is needed for introducing IT in churches, and it can contribute relevant insights for the present purpose.

The origins of ETHICS are mainly with large organizations of full-time staff with many individuals working side by side on similar tasks which are to be organized in a new way of using IT. As documented by Hirschheim (1985), ETHICS involves no less than 25 stages before getting to any definite implementation proposals, and many of these would require more than one meeting of the design group with investigations in between. The present research would thus almost certainly have failed if it simply attempted to condense ETHICS into a formula for churches and voluntary groups, as it would be cumbersome, and would still fail to address many of the special issues identified as being crucial to the effective implementation of IT in this sector. Furthermore, ETHICS gives little attention to the implementation stage — which in large organizations would be handled by IT professionals — but which was found to be crucial for any long term success from IT in a church/community context, where the users are very likely to be the implementers.

ETHICS can assist, though, in offering an extremely helpful format for user participation. The principle of the design group, composed mainly of users, but with a ‘change facilitator’ and some technical input, was found to be a valuable format and is used in ITEM. In the context of ITEM, the group is referred to as the ‘project team’. The emphasis that ETHICS gives to the notion of mission, and encouraging the team to reflect on that, is also valuable in seeking to achieve the strategic use of IT in a voluntary context.

A feature of ETHICS is that the team formulates a number of alternative solutions to the problems and needs its members have identified and these alternatives are evaluated from both social and technical viewpoints. By ensuring in ITEM that the project team balances the technical and the social issues, we ensure attitudinal factors are fully addressed, particularly in the light of the individuals who will be involved, and we also avoid proposing solutions which are technically impractical on the scale of IT that a church is likely to be able to consider, thus addressing major issues that emerged from phases 1 and 2 of the fieldwork.

Also, unlike some methodologies, ETHICS encourages the team to identify constraints, both human and technical, at an early stage, to avoid undue effort being devoted to impossible solutions. This is most important in a voluntary context, where the constraints are enormous. On the technical side, the main constraints will be limited finance for any IT proposed, and the need in most cases to work within what is available through packaged software. On the human side, the main constraints will be to do with the voluntary nature of most of the users. It also emphasizes the need for education of the team, which is vital in the voluntary field if users are to be empowered to choose and implement systems for themselves.

Although the purpose of ETHICS is to consider possibilities of technological change (it is not a purely abstract method) it does not start with any advance assumption that a computer-based solution will result. The end-product is not the introduction of IT, but a better ability to fulfil the mission of the organization, with IT as a possible tool (Mumford, 1984) and this philosophy is firmly reflected in ITEM. This is critical in the voluntary sector: in agreeing to set up a project team, an organization is agreeing to look into the possibilities of IT, but it is not making any advance commitment to IT investment.

## Description of ITEM

As finally revised, following the final field studies, ITEM may be outlined as follows. A report is available (Morgan, 1992b) which is intended as a manual for use by facilitators and others wishing to employ the approach.

The purpose of ITEM is to enable a church or small voluntary organization to devise, evaluate, and (if agreed) implement an IT strategy that will lead to genuine strategic benefits for the organization as a whole.

## The project team and facilitator

The use of ITEM is based on the interaction between a project team from within the organization, and an external facilitator; a pattern justified from Mumford's work discussed above.

The facilitator needs not only to have an appreciation of the method, but requires reasonable experience of IT implementation in similar non-commercial organizations in order to provide the education required. However, bearing in mind the limited resources of churches and community organizations and the need to avoid large consultancy costs, the approach is designed to require direct involvement by the facilitator for only four meetings, typically over a six-month period. After that point, ITEM seeks to give the project team sufficient autonomy to continue without external involvement.

In order to have the required credibility, the project team should be appointed by the body which has ultimate authority in the organization, typically a management committee or church council (referred to here as 'the council'). The team should comprise between five and seven persons, drawn from a number of roles in the organization, including both employees and volunteers. The team should not be filled with computer experts — it is those within the organization who could most directly benefit from IT whose involvement is crucial, although there should be at least one person who would be willing to act as 'computer co-ordinator' if an IT solution is agreed. In a church, for example, the team would include the minister, one or two senior lay-people, the treasurer, someone concerned with publications, and one or two prospective computer operators.

The term ‘users’ is to be understood in the broad sense of those directly affected by information processed, not just operators. It is essential to the method that the users are not involved simply for the purpose of consultation: they are fundamentally in charge of the process, and at the end of the day they make the decisions as to how they wish to proceed — the facilitator only has a supporting role. The project team are directly responsible for making recommendations to the council once they have determined their proposals, and for implementing the system if approval is given.

## Structure of the approach

In order to keep the work manageable, ITEM is structured around five stages, each of which culminates in a meeting of the project team; these are summarized in Table 1.

A major issue in developing the approach was to keep the number of meetings and steps down to a level that would retain the commitment of volunteers, whilst providing the team with sufficient opportunity to explore the issues needed to develop a reasonably coherent IT strategy. Voluntary bodies are well known for creating working parties which make little progress; the structure of ITEM is intended to permit the level of discussion and consultation which is crucial in this sector, but with highly focused actions to be taken at each stage.

The emphasis on communication is a key factor in this. It can be argued that achieving improved internal and external communication is fundamental to the strategy of most organizations, but this is particularly so in the voluntary sector where much is done by persuasion and where contact has to be maintained with many who may give only a small amount of time to the organization. By highlighting the issue of communication, the facilitator can enable the team to move from organizational strategy to IT strategy much more easily than if this had to be done in the abstract.

## Stage 1: Organization strategy and IT possibilities

The first stage involves initial contact between the organization and the prospective facilitator, as arrangements for the project are established. An initial instigator within the organization will need to seek approval from the council for the establishment of a project team for this purpose.

The first meeting of the project team and facilitator will be on all-day basis (in many organizations a Saturday will be necessary if volunteer team members are to be available). The morning agenda will include: initial introductions; explanation of ITEM, briefing of the facilitator by the team regarding the nature of the organization, an initial presentation by the facilitator on IT possibilities in the field concerned, agreement of project boundaries, and appointment of a chair and secretary for the team. Following a working lunch, the team will begin discussions about the strategy of the organization, will seek to identify consequent ways in which communication could be improved, and will form preliminary views about relevant IT applications, and individuals who would be affected by them.

Each stage culminates in a main meeting of the project team, which will come at the end of the period indicated. At stage 3, the team decides whether to proceed, and if so, proposals are presented to the council at the start of stage 4. The remainder of the project only proceeds if the team's proposals are approved.

The facilitator has the task of empowering the team to move from consideration of organizational strategy, to communication, to the beginnings of an IT strategy. Although in large organizations such discussions would take many months, the research suggests that in the context of churches and small community organizations it is realistic to attempt this in a single day.

At the end of the first meeting an action plan is devised, which will mainly involve team members in consulting and interviewing others in the organization who would be affected by their ideas, with a view to assessing the likely strategic benefits of their suggestions and the human and social implications of doing certain work by computer.

## Stage 2: Considering human implications of IT

About three weeks should be allowed for the investigation above. It is important to maintain reasonable momentum with the project to retain the commitment of volunteers.

A second full meeting takes place (on a half-day basis) where the team review the results of their investigations, and begin to refine their initial suggestions into the beginnings of an IT strategy. Key application areas are selected which can be expected to offer strategic benefits, particularly in terms of improving communication. The facilitator then gives a presentation about the principles of systems selection and user responsibilities, to prepare the team for the forthcoming technical investigation. At this meeting the team will consider the possible location for any hardware to be acquired (a crucial issue as noted above), and agree who will be the primary and secondary users directly operating the system. Discussion will also take place on issues of data protection, confidentiality, and security — the initial research found cases of systems which had been largely rejected by churches due to lack of advance considerations of such issues.

Plans are then set for the technical investigation — in most cases it is unlikely in the type of organization considered that any bespoke software will be possible, so the main issues will be to evaluate appropriate software packages and to locate a suitable hardware supplier. Determination of precise costs is also vital.

## Stage 3: Technical issues and formal proposal

Following four to five weeks for the technical investigation, the team will convene for a further full-day meeting with the facilitator. This third meeting is central to the ITEM approach.

Initially the team will review the results of their investigations, and will agree the precise applications to progress (certain areas may have to be rejected for lack of available or affordable software, for example). The facilitator then encourages the team to set out a number of alternative IT solutions to the needs identified. The solutions could well involve different scales of systems (one machine or several), different patterns of user involvement, different software choices. One of the options may be to avoid IT completely. The team must then evaluate these and agree on their ‘best’ solution, taking into account the constraints of the organization in terms of finance, personnel, etc.

At this stage the facilitator changes the atmosphere and presses the team to stand back and consider their ‘best’ solution and agree whether, overall, they believe it offers sufficient strategic benefits to justify proceeding. If the decision is ‘yes’ then the team is committed to proposing this solution to the council and personally implementing it if it is agreed. However, the team must also be completely free to respond ‘no’ if they do not believe their ideas offer sufficient benefits to justify proceeding. (In the latter case a brief report is presented to the council and the project is wound up.)

If the agreed response is to support the proposal, the team continues, with the facilitator's support, to produce an implementation plan, spelling out precisely what applications will be implemented by what dates, and what benefits can reasonably be expected. The implementation plan is vital to prevent good ideas being endlessly postponed, which is a common problem in a sector that is not motivated primarily by finance. Proper allowance is vital in the plan for training time — in most cases the training will be on a self-taught basis, for reasons of cost.

## Stage 4: Decision and implementation

The project team now has to produce a short report or presentation for the council of the organization, regarding their proposals. The nature of this report is crucial — it is not just a costed list of items for purchase — but rather it is a proposal of how exactly the team see IT as contributing to the mission and strategy of the organization, including details of dates from the implementation plan when benefits can be expected. (The facilitator can assist if requested, but it must be the report of the team — not of the facilitator.)

The proposals are presented to the council by one of the team who is a member of the council, for a formal decision to be made. If approval is received, a ‘contract’ comes into effect between the council and the team, in that the council is agreeing to provide resources and the team is agreeing to deliver results which enhance the mission of the organization. The rationale for this is to prevent the tendency for IT plans to drift and for IT usage to lack accountability, which the earlier phases of the research identified as frequent problems in this sector.

If approval is not given, the team has the option of coming up with new proposals or abandoning the project. However, preliminary trials found the nature of the approach makes it unlikely that unrealistic proposals will be put forward by the team, and hence the chances of approval are very good.

Once approval is given, the relevant hardware and software are ordered, and the implementation plan is put into effect in terms of initial setting up and training, and the involvement of the different users agreed. The commitment that team members made as part of their ‘contract’ with the council is crucial at this stage. The chair of the team is responsible for ensuring that members keep in close touch and if problems arise which the team cannot solve, they can contact the facilitator for advice.

About two months after delivery of the equipment, a fourth full project team meeting takes place, the last involving the facilitator. This date will have been fixed well ahead and forms a target for the team, at which they will review progress against the implementation plan. The format of this meeting forms the basis for subsequent meetings of the team as they continue to review progress on a long term basis (but independently of the facilitator). An extensive checklist agenda is used to ensure that a large number of practical issues have been addressed such as data backups, maintenance arrangements, software support, interrelationships between users, and other factors which were found in the preliminary research to be frequent causes of IS failures in the voluntary sector. The aim is to prevent the team falling into major problems by picking them up at an early stage. Where problems are identified, actions are minuted as to who will deal with them.

## Stage 5: The long term contribution of IT

The final stage of ITEM is the ongoing development of the project. The facilitator will have withdrawn at the end of the fourth meeting, and the approach has sought to make the team self-sufficient by this stage.

If the plans are progressing reasonably, the team is advised to meet formally at least twice-yearly to review progress against the implementation plan and to address any new issues that have arisen. Changes of user present a specific issue, if existing users have to leave the organization or move on to other tasks. Findings from the earlier research indicate that a change of user often leads to initially successful systems falling into disuse. ITEM seeks to address this by keeping responsibility with a team rather than an individual.

A further central aspect of ITEM is that the council of the organization must require the project team, as part of its ‘contract’, to report back at least annually on the progress that has been achieved, particularly in terms of contribution to the organization’s strategy. This ensures the council will maintain its ‘management commitment’ to the project — without this, users can be tempted to give up when difficulties arise.

In due course, the team may go on to propose further development of the use of IT, and the ITEM process can then become iterative over a period of years as new proposals are made.

## Evaluation

This paper simply describes the initial development of ITEM, and extensive evaluation of the approach must await further research, including longitudinal studies covering a number of years. However, it is possible to report on the phase 3 case studies, in which a preliminary version of ITEM was used as a basis of action research in three churches, over a period of six months. The results of this led to some further development into the method as described above.

Controlled studies are rarely possible in action research, but the three churches concerned (P, Q, R) were compared with the nearest equivalent churches in phase 2 (A, C, D), to consider the effectiveness which their use of IT had achieved. It should be noted that the phase 2 churches were selected on the basis that they would be expected in the light of earlier research (such as that of Hardcastle (1987)) to be reasonably successful in the use of IT, so if those in phase 3 showed further improvement, this could be significant. In addition, detailed questionnaires were used with all members of all the project teams, asking them to assess from their own perspective what they felt had been achieved, and a group discussion with each team was held subsequently.

In all three cases, the team's proposals were accepted by their church councils (which is in part an endorsement of the work) and team members went on to put proposals into effect.

Church P had achieved as much after 10 weeks as had church A after 12 months, both having similar levels of resources in terms of expertise, finance, and premises; moreover church P had begun to develop a firm vision for IT as a church resource, whereas church A saw it merely as a tool to help the treasurer. The team in church P had set clear objectives for the use of IT, arising from their initial considerations of church strategy and needs for communication; there was every indication that

these objectives would be fulfilled.

Church R had established very clear responsibilities and were firmly committed to a substantial implementation plan (albeit at an early stage) but church D only had one main application operational after two years, and had suffered badly from a change of user. Although there were some difficulties in church R of a rather large project team with some members less than fully committed, there was no doubt that the team saw the effective use of IT as crucial to the development of their church's strategy for reaching the community it served.

Church Q had extremely limited resources in terms of people able or willing to get involved, and there was little prospect of strategic use of IT, but even so, word processing and membership applications were usefully revived that had been set up by a former staff member, whereas in church C, the church computer system was in almost complete disuse.

Thus the evidence tends to suggest that use of ITEM did have considerable impact. The churches in the study achieved much higher levels of IT effectiveness within a given time than might have been expected. In two of the three cases there was clear evidence of commitment to a strategic role for IT, and even in the third case it was clear that the church would see administrative benefits.

## Conclusion

The ITEM approach is an initial attempt to fill the vacuum that currently exists in methods appropriate for the development and implementation of IT strategies in small church and community organizations. Future research, including major long term studies, is required to determine its overall effectiveness as an approach.

How successful it is will also depend greatly on the type of organization, and the commitment of the project team. Even within churches, there were initial indications that higher levels of IT effectiveness are achievable in those churches coming close to Rudge's (1968) systemic model of church organization than in churches of other models. But the field trials clearly demonstrated that it is possible, even within organizations as small as those studied, to enable a team to implement IT in relation to the strategic needs of the organization, rather than just as an administrative resource.

ITEM draws on a wide range of informal literature on IS in the non-profit field, on the existing proven approach of Mumford's ETHICS, on substantial further research into IT effectiveness in churches, and on some limited trials of the method itself. It also focuses on one main strategic area — that of improved communication within the organization and with those it serves — which is demonstrably relevant to the vast majority of community organizations. Its validity rests, therefore, on a combination of supports.

Undoubtedly it is capable of further improvement, but there are good grounds for arguing that ITEM as described offers a very substantial advance on the informal methods of IT introduction which have largely been used in voluntary organizations to date.
