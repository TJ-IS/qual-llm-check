# Implementation strategies for office systems

Faith Noble

Sheffield Business School, Totley Hall Lane, Sheffield S17 4AB, UK

A technology implementation strategy must begin with identification of the potential benefits. Office information systems have generally been regarded as administrative support rather than as strategic systems. However, with advances in technical integration, office systems are developing from a rather narrow conception of 'office automation' into organization-wide systems which do have strategic significance. This paper draws on information-processing theory and other theoretical work on strategic applications of information technology and synthesizes it to develop a model showing four strategic roles for integrated office systems. These are illustrated with examples from the author's research. An implementation strategy must also consider the organizational implications and how to deal with them. Integrated systems have more organizational 'impact' than earlier departmental systems. In some cases, strategic office systems require extensive organization redesign. The relative advantages of alternative approaches to these questions are discussed, and it is argued that traditional methodologies need to be supplemented by a change management approach which can deal with second-order change, such as organizational transformation.

Keywords: office automation, office information systems, implementation, organizational change, organizational transformation

Successful technology implementation requires a match between technology, strategy and organization (Walton, 1989; Scott Morton, 1991). The first part of this paper discusses how developments in technology have created opportunities for office systems to be used strategically. Four broad types of strategic applications of office information systems are derived from the literature and from the author's field research, which consisted of in-depth studies of eight private and public sector organizations and 'snapshot' studies of seven more (Noble, 1993). The second part of the paper considers the organizational change implications of these strategic applications, and compares alternative methodologies or approaches which could be used to implement them.

## The nature of office information systems

Complications in defining office systems arise from the way that their role and nature have changed over time, from the vagueness of the concept of ‘the office’, and from the loose and misleading way in which the term ‘office automation’ has been used. ‘The office’ has been seen as a definite physical location in which certain types of work are performed. As a description this is becoming increasingly inaccurate and inhibits recognition of similarities between information systems in offices and other kinds of situations. ‘Office automation’ is not a single definite type of information system, it has become rather a catch-all for various kinds of office technology, including facsimile, personal computers (PCs), networks and electronic mail (The Independent, 12 May 1993). Also, ‘automation’ implies that all or most office work is routine and predictable and can be mechanized, whereas much of it, and not only that of managers and professionals, requires considerable human skill, knowledge and discretion (Suchman and Wynn, 1984). A brief review of the historical development of office systems gives us a better grasp of the problem.

## From data processing to integrated office systems

Electronic technology has been applied to the office in four major stages: data processing, departmental office systems, isolated applications, and integrated office systems. Each of the earlier stages continues into the present in some form (Figure 1). Computers were first applied in the office to the routine handling of numerical data by clerical workers, which is now almost entirely subsumed under data processing or transaction processing. The focus of this stage was on efficiency, productivity and cost savings (for an early analysis of the effects of these systems on work, see Hoos, 1960). The computerization of routine back-office functions conferred no real strategic or competitive advantage (King et al, 1988), except possibly in sectors where routine clerical labour represented a large and growing element of costs, such as insurance companies.

The next major stage of office systems occurred in the late 1970s and 1980s, when hardware suppliers began to offer ‘office automation’ products, bringing together minicomputers, terminals and peripherals with software packages including word processing, electronic mail, small databases, diary and calendar. The underlying conception was that of automating the individual office or department (KMG Thomson McLintock, 1986), by mechanizing isolated activities such as correspondence, text production, message handling, filing and record keeping (Tsichritzis, 1986). Office activities were seen as suitable targets for software development because they are observable, structured, relatively unambiguous and measurable (Hirschheim, 1985), and generic, ie very similar across many organizations and thus constituting a large potential market. Thus a generic facilities-based approach was developed for the administrative office which was not directed at specific business needs, nor was it concerned with the purpose or function of the office in the organization, which from a business perspective is far more significant than types of activities performed. The lack of strategic focus from the data-processing phase thus continued into this stage of office automation.

Consequently, what we may call departmental office automation had a limited success, both in the sense that take-up was slow (Price Waterhouse, 1988) and that where installed it was frequently under-used (Farmer, 1987). Because use was discretionary, failure was unspectacular; it was represented by unused boxes sitting on desks, in cupboards or storerooms, low levels of exploitation of the facilities available, and low levels of financial benefits realized. Numbers of staff did not noticeably dwindle. The ‘paperless office’ also turned out to be an illusion (Immel, 1985), as where office automation had been introduced paper was not only still in evidence but had actually increased (Long, 1987). A survey by the Institute of Administrative Management and Touche Ross showed that many of these problems continued at least into 1991.

However, ‘generic’ office automation was a step towards integrated office systems because it integrated different facilities into one coherent software package with a consistent interface to the user and consistent procedures across facilities, and provided electronic links between users and previously independent devices.

The introduction in the 1980s of word processing, personal computers and professional/technical workstations, which is the stage I call isolated applications, was at least as significant in the long run as the development of departmental ‘office automation’. Although these applications were introduced piecemeal and, like generic office automation, facilitated the work as it was currently being carried out, their immense popularity and rapid spread served to familiarize the majority of office workers with computers, and thus helped prepare the ground for fuller utilization of integrated office systems in the next stage.

The current stage, which began in the late 1980s, can be described as integrated office systems. The need for integration in office systems had been recognized throughout the 1980s (Simons, 1984; Wainwright and Francis, 1984; Farmer, 1988), but was prevented by technical barriers, such as incompatible hardware and software, lack of common standards, and poorly designed systems. Now most of these technical problems have been overcome or are being addressed, by the development of products such as emulators, open systems standards, and user organizations establishing policies of standardizing hardware and software. Technological integration has been occurring at a number of different levels, including the integration of data and text with image and voice and the convergence of communications and computers, but it is system integration which has strategic implications. This includes (1) the integration of existing systems: the networking of PCs and linking them and/or departmental office automation with mainframe computing, data processing and management information systems; (2) the linking of departmental systems across functional boundaries, into corporate, organization-wide networks, platforms or infrastructure; and (3) the integration of communications across organizational boundaries, including interorganizational systems. The increasing trend towards technical integration, together with improved cost-performance ratios, makes possible strategic applications of office systems, because technical integration facilitates and supports organizational integration.

## An information-processing view of the office

If an office information system is conceived merely as an information system which is used in an office environment, we are immediately confronted by the great variety of offices, ranging from a temporary hut on a building site to the claims processing department of a large insurance company, from a railway ticket office to a large accountancy practice. This has led to a number of attempts to classify offices, for example according to their functions (Gunton, 1983; Gerstein, 1987; Newman, 1987), according to whether they perform a primary or a support activity in the organization (Butera and Bartezzaghi, 1983), and according to the routineness of the work (Panko and Sprague, 1982; Pava, 1983). These distinctions are valid but do not provide a framework for identifying strategic applications.

What all offices have in common is that they exist in order to process information for organizations, rather than producing or handling tangible physical objects (Tsichritzis, 1986). Therefore a classification according to kinds of information-processing needs is more analytical than one based simply on functional types of existing offices.

The main areas of these needs have been identified by information-processing and systems theory. From such a perspective, the three main aspects of an organization are: an operating or technical core concerned with performing primary tasks ('production'), an outer ring or strategic level which deals with the environment, and a co-ordinative level which mediates between the two and integrates specialized activities (Parsons, 1964; Kast and Rosenzweig, 1974). Correspondingly, information is required to deal with or reduce variety and uncertainty (Ashby, 1956), which can arise from any of these three main sources: primary task/technology in the operating core (Perrow, 1971; Galbraith, 1973), the environment, and the interdependencies between tasks which require co-ordination (Thompson, 1967; Tushman and Nadler, 1978). Uncertainty, and therefore information-processing needs, are greater: the greater the complexity of the task, the more dynamic the environment, and the greater the interdependence among subunits. $^{1}$

From an information-processing perspective, therefore, offices serve the vital needs of organizations in three areas: primary (perform the core value-adding task of the organization), boundary-spanning (handle relations with the environment), and co-ordinative (manage the relations between the other two, and among organizational units) (see Figure 2).

1. Primary task. As Porter and Millar (1985) have indicated, the greater the information content of the product and of processes in the primary activity (ie task complexity), the greater the relevance of information technology (IT) to competitive advantage. In industries where the information component is high — eg financial services, information services, government, and many professions — the main activity of the organization is carried on in offices.

In this ‘core’ context, information is used to co-ordinate and smooth the flow of activity from one stage to another, and as a factor of production at any individual stage (Mason, 1984). Uncertainty arises both from task variety and complexity (Perrow, 1971) and from the interdependence of subunits which work together to perform the primary task (Tushman and Nadler, 1978). In internal operations, office systems can improve integration between interdependent activities and for complex tasks, provide better understanding of what is happening within each activity, resulting in improved efficiency and speed of processes, reduced costs and improved customer service. Such integration is a particular factor in time-based competition (Stalk and Hout, 1990).

The concept of the value chain (Porter, 1985; Porter and Millar, 1985) had already focused attention on work and information flows, interdependencies which exist between activities which may well cross functional boundaries, and how to reintegrate them. The current interest in business process redesign, reengineering (Hammer, 1990), or reconfiguration (Venkatraman, 1991) can be seen as an extension of the value chain idea. Just as the value chain does not necessarily correspond to existing organizational demarcations or functions, but provides a broader perspective on organization structure and suggests how functions could be reorganized to promote effectiveness, internal process integration is not simply a consolidation of stages but a ‘reassessment of the fundamental logic of the business process’ (Venkatraman, 1991, pp 137–138). Reengineering thus necessarily implies the reorganization of work, but not necessarily the use of a computer-based information system. However, where the product is information or the information content is very high, internal process integration will most likely be facilitated by a computer-based office system.

The nature of the interdependence of core processes varies. Production tasks are often conceptualized as linear and sequential, but even linear processes often require considerable feedback or reciprocity. Much office work consists of non-linear conversion processes (Pava, 1983), which may be intensive, extensive, or mediating (Thompson, 1967; Mason, 1984) (Figure 3). In intensive technologies (Thompson, 1967), many inputs go into a single unique object, as in a custom product or human services. In the office, this type of technology is represented archetypically by the case management or case processing system (Mason, 1984). Producing a document which is a joint effort, or preparing a bid which involves several different departments is also of this type. Computer-mediated group working and electronic document management are included in this type of activity.

There were several examples in the author's research of the use of office systems in intensive technologies (Noble, 1993). One was a large government department which used an office system to put together White Papers and other important documents, one of its major functions. A manufacturing company invested in an office system to reduce costs and control PC use; however, it also turned out to have a strategic role in compiling the bids for multi-million pound contracts on which the organization's success and survival depended. Customers were requiring increasingly detailed information and the office system enabled this to be put together efficiently and in a timely manner, which improved the company's chance of obtaining contracts. In another case, a major engineering group had a strategy of growth by acquisition; its corporate headquarters used an office system to put together take-over bids. A project directory was set up for each bid, so that teams from different departments could work on it together, which enabled them to be prepared quickly and efficiently. In each case the office system made an important strategic contribution.

Mediating technologies match diverse inputs to diverse outputs; the brokerage firm is the exemplar, but the type also includes estate agents, employment agencies, airline reservations, dating bureaux, banks and other financial institutions, which use information on one set of clients and customers to match them with another. Because information is central, IT is bound to be strategic. Extensive technologies produce diverse outputs from a single input, such as oil refining. The application of IT here is in support of operational research models which aid allocation decision making.

2. Boundary-spanning. The boundary-spanning information function includes marketing and competitor analysis, and surveillance of political, legal and social aspects of the environment. External databases and expert systems are clearly applicable to these and to strategic planning functions. Another strategic application of office systems is in the supply chain or value system, which can be seen as an extension of the internal value chain across organizational boundaries to customers and suppliers. These boundary-spanning activities are information-intensive and are another major focus of time-based competition (Stalk and Hout, 1990). Interorganizational information systems play an important role in joint optimization of activities on both sides and improved co-ordination between value chains. They depend not only on creating electronic links, but on good IT infrastructure. Many of the well-known strategic IT examples come into this category (eg American Hospital Supply, American Airlines SABRE reservation system).

A major bank (Noble, 1993) used an office system to facilitate anticipated rapid growth in a new line of business, syndicated home loans. The assessment and approval of one loan application could involve the writing of as many as 40 different letters. The process was computerized by bringing in an office automation package. Although users had underestimated the volume of work and disk space proved inadequate, customer service targets (an agreement out within 48 hours) were met 80 per cent of the time, and the volume of business quadrupled without increasing staff. On the basis of the experience gained, a larger, bespoke system was designed to accommodate further growth, and the office automation package became more of a shell into user applications. It had served as a prototype to define needs in an unfamiliar area and facilitated delivery of a new product.

A government department engaged in financial services used an office system to turn around the business, which was eventually privatized (Noble, 1993). In the face of rapidly declining market share, research revealed a major competitive weakness was customers' perception of the organization as slow, inflexible, and unresponsive when quality and speed of service were important to them. As a result, along with other organizational changes, the organization automated its core business processes, starting with customers' applications. This was done by subjecting as much as possible of the work to an algorithm, computerizing it, and thus enabling clerks in the regional offices to make decisions in the majority of cases, while only the more difficult ones were referred to professionals at headquarters. The result was that applications were dealt with within 24 hours in 65 per cent of cases, sometimes while the customer was still on the phone making the application. Professionals had their time freed up to deepen their knowledge and so make better informed decisions. (This was in fact a business process redesign before the term became fashionable.)

A manufacturing company (Noble, 1993) used an electronic catalogue and online ordering system to support a differentiation strategy. Distributors and customers could use their own PCs to browse the company's catalogue on a diskette, or connect with the company's ordering system via a modem. They could enter their requirements into the latter and the system would make recommendations from the company's product line, giving price and specifications. The customer could then place an order on the system and, if the product was in stock, the order would go out to the customer within two hours. If the first-choice product was not available, the system recommended alternatives. No competitor had such a system.

3. Co-ordinative. The co-ordination of specialized organizational units is the integrative, managerial function, which is greatly facilitated by the development of an IT-based organizational platform or infrastructure. This can provide 'efficiency-related benefits of compression of time and distance as well as effectiveness-related benefits of information sharing across the business process' (Venkatraman, 1991, p 132), and also provides the necessary base for further strategic applications (Scott Morton, 1991).

Many organizations are now struggling to develop such platforms, often with the rationale of improving communications (Hopper, 1990). More than half the PCs now sold in Britain are being installed in networks, and sales of inter-networking equipment, enabling large users to link several local area networks (LANs), increased by 60 per cent a year between 1987 and 1991 (The Independent, 12 May 1993). All of the organizations in the author's research were using office systems to network different departments and sites. A survey conducted at the Change Management Research Centre in 1989, of managing directors of leading companies, showed that those who viewed office systems in strategic terms most often gave improved communications as their objective (Noble, 1993).

A striking example of the use of an office system to develop a global platform for organizational communications was a multinational company based in England which sells machines and packaging materials to food producers. It is one of the largest packaging companies in the world, and has 46 marketing companies worldwide, with strength in Europe and the Far East. It also has geographically dispersed sites within the UK. This company introduced an office system to overcome the perceived shortcomings of PCs and minicomputers, on a largely experimental basis. They found that electronic mail was particularly successful; it not only reduced communication costs, it answered the problems of different languages, poor information distribution and the disruptive effect of time zones. Mobile staff could now be contacted through any of the company's terminals throughout the world. Foreign customers preferred electronic mail to the telephone because they could cope with written English better than with the spoken word. Also salesmen visiting customers used the system to provide them with up-to-date technical specifications on the spot, providing competitive advantage (Noble, 1993).

4. New organizations. A fourth area for the strategic application of office systems emerges from those described above, and this is to enable new organizational designs (Rockart and Short, 1991). It is believed that IT facilitates the development of organizational forms which are better able to respond to environmental complexity (Huber, 1984, 1990, 1991; Huber and McDaniel, 1986; Keen, 1991), or indeed that IT requires such organizational change for its full exploitation (Child, 1987; Drucker, 1988; Whiston, 1989). Integrated office systems are strongly implicated in many such projects because they help reduce the unit costs of co-ordination, enabling organizations to adopt different forms including more co-ordination-intensive structures, eg internal markets and ‘adhocracies’, and hence become more adaptive (Malone, 1985, 1988). However, Gunson and Boddy’s research on networks (1989) suggested that in Britain few organizations as yet viewed them as a means of achieving fundamental organizational change. Among my case studies, two large organizations, both major multinational computer manufacturers, were conspicuous for using office systems consciously for greater organizational flexibility.

## Organizational change

I now consider the implications of these strategic uses of office systems for organizational change and the kind of implementation approach which seems to follow. Their integrated character causes strategic office systems to have more organizational impact and therefore to be more difficult to implement than earlier forms of office automation. Integrated systems not only include more levels and different types of worker, they cross departmental, functional, site, organizational and national boundaries. They affect a large number of users and other stakeholders. They may be involved with core processes and be critical to the organization's success. They must be centrally planned and controlled but have local support; they may represent radical change but must often be implemented incrementally.

Apart from these organizational ‘impacts’, strategic office systems frequently demand extensive organization redesign. In internal process integration, computer-supported co-operative working on intensive technologies often involves considerable redesign of work processes at the level of the group (Bullen and Bennett, 1990; Lyytinen and Ngwenyama, 1992). Further, in most versions of business process redesign or reengineering (BPR), the reorganization of work is central, with IT in a secondary and supportive role. Some of the best-known approaches to BPR embody the idea of devolving responsibility to lower levels, more diffuse roles, team working and less formal hierarchy, more facilitative management roles and skills (Davenport and Short, 1990; Hammer, 1990; Hammer and Champy, 1993). Most processes also cross existing functional boundaries, which means organizations should really be completely redesigned around processes. These combined structural and human resource management (HRM) changes represent a new paradigm for organizing which challenges many traditional assumptions (Davenport and Short, 1990; Hammer, 1990; Stalk and Hout, 1990; Coates-Walker, 1992).

Some uses of office systems to span boundaries, such as executive support systems, marketing and strategic analysis, do not require any significant organizational change. However, interorganizational systems are extremely likely to do so. While electronic data interchange (EDI) requires only a common technology platform for data interchange, interorganizational systems require much more integration of procedures and sharing of information, while business network redesign (Billinge, 1992; Venkatraman, 1992) results in radically different organizational forms. More internal structural change occurs, and issues of power and trust become more prominent as technological and organizational integration between firms proceeds (Clemons and Row, 1992; Prekumar et al, 1992).

The implementation of an integrated IT platform or network is more complex, technically and politically, than that of departmental systems. While it may not involve organization redesign initially, it makes it possible later on. According to Venkatraman (1991), an IT infrastructure is a precondition for the development of further levels of strategic exploitation of IT. The use of IT to create new organizations, on the other hand, represents major organizational redesign by definition.

## Implementation strategies

Both of these issues suggest that careful attention must be paid to implementing strategic office systems. The likelihood of organizational ‘impacts’ and the frequent requirement for organization redesign immediately suggest socio-technical design approaches (Mumford, 1983; Pava, 1983). The degree of uncertainty about outcomes indicates step-by-step incrementalism, as opposed to an all-at-once or ‘big bang’ approach. The cross-functional character of systems, the multi-stakeholder impact, and the need for commitment and support suggest implementation approaches which emphasize process, such as user/stakeholder involvement and soft systems methodology (Checkland, 1981). The role of politics in major organizational change is widely recognized (Pfeffer, 1992); Buchanan and Boddy (1992) have urged the introduction into project management techniques of ways of recognizing the likelihood of political behaviour and dealing with it.

In the organizations I studied, the implementation approach could be characterized as top-down or at least top-led in every case. Within this, the degree to which adoption and use were voluntary or mandatory varied greatly. The systems which were seen as strategic were mandatory and were also introduced fairly rapidly, while others were much more gradual and laissez faire. Clear business need and a sense of urgency obviously affected the implementation approach adopted.

Big, organization-wide projects were phased in because of their size, and usually piloted. Formal methods of user participation were a conspicuous feature in only the government departments, but that is not to say that users had no influence in the other cases, especially where adoption and use were voluntary. Powerful users, such as some managers and professionals, can have influence without actual formal participation. In several organizations, there was a clear perception of a need to ‘market’ the office system to users in general, and to coax powerful users on board.

The three government organizations in my research implemented their office systems incrementally, piloting before proceeding to the next stage. They made efforts to involve users and paid attention to job design issues. They did not explicitly deal with politics, although its existence was recognized and dealt with informally — for example, by phasing in the senior management teams early in order to secure their support, although they may have had less use for the system than other groups.

A participative, incremental socio-technical strategy is likely to be adequate where:

1. the proposed system is intended for one unit or area, or can be introduced one unit at a time;

2. there is no great urgency;

3. no radical change in technology or work practices is intended;

4. the system is not central to core processes.

Both traditional technology-led and user-led socio-technical design approaches have implicitly been based on a first-order change model; that is, they try to fit the office system into the existing organization with the least possible disturbance rather than challenge it. However, most organizations have passed the phase of localized exploitation of IT and departmental office automation and, as we have seen, are attempting to build an integrated IT platform to redesign internal processes, develop interorganizational systems, or all three. These more strategic uses of office systems require second-order organizational change, which represents a change from one state, type or management paradigm to another. Second-order change involves:

\- changes in organization structures and processes;

\- a new approach to HRM, job design, work, commitment;

\- a different style of management;

\- a change in world view or interpretative scheme.

If this is so, we need to look beyond our accustomed approaches for ways of implementing second-order change. An implementation approach based on organizational transformation, which is directed at creating a new organizational vision, is likely to be particularly relevant.

Information systems implementation theory and practice have drawn for a long time on organizational development, because of a recognition that implementation is a complex behavioural process (Schultz and Slevin, 1975; Walton, 1989; Benjamin, 1993). The Lewin/Schein stage model in particular has been widely used in the literature. Organizational development approaches have usually focused at the level of individual behaviour and attitudes and have consequently not dealt with the context in which behavioural change is to occur (Willcocks and Mason, 1987). Organizational transformation reflects dissatisfaction with organizational development as practised in the early 1980s and a greater concern with large system and strategic change (Porras and Silvers, 1991). Whereas typical organizational development interventions had become concerned with obtaining a better fit between different internal elements of an organization, or a better external fit between the organization and the environment (Goodman and Kurke, 1982; Lundberg, 1984), organizational transformation concerns a major shift in the organization's world-view and meaning systems as well as its practice.

If we look at the remaining obstacles to the strategic use of office systems, they appear to include:

\- a continued focus on cost-displacement;

• quantifying intangible benefits;

• identifying systemic benefits;

\- the treatment of IT as an administrative overhead rather than as capital investment (Keen, 1991);

\- financial and psychological investment in the existing base of incompatible computer systems;

\- fear of the organizational impact;

\- uncertainty about how to go about implementation.

Behind all these issues lies the more general problem of lack of vision, which may be associated with a continued attachment to an organizational paradigm rooted in the pre-IT era (Dosi et al, 1988; Freeman, 1989; Liu et al, 1990; Thurow, 1992). Andriessen (1991) for example argues that the exploitation of IT requires a completely new, ‘non-Tayloristic and non-bureaucratic’ management philosophy, in other words, organizational transformation.

## The process of organizational transformation

Organizational transformation usually results from a crisis in the organization, which leads to criticism of the existing paradigm. In the first stage of organizational transformation, either senior management or a small group on the periphery becomes aware of the shortcomings of the present paradigm (Bartunek and Moch, 1987), and creates or adopts a new one. This group must become strongly committed to change before proceeding to the second stage.

The second stage consists of the diffusion of the new paradigm throughout the organization. One important aspect of this is assessing the obstacles to change. Possible cognitive and emotional barriers must be anticipated, which may include fear, uncertainty, and vested interests in the existing paradigm. Next, second-order change requires legitimation, backed up by a consistent allocation of power and resources. Nutt's empirical research on implementation tactics (1986) and Johnson's discussion of the role of symbolic action in strategic change (1990) indicate approaches to legitimation. The deficiencies of the existing paradigm need to be pointed out, which can be done by contrasting it with the organization's own purposes/mission, or with an external standard (eg bench-marking); by establishing new performance criteria, and demonstrating their feasibility (eg through visits to exemplars, by piloting).

The third stage is institutionalization, which is how persistence of the new paradigm is ensured. The change must be monitored and evaluated through various feedback mechanisms, and maintained by continuing to support it through the reward system (Beckhard and Harris, 1977; Goodman and Dean, 1982). Once it is institutionalized, it takes on a ‘rule-like quality’, becomes part of the world-taken-for-granted.

Organizational transformation in itself does not specify the nature of the new paradigm, nor does it say where it came from. It could be imported by new personnel or by consultants, or generated from within at almost any level. As to content, there is no lack of models, of which high performance work systems (Buchanan and McCalman, 1989), high involvement (Lawler, 1992), innovative (Kanter, 1985; Vrakking, 1990) and learning organizations (Senge, 1990; Garvin, 1993) are some examples. Although the labels differ, there is a good deal of consensus as to the actual content and nature of the new management paradigm, which has to do with customer focus, responsiveness, reducing management levels, empowering employees, and welcoming diversity of views. Information systems will increasingly be expected to further these strategic aims.

## Summary and conclusions

Office information technology has been steadily advancing in its integrative capacity. This means that office systems come to involve potentially all workers who produce or handle information in an organization, provide communication across functional and organizational boundaries, between individuals and groups, and connect with other (eg data processing) systems. Such a technology, which crosses vertical and horizontal boundaries in and between organizations, has great potential for organizational change.

Integrated office technology can support strategic as well as the efficiency/effectiveness goals of earlier computerized office systems. Office systems can be used strategically in order to reduce uncertainty in three major areas: task/production, the environment, and co-ordination, by means of respectively: internal process integration, boundary-spanning systems and organizational infrastructure. They are also significant means of creating new organizational forms.

Successful implementation of such systems requires approaches which attend to organizational impacts, including sociotechnical, soft systems and user-led systems.

However, the achievement of strategic goals often depends on making major changes in organization design, in some cases requiring a shift in the management paradigm. Whether requiring or enabling such change, office information systems can be seen to have moved a long way from their rather humble role as administrative support systems.

Existing first-order implementation strategies will not be adequate for strategic office information systems which involve second-order change. Implementation strategies for these systems can beneficially draw on the theory and practice of organizational transformation, especially the notions of change in state or paradigm, analysis of barriers to change, legitimation and institutionalization.
