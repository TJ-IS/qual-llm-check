# An evaluation of an innovative information technology — the case of Carrier EXPERT

James Heatley

Manufacturing and Financial Systems, United Technologies, Carrier, Syracuse, NY 13221, USA

Ritu Agarwal

University of Dayton, Dayton, OH 45469-2130, USA

Mohan Tanniru

Syracuse University, Syracuse, NY 13244-2130, USA

Introducing a new and innovative information technology into an organization creates both management challenges and opportunities. This article recounts the experiences of the Carrier Corporation, a major air-conditioning manufacturer, in introducing expert systems technology into its operations. Both the technology and the first application developed using it expanded well beyond their intended scope — from a back-office-type automation to becoming a competitive tool for Carrier. The authors monitored the system's evolution over a four-year time period. Both internal and strategic impacts of the technology on the corporation are assessed using a multi-dimensional evaluation model proposed in the literature. Finally, management insights and the lessons learned from Carrier's experiences are presented.

Keywords: organization-wide evaluation, expert systems, strategic impact

The creation of a new device can create new ways of being that previously did not exist and a framework for actions that would not have previously made sense. (Winograd and Flores, 1986)

Beginning with Leavitt and Whistler (1958) hypotheses about the impact of information technology (IT) on organizations have abounded. Contemporary experience validates their hypothesis and suggests that information systems have demonstrated the capability to have a profound effect on the overall structure and functioning of a firm; a majority of information systems applications often exert significant influence within the immediate boundaries of the unit where they are implemented. Such systems aimed primarily at internal efficiencies are valuable — they provide the infrastructure and the foundation for more innovative and effective uses of information technology. Occasionally more ‘visible’ and strategic information systems that either evolve out of a necessity to stay ahead of the competition or provide the firm with a competitive advantage (eg the SABRE system; Quickel, 1989) are implemented (Port, 1988; Clemons and Webber, 1990). On occasion, however, an internally focused system exceeds the expectations of those that conceived and developed it. Not only does the system fulfill its functional requirements and automate a desired task effectively, but its mere presence serves as a catalyst for instituting a process of organizational change in the way managers and employees conduct their business. It changes the way both the technology providers (ie management information systems (MIS) units) and the technology users (ie senior executives and end-users) interact during the system development activity. The purpose of this article is to describe the genesis of an application system and a technology platform — Carrier EXPERT, developed using expert systems (ES) technology over a four-year time period, to present the far-reaching and extensive impact of the system on the corporation’s utilization of IT, and to attempt to understand the reasons for its ‘success’.

Our primary focus in recounting the experiences of Carrier with EXPERT is on a comprehensive evaluation of its impacts both in terms of the influence of the system on internal operations as well as the external effects. Given the paucity of comprehensive assessment procedures that examine both internal and external effects, an objective of this article is to illustrate how such an assessment may be operationalized. In order to perform an internal assessment, we synthesize a framework that can be used to systematically evaluate the impact of an IT. The framework derives from two existing conceptualizations: DeLone and McLean's (1992) classification of levels of system success, and Porter's (1985) value chain. We argue that the impact of an IT on a task must be evaluated by taking into account both direct (intended) and induced (unanticipated) effects. We further suggest that the impact of IT on a task must be propagated from the local level to the global level in order to fully assess the organization-wide effects. This framework is developed in the following section and used subsequently to evaluate the impacts of Carrier EXPERT.

Carrier EXPERT was an unusual system in that it began as a strictly operational one and progressed to one with strategic potential. Thus, the evaluation of the system necessitated an ex post analysis of how the shift in system scope occurred. The external impact of EXPERT is evaluated using existing perspectives on how IT can affect a firm's competitiveness (Porter and Millar, 1985; Clemons, 1986; Clemons and Weber, 1990; Clemons and Row, 1991). Such an analysis can help our understanding of the strategies that can be used to deliver successful and, possibly, strategic systems in the future.

Methodologically, our study is an in-depth case analysis (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1984), with a focus on ‘description’, rather than exploration or explanation. To conduct this study, data from the Carrier corporation were collected using the qualitative ‘key informant’ method (Seidler, 1974), which requires the gathering and synthesis of information from several key individuals involved with technology implementation (viz a director of MIS, a knowledge engineer on the project team, a Vice President and general manager of the business unit within which the technology was introduced, project leaders involved in the implementation of EXPERT, product design engineers, and several end-users). The key informant method, using in-depth interviews, is appropriate for case-study and process-based research as it provides insights from multiple perspectives which may not be available through a survey-type data collection method. All authors have been closely associated with and have been monitoring the genesis of the system and its subsequent evolution over a four-year time span. The first author has overall responsibility for the use of information technology in Carrier's manufacturing division where EXPERT was developed. The second author has been involved in the project from a research perspective, while the third author served as a consultant for the conception of EXPERT in its very early stages.

## A framework for IT impact evaluation

The measurement of IT impacts has been an area of considerable interest for both academic and practitioner communities (Bjorn-Anerson et al, 1986; DeLone and McLean, 1992). Prior research suggests that IT impacts need to be evaluated using various pre- and post-implementation metrics, including intangible benefits such as user satisfaction and system quality, and more tangible benefits such as cost savings and revenue generation (DeLone and McLean, 1992). This type of evaluation, while no doubt essential, has the potential limitation in that it tends to focus attention on the immediately observable (or 'local') impact. Often, systems developed to support a specific activity, through its interdependency on other tasks that either precede or succeed it, can have a broader positive or negative ('global') impact on the organization. Recognizing the danger in focusing only on local impact, DeLone and McLean (1992) synthesize the many success measures researchers and practitioners have used in prior studies and propose a model for studying IS impact across system boundaries. This model examines a system's success at the system level (system and information quality), user level (use and user satisfaction), task level (within the context of the task a user performs), and organization level (how it affects other tasks within the organization).

Although the measurement of technology impact at the system and user level is relatively straightforward, the same cannot be said about the task and organizational levels. IT has been shown to impact a task in unpredictable ways (Agarwal and Tanniru, 1992a), thus complicating the measurement of task level impact. Similarly, there are very few established procedures discussed in the literature to perform a systematic organization-wide assessment of IT impact. In this context, Porter's value chain and the competitive factors model (Porter and Millar, 1985; Clark, 1993) have been utilized to isolate some of these organizational level effects.

Typically, IT can influence a task in two ways: either through its impact on the product of the task (eg quality of the information generated) or the process (task behavior) used in generating the product (eg the process used to forecast sales). Often the impact on the process can also influence the product that is generated by the task (a better sales forecast in the example above). Hence, the net impact of technology on a task can be either positive or negative depending on how the benefits achieved by improving the product/process (direct impact) are possibly off-set or complemented by changes ‘induced’ by technology on the process/product (indirect impact). Both the direct and induced impacts on a task have to be propagated to the global level in order to assess the impact of the system at the organizational level.

To perform an organization level assessment of IT, one can systematically move outwards from the target task to those that succeed or precede it and analyze the impact on the same dimensions (system, user and task). An organization's value chain (Porter and Millar, 1985) is a useful conceptualization for facilitating such an assessment. The value chain can be used to understand the interdependencies among various primary and support activities that, together, are instrumental in bringing a product or service to the customer. The primary activities in the value chain include inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. The support activities include the corporate infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, and procurement.

In summary, our framework (Figure 1), consistent with that proposed by DeLone and McLean, suggests that IT impact ought to be assessed along system, user, and task dimensions. However, we propose that the impact of IT on a task needs to be examined for both direct and induced effects. Finally, we suggest that an appropriate operationalization for assessing organization-wide impact is Porter's value chain; system, user, and task dimension impacts must be propagated from the local to the global level to all of the activities in the value chain.

The framework provides a holistic basis for evaluating the impact of an IT. Its roots derive from prior conceptualizations that have had significant research support. Its comprehensive nature helps to ensure that no impact is inadvertently overlooked and it can help to provide insights into how a system might become strategic over time. In a subsequent section we apply the framework to the assessment of EXPERT's impact on the Carrier Corporation.

## Introducing ES technology at Carrier

Carrier is the world's largest manufacturer of heating, ventilating and air-conditioning (HVAC) equipment, with sales over \$4 billion in 1990. Headquartered in Farmington, Connecticut, Carrier is part of United Technologies which had over \$22 billion in sales in 1990. Carrier's principal global competitors are large Japanese conglomerates, such as Matsushita, Hitachi, Mitsubishi Electricity Co, Toshiba, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Daikin and Sanyo. Carrier's sales volume is greatest in North America. In addition to HVAC, Carrier Transicold Division focuses on container refrigeration of trucks, truck-trailers, bus, rail and subway air-conditioning, and is the market leader in such types of refrigeration.

The Carrier computing environment consisted of two IBM 3090 mainframes networked to about 300 domestic and several international locations. Information systems applications decisions are largely centralized, ie resource allocation is, in large part, the responsibility of a central organizational unit, under the control of a Vice President of Information Systems. Reporting to this VP are three directors who both direct the application development for functional units, such as manufacturing, finance, human resource, marketing and sales, and provide support functions such as technology planning and management.

Historically Carrier has sought effective IT-based solutions to business problems. Technologies such as telecommunications, electronic data interchange, office automation, and computer-aided software engineering (CASE) methodologies are continuously explored and used to integrate many of its operations. These technologies, however, have varying degrees of success within the organization. The organizational structure of the MIS group has been revised recently to provide an even greater focus to the effective deployment and dissemination of newer information technologies.

As part of an impetus to apply new and innovative technologies to provide better service, the MIS organization at Carrier undertook the investigation of ES technology with the aid of an external consultant in 1989. The goal of the exploratory project was simple — obtain the technology with no long-term commitment, evaluate its potential, and then make an informed and considered decision about its future within the corporation. Potential was to be evaluated via an application that would convincingly demonstrate the technology's strengths and capabilities to applications managers. The overt intent was to obtain their buy-in should the technology prove to be viable in the long-term. MIS used its discretionary funds to get the project off the ground, and placed an applications manager in charge of the exploration and a technical specialist to coordinate the project activities.

Carrier considered as a major goal its ability to streamline the organizational processes required to deliver a quality product with prompt service. While engineering has always been at the forefront in improving Carrier equipment, the continued success of the organization in the market place is critically dependent on its ability to reduce the order cycle time — the time between the acceptance of an order and the delivery/installation of the product to/at the customer site. Configuring a product quickly and accurately has important implications for reducing the order cycle-time and, in turn, improving customer service. Indeed, order cycle automation has been identified as a key component of competitive positioning.

The choice of product configuration as the first application for experimenting with this innovative technology had several benefits. Because of the success of Digital's VAX configurator application (Barker and O'Conner, 1989; Sviokla,

1990) and Honeywell's use of expert systems to service complex air-conditioners, Carrier's VP of business operations became interested in the potential of such applications. Delays in the ordering process, because of errors in configuring product numbers, had been a source of concern to engineering and field personnel. Given that a configuration application written using a procedural language had been implemented before by the MIS group with mixed results, the problem provided a controlled test of the new ES technology.

The configurator expert system, now known as Carrier EXPERT, configures a set of part numbers for a particular air-conditioning equipment series based on customer request. While this is not a task that requires considerable expertise, its complexity arises from the number of design combinations that have to be considered before an equipment can be chosen to meet a customer need. The potential pay-off rested in the ability of the system to address several weaknesses that were inherent in the existing method for configuring such equipment. Some of the weaknesses include order processing delays attributable to configuration errors, shipment delays when some of these errors propagated to the manufacturing and assembly floor, and pricing errors. All have significant impact on the cost and quality of service and, in turn, lead to dissatisfied customers and a potential loss of existing or repeat business for Carrier.

Carrier EXPERT was conceived for use by salespeople or sales assistants to support the process of filling orders. The system ensures that all orders constructed are complete and correct. Design dependencies are stored in a database and accessed by the expert system. Thus, a salesperson need not provide all of the information required for a specific order since part of it can be deduced by the system based on its knowledge of design dependencies. Valid orders prepared by EXPERT then serve as key inputs for pricing and bill of material preparation. The system was originally developed and implemented in IBM's Expert Systems Environment (ESE) running on the 3090 with links to DB2 tables. It has been recently re-implemented in Trinzsic Corporation's ADS environment to exploit performance improvements and more expressive knowledge representations (specifically, an object-oriented model). When a field salesperson signs onto the system using IBM's CICS via an online communication link, the first screen they encounter is the expert system logo, together with a message for the user. At this time the user may either retrieve the previously stored quote for an order or initiate a new order. The system requests lead-time desired and, based on this, the required shipdate is established. From this point on, the system interactively asks a series of questions (Figure 3a) and, by consulting design dependencies stored explicitly as rules or as instances in database tables, configures a model number (Figure 3b), prices the order, and prepares the job-order and a summary of the quote. If needed, additional quotes can be initiated at this time. If the quote is accepted, it is stored in a DB2 file and, at the end of each day, accessed by an interface program of the manufacturing system.

Even though the initial objective of the exploratory expert system project was to simply evaluate the technology for future use, it has been adopted by a large user group — sales representatives, order handlers and the engineers. It met many of their concerns with respect to the accuracy and speed of order entry and validation activities. It created significant excitement within the user group and several changes were suggested by them. After a series of refinements, the production version of the system was installed in the field in 1991. Currently, the system is in use by more than 400 field sales personnel from many locations across the USA.

Carrier used conventional languages such as COBOL to develop such systems in the past and found that the use of ES technology allowed for increased flexibility in system evolution. The system grew from one with 120 rules to over 900 over a three-year time span. The use of expert system software and a prototyping development methodology allowed participatory system development and many new features were added based on user feedback on a continual basis. The software also facilitated the provision of explanations for the recommendation (ie why a given configuration was chosen). The application of ES technology for configuration systems is not uncommon, and the success of such systems, often referred to as operational expert systems, has been widely reported (eg Sviokla, 1990). Indeed, as will be evident in the discussion that follows, the effects of EXPERT are very similar to those observed in the case of XCON (Barker and O'Conner, 1989; Sviokla, 1990).

Given the unanticipated transformation of the system from a proof-of-concept, exploratory project, to a fully-functional support tool, Carrier management was interested in evaluating the overall impacts of EXPERT on the corporation. The framework described earlier is used below to perform such an organization-wide assessment.

In order to assess the impact of EXPERT, semi-structured interviews with several individuals involved in the project were conducted. The objective of the interviews was to obtain data on EXPERT's impacts from all individuals directly or indirectly affected by the system. The interviews were structured around three major questions: (1) What has been the impact of EXPERT on the activities of and individuals in the specific business unit with which the interviewee was most closely associated?; (2) If the system had to be developed all over again, what would the interviewees like done differently, both from a technical and process standpoint?; and (3) What did the interviewees foresee for EXPERT in the future? Interviewees were encouraged to reflect on both the positive and negative effects of EXPERT. To the extent possible, multiple individuals were interviewed from each business unit and their responses consolidated.

The impact on the sales and marketing activity was described by order correspondents and sales personnel, and the project leader who represented user interests. The impact on the operations activity was described by the engineers who championed the use of ES technology initially for order validation and later for product design. The impact on support functions was discussed in interviews with the technology group (the architect of the EXPERT project and the MIS director in charge of this application domain), as well as the corporate VP and General Manager in charge of this product line (Commercial Applied Equipment Systems (CAES)).

## Impact on primary activities

The technology was overtly intended to support the order validation task (target task) of the ‘marketing and sales’ activity, but its impact on other tasks within that activity, as well as on tasks in the operations activity such as design, scheduling and bill of material preparation has been significant. These impacts are discussed below.

## Sales and marketing

The order validation task, for which the initial ES was intended, is a part of the marketing and sales activity. Prior to the introduction of expert systems technology, the task of configuring air-conditioning equipment required a validation task which included manual access to various design options to determine if the user's specifications could be met, ie design feasibility was assessed. Errors in design options resulted in the sales person and/or customer being contacted for clarification and correction before the order was processed further. Structurally, this task corresponds to an operational decision where most of the knowledge, although technical in nature, is well defined, and primarily requires access to complex design interdependencies. The number of potential design configurations for the series of equipment handled by EXPERT was of the order of several billion, resulting in a high probability of error on the part of the salesperson. Prior to the introduction of EXPERT, 40 per cent of the orders configured by sales people contained errors and were not manufacturable.

The introduction of EXPERT to validate orders has reduced the number of errors propagated past the validation phase, the time required to validate these orders, and the costs of rework that would have occurred if an incorrectly configured system had gone to manufacturing. In addition, the introduction of EXPERT has eliminated the labor utilized to validate the order. These impacts, referred to here collectively as order efficiencies, are summarized in Table 1 (pre-and post-system implementation). The data in Table 1 were gathered by Carrier personnel over a three-year time frame.

Table 1 provides compelling evidence for the substantive impact that EXPERT has had on Carrier. Through the use of EXPERT, the average selection time per unit has been reduced by a factor of 20. Previously the time spent in configuring a unit represented a suboptimal use of an important resource, the sales people; this saved time could now be better spent interacting with customers and generating additional sales. The order throughput cycle was cut by one-sixth; according to the Vice President and General Manager of CAES, this enabled Carrier to ask a price premium from their customers as the competition was unable to match the order cycle time. Configuration errors, previously at a level of 40 per cent, were completely eliminated.

The automated validation function has allowed a sales person to enter order information interactively and develop a customized configuration quickly. When, as a logical next step, the pricing task was added to the system, where prices for different configurations could be obtained from component price tables, the enhancement provided a means with which a sales person could respond to user requests quickly and provide price information in a timely manner. This feature not only enhanced the functionality of the system by increasing pricing accuracy in orders from 20 to 100 per cent, it was instrumental in the sales force's satisfaction with the system. See Table 2 for some general comments by field personnel.

With the involvement of the sales personnel, the system went through several refinements to reflect their selling style which gives them a competitive edge in the market place. Some refinements to EXPERT suggested by the sales force include flexibility in handling multiple orders and interrupting the order taking process so that orders can be recalled and changed based on revised customer requirements. The sales personnel were excited about the way the system evolved over time and the sense of control it provided them over their selling activities. The frequency of use was monitored for some time, since in the initial stages system adoption was entirely voluntary. However, in spite of extreme satisfaction with the system by several individuals, it was adopted by only 42 per cent of the sales force. A policy change that required sales people to submit only EXPERT-based orders was necessary before system utilization reached 100 per cent.

While EXPERT, as initially proposed, was intended to affect the validation function, it has had an impact on other sales functions, such as pricing and order taking, as well. This is not completely unexpected as efficiencies contributed by IT in one task do impact others that either precede or succeed it. However, the impact has been much broader than anticipated, as EXPERT started affecting task behavior in an unexpected manner (Barley, 1986). The ease with which complex products can be ordered through EXPERT makes a sales person willing to sell products with a high premium. A sales person that typically sold \$2 million worth of equipment can now sell up to \$4 million because of the selling efficiencies attributable to the elimination of the time and difficulty elements in the configuration of the more complex products. ## Operations

While some impact of EXPERT on the tasks within the sales and marketing activity is expected, its impact on the operations activity is quite unanticipated. The impact observed here has been on the engineering and bill of material preparation tasks.

Although the early development of EXPERT included relatively simple products which are configured using 12, 16-digit part numbers, more complex products have about 30–40 such product numbers. While adding complexity to a product makes the associated engineering and testing a challenge, EXPERT did enhance product design with new options. The technology has allowed changes to the configuration of product options quickly by storing much of the design information in databases for easy update and maintenance. By using a database as opposed to conventional rules as a repository of design knowledge, the timing and frequency of changes can now be controlled by engineers, thus empowering them.

In addition, the engineering staff was able to increase product complexity with competitive options (ie options that others in the industry cannot duplicate quickly), because they knew that the technology embedded in configuring these applications would not unduly complicate the order taking task of a sales person. According to one of the engineers, who was also an expert for the initial system prototype,

EXPERT has allowed us to design complicated products that we would not have designed if we didn't have EXPERT. (Charles Griffin, Design Engineer)

Even though engineers have used the system actively to change many of the design options they offer to customers, primarily because of the ease with which they can change them through databases, they do not appear to be quite satisfied with this approach. While the storing of design knowledge in databases for easy maintenance may have been useful, it has contributed to a serious discussion on how engineering knowledge representation should be handled in the future. An engineer often views the product world in terms of components and their interaction in ways that is different from the two-dimensional table representation that a database necessitates. An alternative representation or technology is being sought to accurately manage product design knowledge.

In the case of XCON also, a similar shift in responsibility and importance has been reported. As pointed out by Sviokla (1990), the development and widespread use of XCON reinforced the critical role of the ‘expert’ in the business.

EXPERT provides smooth hooks to two major systems: automatic queuing of the order into the scheduling system, and bill of materials (BOM) preparation in the manufacturing system. Whereas the impact on scheduling is primarily one of increased accuracy, the impact of EXPERT on BOM preparation is quite interesting. EXPERT guarantees that the part numbers generated are correct, so that they can be planned and manufactured at the lowest component level (referred to as piece parts), rather than work with intermediate sub-assemblies. If one is not certain about the accuracy of the piece parts used to make a sub-assembly, there is a tendency to use the details of many possible sub-assemblies to ensure validity. For example, the BOM for a fan sub-assembly, prior to EXPERT, was constructed from a variety of sub-assemblies, as piece parts used to construct such sub-assemblies were not always considered accurate. With EXPERT, no information need be maintained by the manufacturing system on sub-assemblies as the configured piece parts can be accurately put together to form a correct fan sub-assembly. Thus, EXPERT has simplified BOM preparation of the manufacturing unit.

The effects of XCON on the manufacturing process at DEC were equally dramatic. Not only did it facilitate a realignment of manufacturing capacity where product manufacture could be moved between plants without the need for expensive training, it also resulted in the elimination of one entire step in the manufacturing process, viz final assembly and test (Barker and O'Conner, 1989). This, in turn, affected inventory levels, cycle times, and overall manufacturing costs positively.

In summary, EXPERT contributed to both anticipated and unanticipated impacts on two primary activities of the value chain — marketing and sales, and operations. We have limited our discussion here to only those impacts that have been explicitly observed as of today and articulated by individuals closely associated with or affected by the system. However, it is likely that these impacts will, at some point in the future, affect other primary activities as well. Examples of such potential effects include those on service (better service to the customer, given that the configuration process is customized and can be tracked), in-bound logistics (better tracking of customer orders and accurate scheduling will allow the organization to procure raw material as needed, especially if this system forces the organization to move to a demand flow manufacturing type environment), and outbound logistics (better estimation of ship dates will permit accurate shipment scheduling).

## Impact on support activities

The discussion below focuses on the impact of EXPERT on two support activities (technology development and corporate infrastructure) along the dimensions proposed in Figure 1. Both MIS (the technology enabler) and corporate management (the facilitator of technology adoption and diffusion) perspectives were sought to assess these effects. In analyzing the impact of EXPERT on technology development, focus has been placed on the way it has altered the delivery of IS technology at Carrier in support of various activities. In analyzing the impact on corporate infrastructure, the focus is on understanding changes in the role of and relationships between various organizational entities (IS and user groups, IS and top management, etc) induced by the evolution of EXPERT.

## Technology development

The configuration projects were successful because the development process was able to take ideas generated by the sales staff, build prototypes, test their feasibility, and provide quick feedback to the user group. This exposure to the prototyping methodology made individuals aware of both the process involved and the role they need to play if they were to take full advantage of new technologies effectively. According to the knowledge engineer in charge of the initial project development,

The prototyping approach used to extract user needs, interact with users as the system evolved, and solicit and incorporate whatever changes they requested to make the system useful, have all contributed significantly to the success of the system. The entire system became a joint effort of the users, knowledge engineer and the expert. As we pursue the use of new technologies, it is critical that conventional systems development methodologies be modified to accommodate the organization's ability to manage these technologies. (Richard Linck, Knowledge Engineer)

The prototyping strategy followed for systems development in the case of EXPERT allowed sales personnel to better define their requirements, as they were able to see the system potential in each stage of its evolution. For example, ideas generated during the early stages allowed them to establish broader guidelines for screen design to be followed for the configuration of all future products. EXPERT encouraged the development of a common user interface for configuration applications that support the marketing and sales activity, thus improving the overall quality in the delivery of such applications.

Prototyping, while an excellent approach for rapid system development, has to have some controls built into it, if a project ever is to leave the prototyping mode to a completed productional system. (Kevin Dudash, Project Leader)

For the most part the outcomes of the prototyping approach were positive for the corporation. Some negative effects, however, have also been observed. A lack of clear guidelines on when prototyping, after serving the needed function of extracting customer requirements, should be replaced with a firm timetable for implementing a productional system has actually contributed to significant delays in introducing a more complex product (39NX). Only after an agreement was reached with the user on implementing certain features and delaying the others for a future version was the system able to move from prototyping to productional mode. Carrier MIS management now recognizes that it is imperative to plan this transition as a part of the development process.

EXPERT requires engineers to store most of the design knowledge in database tables so that it can be easily maintained. This is in contrast with traditional approaches to expert system design where declarative knowledge is generally represented as facts in the expert system programming environment itself. These knowledge base tables offer a means for engineers to take on the role of system maintenance that has traditionally been reserved for MIS professionals. This empowers the engineers with the ability to make changes to design options as and when needed. The shift in responsibility is also expected to have broader implications in the long run by redefining the roles that IS and user groups play in systems development. However, the flip side of this benefit is the complexity in testing the system prior to its transfer to production, as pointed out by the project leader. Unless the change process is streamlined, and the knowledge base possibly modularized (Agarwal and Tanniru, 1992b), it is difficult to isolate changes for testing. Tools are currently being evaluated to facilitate the testing process.

While the experience with the application far exceeded the expectations of individuals in the operations and marketing/sales area (ie service goals in terms of satisfaction and functionality were more than adequately met), the development process used has not been without user and developer frustrations. Most of what the organization has learned in introducing the technology is through trial and error. For example, the shift in the maintenance responsibility to engineers alluded to earlier and interactive system development using continual sales person feedback were not planned for ahead of time, thus contributing to some delays in the development process. Also, many issues such as securing access to knowledge bases and possible downloading of the software/knowledge to a PC-platform were not initially addressed, partly because no one anticipated the enthusiastic acceptance of the system. The ensuing delays in the system delivery have resulted in some negative effect on user satisfaction with the development process.

The prior experience of the systems development staff was primarily with systems that are data intensive. Most of the applications they have been responsible for, such as an order and distribution system, are heavily business transaction driven and require significant interaction among a number of database files. The new technologies such as ES, however, are process intensive, often requiring a manipulation of knowledge that is qualitative, and interaction with people on a more frequent basis. User education on issues such as prototyping and the need for continual commitment to see a project through to its completion are important.

In essence, EXPERT forced MIS to take a hard look at the skills of its staff in terms of their training, their understanding of the prototyping process (which often requires analysis, design, and implementation to be integrated as opposed to being handled by different people in a sequential manner), and how to estimate the effort required for undertaking new expert systems projects. The frustrations experienced during the development of EXPERT led to a series of training programs on expert systems and prototyping as a development methodology for the IS group. Thus, although initially ES technology was viewed as something different, it is now being viewed as an integral part of the suite of technologies MIS uses to support business needs.

Although MIS actively participated in the ES evolution, it felt it needed to take an even more active leadership role in seeking out opportunities for introducing new technologies. This implies altering the prevailing culture in MIS from one that views projects in phases for establishing deliverables and estimating costs, to one that encourages iteration in system development. Already efforts are underway to estimate projects of this type using modifications to conventional function point analysis. MIS management has become cognizant of the need for a total systems approach both at the technology and methodology levels. This may imply that the development process has to use a mix of technologies to solve a user's problem and a hybrid approach to develop the systems. As stated by Kevin Dudash, from a technical standpoint, additional tools are being sought for testing and change management to facilitate faster development by having multiple developers work on the same knowledge base.

For DEC, XCON resulted in similar induced effects (Barker and O'Conner, 1989). It provided a test bed for ES applications and led to the evolution of several software engineering tools, techniques and languages. The process of technology introduction at Cerrier, however, was distinct from that at DEC, where an infrastructure to support new technology introduction already existed prior to the development of XCON.

## Infrastructure

MIS has realized that if new technologies are to make significant inroads into the organization's culture and work-patterns, they have to be made available in the form of tool kits — like spreadsheet packages — so that users can play a more productive role in identifying potential applications for the technologies. It is imperative that this type of knowledge sharing should take place if the role of new technologies in application domains is to be discovered quickly and effectively for competitive advantage. Thus, MIS is now taking a leadership role in ensuring user participation in new technology deployment.

The general impression was that some of the frustrations experienced during the development of EXPERT could be avoided in the future when new technologies are introduced, if they are assisted by those that have travelled the ‘technology road’ before. Also, the organization now recognizes that for systems where the a priori requirements are fuzzy, system evolution has to follow a single and continuous process orientation, rather than a bunch of discrete steps typically associated with a structured methodology. This experience actually made MIS look for a different learning paradigm for technology transfer in the future. As an example, the implementation of CASE technology recently has followed a more methodological approach to systems evolution.

In the words of one of the MIS directors,

Underestimating the impact from both the business and IS perspective has created a situation where the demand for services that can use this technology far outstripped our ability to deliver. This is, in fact, a good news situation, since the accelerated demand is caused by the significant impact the technology had on the business. (Jim Heatley, Director, MIS)

Expert systems have become a way of life for those individuals associated with the air handler product. This technology is no longer a new and radical concept; it has become the status quo. The challenges now are to provide modifications quicker, and to design new configurations and better interfaces in a shorter time frame. The use of the same technology to develop all configuration applications has the benefit of reusing what has been done already whenever a new product line is added. Since a common interface being developed to configure products has shown promise towards a standardization of the ordering process in the USA, there has been some interest in exploring the use of a similar approach to order products globally by rendering all national differences transparent. The organization is now looking for a customization of technology for product development, ie multiple versions of EXPERT, to meet the diverse needs of customers across continents.

It is exciting to see how this technology has grown from an initial concept just a few short years ago to the standard it has become today. (Kevin Dudash, Project Leader)

Also, the success of EXPERT has, in general, enhanced the technology support for the marketing and sales function, both in terms of hardware and software. EXPERT made user management seriously examine the possibility of investing in PC technology for the salesperson's use in the field, thus potentially affecting the way sales activities will be performed in the future.

In summary, the effort expended in the development and implementation of EXPERT has resulted in a significant impact on both primary and support activities of the value chain, and has caused beneficial shifts in organizational processes. The next section will examine EXPERT's overall impact from an external perspective and discuss the competitive and strategic effects of the system.

## Evaluating the strategic impacts of Carrier EXPERT

A mind that is stretched to a new idea never returns to its original dimension. (Oliver Wendell Holmes)

Even though EXPERT was initially conceived as a way to validate an order, the idea of having design knowledge stored in a system for facilitating both sales generation and continuous product upgrades has become a de facto standard at Carrier today. It is inconceivable now to configure a new product without an expert system component; an effect that is quite significant by itself. There have been, however, some strategic effects also observed; this section provides a brief discussion of the transformation of EXPERT from an operational to a strategic system and its contributions to Carrier's competitive advantage.

Porter and Millar (1985) suggest four ways in which IT can provide an organization with a strategic advantage — reduce the bargaining power of buyers, reduce the bargaining power of sellers, reduce the threat of substitute products/services, and reduce the threat of new entrants. In this case, IT thus far has had no noticeable impact on the bargaining power of sellers or on the possible entry of substitute products into the market. However, by providing an effective system to configure products quickly and effectively, and by facilitating the design of complex products, it may have some impact on the bargaining power of the buyers and the threat of new entrants into the market place.

Carrier EXPERT allows the sales force (independent and company owned) to order complex products with high price premiums (both to the distributors and the company). EXPERT, with its simple user interfaces, accurate configurations, and timely ordering and pricing, can make it very attractive for a buyer (ie an independent distributor) to sell products that have a high price premium. This has the net effect of making it disadvantageous for a buyer not to sell Carrier equipment, thus implicitly reducing the bargaining power of the buyer.

EXPERT, with its embedded expert systems technology, by itself is not expected to create competitive advantage. The use of this type of technology has been widely discussed in the literature and can be easily introduced by competitors. It appears that some competitors have similar types of technologies already in the market place for use by order correspondents and distributors, thus making use of this technology more a ‘strategic necessity’ rather than a ‘strategic advantage’ (Clemons, 1986), or will become part of the base case (Clemons and Weber, 1990). However, there is a possibility of achieving a competitive advantage in a more subtle way, as is discussed below.

Clemons and Row (1991) argue that one can look at the characteristics of a technology and its reliance on strategic resources to determine whether it can provide sustainable strategic advantage. They define a strategic resource as one which contributes significantly to the investment base of a firm and is not freely available in the factor markets, thus providing competitive advantage to those firms that control it using innovative technology. While vertical integration (such as linking directly to critical suppliers and customers) might limit the availability of some resources to competitors, this was not observed at Carrier. However, Clemons and Row suggest the use of a transaction cost approach that focuses on the interaction between multiple agents in the value chain (ie vertical interfaces) to predict the role IT might play in providing competitive advantage to a firm.

From the observations elicited from user management, one can conclude that EXPERT will enable customers to order custom-configured products that have many complex options. This can, hence, enhance the value associated with the sales transaction (as high premiums are charged for these products), without significantly impacting the cost of processing these transactions. Since Carrier prides itself on engineering expertise and its perceived competitive edge over its competitors on complex products, it is anticipated that ES technology will continue to provide them competitive advantage by leveraging this knowledge effectively to field sales personnel. This appears to be one case where IT, tied so closely to the successful sale of the product, alters the product mix that is offered and, in turn, contributes to differentiation in favor of the innovator (Clemons and Row, 1991). In other words, EXPERT may be providing the organization with an ability to gain advantage in product lines that are complex, thus giving the firm a competitive advantage over its use of another asset, ie manufacturing and engineering expertise. The net effect may be a reduced threat of new entrants into these premium, complex products, which will strengthen Carrier's position. This outcome is similar to the capability offered by XCON to DEC, the construction of à la carte configurations which has, historically, been a competitive advantage for DEC (Barker and O'Conner, 1989).

In summary, a broader use of EXPERT in both engineering and sales has allowed the firm to design complex yet customized products in which Carrier has a manufacturing and engineering edge and that command a price premium, and provide competitive service by allowing the products to be shipped within a short time. Hence, an operationally conceived system, through a series of enhancements, has taken on a ‘strategic’ role and now has considerable organizational influence.

## Management insights and issues

## The process of change

Markus and Robey (1988) suggest that causal theories examining the relationship between information technology and organizational change ought to specify three dimensions of the underlying theoretical model: the causal agency (the entity to which change is attributable), the logical structure (the time span of the theory), and the level of analysis (entities about which the theory concerns itself). While the case described in this article does represent a single data point, it is nevertheless instructive to examine the particular aspects of the theory which apply.

What is interesting in this case is that the causal agent shifted over time so that all three imperatives identified by Markus and Robey were observed. Initially, the commercial availability of expert systems technology and its success in some product configuration applications made the organization explore the technology and select and develop an application to meet specific objectives. Although the technology provided the initial impetus for change (the technological imperative), it was an identified need by organizational actors that contributed to a change in the configuration process (the organizational imperative). Subsequently, much of what has occurred has been caused by the complex, synergistic, and in large part, unanticipated interaction between several organizational entities (viz the MIS unit, users, engineers and managers), resulting in the technology becoming a critical component of business operations (the emergent perspective).

Change can be assessed either by using a set of hypothesized relationships between antecedent conditions and observed outcomes (variance theory), or by looking for conditions whose occurrence is necessary to make a technology venture successful (process theory). At Carrier, several conditions identified in the literature for successful project outcomes — such as having an executive champion, committing needed resources to keep the project going, designing systems in a flexible manner to meet changing user needs, and employing a participatory development methodology — were observed. What is more crucial, however, is that the timing of these initiatives affected their influence and contribution to project success, a point elaborated upon further below.

In Markus and Robey's conceptualization, the final dimension of a causal model is the level of analysis — whether the theory hypothesizes or tests relationships about individuals or collectives. In our study of EXPERT, we notice that change has occurred at many levels and focusing on one single level of analysis would not allow for a complete examination of the impact. While one could analyze change at a micro or macro level, we have chosen to look at the changes that have occurred on all entities that have a stake in the success of the system, ranging from the micro perspective of a single salesperson to the more global perspective offered by EXPERT's competitive impacts. Indeed, such a mixed level analysis is necessary in order to isolate both the anticipated and unanticipated impacts of the technology. Figure 5 lists various events germane to EXPERT's evolution that span a four-year time period. Our objective here is to draw the reader's attention to a variety of events whose occurrence over time has created the needed environment to foster sustained interest in the technology. In summary, we discuss some of the causal process factors that were observed over this four-year time span and which we believe were the primary drivers for the success of the system and the technology.

## The lessons learned

The identity of the project champion. A distinct pattern that emerges from the timeline is the identify of the project champion. At Carrier, several key individuals have provided the needed support at various stages, and the mantle of project champion has been passed on depending on the needs of the project at a point in time. For example, while MIS played a lead role in initiating the exploration of the technology, the CAES group played an important role in moving the project forward. When performance weaknesses of the system could have hampered further development, the new management in MIS invested in more efficient and better integrated software to keep the project expansion feasible. The project leader's emphasis on common 'look and feel' user-friendly interfaces made the system highly marketable. The active participation of the user group in testing the product at various phases during product enhancement, and user management's commitment to provide the needed support to ensure that the system would be available to the sales force in a convenient form, were also instrumental in the success of the overall effort.

Thus, although the literature has emphasized the importance of a project champion (eg Beath, 1991), this case study illustrates that there is a greater need for process champions — one at each stage of the project who is responsible for overcoming the next obstacle. At Carrier a single user and an expert were the champions in the exploration stage; user management and MIS management in the adoption stage; and dedicated user groups in the diffusion and implementation stage. For IT applications that use new technology, that require a significant time lag between conceptualization and implementation, and that require the collaborative efforts of several units within a firm, multiple champions that address different needs of the project at various stages of its evolution may be critical. A similar shift in the mantle of the project champion over the evolution of the system has been reported for the case of XCON as well (Barker and O'Conner, 1989), where several roles crucial to the success of the overall development effort have been identified.

The role of communication. Gaining the support of multiple individuals at varying points in time to champion a project requires that each individual has a vested interest in the project's success. Often the level of commitment may be related to the extent to which they perceive that benefits outweigh costs. At Carrier, the extent of impact has influenced the level of commitment and degree of support. In the early stages, it was recognized that the system would help to speed up order entry and validation. Later, its potential for providing pricing support increased the interest of the sales force (a major user group), and its potential for handling complex products excited engineers to build and manage complex products. Finally, its impact on reducing the order, pricing and scheduling cycle, and its acceptance by sales people, made management treat it as a strategic application. Throughout the project life-cycle management made a concerted effort to not only assess benefits, but to communicate this assessment to relevant parties. An ongoing evaluation of system impact, disseminating knowledge about potential benefits throughout the organization, and a policy of continued communication were all instrumental in gaining commitment from multiple individuals as the project progressed.

The management of expectations. Although providing sponsors, champions and resource allocators with information about system benefits helps to ensure their initial commitment, ensuring that the project meets their expectations by delivering what is promised is even more critical to keep the commitment on-going. This is especially important when the project has a long gestation cycle and spans several time periods. By keeping the overall project effort highly visible, testing it against various contingencies (eg interfaces with other systems, and operating under realistic order processing conditions), the organization was able to convince people of its workability and minimize any risk of potential system failure. Although a conservative strategy that limits technology exposure until it has proved to be useful might appear attractive, a better strategy seems to be one of letting it prove its worth under tough scrutiny, by having its progress seen and criticized by various stakeholders in the organization. This final point is particularly crucial for newer technologies where initial user disenchantment may effectively eliminate any chance of eventual success in technology diffusion.

## Conclusions

Information technology is a critical ingredient in an organization's ability to successfully compete in the market place, but it is not always feasible to predict which technology will be most effective in meeting its competitive needs ahead of time. In this article we have documented the experiences of a large manufacturing organization with an information technology and a system which escalated well beyond its intended scope. The system moved from a back-office type automation to become a competitive tool for Carrier. It affected the way the organization does business by altering the way products are designed and sold, and by causing shifts in business processes. Moreover, the system and the technology provided both user and MIS management with a new perspective on information technologies and their management. We discussed the impact of the system on the Carrier Corporation along a multitude of dimensions using a comprehensive evaluation model. The effects of the system were compared with those of a similar application developed at Digital Equipment Corporation. Finally, management insights gained from observing the project for a four-year time span were presented. These insights can provide direction in the management of advanced technologies.

Carrier's experience with EXPERT and ES technology underscores the importance of assessing the overall impact of any new information technology, so that lessons learned either at the system level or at the organization level can be properly applied in the future. Also, it prevents an organization from claiming the success or failure of a technology prematurely. Impact assessment after project completion is particularly crucial as impacts turn out to be, more often than not, fortuitous rather than the outcomes of a carefully planned process. Similar studies that examine impact through an in-depth case analysis may help us to build a body of research that will allow us to start to predict the possible success of a project, based on how a technology is planned for implementation. We are currently examining impacts of similar systems across a larger number of organizations with the intent of discovering any causal relationships that may help to improve our predictive ability.
