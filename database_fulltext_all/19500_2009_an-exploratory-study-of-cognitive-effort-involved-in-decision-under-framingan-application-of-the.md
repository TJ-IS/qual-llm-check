# An exploratory study of cognitive effort involved in decision under Framing—an application of the eye-tracking technology

## a b s t r a c t

The framing effect, proposed by Tversky and Kahneman [A. Tversky, D. Kahneman, The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice, Science 211 (4481) (1981) 453–458.], refers to the phenomenon that varying the presentations of the same problem can systematically affect the choice one makes. In this research we have reviewed a literature related to the framing effect and neurobiological studies of emotion. This review leads us to conceptualize that framing may induce emotion, which in turn impinges on the level of cognitive effort that subsequently shapes the framing effect. We then employ the eye-tracking technology to explore the differences in cognitive effort under both positive and negative framing conditions. Among the four experimental problems, disease and gambling problems are found to exhibit the framing effect, while the kittens' therapy and the plant problem do not. In analyzing the level of eye movement for the four problems, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that cognitive effort asymmetry plays a critical role in the production of the framing effect. That is, for the two problems that display the framing effect, subjects expend more effort in the negative framing condition than they do in the positive, yet the framing effect persists, indicating that they cannot change their cognitive inertia despite this increase in cognitive effort. The <sup>fi</sup>nding has potential implications for the design of information presentation to facilitate decision making.

## 1. Introduction

The world surrounding people is comprised of a constant ebb and <sup>fl</sup>ow of events, people, and objects, a<sup>fl</sup>oat on a vast sea of information that is constantly generated, updated, and disseminated. People are creatures of bounded rationality; they cannot summon knowledge that exists in the long-term memory into their immediate <sup>fi</sup>eld of awareness [19]. To complicate matters further, people can be easily biased through the way in which information is presented [65]. Furthermore, thanks to advances in devices such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), several studies [8,64] have suggested that the framing effect is associated with the brain's emotional system, playing a key role in mediating decision biases. In the development of a Decision Support System (DSS), this limitation presents designers with a critical issue concerning what and how to structure the information to be presented to users. For example, Jones et al. [24] demonstrated that attention and weighting that decision makers give to information available to them may lead to underperformance. George et al.'s [16] research also demonstrates that, despite the warnings provided by a DSS, subjects fail to change their biased judgment induced by anchoring. Thus, there is a great need for DSS researchers to understand how information presentation may affect decision making so that DSS' user interfaces can be designed to improve the quality of decision making [19]. To this end, our review of the literature related to cognition and emotion involved in the framing effect suggests that framing may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the subjects' state of emotion [8,17,64], which in turn affects the level of cognitive effort that subsequently shapes the framing effect. Furthermore, as attention plays a central role in cognitive processing, eye movements may serve as a window into the operation of the attention system [18] and re<sup>fl</sup>ect the mental state of an individual [53]. Therefore, in this research we have applied the eye-tracking technology to investigate cognitive effort involved in attending to information presented in either positive or negative frame as suggested by Tversky and Kahneman [65]. By examining eye movements, we hope to discover the role of cognitive effort in the production of the framing effect.

The framing effect, proposed by Tversky and Kahneman [65], refers to the phenomenon that varying the presentation of the same problem can systematically affect the choice one makes. A wellknown example is the Asian disease problem:

Imagine that the United States is preparing for the outbreak of an unusual Asian disease, which is expected to kill 600 people. Two alternative programs to combat the disease have been proposed.

Assume that the exact scienti<sup>fi</sup>c estimates of the consequences of the programs are as follows. Which of the two programs would you favor?

(Option framing: positive)

If program A is adopted, 200 people will be saved.If program B is adopted, there is a 1/3 probability that 600 people will be saved, and a 2/3 probability that no people will be saved.

(Option framing: negative)

If program C is adopted, 400 people will die.If program D is adopted, there is a 1/3 probability that nobody will die, and a 2/3 probability that 600 people will die.

Tversky and Kahneman considered these four options to be logically equivalent in information content, but differing in framing. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two alternatives were described as gains (positive frame) while the latter two were losses (negative frame). In their study, Tversky and Kahneman demonstrated that subjects were prone to select the risk-averse option in response to the positive frame and the risky option in response to the negative frame. These framing phenomena have been widely investigated in a variety of research <sup>fi</sup>elds, ranging from traditional economic problems to consumer decision making [22,34,42,71], health-related decision making [2,28,44,54,70], and negotiation bargaining behavior [4,46]. A metaanalysis by Kuhberger [30] concluded that framing was a reliable phenomenon and further suggested that, in addition to framing, other variables such as risk characteristics, task characteristics, and participant characteristics might moderate the framing effects.

The general theory explaining the framing effect [27] in terms of the value function for goods is Prospect Theory, in which value is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as gains and losses from a reference point (see Fig.1). The value function is concave in the gain area and convex in the loss area, while the slope of the value function is steeper for losses than for gains, implying that decision makers are more sensitive to losses than to gains. Using the previous Asian disease problem as an example, in positive framing, people face two options: save 200 people with certainty (program A) or save 600 people with a 1/3 probability and no one with a 2/3 probability (program B). The Prospect Theory predicts the result using the following logic: The positive framing induces an adoption of the reference point so that the disease is allowed to take its toll of 600 lives. The outcomes of the two options are perceived as possible gains. Saving 200 people with total certainty is more attractive than saving 600 people with a probability of 1/3 as 600 is subjectively less than 3 times 200 at the convex value function for gains. In the negative framing, the reference point is no people dying (i.e., zero), and the outcomes are perceived as possible losses. Having 400 people dying with total certainty is less attractive than having 600 people dying with a probability of 2/3 as 600 is subjectively more than 3/2 times 400 at the concave value function for losses.

Although conceptually powerful, the Prospect Theory has suffered from its dif<sup>fi</sup>culty to reliably identify and measure the reference point. For example, Fischhoff's experiment [14] demonstrated the existence of disparity between a self-reported reference point and one derived from choice based on the Prospect Theory. In addition, Maule [43] relied on verbal protocols to collect the decision data involved in the Asian disease problem, <sup>fi</sup>nding that 5 out of 12 subjects adopted two different reference points. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of <sup>fi</sup>nding speci<sup>fi</sup>c and consistent reference points is not surprising given that values underlying the perception of risk and gains are both situated and intrinsic. Moreover, due to the limitation of research instruments, it has been dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to collect empirical data to measure values reliably, making it unfeasible for researchers to explicate the information from the subjects' mind for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c analysis.

However, recent breakthroughs in physiological apparatuses such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have helped disclose the underlying cognitive activities during decision making. Cognitive effort refers to the total use of cognitive resources required to complete the task [47]. It has been considered as an important theoretical construct in cognitive psychology [45,47] as well as in behavioral decision research [23]. In the context of the Prospect Theory, Trepel et al. [64] have surmised that passive framing, valuation, and probability weighting processes may be governed primarily by the emotional part of the brain. They theorize that, the tendency to avert risk may be related to the part of the brain that mediates anxiety and stress. Along this line of reasoning, De Martino et al. [8] have found that the framing effect is associated with the brain's emotional system, which plays a key role in mediating decision biases. Furthermore, Gonzalez et al. [17] have applied fMRI to understand the mediating role of cognitive effort in making a decision under different framing conditions. They hypothesize that, because negative emotion would stimulate more cognitive effort than positive emotion, a difference would exist in cognitive effort involved in professing decisions under different frames. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that cognitive effort expended under negative framing was more than positive framing.

Collectively, these studies suggest that the theoretical understanding of the framing effect can be advanced by reorienting the Prospect Theory from value asymmetry to cognitive effort asymmetry (see Fig. 2). In other words, rather than attempting to identify and measure the reference point, researchers can explain the framing effect through an examination of cognitive effort, as depicted in the conceptual framework shown in Fig. 2. In this framework, framing in<sup>fl</sup>uences the subjects' state of emotion, which in turn affects the level of cognitive effort that in turn shapes the framing effect.

This paper will report the results of an exploratory study that applies the eye-tracking technology to examine the relationship between framing and cognitive effort asymmetry and the relationship between cognitive effort asymmetry and framing effect (see Fig. 2). The next two sections present the development of hypotheses and the research methodology, followed by the presentation of results and discussion. Finally, the paper concludes with contributions and limitations.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

Several researchers have conceptualized that cognition and affection interplay in decision making in a manner in which the mood state in<sup>fl</sup>uences decision makers' motivation, thereby changing their allocation of cognitive effort to make decisions [11,41,56,57]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these theorists propose that people in a good mood are less motivated and thus invest less cognitive effort in processing relevant information, whereas people in a bad mood have higher motivation and thus exert more cognitive effort in information processing.

In neuroscience, several studies have investigated the relationship between framing and the activation of the brain parts that govern emotion [8,17,64]. Trepel et al. [64], for example, have suggested that anticipated losses and underweight, which contribute to the framing effect, may be associated with amygdala. Amygdala is the portion of the brain responsible for emotional fear responses [33]. Memories of emotional experiences imprinted in reactions of synapses in the lateral nuclei elicit fear behavior through connections with the central nucleus of the amygdala, which are involved in the genesis of many fear responses, including freezing (immobility), tachycardia (rapid heartbeat), increased respiration, and stress-hormone release. Gonzalez et al. [17] also propose a cognitive–affective trade-off model that explains the framing effect due to a trade-off between the cognitive effort required to calculate expected values of an alternative and the affective value of the alternative. Individuals spent more cognitive effort making decisions framed in terms of losses rather than in terms of gains. Especially intriguing is the study by De Martino et al. [8], published in Science, which provides a neurobiological account of the framing effect both within and across individuals. They show that subjects' tendency to be risk-averse in the gain frame and risk-seeking in the loss frame are correlated to increased activation in the amygdala, con<sup>fi</sup>rming that the framing effect is driven by an affect heuristic underwritten by an emotional system.

Thus, as depicted on the left side of Fig. 2, framing may induce emotion that in turn leads to the framing effect. Note that Fig. 2 also depicts a linkage between emotion and cognitive effort. This linkage is evidenced in the study by Pinon et al. [51], who demonstrate that subjects under negative framing feel a negative mood and spend more time processing the negatively framed alternative than they did on the positively framed alternative. Positive affect may promote less effort in the analytical process of message [11,41], and people in positive moods are more likely to engage in ef<sup>fi</sup>cient stereotype thinking than those in neutral moods [3]. Schwarz's review [57] further indicates that individuals in a happy mood are more likely to rely on preexisting general knowledge structures, with less attention to detail. Conversely, Luce et al. [39] discover that people in negative moods spend more time making attribute-based comparisons than alternativebased comparisons, whereas Schwarz [57] suggests that individuals in a sad mood are more likely to employ a systematic, rigorous strategy of information processing, with considerable attention to detail. Finally, the positive–negative Asymmetry (PNA) Theory prescribes that people may use an asymmetrical approach to deal with positive and negative stimuli [37,49]. A negative stimulus evokes individuals' strong volition to gain control over that stimulus in order to avoid undesired or harmful consequences. This volition generally leads to more attention and cognitive work toward this negative stimulus. Taken together, it is expected that, when a person faces a negative framing, his or her cognitive effort will increase due to a negative mood. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1. Cognitive effort asymmetry exists between positively and negatively framed problems.

H1a. Subjects will exert more cognitive effort, measured by the processing time per word, to process a negatively framed problem than a positively framed problem.

H1b. Subjects will exert more cognitive effort, measured by <sup>fi</sup>xations per word, to process words in a negatively framed problem than a positively framed problem.

Furthermore, human rationality is bounded [58]. De Martino et al. [8] con<sup>fi</sup>rm that intensive effort in the amygdala and the lack of activation in the orbital and medial prefrontal cortex combined contribute to the framing effect. Indeed, previous research has shown that the framing effect persists despite more effort expended by the subjects. For example, in the study by Fagley and Miller [12], subjects initially demonstrate risk-aversion in the positive framing condition and no preference for the risky choice in the negative framing condition. They subsequently receive training in rational decision making, yet no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes of the framing effect occur. Another study by LeBoeuf and Sha<sup>fi</sup>r [32] has investigated whether deep thought might reduce the incidence of the framing effect. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that, for subjects who conduct more thinking because they are asked to provide justi<sup>fi</sup>cations, their proclivity to be framed remained the same as those who do not. Similarly, Igou and Bless [20] have conducted a series of three studies to investigate if effortful processing reduces or increases the impact of frames on individuals' decisions. Studies 1 and 2 examine how the extent of elaboration and accountability affect the framing effect, respectively, while study 3 investigates how the ability of subjects in<sup>fl</sup>uences the framing effect. The researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd that effortful constructive processing heightens the framing effect. In other words, when subjects expend time editing the framed problem, their additional labor to enrich the problem scenario with subjective concepts increases rather than decrease their biased decision making.

Collectively, the aforementioned studies suggest that a negative correlation exists between the cognitive effort and the framing effect. Once a loss is perceived, people are more likely to expend effort to con<sup>fi</sup>rm this loss than to discon<sup>fi</sup>rm it [62]. Moreover, because the framing problem contains little information, more effortful processing leads to more framing of congruent cognitive associations and results in greater framing effects than less effortful processing. Hence, the larger the cognitive asymmetry is between the positive and the negative framing, the more likely the framing effect is. Hypothesis 2 is therefore proposed:

H2. The greater the cognitive effort asymmetry is between the positive and the negative frame, the more likely it is that the framing effect will be produced.

H2a. The greater the cognitive effort asymmetry is, measured by the differences in processing time per word between the positive and the negative frame, the more likely it is that the framing effect will be produced.

H2b. The greater the cognitive effort asymmetry is, measured by the differences in <sup>fi</sup>xations per word between the positive and the negative frame, the more likely it is that the framing effect will be produced.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Participants

A total of 56 college students were recruited as subjects from the Southern Taiwan University of Technology, Taiwan. Their ages ranged from 18 to 22 years, and each was paid a cash reward of \$100NT for their participation.

## 3.2. Stimulus material

Fagley and Miller [13] found that, in the domains of life and money, subjects consistently demonstrated different risk attitudes. Four riskychoice problems were employed in the current research, including two in the life domain (the disease problem and the kittens' therapy problem) and two in the money domain (the gambling problem and the plant problem). The disease and plant problems were adopted from previous studies [27,29]; the gambling and kittens' therapy problems were created by the researchers. The problem description was translated from English into the local language and was validated by several experts. Some minor revisions to these problems were necessary in order to <sup>fi</sup>t local contexts; for example, the word “Taiwan” was included in the disease problem. The details of all problems are presented in the Appendix.

Each problem in this study had both a certain and a risky alternative. The order in which the two kinds of alternatives appeared in a problem was counter-balanced. For each problem, two versions were generated: the positively framed version and the negatively framed version. Fig. 3 shows the screen image of the disease problem. Furthermore, in order to avoid the subjects' learning effect, we inserted four <sup>fi</sup>lter problems, which differed from the main experiment problems in that the expected values for the two risky choices were not equal (see Appendix). Filter problems and experimental problems were randomly shown to subjects.

## 3.3. Apparatus and cognitive effort

Given that the study by De Martino et al. [8] and Treptel et al. [64] <sup>fi</sup>rmly established the neurobiological account of emotion underlying the framing effect and that we did not possess fMRI, we did not measure emotion in our study. Rather, we decided to focus the study on cognitive effort by employing EyeLink II with a sampling rate of 500 Hz for tracking and recording subjects' eye movements. Various previous studies tracking eye movements have proven that eye movement is a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient and valid re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the decision process [50,55]. Prior research also has con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the eye–mind assumption— namely, that eye movements are directly related to the underlying cognitive process [5,52]. Carpenter and Just [5], for example, found that eyes often <sup>fi</sup>xate on the external referents as their corresponding internal representations are processed.

Kahneman [25] has also suggested that cognitive effort be measured in terms of intensity and time, both of which can be captured by means of eye-<sup>fi</sup>xation and eye-movement time. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, eye tracking can provide information about the location and duration of eye <sup>fi</sup>xation on the computer screen, which—in turn— provides the two measurements of cognitive effort employed in this study. The <sup>fi</sup>rst measurement is the processing time per word, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a Kanji character in this study. The other measurement is the <sup>fi</sup>xations per word (Kanji character) on the target object. For example, the display may receive 150 <sup>fi</sup>xations that take 40 s to read. As the number of words in a display may vary, the ratio of the aggregated time (of all eye <sup>fi</sup>xations) to the total number of words in that display is computed for the purpose of data analysis. Following this example, if the display included 100 words, its time-based ratio of cognitive effort would be 0.4. Similarly, the frequency-based ratio is the ratio between the total number of eye <sup>fi</sup>xations and the total number of words. Using the same example, the frequency-based ratio would be 1.5.

## 3.4. Design and procedure

Learning effect was an important issue in designing our experiment because, in the between-subject experiment, subjects who were presented with only a positive or negative frame would have learned to respond quickly to the stimulus due to habitualization. This was demonstrated in a pilot test in which the subject's eye movement became faster due to repeated exposure to the same type of framing. Similarly, in a complete within-subject design, subjects who were presented with both positive and negative frames for the same problem might have become familiar with the stimulus and learn to respond in a way not desirable for our research purpose. Thus, in order to avoid the learning effect, we decided to employ neither the between-subject experiment design nor the complete within-subject design.

As a result, an incomplete within-subject design was employed in our experiment. Subjects received both positive and negative framing, but not for the same problem (see Table 1 for an illustration of our experimental design). The 56 subjects were randomly divided into two groups, termed Group 1 and Group 2. Group 1 included 22 subjects, while Group 2 had 34 subjects. Two positively framed problems and two negatively framed ones were assigned to each group. As four problems were used in this study, Group 1 was assigned the positively framed version of P1 and P2 as well as the negatively framed version of P3 and P4. Conversely, Group 2 was assigned the negatively framed version of P1 and P2 as well as the positively framed version of P3 and P4. Four framing problems were randomly presented to each subject as stimuli; the order of the two options was also randomized. Moreover, in order to keep the results of the two framing versions of a problem comparable, the number of words in each version was made as equal as possible.

Each participant was individually led to the experimental room and asked to sit in front of the experimental PC. The distance from the screen to the subject's eye was approximately 67 cm. After a subject sat on the chair, the experimenter put the eye tracker's leather-padded headband on the participant's head and then calibrated the eye tracker. The calibration and a subsequent validation took approximately 7 min to complete. Next, in order to acquaint the subjects with the experimental system and the eye tracker, subjects were asked to practice two sample problems before the formal experiment. An experimental program designed for the present study was then launched, and the subject was told to read the instructions on the screen to make a choice, which he or she had to enter via the keyboard. Each subject needed to <sup>fi</sup>nish eight trials (four main experimental problems and four <sup>fi</sup>lter problems), which were randomly presented. ## 4. Results

The eye movement data from each participant were inspected using a custom-made program to determine whether or not the data were invalid due to incorrect calibration (i.e., the <sup>fi</sup>xations were out of screen positions). Three participants' <sup>fi</sup>xations were invalid; their data were discarded. The resulting data of 53 participants were available for analysis. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 21 subjects remained in Group 1 and 32 in Group 2.

## 4.1. Choice behavior

A SPSS-based $\chi 2$ test was conducted to examine whether or not individuals made different choices due to the difference in framing. Fig. 4 shows the overall results. In Fig. 4, the proportion of risk-seeking choices is 66% under negative framing and 50% under positive framing $( \chi 2 ( 1 ) = 5 . 5 9 7 , p { < } . 0 5 )$ , respectively. The result indicates that subjects in this study were more likely to choose the risky alternative than the certain one in response to a negatively framed problem.

## 5. Discussion

In this study, two of the four experimental problems produced the framing effect. Consistent with the prediction of the Prospect Theory, the disease problem indicated a strong risk-seeking tendency (75%) under the negative framing condition, but no such tendency under the positive framing condition (48%). Similarly, the gambling problem indicated a strong risk-seeking (78%) orientation under the negative framing condition as opposed to a risk-aversion (57%) under the positive framing condition. Conversely, the kittens' therapy problem deviated from the prediction of the Prospect Theory in that the participants demonstrated a risk-seeking orientation (56%) under the positive framing condition and a risk-aversion (52%) tendency under the negative framing condition. The same was true for the plant problem.

To further investigate the effect of cognitive effort asymmetry, we have evaluated Fig. 5a and b, which depicts the distribution of cognitive effort under both the positive and the negative framing for all four problems. An immediately noticeable observation is that the cognitive effort, in terms of <sup>fi</sup>xations, expended for negative framing is high, with the exception of the kittens' therapy problem. This result supports Hypothesis 1, which predicts that subjects expend more cognitive effort on the negative stimuli than on the positive due to the positive–negative asymmetry. Interestingly, in our research, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the participants expend more cognitive effort in a negative frame than in a positive one, irrespective of whether they are considering a certain or a risky option.

Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 5a and b, a great asymmetry exists between the cognitive effort expended under the positive framing and that under the negative framing for both the disease problem and the gambling problem, which also demonstrate a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant framing effect. This suggests that the larger the cognitive effort asymmetry, the more likely subjects are to fall under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing. As illustrated in Fig. 5b, the cognitive effort expended in the negative framing condition for the two problems is 0.85 and 0.82, respectively— much higher than that (0.64 and 0.58 respectively) in the positive framing condition. Therefore, it is possible that, for both the disease and the gambling problems in this study, the high level of value asymmetry between the positive and the negative frames stimulated a greater level of negative emotion.

Note that in both the disease problem and the gambling problem, the framing effect persists despite the fact that subjects expend signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more effort in the negative framing than in the positive framing. This may have been caused by cognitive inertia, which refers to the phenomenon in which decision makers are unable to consider alternative courses of action with high potential rewards [36]. Even if their assessment of a situation is not accurate, people still cannot change their cognitive inertia [38]. This inability is fundamental to a human's bounded rationality [58], evidenced by the fact that framing effects are most pronounced when conditions allow for more cognitive effort [20]. Indeed, the position that cognitive effort should reduce the framing effects has been challenged in many studies [12,32,35] that demonstrate that more cognitive effort may result in a higher level of selective attention to framing congruent information and therefore increases the framing effect more than less cognitive effort [20]. Thus, while the traditional wisdom has been that more effort can alleviate bias, our study indicates that this may not occur when people face decisions involving emotion. Certainly, our study does not suggest that cognition expended under framing is useless. Rather, it is likely that, when encountering framed problems, people form their judgment instantaneously and expend effort to con<sup>fi</sup>rm their judgment, as they often do in impression formation when encountering strangers [15]. This may explain the <sup>fi</sup>ndings by George et al. [16], who demonstrate that, despite warnings provided by a DSS, subjects fail to change their biased judgment induced by anchoring.

For the kittens' therapy problem, subjects' cognitive effort under the negative framing condition is relatively low (0.64) when compared to the other three problems (0.85, 0.82, 0.80) (see Fig. 5b). Consequently, cognitive effort asymmetry does not exist. We suspect that this lack of cognitive effort is caused by the low level of subjects' risk perception in the kittens' therapy problem, which is about the life of only four kittens, as opposed to the disease problem about the life of 600 people living in Taiwan. Fagley and Miller [12] have found that a high probability reduces the framing effect, and the series of studies by Wang and his colleagues [67–69] have shown that little framing effect exists when the “life or death” problem is presented in small group contexts. The case of the life or death of four kittens with a probability of 1/2 in our study may not have aroused the negative emotion of our subjects. Consequently, little cognitive effort asymmetry is induced and, accordingly, no framing effect is found.

Finally, for the plant problem, Fig. 5a and b indicates that the subjects' cognitive effort under the positive framing condition is the highest of all four problems, thereby reducing the cognitive effort asymmetry. Why would subjects make more effort in this problem? Past research has shown that subjects with more domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c cognitive ability could counteract the framing effect [59,61,71]—that is, the level of domainspeci<sup>fi</sup>c competence may play a critical role in determining the level of cognitive effort. In this study, we have recruited students majoring in business as our subjects, who probably are both interested in and capable of dealing with the plant problem. They may have thus expended a larger amount of cognitive effort under the positive framing condition for the plant problem than they have for the other problems. As a result, despite the fact that the high cognitive effort under negative framing is considerable, the cognitive effort asymmetry between these two conditions is very low, leading to a little framing effect.

## 6. Conclusion

In this research we have reviewed the literature related to the framing effect and the neurobiological studies of emotion. This review allows us to conceptualize that framing may induce emotion, which in turn impinges on the level of cognitive effort that subsequently shapes the framing effect (Fig. 2). In our empirical work, the results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the larger cognitive effort asymmetry is, the more likely framing effect is to exist. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is important in that it provides a theoretical approach that differs from the Prospect Theory for explaining the framing effect. Unlike the Prospect Theory, which relies on reference points and perceived values that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure, our work demonstrates that eye movement data can be employed to measure cognitive effort, which in turn is correlated to the framing effect. This may contribute to the studies of cognitive processing (i.e., the operation of the attention system [18] and the mental state of an individual [53] that underlies human decision making when faced with framed information presentation).

Indeed, recent progress in physiological apparatus such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and eye tracking have brought new insights into both the emotion and cognition underlying the decision making process. This is especially intriguing in the context of electronic commerce, in which consumers—who are constantly subjected to the stimulation of emotion—rely on the Internet to make purchase decisions, as demonstrated in the study by Delgado et al. [9] who have investigated people's overbidding in auctions through fMRI. By combining neuroeconomic and behavioral economic techniques, they <sup>fi</sup>nd that loss contemplation in a social context may mediate overbidding in auctions. In other words, contradicting the assumption that people bid to win, the researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd that people may overbid because of their fear to lose in the auction.

In this study, we demonstrate the application of this technology in measuring the cognitive effort involved in decisions under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of framing. In the past, it has been dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for researchers to ask subjects to give accurate descriptions of their level of information processing [63], and researchers have relied mostly on self-reports, which can be rather unreliable. For example, Kuvass and Selart [31] have discovered that no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference exists in cognitive effort for the positive and the negative framing. They hypothesize that social desirability bias would lead participants to report having thought more carefully about their decisions than they actually did. Other studies, such as those by Fagley and Miller, Igou and Bless, LeBoeuf and Sha<sup>fi</sup>r [12,20,32], have also relied on experimental design to manipulate the level of effort by different groups of subjects without speci<sup>fi</sup>c measurements of effort. Now, new research apparatuses provide a reliable way of tracking the process underlying decisions and offer new opportunities to advance the theories of human decisions and DSS design.

In addition, our research may contribute to the investigation of the role of emotion in decision making. Along these lines, how cognitive effort is related to the different emotions and how this effort may impact the decision performance remain important to DSS researchers. In the past, researchers have focused on the impact of emotion on effort [3,11,17,39,41,51,57], effort on decision [12,20,32], and emotion on decision [8,64]. The conceptual framework depicted in Fig. 2 may guide researchers in studying emotion, cognitive effort, and decision judgment in an integral manner. In judgment and decision making research, emotion has received increasing attention in recent years [26,57]. Emotion is critical to decision making and is an integral part of decision making that occurs in naturalistic conditions. Yet, emotion is absent in most DSS theories. The framework depicted in Fig. 2 and the work in the current research open the possibility to further articulate these theories. For example, the effort–accuracy trade-off model proposed by Johnson and Payne [48] prescribes that rational and adaptive decision makers have a reservoir of strategies from which they can realize their goal; they will choose more sophisticated and, accordingly, more rigorous strategies when their goal is to seek accurate results. However, a study by Luce, Payne, and Bettman [40] has shown that subjects whose negative emotion is elicited may increasingly rely on a choice strategy that maximizes quality at the expense of price. That is, subjects may exhibit cognitive inertia, i.e., repeatedly sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce decision accuracy, when their negative emotion is aroused. Yet, in a subsequent study, Drolet and Luce [10] show that this cognitive inertia induced by negative emotion can be lessened by an increase of cognitive load in working memory. These studies imply that emotion plays a critical role in both the effort-accuracy trade-off and the quality–price trade-off and deserves future research. Similarly, the cognitive <sup>fi</sup>t theory [66] postulates that a <sup>fi</sup>t between the task type (e.g., spatial or symbolic) and the presentation (e.g., graphical or tabular) will enhance problem solvers' performance. Nevertheless, current technology such as multimedia and animation can be easily integrated into the presentation and, hence, emotion would become a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor to be considered. Our work provides a way to study how emotion can be incorporated into these theories.

User interface design is an important aspect of DSS development [60] and some empirical studies have indicated that the nature of DSS user interface can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the quality of decision making [1]. For DSS interface designers, while the traditional focus is increasing the degree of user friendliness to lower the level of operational effort by the DSS users, our study indicates that the cognitive process in decision making is highly sensitive to the decision frame and the state of emotion. The designer's decision to frame the decision positively or negatively may impact both effort and choice by the DSS users. Cognitive effort is highly dynamic, subject to the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of factors such as risk characteristics (e.g., the possible death of 600 people vs. the death of four kittens), as well as task and subjects' characteristics (e.g., business students' elaboration of the plant problem). This is important because now there is a reliable way of tracking the cognition that occurs during the decision making process in framing conditions, which offers a new perspective for the design of DSS—namely, we may now approach the DSS design by evaluating the cognitive process such as attention involved in a decision. Therefore, DSS designers can not only focus on easy-to-use interface but also how interface design may distort the level of effort and the quality of judgment. DSS user friendliness should not be limited to the notion of minimizing time to operate (i.e., keystroke time) but expanded to include cognitive effort and decision effectiveness. In other words, it is now possible to research how framing, in conjunction with risk, task, and participant characteristics, may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the cognitive process underlying a decision-maker's judgment. George et al. [16], for example, concluded that one lesson learned from the persistence of bias induced by anchoring is “the rationalization of a decision making process by formalizing it within a computer-based information system does not seem to make the process itself more rational. The bias that operates without the information system continues to operate within it.”

## 7. Research limitation

An important limitation of our study is that we didn't collect subjects' self-report of emotion that was induced by framing in this study due to the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in incorporating the questionnaire into the experimental process of tracking the eye movements. In the future, it is possible to integrate devices such as EEG with the eye-tracking tools for this purpose. In addition, one important area of research is related to the role of positive affect in the state of <sup>fl</sup>ow, in which people are so intensely absorbed in activities that nothing else seems to matter [6,7]. That is, because the experience itself is so enjoyable that people will do it even at a great cost. Recently, Isen and Reeve [21] have shown that positive affect may foster intrinsic motivation and promote responsible work behavior in a situation where the work needs to be done. Thus, our research result should not be overly generalized to situations that involve change of intrinsic motivation through positive affect in the state of <sup>fl</sup>ow.
