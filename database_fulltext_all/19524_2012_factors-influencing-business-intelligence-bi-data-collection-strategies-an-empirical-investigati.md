## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines external pressures that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relationship between an organization's business intelligence (BI) data collection strategy and the purpose for which BI is implemented. A model is proposed and tested that is grounded in institutional theory, research about competitive pressure, and research about the purpose of BI. Two data collection strategies (comprehensive and problem driven) and three BI purposes (insight, consistency, and transformation) are examined. Findings provide a theoretical lens to better understand the motivators and the success factors related to collecting the huge amounts of data required for BI This study also provides managers with a mental model on which to base decisions about the data required to accomplish their goals for BI.

## 1. Introduction

In the last decade, Business intelligence (BI) has evolved as one of the critical applications in organizations to provide useful insight, support decision-making, and drive organizational performance [10,54]. BI has permeated various industries including retail, insurance, banking, <sup>fi</sup>nance and securities, telecommunications, and manufacturing [58]. Companies such as Continental Airlines and First American Corporation (FAC) have successfully implemented BI to improve their customer loyalty and increase their return on investments (ROI) [2,19]. There are other organizations, however, that have not been as successful in utilizing BI to increase their pro<sup>fi</sup>t and achieve their expected performance [31]. Such mixed results have motivated researchers to examine a variety of factors that contribute to the successful implementation of BI [21,28,40,41].

The effectiveness of BI lies in its ability to present business information in a timely manner [18]. Thus, the success of any BI project depends on the data available. Furthermore, research indicates that data consistency and data quality are a major cause of the success or failure of BI initiatives [3,8,24]. Data collection does not refer just to the collection of data for BI applications, but it is also concerned with providing clean, consistent, high quality, and integrated data for BI applications [52,56,82]. Therefore, organizational data collection strategy may form a crucial foundation for BI success and differentiate between organizations that successfully implement BI to realize high bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and organizations that are unable to gain high bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through BI.

The data for BI is generally stored in a central repository known as the data warehouse [22,74]. Data warehousing strategies involve the collection and integration of data for BI purposes [25]. Although research has examined some factors that affect the selection of different data warehousing strategies [12,41], these studies are largely descriptive. There are few theoretically grounded studies that focus speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally on the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence BI data collection strategies. The purpose of this research is to examine the forces that in<sup>fl</sup>uence an organization's BI data collection strategy. The model developed and tested in this study provides a theoretical lens to better understand the success factors and the motivators related to collecting the huge amounts of data required for BI. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study also provide managers with insight into how to develop strategies and plans for collecting data that match the purpose of BI. This will further help BI managers and users by providing them with a mental model on which they can base their decisions about the data required to accomplish their goals for BI.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses development

In order to understand the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence adoption of speci<sup>fi</sup>c data collection strategies during BI implementation we draw on data warehousing research, research on BI implementation, as well as organizational theory and strategic management literature. Some important concepts used in the proposed theoretical model are discussed next.

## 2.1. Institutional isomorphism

Institutional isomorphism is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a constraining process that forces one unit in a population to resemble other units that face the same set of environmental conditions” [23, p. 149]. Institutional isomorphism arises from external in<sup>fl</sup>uences and the need for legitimacy [23]. It is rooted in the cultural expectations of society and other organizations in the industry within which a <sup>fi</sup>rm operates and plays an important role in explaining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of external political, social, and technical environments on organizational behavior [44]. It describes the process through which structures such as norms, rules, and routines become authoritative guidelines for social behavior [42,73]. Institutional isomorphism is instrumental in explaining diffusion of technological innovations because organizations often adopt technology to gain legitimacy within the industry [23]. In this context, it is mainly used to explain the role of external pressures in the adoption and assimilation of different information systems in an organization, particularly systems for which the technology is so widespread as to be viewed as ubiquitous [32]. Thus, this lens is appropriate to examine BI strategies because BI technology is wide spread across organizations throughout most industries [58].

Data warehousing is thought to be one of the more recent technologies that is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by institutional forces [76]. As managers grapple with the data warehousing technology and how best to make sense of it for their particular organizations, they often look to how others have implemented it and to the best practices of their industry or those suggested by vendors [76]. As data warehousing is considered an underlying foundation of most BI implementations, we consider institutional isomorphism to be an important factor driving BI implementations and in<sup>fl</sup>uencing BI data collection strategies.

## 2.2. Competitive pressure

Competitive pressure is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the degree of pressure that a company feels from competitors within the industry” [90, p. 70]. The competitive pressure under which an organization operates is a driving force behind innovative strategies in an organization [11,35]. Competitive dynamics research suggests that there are different ways in which <sup>fi</sup>rms address competitive pressure [63,75,14]. Faced with competitive pressure, <sup>fi</sup>rms undertake competitive action that could be either tactical action or strategic [14]. Strategic actions are characterized by long time horizons, large expenditures, and great deviations from the status quo, such as major facility expansions or introduction of new products/services [14,15,55]. Firms may implement BI which is a competitive action to address competitive pressure within their environment and to stay ahead of the competition within their industry sector [14]. Competitive pressure increases the uncertainty within the external environment and thereby increases the necessity to implement new information systems to deal with this uncertainty [27,78]. Organizations adopt and implement technologies such as ERP, EDI, data warehousing, and other computer based inter-organizational technologies in response to competitive pressure [11,35,37,64,66]. Research indicates that competitive pressure is very in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the adoption of data warehouses — an underlying technology for BI [37]. Faced with competitive pressure <sup>fi</sup>rms may reexamine their current strategy for managing data in their data warehouses and implement different strategies to leverage their data and stay ahead of competition. Therefore, competitive pressure can provide insight into organizational data collection strategies.

## 2.3. BI purpose and its relationship with institutional isomorphism and competitive pressure

The overarching purpose of BI is to support decision-making [54]. This is, however, more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained than overall decision support [18]. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the purpose of BI as “the primary reason that an organization initiates a BI project.” Three general purposes for which an organization may undertake a BI initiative are to gain insight, to provide a single version of the truth, or to enable organizational transformation [47,80,81].

One reason organizations implement BI is to gain better insight into its business processes, strategies, and operations [47]. Companies rely on BI to make sense of the transactional data collected by ERP and other data intensive applications [35] in order to provide decision makers with a better understanding of underlying trends and dependencies that affect the business [47]. Such understanding is posed to enable more informed decision-making and empower employee decision capabilities [17].

The second reason organizations implement BI is to achieve a single consistent view of business information [81]. Enterprise data are constantly changing especially when organizations go through mergers and acquisitions [25,59]. Therefore, obtaining a single consistent version of business information is important for aiding in strategic and tactical decision making and for managing business process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently [26]. Obtaining a single version of the truth with regard to enterprise information helps in achieving high quality data and better data analysis [3,26]. Having a single view of enterprise wide information also facilitates the development of new applications and saves time for users [81]. It also facilitates communication among different stakeholders within the organization because they have access to the same information [54]. Finally, the third key purpose of BI is to enable organizational transformation. Here, the purpose of BI is to enable change to the existing business model of an organization and to support the implementation of a new business model to take advantage of external market [80,84].

Organizational reasons for adopting technology may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a variety of factors including institutional isomorphism and competitive pressure. Adoption decisions are likely to be framed and justi-<sup>fi</sup>ed differently, depending on the main driving forces in<sup>fl</sup>uencing them. In the case of BI implementation, institutional isomorphic pressures are likely to take a variety of forms. Failure to implement a technology such as BI that has a high perceived value can be interpreted as a sign of incompetence [69]. Furthermore, failing to implement BI to support decision-making may cause business partners, competitors, customers, investors, and other stake holders to question the decision-making ability of the <sup>fi</sup>rm and thereby the legitimacy of the <sup>fi</sup>rm because it does not adhere to the norms of its industry [1]. This may push organizations to adopt BI without a full understanding of how BI can bene<sup>fi</sup>t their particular organizations. In such cases, organizations are likely to view BI implementation as adoption of industry best practices and adopt generic justi<sup>fi</sup>cations of BI offered by organizations promoting BI, such as BI system vendors, consultants, or industry associations [44]. Some of the most commonly stated and promoted bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of BI implementation include achieving consistency among data and gaining insight into business operations. Therefore, these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are likely to be most cited as key justi<sup>fi</sup>cations and goals for BI projects that are driven by isomorphic pressures.

In addition, regulatory and industry associations agencies represent a source of coercive isomorphic pressures because they require the <sup>fi</sup>rms in certain industries to get better control of their data and to provide better information to the agencies. For example, compliance mandates arising from the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and Sarbanes–Oxley Act (SOX) require companies to ensure accurate reporting [88]. Organizations initiate BI projects to effectively utilize large volumes of data and to comply with regulatory mandates such as SOX [71]. The process of implementing BI can result in improved data quality, better data consistency and a single version of the truth [26,70], all of which are helpful in complying with regulatory mandates and industry based pressures [48]. Thus institutional isomorphism is also likely to result in implementing BI to gain greater consistency of information and data across the organization. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H1a. There exists a positive relationship between institutional isomorphism and implementing BI for the purpose of achieving insight.

H1b. There exists a positive relationship between institutional isomorphism and implementing BI for the purpose of achieving consistency.

Competitive pressure has also been recognized as an important factor in the adoption, innovation, and implementation of information systems by different organizations to achieve a competitive edge over other <sup>fi</sup>rms operating within the same industry [89]. It drives businesses to be highly innovative and has been shown to increase the likelihood of innovation adoption [46,78]. Organizations operating under high competitive pressure need to constantly assess and adjust strategies to address competition [63]. Faced with competitive pressure, organizations focus on implementing strategies that have not been implemented by other <sup>fi</sup>rms within their industry sector [53]. Organizations achieve this by either cutting costs, differentiating themselves from other <sup>fi</sup>rms through the product they offer or the service they offer, or by creating new value propositions for their various stake holders [19,62]. Thus, organizations address competitive pressure by changing the ways they compete in the market. This may entail organizational transformation. Harrah's entertainment and FAC are examples of two such organizations that transformed their organizations through BI to address competitive pressure [19,80,84]. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H2. There exists a positive relationship between the competitive pressure faced by an organization and implementing BI for the purpose of achieving organizational transformation.

## 2.4. BI data collection strategies and their relationship with BI purpose

The literature on BI and data warehousing suggests two strategies that can be employed for collecting and integrating data for BI purposes [12,65]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst strategy involves collecting, integrating, and storing all the data present in the organization into a single repository called the enterprise wide data warehouse [4,5]. Because this strategy consists of collecting, integrating, and storing the majority of the data present in the organization into the data warehouse, we term this the comprehensive data collection strategy. Implementing a comprehensive data collection strategy requires extensive infrastructure planning and requirements de<sup>fi</sup>nition [5,83], which is very time consuming and expensive [74,85].

The second strategy involves collecting and integrating data to provide solutions to a particular business need or problem. This strategy centers around a single application or a small set of applications that can provide potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [83,85]. This type of strategy is ideal for achieving quick win solution and can also serve as proofof-concept for bigger BI initiatives [83]. The BI application is seen as a powerful problem-solving tool that can enable access to relevant integrated data and thus enhance the decision making process of individual business units [54]. Therefore, we term this strategy as the problem driven strategy for BI data collection. The problem driven strategy takes an application-centric approach where the application required to solve the speci<sup>fi</sup>c problems drives the collection and integration of relevant data sources [74].

As the primary goal behind BI implementation is translated into speci<sup>fi</sup>c system requirements and expectations, organizations are likely to adopt different data collection strategies. If the goal is to gain insightfulness through BI, the BI initiative is likely to be driven by decision-makers looking to address speci<sup>fi</sup>c business challenges. Such decision makers are likely to focus on identifying, mining, organizing, and presenting the data relevant to their business needs [17], thus leading to the adoption of the problem-driven data collection strategy. Anecdotal evidence supports our contention that a problem driven data collection strategy is selected if insightfulness is the primary goal of BI. For example, Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Depot used a problem driven strategy for collecting and integrating all the sales data when implementing a BI to make better sense of all the sales data <sup>fl</sup>owing from the different stores and to assist the sales division in better serving their customers [20]. Thus we hypothesize the following:

H3. There exists a positive relationship between implementing BI for the purpose of achieving insight and initiating a problem driven BI data collection strategy.

A different data collection strategy may be more appropriate when organizations implement BI to provide a single version of the truth to the various stake-holders. In this scenario, organizations are attempting to better manage data governance, improve data quality, and remove data inconsistencies [26]. They seek to implement a single comprehensive integrated view of enterprise wide data. Using a comprehensive data collection strategy may best help achieve consistency and a single version of the truth [34]. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H4. There exists a positive relationship between implementing BI for the purpose of achieving consistency and initiating a comprehensive BI data collection strategy.

When organizations implement BI to assist in organizational transformation they seek to develop a new business model in order to take advantage of external markets or to achieve a competitive advantage over other organizations within the same industry [19]. In this situation it is important to understand and manage the <sup>fl</sup>ow of entire data within the enterprise [9]. Therefore, it is important for organizations to have a single consolidated view of enterprise wide data to enable organizational transformation. Therefore, they employ a comprehensive data collection strategy for collecting and integrating BI data. We hypothesize the following:

H5. There exists a positive relationship between implementing BI for the purpose of achieving organizational transformation and initiating a comprehensive BI data collection strategy.

## 3. Research methodology and data collection

We used a survey method to test our model. The constructs were operationalized and survey items were developed by identifying appropriate measurements from a review of the extant literature. The items for measuring institutional isomorphism were adapted from Liang et al. [44] and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to suit the context of BI. Items for measuring competitive pressure were developed from [78], [37], and [89]. We found no previously validated scales for measuring BI purposes and BI data collection strategies. Therefore, the items for measuring these constructs were developed based on reading and interpretation of prior research on these topics.

The target population for this study includes information technology (IT) professionals and top level managers involved in developing and managing BI. Data were collected from 63 BI developers across a range of industries and organizations within the United States. Several criteria were used to select participants that could be expected to have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge about their organizations' BI data collection strategies. Thus, one criterion was that respondents have several years of experience both with their organizations and with BI. These respondents averaged just over 11 years of experience in their respective organizations and over 10 years of experience working with BI (Table 1a). Another criterion was that they be developers or analysts of BI systems or business managers of BI, rather than end users of BI. As seen in Table 1b, over 50% worked with BI architecture and infrastructure development or managed BI systems, while most others worked as analysts, systems maintenance, or other technical positions. In addition, approximately 70% of the respondents identi<sup>fi</sup>ed themselves as having a technical orientation (Table 1b). Another criterion was that respondents should work in organizations large enough to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant data collection needs and that would be most likely to have structured data collection strategies and that would be able to implement BI to respond to institutional or competitive pressures. Respondents are from a variety of industries and from <sup>fi</sup>rms that are fairly large (Table 1c).

## 4.1. Measurement model

Convergent validity is evaluated by examining the average variance extracted (AVE) and the composite reliability of the constructs [36]. The AVE is the amount of variance explained by the indicators of a particular construct relative to the amount of variance captured due to the measurement error [16]. An adequate model should have an AVE greater than 0.5 [43]. The AVE for the constructs organizational transformation purpose of BI and problem driven data collection strategy was 0.352 and 0.456 respectively. Therefore, we removed the indicator variables that had the least path weights from each of these constructs. The <sup>fi</sup>nal results of the AVE scores are given in Table 3. The AVE scores and the composite reliability scores provide support that the items measure the constructs which they are hy pothesized to measure insofar as they exhibit adequate convergent validity.

Discriminant validity is evaluated by examining the relationship between the square root of AVEs and the correlation among the latent variables [30]. For the constructs to exhibit adequate discriminant validity, the square root of the AVEs should be greater than the correlation among the latent variables. The relationship between the square root of AVEs and the correlation among the constructs for this study is shown in Table 4. The square root of all the AVEs (diagonal elements) is greater than the correlations among the constructs (non-diagonal elements). This indicates satisfactory discriminant validity for all of the constructs.

## 5. Discussion

Our results show partial support for the proposed theoretical model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that institutional pressures lead organizations to implement BI solutions with the primary purpose of achieving consistency, which in turn leads to these organizations adopting a comprehensive data collection strategy. In addition, implementing BI for organizational transformation is linked to using a comprehensive data collection strategy. We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the hypotheses that competitive pressure in<sup>fl</sup>uences the purpose for which BI is implemented. Neither do <sup>fi</sup>ndings support a relationship between institutional isomorphism and implementing BI for insightfulness nor between insightfulness and a problem driven data collection strategy.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer interesting insights into the role of institutional and competitive pressures in shaping organizational goals with regard to BI implementation. The link between institutional pressures and implementing BI to achieve consistency of data is not surprising given the widespread acknowledgement that BI is useful for gaining a single version of the truth and that data underlying a

BI must be clean and consistent for a BI to be useful. Many organizations to date have implemented BI with consistency as, at least, a preliminary goal. Thus, a link exists between institutional isomorphism and the goal of achieving consistency which arguably provides the highest bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to institutional stakeholders of the organization, such as regulatory agencies, industry associations, and powerful vendors, suppliers, and customers. Furthermore, these stakeholders exert pressure on organizations for accuracy and consistency in reporting or other externally visible information that makes it easier for institutional stakeholders to exert control over organizations. In response to such pressures, organizations implement BI with the main goal of providing consistency of data and information and thereby strengthen the legitimacy of their information.

A positive relationship between institutional pressures and implementing BI for gaining business insight was hypothesized based on the fact that providing insight is publicized as a key bene<sup>fi</sup>t of BI by BI promoting organizations. The lack of support for this relationship suggests that organizations do not adopt the insightfulness justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for BI if BI implementation is driven by institutional isomorphism. A possible explanation is that the insightfulness goal for BI implementation is internally motivated. Although external stakeholders i.e., regulatory agencies or external auditors, could indirectly prompt the desire for such insight, it may be more likely that the goal directly arises from an organization's internal desire to enhance ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency or effectiveness.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also indicate that competitive pressure does not drive organizations to implement BI for organizational transformation. This is surprising given the well known cases of companies such as Harrah's and First American Corporation that have successfully used BI as they responded to competitive pressure to transformation themselves [19,84]. One possible explanation for this is that competitive pressure is not strong enough to prompt most organizations to initiate a BI project for organizational transformation. Rather, competitive pressure may motivate <sup>fi</sup>rms to gain ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in more focused areas. For example, using BI to better understand customers or to enhance customer service is a common starting point for BI initiatives [86]. Even <sup>fi</sup>rms whose BI is more mature may still respond to a perceived competitive pressure with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c instantiation of BI. Another possible explanation is that when <sup>fi</sup>rms are faced with increased competitive pressure one response is to reduce costs rather than seek organizational transformation, which may be perceived as more costly, unless they are far behind or far ahead of their competitors [79]. Initiating a transformative BI project requires high investments [31]. Therefore, faced with high competitive pressure, initiating a BI project for organizational transformation may not be perceived as the best option for the organization. Organizations may perceive that it is more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to leverage their existing technology rather than embark on developing a new BI system in highly competitive situations.

Another explanation may be that despite the widespread usage of BI, it is still not high on the maturity curve in most organizations. BI users are more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with basic BI capabilities that support answer ‘what has happened’ than with those to help answer questions of why or what will happen [38]. Therefore, it may be that organizations think of BI as a means to answer basic questions, and they largely do not yet think of BI as a way to transform themselves. Thus, competitive pressure does not in<sup>fl</sup>uence them to turn to BI for organizational transformation because that instantiation of BI is not part of their repertoire for addressing competitive pressures.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also provide insight into the relationship between the purpose for which BI is implemented and the strategy for collecting the requisite data for the BI. Findings provide support that there is a relationship between using a comprehensive data collection strategy and two of the purposes; implementing for consistency and for organizational transformation. While both BI purposes, achieving consistency and organizational transformation, are associated with the comprehensive data collection strategy, we argue that the reasons a company employs this data collection strategy for the purpose of transformation differ from those in the purpose of consistency. Organizational transformation includes developing new business models to take advantage of the external market [2,19,80]. In order to do so, it is critical for the organizations to have a single consolidated view of their enterprise wide data. Therefore, organizations, when implementing BI for organizational transformation collect, integrate, and store the entire data present within the organization.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not provide support that a problem driven data collection strategy is related to implementing BI for insight. One possible explanation may be that majority of the BI guidelines suggest laying out the infrastructure for collecting and integrating all the data within the organization before implementing a BI solution [4,5,12,41]. Therefore, organizations may be biased towards the comprehensive data collection strategy without considering the purpose for which they are implementing the BI.

The nature of using BI for insightfulness could help to explain why it is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly linked to a problem driven data collection strategy. Gaining insight might be focused on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c problem area or it might be focused on a better understanding of the business overall. In the former, managers isolate and differentiate the context within which they need better understanding of their business. This context could be the current business environment, competitors, customers, economic issues, and markets [47]. Once they identify the need for insight into business within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context, the managers can then identify, collect, and integrate the relevant data source to address the problem area. In this case, it is expected that data collection is driven by the problem for which it is needed. However, if BI is used to gain insight into the broader context of the organization's current business rather than any speci<sup>fi</sup>c context, then data collection would not be driven by a given problem.

## 5.1. Implications for research and practice

The goal of this study was to examine the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence how BI is implemented in organizations with respect to data collection strategies. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we set out to examine the link between external pressures, high level goals behind BI implementation and speci<sup>fi</sup>c approaches to implementation. Although not all of our hypotheses were supported, both signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant results have implications for both academicians and practitioners. From the research perspective, our study indicates that there exists a relationship between external factors (institutional pressure) and speci<sup>fi</sup>c approaches adopted in implementation of IS-supported initiatives, such as BI. Grounded in a strong theoretical base of the institutional theory, this suggests that technology structuring in organizations [60] is not only a function of the structures embedded in the organizations, but also of structures that lie outside of the organizational boundaries.

This study also extends existing research on how organizations approach data-related decisions in system implementations. Prior studies within this area have examined some factors that affect the selection of different data warehousing architectures [4,5]. However, these studies examine the data warehousing architectures from the perspective of factors that arise from within an organization, and they are largely silent about external environmental factors. This study provides an empirical and theoretically grounded lens through which to better understand the motivators and success factors associated with collecting the vast quantities of data required for BI by investigating into the data warehousing strategies, research about competitive pressure, and institutional theory.

The existence of a relationship between external factors, organizational goals and approaches to technology implementation also has important implications for practice as well. Organizations embarking on system implementation project should be aware of the institutional pressures and how those in<sup>fl</sup>uence system goals and implementation approaches. If such institutional pressures are recognized, organizations will be in a better position to make a conscientious choice of whether to succumb to such pressures or to resist them. The lack of support for the link between the competitive pressures and implementation goals is troubling, because it may indicate the lack of strategic alignment between businesses and IS strategies.

Organizations should also consider if the adopted data collection strategies match their speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational goals for implementing BI. A comprehensive data collection strategy is certainly appropriate for some BI implementations, and our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide opportunity for organizations to think through how the data collection strategy matches the reason for implementing the BI in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place.

When organizations implement BI to provide clean and consistent information to their various stakeholders then they collect, integrate, and store the entire data present in the organization. It is critical that the data be clean, reliable, and valid in order to achieve a consistent version of the truth. Failing to collect data from across the organization or unit in which consistency is desired could easily leave data from other sources in place that contradict or confound the data on which the BI relies. Although this may seems tautological, re<sup>fl</sup>ection reveals that this goes deeper than it may appear on the surface. When an organization collects data for BI, it should consider the underlying foundation the data builds and why that is important to how they plan to use the BI. We use the term ‘data collection strategy’ to suggest that gathering the data foundation for the BI is, or at least should be, an intentional activity that goes beyond due diligence or best practice. Therefore, it is not enough to simply engage in comprehensive data collection because that is the way it seems to be done by others or suggested by vendors. We argue that organizations think though why they need the data that they are gathering and how it <sup>fi</sup>ts the purpose for which they will use it. Although additional studies with a larger sample size are needed to reaf<sup>fi</sup>rm the non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results, we suggest that organizations need to pay more attention to competitive pressure when setting out implementation goals for such strategically important implementation projects as BI.

## 5.2. Limitations and directions for future research

Because the goal of this study was to examine the link between external factors, high level strategic goals (BI goals) and speci<sup>fi</sup>c implementation approaches (data collection strategies), we faced a number of challenges related to identifying organizational members that could answer questions at all three levels. This led to several limitations, which need to be considered when interpreting the results of this study. First, although a sample size of 63 was statistically suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient for the analyses we conducted, a larger sample size may have allowed for more robust and/or comprehensive analyses. The other limitation is related to a potential selection bias among the respondents: seventy percent of the respondents had technical orientation with respect to BI, whereas only thirty percent of the respondents had business orientation with respect to Bl. This could have influenced the ability of the respondents to accurately assess competitive pressures thus leading to the lack of support for related hypotheses. Technical respondents may have skewed perceptions with regard to the key goal of the BI implementation, and focused more on the consistency goal than on the more business-centric goals of insightfulness and organizational transformation.

Another potential methodological limitation is the existence of common method variance [50,61]. Common method variance refers to the systematic error that might be included in the study due to potential respondent biases. This is common in survey studies where the same respondent responds to the survey items in a single questionnaire at the same point in time [45]. This could cause spurious correlation among the shared variables due to the common method used to collect the data [13]. In order to minimize this effect we separated the items measuring independent variables, mediating variables, and dependent variables into different sections.

The study also has limitations in terms of the scope of the factors considered. This study examines the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of external environmental factors on BI data collection strategies when mediated by the purpose for which BI is implemented. Institutional isomorphism and competitive pressure forms the independent variable for this study. However, there are also other internal factors such as technical issues, organizational infrastructure, information interdependence between organizational units, and resource constraints [4,12,72,74] that may affect the BI data collection strategies. Expanding this study to include these factors is a future research direction.

Further, in this study we have looked into three major purposes of BI. There, however may be more purpose of BI than that is discussed in this study. Thus, future research could explore the impact of institutional and competitive pressures on other BI purposes. Selecting the right BI data collection strategy for the right BI purpose may further improve the success of BI implementation. Therefore, another direction for future research includes examining the relationship between BI purposes, BI data collection strategies, and BI success. Further research direction also includes re<sup>fi</sup>ning the survey instruments used in the current study, collecting diverse data, and running a more comprehensive analysis.

## 6. Conclusions

As organizations embark on new IT-enabled organizational initiatives, such as BI implementation, their strategic and tactical choices are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by external factors such as competitive and institutional pressures. Drawing on institutional theory and strategic management literature, we developed and tested a theoretical model that links various external factors to BI implementation choices including the goal of BI implementation and the data collection strategy used. Our results offer support for a positive relationship between institutional pressures to adopt BI and implementing BI for consistency. Our results also offer support for a positive relationship between such BI implementation goals as consistency and organizational transformation, and selecting a comprehensive data collection strategy. Surprisingly we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd support between competitive pressure and BI implementation goals, which may point to a lack of strategic alignment between business and IT. Additional studies are necessary to investigate such nonsupported relationships. We believe that our model has important implications for both researchers and practitioners because it highlights the importance of taking into account external factors when making and evaluating BI implementation decisions. BI technology has pervaded every large industry sector including banking, <sup>fi</sup>nance, insurance and security, government, manufacturing, retail, and services. Therefore, understanding the relationship between the purpose for implementing BI and BI data collection strategies may prove vital for the success of any BI initiative. This study may serve as a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of external environmental factors on BI purposes and BI data collection strategies.
