# Performance evaluation of production of IT capital goods across OECD countries: A stochastic frontier approach to Malmquist index

Yen-Chun Chou <sup>a</sup>, Benjamin B.M. Shao <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Winston T. Lin <sup>b</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

As integrated supply chains, ubiquitous computing, and mobile applications have become the backbone for conducting business nowadays, the economic signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of information technology (IT) is self-evident. In this paper, we study IT value from an unconventional perspective: the production of IT capital goods. Using the true <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects model of translog stochastic production frontier, we evaluate the performance of IT industries for 19 Organization of Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) countries over the period of 2000 to 2009. We examine the productivity growth of these IT industries based on the Malmquist index and further analyze these productivity patterns through technological change and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change. Overall, these IT industries are found to enjoy greater productivity growth than other industries when compared with previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Our results show that technological progress is the main driver of productivity growth for the IT industry, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change has a negligible effect, and each country's IT industry exhibits a distinctive performance pro<sup>fi</sup>le. Policy implications are drawn from our results and related issues are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for future research. We also highlight the advance of research methodology used in the study that can account for measurement errors, random <sup>fl</sup>uctuations, and unobserved heterogeneity commonly encountered in empirical information systems research.

## 1. Introduction

The performance of information technology (IT) industry is critical to the economies in which it resides because of the interdependent relationship of supply and demand that exists between IT-producing and IT-using industries [35,37]. The IT productivity literature shows that investments in IT capital increase marginal outputs of IT-using industries and improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of other factors of production like capital and labor [9,55]. In this regard, the performance of IT industry closely relates to the productivity of IT-using industries through IT capital accumulation [36]. According to the Organization of Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) IT Outlook [61], global spending on IT capital goods in 2009 was estimated at US\$ 447 billion, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the growing demand for IT capital goods and the increased signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of IT-producing industries. In 2008, the average contribution of the information and communication technology (ICT) sector to the overall business sector's value added for 28 OECD countries was found about 8%. The importance of IT-producing industry also has extended beyond country boundaries in the form of job transfers, value added, and trade imbalance [44]. Among OECD countries, IT capital goods is the largest segment of OECD ICT goods trade, accounting for 25% of the total, and the dollar amount reached US\$ 525 billion in 2008 [61].

Despite the unequivocal importance of IT industry to the global economy as attested by these of<sup>fi</sup>cial statistics, few studies so far have looked at the production of IT capital goods in the context of IT value creation. In the literature of information systems (IS), most previous studies on IT business value focused on the returns from IT investments. The performance metrics used to assess the contributions of IT include: productivity [9,20], ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [48,75], pro<sup>fi</sup>tability [33,81], quality [17,56], cost [54], and variety [82], among others. The level of analysis also runs the gamut from individual workers [2,28] to business organizations [14,86] and from industries [29,64] to developed vs. developing countries [19,20]. In the literature of production economics, researchers mainly focused on IT's contribution to productivity growth through IT capital deepening and spillover effects of IT adoption [39,65].

Thus, to close the seeming gap and generate additional insights, we intend to address the following research question: How can one empirically evaluate the production of IT capital goods – the foundation on which subsequent IT contributions are based – using different performance metrics in a cross-country context? In doing so, our endeavor also responds to the research call by Park et al. [68] for more studies on IT value creation at the macro level. It is noted that OECD refers to IT production as information and computer technology industry [61]. In this paper, we use the data collected by OECD but call them IT industry instead of information and computer technology industry to avoid a possible confusion of the latter with the other widely used term for ICT – information and communication technologies – which includes telecommunication technologies. We also recognize that IT industries in OECD and non-OECD countries have different characteristics, and their reactions to the activities occurring in the global IT market may vary. In this study, we mainly focus on OECD countries since those countries accounted for 84% of global ICT spending and 52% of global ICT trade [61].

We address the research question using a panel data set of 19 OECD countries over the period of 2000 to 2009. The time period of the data is inherently insightful, as it allows us to evaluate the performance of IT production during and after dramatic changes of economic conditions since 2000. For example, the bursting of the dotcom bubble in 2000 and the two economic recessions of March 2001 to November 2001 and December 2007 to June 2009 all led to sharp pullback in IT investments. Oliner and Sichel [65] and Oliner et al. [66] found that the acceleration of technological advancement of IT industries and the increased purchase and accumulation of IT products (i.e., IT capital deepening) were key drivers of the U.S. economic growth after 1995. They further noted that contributions from IT after 2000 did become relatively smaller but were still signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. On the other hand, the industries which invested heavily in IT and utilized IT equipments innovatively have become major contributors to the U.S. productivity growth since 2000. In other words, IT as a general-purpose technology can spur further innovations (e.g., integrated supply chain systems) for IT-using industries over time [38]. As a result, our evaluation of IT production over a period that included recent economic turbulences can potentially offer insightful implications for both IT-producing and IT-using industries in terms of continuing innovations and sustaining productivity growth of the economy.

Our intention is to assess the productivity performance of 19 OECD countries' IT industries and further identify sources of their growth (or causes of their underperformance) by means of the Malmquist total factor productivity (TFP) index. We adopt the Malmquist TFP index to measure productivity growth of IT production in these countries because we can further break down the index into its two components of technological change and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change. Technically speaking, technological change represents the shift in the production frontier over time and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change refers to the adjustment in the relative distance of the actual output to the ideal output speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the production frontier. In terms of practical meanings, technological progress re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree of innovative potential and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change reveals the capacity of a production unit to catch up with its leading peers [3]. Through the Malmquist TFP index, our analysis evaluates the relative competiveness of a country's IT industry in the global IT market. The decomposition of the Malmquist TFP index into the degree of technical innovation and the distance from maximum of production capacity provides considerable implications for IT industry policy at the government level. Policy makers and corporate executives can, accordingly, develop and implement effective competitive strategies to advance their IT industries.

We apply the parametric time-varying stochastic production frontier (SPF) method to calculate Malmquist TFP indices. When choosing the functional form for the production frontier, we prefer the translog function to the Cobb–Douglas function so that we can relax the assumptions of <sup>fi</sup>xed returns to scale and unitary elasticity of substitution [74]. In addition, this paper notably chooses the stochastic production frontier over the conventional data envelopment analysis (DEA) [4,12] to account for measurement errors of variables and other stochastic in<sup>fl</sup>uences that would otherwise show up as part of inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. These issues regarding stochastic errors are especially prominent for analyses of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and productivity at the macro level. While the use of stochastic production frontiers for measuring technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is well-documented in the literature, its application to the Malmquist TFP index is a recent novel proposition [25,67].

To highlight the methodological contribution of the paper, Table 1 compares the stochastic production frontier approach with DEA based on their characteristics, strengths, weaknesses, and applicable contexts [15]. Given the research problem and context addressed by this study, evidently it is more appropriate to employ the stochastic production frontier other than DEA for our empirical analysis. Additionally, our study involves stochastic frontier estimation with cross-country panel data. We use the true <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects model to accommodate unmeasured country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c time-invariant heterogeneity that would otherwise be included as part of inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [31]. The intended methodological contributions of the paper are to introduce the stochastic production frontier approach that can account for random errors and unobserved heterogeneity and to demonstrate its application to the Malmquist TFP index to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IS.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on the productivity impacts of IT and explains the link between this study and prior research. Section 3 presents the stochastic production frontier model for computing the Malmquist TFP index and its two deciding components. The data collected for our crosscountry analysis is presented in Section 4. Section 5 presents our empirical results, discusses policy implications, and evaluates issues that our methodology addresses. Section 6 concludes with comments on the overall contribution of the research, limitations, and topics for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

IT is a major enabler of economic change because it essentially reshapes the way a business operates and competes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, IT can reduce production cost and transaction cost for a <sup>fi</sup>rm. The conventional view of IT economic value is to treat IT as a substitute for other factors of production in the production process. In particular, the extraordinary computing power of IT diminishes the need for expensive labor and cuts down on employee headcount [10]. At the same time, IT also plays a dual role of facilitator for coordination. Interconnected computer networks decrease the time to gather, analyze, store and disseminate information. The ability of IT to support information exchange across functional, geographical and temporal boundaries enables <sup>fi</sup>rmspeci<sup>fi</sup>c business practices that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for competitors to replicate. In this respect, IT embodies a potent means to decrease transaction cost of coordinating economic activities, both within and across organizations [32,52]. Taking both perspectives into account, one can see why IT represents a unique opportunity to enhance output levels and enable fundamental changes in the production process. Using the resourcebased view of <sup>fi</sup>rms, Melville et al. [53] argued that IT has potential to create value but the extent of its realized value hinges on contingent factors like complementary resources of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, trading partners, and the competitive environment.

The literature of IT productivity impacts can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three categories. The <sup>fi</sup>rst category evaluates IT productivity gains based on the production function framework. The studies of this category consider traditional factors of production like capital and labor and treat IT as a separate factor of production. They assume speci<sup>fi</sup>c functional forms for the production frontier and measure marginal product, output elasticity, and output/input ratio for IT [21,33]. The goal is to determine if IT investments have a positive effect on output. A familiar debate related to this research stream was the productivity paradox coined by Solow [78], which cast doubts on the productivity returns expected of IT due to the seeming absence of positive evidence at that time. The productivity paradox was later resolved by Brynjolfsson and Hitt [9] and by convincing evidence of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant IT-led productivity revival evinced by many countries in the late 1990s [29,35]. Willcocks and Lester [87] provided a review of the origin, evolution, and assessment of this subject.

The second perspective to look at IT effects on productivity has to do with the relationship between IT capital accumulation and economic growth. Neoclassical growth theory maintains that the rate of change in capital accumulation (called capital deepening or capital intensity) is essential for an economy's growth. Jorgenson and Stiroh [37] used neoclassical growth models and found that IT capital deepening has been instrumental in spurring economic growth over the last forty years. Oliner and Sichel [65] noted that the increase in IT capital per work after 1995 contributed to the observed growth of U.S. labor productivity while non-IT capital per worker did not. Triplett and Bosworth [84] also found that increased IT capital per worker contributed about 0.8% to labor productivity after 1995 and 0.5% to its acceleration. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of these studies suggest that IT may make up only a small portion of total capital, but its contribution to output productivity can be signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant if increases in IT capital accumulation are made on a sustained basis. It is noted that such increases in IT capital accumulation have to be complemented by education and other essential non-IT infrastructure [19,20]. Without these complementary resources in place, it would be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to realize the productivity bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from IT capital.

The third research stream evaluates the role of production of IT capital goods in the productivity revival and aims to answer the question of how IT industries contribute to economic growth. Oliner and Sichel [64] and Gordon [29] found that two-thirds of the total factor productivity growth in the U.S. after 1995 was due to the industries that produce IT capital goods. Oliner and Sichel [65] and Oliner et al. [66] concluded that technological progress of IT industries and increased expenditures on IT products were the main drivers of the observed growth of U.S. productivity. Due to the dot-com crash in 2000 and the economic recession in 2001, contributions from IT after 2000 were found smaller but still signi<sup>fi</sup>cant [38]. Basu and Fernald [7] and Jorgenson [36] found that the industries employing IT-enabled innovations, business models, and systems have contributed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the U.S. labor productivity after 2000. It is noted that IT usage and IT production are linked but different. They are linked as IT industries supply IT capital goods that are then utilized by other industries in an economy. In other words, IT usage occurs after IT production and this chain of events is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the phenomenon of “production close to use” for IT products [18]. On the other hand, industries that provide IT capital goods are expected to exhibit distinctive performance patterns along the dimensions of productivity growth, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change, and technical progress, each of which can affect the overall productivity of an economy through IT capital accumulation.

We are keen to study the economic value of IT from the second and third perspectives by assessing the productivity performance of IT industries in providing IT capital goods. For most sectors in an economy, IT has a substitution effect for other factors of production (particularly for labor) [49]. With the continued decline in prices of IT capital products, companies are expected to exploit any cost advantages associated with IT [57]. This cost-based incentive, in turn, leads to greater demand for IT capital products. When IT capital accumulation enhances the aggregate productivity of an economy's output [29,64], IT industries should have comparatively better performance than most other industries in order to meet the higher demand created by IT capital deepening. In other words, IT industries ought to display distinctive performance characteristics in their own production processes that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence an economy's aggregate output level and productivity growth. The objective of our study is to answer this research question based on empirical advances that can yield interesting insights. The empirical advances refer to the new methods of parametric decomposition of the Malmquist index through the stochastic production frontier [25,67]. This study represents the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to use these methodologies in the IS domain to analyze the productivity performance of IT industries.

## 3. Research model

We use the Malmquist total factor productivity index (MPI) [11] to measure the productivity of IT industries of 19 OECD countries from year 2000 to 2009. There are a number of reasons for choosing the MPI over other productivity indices such as Törnqvist and Fisher. First, the MPI does not require data on input prices. Second, the MPI can combine multiple inputs and outputs without having to specify how to aggregate them. Third, the MPI does not make any behavioral assumptions like cost minimization or pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization for the economic units under consideration. Finally and most importantly, the MPI can be decomposed into two components of technical change and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change that represent speci<sup>fi</sup>c sources of productivity growth [22]. Although the computations of the MPI index involve distance functions and hence are more demanding, it is also more insightful because of its ability to separate sources for productivity growth. Moreover, the decomposition of the MPI index represents an empirical innovation in terms of analysis and modeling, and it separates our study from earlier research based on the Törnqvist index (e.g., [64,84]).

The MPI was initially proposed by Malmquist to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a distance function on a consumption space [51]. The distance function is similar to a producer's Shepard input function and is used to develop a quantity index [23]. The distance function was later extended to production theory and took on a different economic meaning of productivity measurement. It is noted that the distance function on which the MPI is based produces a value that is equivalent to technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Farrell [24]. As a consequence, the same methodologies for measuring technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency like stochastic production frontiers and DEA can be applied to the computation of the MPI.

## 3.1. Malmquist productivity index (MPI)

A production unit uses input vector $\pmb { X } ^ { t }$ to produce output vector $\pmb { Y } ^ { t } ,$ where superscript t represents the time period. In production theory, this production process is de<sup>fi</sup>ned on an output set:

$$
P ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t}\right) = \left\{\mathbf {Y} ^ {t}: \mathbf {X} ^ {t} \text {   can   produce   } \mathbf {Y} ^ {t} \right\}.\tag{1}
$$

The output set $P ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } )$ is assumed to be closed, bounded, and convex. It also satis<sup>fi</sup>es the axiom of strong disposability of inputs and outputs [15]. The output distance function $\bar { D } ^ { t } ( { \pmb X } ^ { t } , { \pmb Y } ^ { t } )$ is de<sup>fi</sup>ned on the output set $P ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } )$ as:

$$
D ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t}, \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right) = \min \left\{\theta : \left(\mathbf {Y} ^ {t} / \theta\right) \in P ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t}\right) \right\}\tag{2}
$$

$$
= \left[ \max \left\{\rho : \left(\rho \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right) \in P ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t}\right) \right\} \right] ^ {- 1}.\tag{3}
$$

Given an input vector $\mathbf { } _ { \mathbf { } } ^ { X ^ { t } } ,$ the output distance function $D ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } , \pmb { Y } ^ { t } )$ considers a radial expansion of the output vector $\pmb { Y } ^ { t }$ that still resides within the boundary of the output set $P ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } )$ [15]. In $\operatorname { E q . } \ ( 2 ) , \theta$ is the radial factor that adjusts the position of output vector $\pmb { Y } ^ { t }$ to $( \pmb { Y } ^ { t } / \theta )$ and $\theta \in ( 0 , \infty )$ where $\theta = 1$ represents staying at the current position, a value of $\theta \in ( 0 ,$ 1) indicates an expansion, and a value of $\theta \in ( 1 , \infty )$ indicates a contraction. Since the goal is to expand, the range of interest is $\theta \in ( 0 , 1 ]$ where a smaller θ indicates a longer distance to the frontier of the output set, a greater θ denotes a shorter distance to the frontier, and $\theta = 1$ speci<sup>fi</sup>es that no expansion is possible as the output vector $\pmb { Y } ^ { t }$ is already on the frontier. Eq. (3) expresses the same concept with $\rho { \in } [ 1 , \infty )$ , and since it is the reciprocal, the valid range for the output distance function is still (0, 1]. As discussed, the maximum of the output distance function $D ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } , \pmb { Y } ^ { t } )$ is 1, denoting that the production unit is operating on the frontier and is perfectly ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. When the value of the output distance function is less than 1, the production unit is producing below the frontier and is considered technically inef<sup>fi</sup>cient [15]. Based on the output distance function $D ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } , \pmb { Y } ^ { t } )$ , the Malmquist productivity index is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as [22]:

$$
M ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t}, \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}, \mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1}, \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1}\right) = \sqrt {\frac {D ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1}\right)}{D ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right)} \times \frac {D ^ {t + 1} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1}\right)}{D ^ {t + 1} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right)}}\tag{4}
$$

$$
= \frac {D ^ {t + 1} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1}\right)}{D ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right)} \sqrt {\frac {D ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1}\right)}{D ^ {t + 1} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1}\right)} \times \frac {D ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right)}{D ^ {t + 1} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right)}}\tag{5}
$$

In Eq. (5), the leading ratio of $D ^ { t + 1 }$ to $D ^ { t }$ outside the square root is equivalent to the change in technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency between periods t and $t + 1$ . This ratio represents the change in the relative distances from the observed output to the potential maximum output at time t and $t + 1 .$ . On the other hand, the parts inside the square root of Eq. (5) represent technical change. We note that the output distance function $D ^ { t }$ represents the level of observed outputs relative to maximum outputs by using the production technology available at time t. Thus, technical change is calculated as the geometric mean of the shift in the production frontier from time t to $t + 1$ . The <sup>fi</sup>rst component inside the square root is the shift in the production frontier evaluated at $X ^ { t + 1 }$ and $\pmb { Y } ^ { t + 1 }$ relative to the maximum outputs speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the production technology available at time t and t+1, respectively. Similarly, the second component is the shift in the production frontier evaluated at $\pmb { X } ^ { t }$ and $\pmb { Y } ^ { t }$ relative to the maximum outputs speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the production technology available at time t and $t + 1$ , respectively [17]. To summarize:

$$
\text { Technical   efficiency   change(TEC) } = \frac {D ^ {t + 1} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1}\right)}{D ^ {t} \left(\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t}\right)}\tag{6}
$$

$$
\text { Technical   change   (TCH) } = \sqrt {\frac {D ^ {t} (\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1})}{D ^ {t + 1} (\mathbf {X} ^ {t + 1} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t + 1})} \times \frac {D ^ {t} (\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t})}{D ^ {t + 1} (\mathbf {X} ^ {t} , \mathbf {Y} ^ {t})}}\tag{7}
$$

It is noted that MPI, TEC and TCH are each calculated as an index with a threshold value of 1. If a certain index value is equal to 1, then it indicates that the performance of a country's IT industry remains unchanged with regard to that measure from one period to the next. On the other hand, an index value greater than 1 represents an improvement, and a value less than 1 indicates a decline.

## 3.2. Stochastic production frontier

Eqs. (2) and (3) suggest that the output distance function $D ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } , \pmb { Y } ^ { t } )$ corresponds to Farrell's output-oriented technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [33]. This correspondence enables us to compute the output distance function $D ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } , \pmb { Y } ^ { t } )$ using the methods of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency measurement like parametric stochastic production frontiers and non-parametric DEA. In this study, we use the stochastic production frontier approach with a translog functional form to measure technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of each country's IT industry (i.e., the output distance function $D ^ { t } ( \pmb { X } ^ { t } , \pmb { Y } ^ { t } ) )$ , TEC, TCH, and ultimately MPI. According to Table 1, there are a number of reasons for choosing stochastic production frontiers over DEA for the computation of the Malmquist TFP index. First, given the aggregate nature of the data used for our analysis, we expect the data to have measurement errors and random noise. The “stochastic” part of the model would allow us to appropriately handle these measurement errors and random noise. Second, the data we analyze include one output variable. Third, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates of parameters in the production frontier would be needed in the computation of technical change before we can obtain MPI.

A translog, true <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects stochastic production frontier is de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows [25,67]:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {i} + \beta_ {K} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {L} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {K K} \ln K _ {i t} \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {L L} \ln L _ {i t} \ln L _ {i t} \\ + \beta_ {K L} \ln K _ {i t} \ln L _ {i t} + \beta_ {T} T + \beta_ {T T} T ^ {2} + \beta_ {T K} T \ln K _ {i t} + \beta_ {T L} T \ln L _ {i t} + v _ {i t} - u _ {i t} \end{array}\tag{9}
$$

where

$Y _ { i t }$ is the output of the ith country's IT industry in the tth year; $K _ { i t }$ denotes the capital of the ith country's IT industry in the tth year;

$L _ { i t }$ denotes the labor of the ith country's IT industry in the tth year;

$T$ is a time variable (1 for Year 1, 2 for Year 2, etc.);

$\beta _ { i }$ is a parameter to account for cross-country heterogeneity of the ith country;

$\nu _ { i t }$ are random errors, assumed to be i.i.d. with $N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } )$ distribution; and

$u _ { i t }$ are technical inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, assumed to be $| N ( \mu _ { i t } , \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } ) |$

The translog functional form (9) has the time variable T interact with input variables $K _ { i t }$ and $L _ { i t }$ which allows for non-neutral technical change [15]. The error term of Eq. (9) consists of random noise $\nu _ { i t }$ and technical inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency ${ { u } _ { i t } }$ so as to have a more accurate measurement. The stochastic frontier model distinguishes random noise (caused by factors like inclement weather that are outside management's control) from technical inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (caused by factors like uninformed decisionmaking that are managerially recti<sup>fi</sup>able). The truncated normal distribution $| N ( \mu _ { i t } , ~ \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } ) |$ indicates that technical inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency $u _ { i t }$ is nonnegative and re<sup>fl</sup>ects country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors that lead to inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency [74].

The true <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects part of the model speci<sup>fi</sup>es the intercept term as $\beta _ { i }$ instead of $\beta _ { 0 } .$ This speci<sup>fi</sup>cation permits us to separate crosscountry heterogeneity from inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and has the virtue to allow for a correlation between heterogeneity and factors of production [30]. In the traditional <sup>fi</sup>xed effects models of Schmidt and Sickles [71], inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency term not only captures country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency but incorrectly absorbs cross-country heterogeneity [31]. Besides, the traditional models make the assumption that inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is timeinvariant. A more reasonable assumption made by the true <sup>fi</sup>xedeffects model is to allow inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency to change over time. Since our aim is to estimate the production frontier for the IT industries across OECD countries over the period of 2000 to 2009, the true-<sup>fi</sup>xed effects model is preferred as it can separate country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c heterogeneity from inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and allow technical inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency to vary over time.

The technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency score TE for each country i in each year t can be derived as:

$$
\mathrm{TE} _ {i t} = \mathrm{E} [ \exp (- u _ {i t}) | e _ {i t} = v _ {i t} - u _ {i t} ].\tag{10}
$$

Because $u _ { i t }$ is a non-negative random variable, the technical ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency score $\mathrm { T E } _ { i t }$ falls between 0 and 1 with a higher value indicating a higher technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. After obtaining $\mathrm { T E } _ { i t }$ (and hence the output distance function $D ^ { t } )$ , technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change TEC in Eq. (6) can be obtained as:

$$
\mathrm{TEC} = \mathrm{TE} _ {i (t + 1)} / \mathrm{TE} _ {i t}.\tag{11}
$$

This approach for $\mathrm { T E } _ { i t }$ however, does not work with the calculation of $D ^ { t } ( { \pmb X } ^ { t + 1 } , { \pmb Y } ^ { t + 1 } )$ and $D ^ { t + 1 } ( X ^ { t } , Y ^ { t } )$ in Eq. (7) for technical change TCH. Instead, TCH between years t and t+1 for the ith country needs to be calculated directly from the estimated parameters β's [15]. We <sup>fi</sup>rst evaluate the partial derivatives of the production function with respect to time using the data for the ith country from year t and t+1. Then we calculate technical change TCH as the geometric mean of these two partial derivatives. For our translog functional speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, this is equivalent to the exponential of the arithmetic mean of the log derivatives, computed as follows:

$$
\text { Technical   change   (TCH) } = \exp \left\{\frac {1}{2} \left[ \frac {\partial \ln Y _ {i (t + 1)}}{\partial (t + 1)} + \frac {\partial \ln Y _ {i t}}{\partial t} \right] \right\}.\tag{12}
$$

Finally, as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by Eq. (8), the Malmquist TFP index (MPI) is derived by multiplying TEC of Eq. (11) and TCH of Eq. (12).

## 4. Data description

We collected the data for IT industries of 19 OECD countries over the period of 2000 to 2009. The data were under the category of “Of<sup>fi</sup>ce, Accounting and Computing Machinery” following the ISIC Rev. 3 Classi<sup>fi</sup>- cation from the OECD STAN Database for Industrial Analysis [59]. As shown in Fig. 1, OECD de<sup>fi</sup>nes the ICT sector as the production of (a) IT equipment, (b) telecommunication equipment, (c) electronics and components, (d) software, (e) IT services, and (f) telecommunication services [61]. Thus, ICT goods and services production includes both tangible goods of $( \mathsf { a } ) \mathsf { - } ( \mathsf { c } )$ and intangible software and services of (d)–(f). In this study, we mainly focus on the production of (a) IT equipment (i.e., IT capital goods) to examine IT economic value from the producer perspective. Our data set hence consists of computer hardware and peripheral equipment but does not cover other ICT goods and services such as telecommunications and software.

We note that the variable of capital stock includes all the past capital investments adjusted for depreciation. As a result, potential lag effects of capital investments are captured by the stock measure of capital.

All of the variables were then converted to the constant price value with a reference year of 1995. ## 5.2. Interpretations and policy implications

The results in the last subsection present a collective evaluation of production of IT capital goods in the 19 OECD countries. Next, we examine the empirical results more closely by country and provide interpretations for economic and governmental policy considerations. In addition, our results embody the empirical advance made possible by the use of the Malmquist TFP index and the true <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects model of translog stochastic production frontier. Therefore, we discuss economic implications and methodological advance separately.

In terms of technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (TE) in Table 4, the 19 countries can be broadly divided into two groups of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient vs. less-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient ones. The <sup>fi</sup>rst group consists of such countries as Belgium, Denmark, Korea, and U.K. that have an average ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency score above 0.700 during the period of 2000 to 2009. The second group includes countries like

Austria, France, and Greece that have an average ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency score lower than 0.700. In comparison with the second group, countries in the <sup>fi</sup>rst group have shown more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient production. That is, under a given production frontier, their IT industries are able to produce a greater amount of output using a given set of input resources. As a result, for the countries in the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient group to improve their IT industry productivity levels, the logical approach is to place more emphasis on technical improvements (e.g., new technologies, process innovations, or <sup>fl</sup>attened organization structures) that can shift the production frontier outward and augment the capacity of inputs.

On the other hand, in order for the countries in the second group to catch up with ef<sup>fi</sup>cient countries in the <sup>fi</sup>rst group, they should <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to become more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in their production processes. In other words, under the same production frontier, the countries in the second group should eliminate process inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency or resource waste so that they can produce more output using the same amount of inputs or they can produce the same output level using a less amount of inputs. After gaining higher ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and catching up with their ef<sup>fi</sup>cient counterparts, they can pursue technical improvements to increase capacity of inputs and further enhance their MPIs. Alternatively, they can attempt to make simultaneous efforts to improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and make technological progress at the same time, but such a combined undertaking tends to be more costly and their respective progress would be more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure if one is interested in tracking each speci<sup>fi</sup>c driver for performance improvement.

Some interesting observations can be made from the decomposition of MPI in Table 5. First, technical change TCH of each country plays a critical role in enhancing the TFP growth of its IT industry. Collectively, driven by 10.5% of technical progress, the productivity growth of IT industries in the 19 countries, on average, grew at 10.7% for the last decade. This growth rate was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant compared with an overall multifactor productivity growth of 0.4% at EU-15 countries from 1995 to 2005 [58]. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that IT industries are capable innovators at improving their production process through technological progress. These results thus conform to Solow's growth model [77] and Kuznets' economic growth theory [46], both of which maintain that technological progress is the driving force for economic and productivity growths.

Moreover, it is known that IT industries produce IT capital goods that are typically used to advance the production processes for other industries. So it is not surprising to see that IT industries themselves are very innovative at improving their own production processes. Since 1995, the acceleration of technological progress in IT production and the rapid decline in quality-adjusted prices led to more purchase of IT capital goods and, consequently, enhanced IT capital deepening. Stiroh [79] reported that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of the observed U.S. productivity growth was due to IT industries as well as to the industries that utilized IT intensively and creatively. After 2000, the industries that invested in IT heavily have developed IT-enabled innovations (e.g., digitalized insurance claim process and B2B ordering systems), and they have become the main driver for sustaining productivity growth [38,39]. Oliner et al. [66] found that IT capital deepening, on average, contributed as much as 30% to labor productivity from 1995 to 2006. Our empirical results also reinforce these previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings by showing that the IT industry has implemented superior production process to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll increased demand from IT-using industries in the economy. Thus, this study establishes the notion of IT value creation from the production perspective.

A decomposition of the MPIs further reveals that each country's IT industry has its particular aspects to work on for performance improvement. Table 7 presents the rankings of countries by their MPIs and the two components. Norway had the highest productivity growth of 15.7% due to its impressive performance in both technical change (TCH=1.131) and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change (1.022). Austria, however, had the lowest productivity growth (MPI=1.047) among all the countries because of its insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient technical change (TCH=1.067) and deterioration in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (TEC=0.981). There are countries with mixed performance patterns. For example, the average productivity growth MPIs of U.S. and Germany over the period were 11.4% and 8.9%, respectively, resulting from relatively high ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change (TEC=1.024 and 1.009, respectively) and moderate technical progress (TCH= 1.088 and 1.079, respectively). Belgium and France, however, exhibited the opposite case. Their productivity growth MPI (=1.103 for Belgium and 1.097 for France) mainly came from improvements in technical progress TCH (=1.117 and 1.138, respectively) rather than changes in technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency TEC (=0.988 and 0.964, respectively).

From a competition perspective, each country should be cognizant of its competitive position in the global IT market, understand the strengths and weaknesses of its IT industry compared with other countries, and make conscientious efforts to enhance its productivity on a continued basis. From the perspective of IT industry policy, researchers have been engaged in a debate regarding the effectiveness of marketdriven vs. plan-directed strategies for developing a country's IT industry [18,81]. The argument for a government to institute a national IT policy and to take the lead in building a viable IT industry is that the government will make better resource allocations and develop an IT industry that <sup>fi</sup>ts well with its national characteristics. For this reason, it is particularly important for plan-directed countries like Korea [44] to carefully assess their own performance in technological progress, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change, and productivity growth before drawing up and implementing their IT industry policies. Furthermore, the practice of using incentives to implement a policy of “industrialization by invitation,” as in Ireland's attraction of such <sup>fi</sup>rms as IBM, Dell, and Intel, can be pursued [80]. By the same token, for market-driven countries, our analysis also sheds light on the competitive advantages of different parts of the world when their IT <sup>fi</sup>rms, in hopes of staying competitive on a global stage, contemplate investing in or outsourcing their operations to these foreign countries.

## 5.3. Empirical advance

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are obtained using the Malmquist TFP index as the main performance metric and the translog stochastic production frontier as the estimation model. When compared with their conventional counterparts, both approaches that we adopted in this study represent an empirical advance in the performance evaluation of production process. They also present promising directions for future empirical IS research.

First, the choice of the Malmquist TFP index over the other frequently used TFP indices (e.g., Törnqvist and Fisher) is made on the grounds that it can be decomposed into two components of technical change and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change. This decomposition helps us identify the sources of productivity growth or pin down the causes of underperformance. As a consequence, our results based on the Malmquist TFP index are more insightful than they would be otherwise if we used the Törnqvist or Fisher index. Next, the use of a translog production function also assists us in overcoming the inherent constraints of unitary elasticity of substitution and <sup>fi</sup>xed returns to scale associated with the Cobb–Douglas production function. Finally, stochastic production frontiers are parametric and can account for measurement errors and random noise frequently encountered in empirical studies. On the other hand, DEA is nonparametric and typically does not consider measurement errors and random noise, which then would be absorbed into technical inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency measures and bias ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency scores. The true <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects model of our translog stochastic frontier also helps us capture unobserved countryspeci<sup>fi</sup>c heterogeneity, rendering our ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency measures more accurate.

Dedrick and Kraemer [18] tracked the historic development of the global computer industry with a focus on the Asia-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region. They criticized the two traditional economic models that were commonly used to explicate the growth and evolution of industries. The <sup>fi</sup>rst model has to do with the roles of national industry policy vs. market forces. The second model compares the competition among countries with the competition within industries. They argued that both models failed to fully explain the global IT industry's dynamics, and they proposed an alternative model called the global IT production network. Although we do not directly address this issue, our study incorporates both economic models of plan vs. market and country vs. industry. Furthermore, with a cross-country context, this study can be extended to examine their alternative model of the global IT production network. In other words, at the macro- and industry-level of analysis used in this study, we are looking for ways to generate empirical results that carry meaningful implications for industry policy considerations at the country level and for competitive strategy interpretations at the industry level.

There are many interesting phenomena that will reshape the way we evaluate the productivity impacts of IT. Several innovative (and sometimes destructive) forces have already taken place in the IT domain: globalization [44], offshore outsourcing [73], contract manufacturing [45], deregulation [69], electronic medical records [26], social networks [27], and cloud computing [1], to name a few. In order to fully understand the potential of these innovations and assess their impacts on IT productivity, existing theories and methods are likely insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient because the environment for IT production and deployment will be dramatically changed by these new phenomena. We are in need of theories and methods that are either emerging or well-established but yet unfamiliar to IS researchers. Possible theoretical sources may come from disciplines like production operations management, competitive strategy, sociology, and organization behavior. In terms of methodology, we should keep abreast of advances that are to be made in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of production economics and operations research, especially when new methodologies of performance evaluation have been proven effective. For example, two-equation production frontier models like Refs. [13,50] may be suitable for measuring the effects of these innovative and destructive factors.

This constant scan of horizon will help strengthen the depth of empirical IS research. For example, IS researchers have now recognized the inherent limitations of the Cobb–Douglas production function due to its underlying constraints and lack of <sup>fl</sup>exibility, and they have elected to use more <sup>fl</sup>exible functional forms like translog, Box–Tidwell, and constant elasticity of substitution (CES) (e.g., [47,49]). Another case can be found in a study of modern manufacturing plants involving IT [5], which emphasized the importance of manufacturing and organizational capabilities in studying the impact of IT and found evidence that manufacturing capabilities mediate the impact of information systems on plant performance. The performance of producing intermediate outputs turns out to be as important as the production of <sup>fi</sup>nal outputs.

Our results show that IT industry displays superior performance in the production process compared with those of other industries (e.g. [34,43]). In this light, input characteristics across industries may play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in explaining such difference in performance. For instance, the intensity of capital and labor in the production process can be a possible factor contributing to performance differences across industries. Moreover, Dewan and Min [21] showed that IT capital is a net substitute for both ordinary capital and labor. Shao and Lin [74] found that IT investments improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's production process. In other words, the relative intensity of an input compared with those of other inputs and the extent of substitution among these inputs should shed light on an industry's technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and variations in productivity. Additionally, the quality of employees (e.g., knowledge and skills) can be another explanatory variable for performance differences, as Violante [85] concluded that IT complements and favors skilled labor. Although our study does not directly address these issues, our results provide the evidence that signi<sup>fi</sup>cant productivity differences exist between IT industry and other industries, and future research can further elucidate why such differences occur by looking at the roles each particular input plays in the production process.

Empirical research has experienced a persistent problem in collecting accurate data and identifying reliable data sources for the production and consumption of goods. This data problem is even more severe for IS research because outputs of the ICT sector include not only tangible computer and communication equipment but also intangible software and services that may be purchased in package from a software vendor or developed in-house for internal use. We note that the intangible part of software and services has been under-estimated, and hence their outputs measured tend to under-report economic activities and the input levels required for these activities. Although our research only focuses on the production of computer hardware, the advanced research methodologies introduced (i.e., the true <sup>fi</sup>xedeffect stochastic production frontiers) can also provide researchers interested in evaluating the performances of intangible software and services with a useful tool to accommodate potential measurement errors. For example, as internal customization of software has been excluded from the standard measurement of economic outputs of software production, stochastic frontier models allow such uncontrollable measurement errors to be separate from inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency evaluation. The true-<sup>fi</sup>xed effects speci<sup>fi</sup>cation further distinguishes heterogeneity in each country's capability of software production from measurement errors. Of course, the most effective solution still lies in the collection of accurate data from reliable sources. The availability of new of<sup>fi</sup>cial data sources like Refs. [16,84] offers great opportunities for empirical IS research to measure IT productivity impacts at the industry level.

## 6. Conclusions

In this paper, we have used the translog stochastic production frontier to conduct a comparative study of IT industries for 19 OECD countries over the years of 2000 through 2009. We adopted the Malmquist TFP index to measure their productivity performance. On average, these countries displayed 10.7% of productivity growth associated with their IT industries, which was greater than those of most other industries and the economy as a whole when compared with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of previous studies. A summary comparison of our study and previous studies is shown in Table 8. It has to be pointed out that the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant productivity growth reported here represents the baseline, as the real numbers are likely to be even more impressive if one takes into account quality improvements in IT hardware. While our measurement of outputs captures monetary value, IT production ideally should re<sup>fl</sup>ect quality improvements as well. In other words, a computer purchased today is much more powerful than one that could be purchased with the same amount of money just a few years ago. We can use hedonic price index to adjust outputs for quality improvements. Given that inputs remain unchanged, the quality-enhanced outputs will result in a systematic increase in productivity. However, since currently there is no consistent agreement on what quality characteristics (e.g., CPU speed, memory capacity, resolution of computer displays, etc.) ought to be considered in calculating hedonic price index [83], we in the study choose to report the productivity performance of IT hardware industry without quality adjustment as the baseline. Decision makers can make their own calls in what quality characteristics to consider and to what extent outputs would be in<sup>fl</sup>ated by these characteristics, and make adjustments in productivity measured accordingly. In sum, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT industry evinces a superior production process to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll increasing demand from IT-using industries in the economy.

We also decomposed the MPI into its two components of technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change and technological change. Our breakdown analysis suggests that IT industries exhibit distinctive patterns with regard to the performance measures of productivity growth, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change, and technical change. We also learned that technological progress in the production process is the main cause of productivity growth for these IT industries. Furthermore, we observed that technical ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change seems to have a rather small effect on the productivity growth. Finally, based on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we presented implications for formulating IT policies and conducting empirical IS research.

Most of the existing studies using the Malmquist TFP index for productivity evaluation are based on the conventional DEA approach to calculate the underlying distance functions. An alternative to DEA for measuring ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is stochastic production frontiers, but it was not until recently that the latter had been applied to the Malmquist TFP index [25,67]. There are, however, situations where stochastic production frontiers are more appropriate than DEA as the analysis instrument. For analyses at the macro level, measurement errors and random in<sup>fl</sup>uences like market <sup>fl</sup>uctuations are inevitable factors that can result in estimation bias. The “stochastic” part of the production frontier used in this paper is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed to handle these issues at the aggregate level. Moreover, country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c heterogeneity in a cross-country analysis presents another concern for empirical research. The true <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the frontier model allows us to account for innate idiosyncrasy of each country (e.g., cultural differences).

While our study represents one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst endeavors to analyze productivity growth, technical change, and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change of IT industries in a cross-country context, several limitations have to be pointed out to help interpret the results accurately. These limitations are mainly due to the lack of available data and can be addressed by future research. First, this study focused on common factors of production (i.e., capital and labor) in evaluating the output of IT industries. There are, however, certain characteristics associated with IT industries that we did not consider. For instance, we did not incorporate any treatment of complementarities or substitution effects between countries [41]. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences also exist in labor costs and the quality of inputs across countries, which can further complicate these effects. The U.S., for example, has competed with Japan and Korea on the design and development of several cutting-edge IT products, but at the same time it cooperated with other East Asian countries for their production [18]. Such complementarities and substitution effects for IT design and production may shed different lights on the relative strengths and weaknesses of a country's IT industry in terms of productivity, technical advances, and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. These effects can be incorporated in the stochastic production frontier model using the approach suggested by Battese and Coelli [8]. Therefore, with the availability of additional data, future research can investigate these effects to see if similar results can be obtained.

Second, subject to data availability, we are limited to 19 OECD countries. We were not able to include data on other newly-industrialized economies (NIEs) like Singapore, Taiwan, and Hong Kong in our analysis. These NIEs have been major providers for a number of key IT products since the early 1990s. For example, Singapore has been a principal provider of hard drives and sound cards. Taiwan assumes a similar role for notebooks, tablet computers, and motherboards. Most of these NIEs are plan-directed and have formulated speci<sup>fi</sup>c industry policies deemed critical for the successful development of their IT industries [88]. It would be insightful to examine these NIE countries and evaluate how they perform along these dimensions of productivity growth, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency change, and technical change. Furthermore, Kraemer and Dedrick [44] claimed that the U.S. has retained only the production activities with increasing returns to scale and has outsourced most of the other activities with decreasing returns to scale to East Asian NIEs, China, India, and parts of Europe. This division of labor theoretically should bring about mutual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the countries involved. A crosscountry study that includes these countries will provide empirical evidence to corroborate or refute such claims.

Finally, our study evaluates the provision of IT capital goods that consist of essential IT hardware products. The IT equipment industry as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by OECD does not include telecommunications equipment or software, each of which is an important industry of ICT market in its own right. This limitation can be explained by production theory and addressed by data availability. In theory, production units under consideration are assumed to operate with a similar production process. The production process of IT equipment industry is different from those of telecommunication and software industries. Thus, we should examine the performance of the three industries individually. Our research embodies the <sup>fi</sup>rst step to complement the literature with the view of IT production and a focus on IT hardware industry. Interesting follow-up can be performed for the broader ICT sector, and advanced methodologies introduced in our research can assist researchers in conducting similar studies of telecommunication and software industries. Moreover, the constraint of data unavailability necessitates further efforts to measure outputs of telecommunication and software industries. It is noted that the sector of ISIC 3832 “Radio, TV and Communication Equipment” [60] contains not only telecommunication products but also radio and TV merchandises which are not deemed ICT goods. By the same token, OECD does not report data on software, which has grown to be a very critical industry that warrants special attention and analysis [76]. Kemerer [40] noted that software costs have skyrocketed but we have little understanding of how to control the costs of software development. Consequently, another venue for future research is to examine the software industries across country boundaries (e.g., India and the U.S.), measure and compare their productivity performance, and understand their interdependent relationships. The research methodology introduced in this paper provides a possible way to address measurement errors if accessible yet imperfect data are available.
