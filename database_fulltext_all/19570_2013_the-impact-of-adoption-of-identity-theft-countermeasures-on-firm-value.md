# The impact of adoption of identity theft countermeasures on <sup>fi</sup>rm value

Indranil Bose <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Alvin Chung Man Leung b,1

## a b s t r a c t

Identity theft poses a huge threat to the e-commerce community. Although the crime is a growing menace, <sup>fi</sup>rms are uncertain about the payoffs obtained from adoption of identity theft countermeasures. As the cost of implementation of relevant countermeasures is high, senior managers are hesitant to make such investments. Using the event study method, we empirically demonstrate that it is worthy for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to adopt such countermeasures. We show that the news of such adoption increases the short term market value of the announcing <sup>fi</sup>rm by 0.63% on an average. Our research also <sup>fi</sup>nds that early adopters, adopters of sophisticated identity theft countermeasures, <sup>fi</sup>rms with high growth potential, and <sup>fi</sup>rms with high credit rating show a strong and positive return in market value, whereas small <sup>fi</sup>rms demonstrate a moderate but positive reaction. As shown in our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the market rewards early adopters of security technology and adopters of sophisticated measures greatly. To reap the market premium, the industrial practitioners should adopt newer identity theft countermeasures at an earlier time. Furthermore, our study shows that investors reward growing <sup>fi</sup>rms and small companies more if they adopt the countermeasures. This shows that the market views the investment in anti-identity theft as a tool to enhance competitive advantage. Our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings should encourage <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt identity theft countermeasures more proactively.

## 1. Introduction

Information security has become a major concern for corporations. Among various cybercrimes that threaten the security of <sup>fi</sup>rms, the number of occurrences of <sup>fi</sup>nancially targeted identity theft has surged in recent times [57]. According to the Federal Trade Commission, identity theft is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the crime where an adversary appropriates another person's identity for illegal activities such as fraud and theft [54]. Such crime has caused serious <sup>fi</sup>nancial loss. An identity fraud survey reports that about 3.5% of the US population (i.e., 8.1 million Americans) was the victim of identity thefts with an estimated total loss of US \$37 billion [52]. Recent incidents, such as the Facebook scam targeting the popular movie Twilight [73] and the credit card loss of 63 stores of Barnes & Noble [17], have attracted global attention and have damaged customers' con<sup>fi</sup>dence. The spate of security breaches has prompted researchers to borrow techniques from the <sup>fi</sup>eld of <sup>fi</sup>nance to study their impact on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial well-being of <sup>fi</sup>rms. Some studies have made use of the event study method that analyzes the impact of announcements of events on stock price of <sup>fi</sup>rms to show that data breaches and identity theft have caused a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative impact on their market value [15,18,33,43,46,75]. A signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative loss of 1% in market value has been caused by the announcements of security breaches [33]. Personal information security breaches (e.g., hacking, virus, and computer break-in) have resulted in a cumulative abnormal return (CAR), which measures the sum of the difference between expected stock return and actual stock return of <sup>fi</sup>rms, of −1.88% [15]. Internet security breaches (e.g., hacking and computer break-in) have decreased market value of <sup>fi</sup>rms by 2.1% [18]. The negative impact of security breaches among pure e-commerce <sup>fi</sup>rms is found to be more severe than brick-and-mortar companies [75]. Among the three categories of security attacks (con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, integrity, and availability), it is found that integrity attacks, which compromise corporate data and information assets, have caused the greatest loss in the short run but all three types of attacks caused insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant loss in the long run [43]. Phishing has diminished the cumulative standardized abnormal return (CSAR), which is CAR adjusted for standard errors, for global <sup>fi</sup>rms by 5.1% [46]. It is estimated that identity thefts caused a loss of US\$ 52.6 billion in the US each year [12].

To combat the crime of identity theft, government legislation, consumer education, and corporate security policies are crucial [54]. In particular, institutional and regulatory forces are the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant antecedents that can urge a company to improve its security [39]. As a result of that, various identity theft countermeasures (ITCs), such as dynamic password generators, SMS based one-time passwords, personal digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and electronic signatures, have been adopted by <sup>fi</sup>rms. It is reported that there have been widespread adoption of two-factor authentication (2FA) after the government of Hong Kong made it mandatory for banks to adopt it [11]. Addressing the inadequacy of traditional password (what you know), 2FA is an ITC tool that provides an additional layer of security (what you have). Though some companies have claimed that adoption of ITC has increased sales [19], the high cost of implementation of these measures has scared away many e-commerce service providers. According to RSA, a security division of EMC Corporation, a dynamic password generator costs around \$35–\$40 per device [32], and Gartner has reported that a commercially available brand monitoring and anti-phishing package may cost nearly \$70,000 per month and per module [48]. Some organizations have planned to recover these high setup costs from customers. However, in a recent survey, 72% of customers who practiced online banking have expressed their unwillingness to pay higher service charges for a more secure environment [53]. Besides, the enhanced security functions have also created inconvenience to some customers. Due to uncertainty, many e-commerce service providers worry that the return on investment (ROI) may not be able to recover the costs of ITC implementation. All of the above factors have resulted in a dilemma about whether <sup>fi</sup>rms should invest in ITC. The dilemma has led security of<sup>fi</sup>cers around the globe to search for quantitative metrics that can support the adoption of ITC.

Current studies on information security mainly advocate the negative <sup>fi</sup>nancial impact of security breaches. The positive impact of investments in information security is rarely analyzed. In spite of the dearth of literature in this area, we believe investments in ITC make economic sense for <sup>fi</sup>rms. To support this belief, we conduct an event study by collecting data related to announcements of adoption of ITC by US <sup>fi</sup>rms, and examining the immediate impact of those announcements on the stock prices, and market capitalization of <sup>fi</sup>rms. Event study is a suitable research method for this study because market response is immediate and it represents the general expectations of investors towards a <sup>fi</sup>rm's future performance as a result of today's corporate action. Using event study methodology, we do not have to rely on traditional accounting measures (e.g., returns on assets) to analyze the effectiveness of IT investment because those measures are backward-looking [8]. The objective of this study is to bridge the gap in the information security literature about economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of investments in security measures, and to show that it is economically justi<sup>fi</sup>able for <sup>fi</sup>rms to invest in ITC. To the best of our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst quantitative assessment of ITC adoption on market value of <sup>fi</sup>rms. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne market value as market capitalization, which is the product of stock price and total number of shares outstanding. We make several contributions in this paper. First, we enrich extant information security literature by documenting the stock market reaction to ITC investments in general. Our research results show that ITC adoption has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive impact on market value. Also, in our subsample analysis, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that early adopters, adopters of sophisticated ITC, <sup>fi</sup>rms with high growth potential, and <sup>fi</sup>rms with high credit rating have higher market returns as a result of ITC announcements. Second, we provide a robust quantitative method to quantify the <sup>fi</sup>nancial impacts of such investments. Third, our research results suggest that ITC can bring in positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant market returns to the adopters, resolving the dilemma of whether to invest in information security for industrial practitioners.

## 2. Theoretical background

The theory of dynamic capabilities and the principle of shareholder value maximization form the basis of the theoretical foundation of this research. Dynamic capabilities are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the ability to integrate, build, and recon<sup>fi</sup>gure internal and external competencies to address rapidly-changing environments” [70]. Managers of e-commerce enabled companies are constantly challenged by rapid changes in the business domain and they cannot depend on core resources to sustain the companies' competitive advantages [25]. In a moderately dynamic environment, variation can facilitate the development of dynamic capabilities whereas in a high velocity environment, selection is the primary factor for enhancing the capabilities [30]. Competitive advantages erode quickly in a dynamic environment. To maintain the competitive market position, the managers should replace the fading advantages with new advantages by developing, adding, integrating, and releasing key resources constantly [72]. Online crime is one of the fastest growing criminal activities that undermine the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of online customers. Therefore, e-commerce security measures such as ITC are one of the dynamic resources that can help sustain the competitive advantage of adopters by restoring the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of their customers. By adopting appropriate security measures, the companies can maintain the strategic development of e-commerce. Prior studies have shown that investments in speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT resources that are closely aligned with corporate strategic objectives can bring in additional bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in terms of pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, market valuation, operational performance, and innovation [3].

The principle of shareholder value maximization suggests that managers are compelled by market forces to conduct activities that increase the wealth of shareholders [31,59,61]. If investors agree about the actions that increase shareholder value, the approbation of the investors is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the positive market value of the companies. Prior studies have shown that in line with this principle, corporate strategic investment decisions lead to positive market reaction in general [74]. In this research, we analyze the impact of investments in ITC on market value and investigate whether investors generally believe that such investments can facilitate strategic development of the companies and enhance the wealth of shareholders. If ITCs are important dynamic resources that enable strategic development of e-commerce, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts will be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the positive market value created by its adoption.

## 3. Hypotheses' development

Prior event studies generally agree that investments in IT, especially innovative, strategic, transformational, and infrastructure IT investments, can give rise to positive excess market returns for shareholders [21,26,27,40]. Though some studies have shown that IT investments have unidenti<sup>fi</sup>able immediate impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm productivity [13,62], it is generally believed that companies take time to leverage the investments to develop IT capability and outperform their peers in pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost-based performance measures [7,64]. Forward-looking investors generally take the long-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT investments into account and their actions re<sup>fl</sup>ect their future expectation about the <sup>fi</sup>rms. Investments in ITC ensure safety in e-commerce, and reduce the impact of security threats on organizational resources. With better countermeasures, companies can encourage customers to use the electronic services offered by them with less concern about identity theft. Better security measures can retain current customers and attract new clients who value online safety. In fact, a prior study has shown that out of the four trust indices including third party privacy seals, privacy statements, third party security seals, and security features, online customers are most concerned about security features [6]. Therefore, adoption of ITC can relieve the concern of customers. It is also well known that recognition of security threats and adoption of appropriate countermeasures allow companies to focus more on core business activities and generate more revenue [35]. Therefore, we conjecture the following hypothesis:

H1. Adoption of ITC gives rise to an increase in market value for the adopting <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Previous research on performance of dynamic innovation shows that small <sup>fi</sup>rms are more technically innovative than large <sup>fi</sup>rms in a dynamic environment [67]. The primary reason is that large <sup>fi</sup>rms are more bureaucratic and small <sup>fi</sup>rms are more agile in decision making and adapting to the changing environment [44]. Therefore, small <sup>fi</sup>rms may react faster than large <sup>fi</sup>rms in response to the threat of identity theft. The market may reward the <sup>fi</sup>rms more for the faster response. Furthermore, prior studies have shown that the payoff of IT investments is negatively correlated with <sup>fi</sup>rm size [5,36,37,40,51]. Large <sup>fi</sup>rms are usually followed by more professional analysts and traders [23]. In general, they have more pre-disclosure information than small <sup>fi</sup>rms [4]. As a result, the actual announcements of small <sup>fi</sup>rms contain more undisclosed information than big <sup>fi</sup>rms. Thus, the market reaction of small <sup>fi</sup>rms to corporate announcements is more vigorous than that of big <sup>fi</sup>rms [40]. Furthermore, smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms usually have lower trading price. If any IT investment causes the same amount of market reaction for both big and small <sup>fi</sup>rms, then the percentage change in the stock price of the small <sup>fi</sup>rms in response to this investment related announcement will be greater than that of the large <sup>fi</sup>rms in general [40]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H2. From a market perspective, small <sup>fi</sup>rms are more positively affected when they announce the adoption of ITC than big <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Apart from <sup>fi</sup>rm size, the impact of growth potential of <sup>fi</sup>rms on the market value has been studied in prior event studies [21,56]. Growth potential is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ratio of market value to book value of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The higher the ratio, the higher is the relative growth potential of a <sup>fi</sup>rm. In a dynamic industry, growing <sup>fi</sup>rms have an advantage over well-established <sup>fi</sup>rms in learning new routines [34]. To sustain the momentum of high growth rate, high growth potential <sup>fi</sup>rms are more willing to invest in dynamic resources so as to meet the challenges of the market. Prior research has shown that growing market demand may stimulate new product development and innovation [66]. In the e-commerce market, security is a major concern for both customers and investors. Implementation of ITC can be a step towards safeguarding the e-commerce platform of the <sup>fi</sup>rm that often plays a major role in conducting the business operations. Therefore, when a <sup>fi</sup>rm announces adoption of ITC, the investors interpret this as a responsible step taken by the <sup>fi</sup>rm to protect its infrastructure and the interests of its consumers. The announcement often prompts them to reward the <sup>fi</sup>rm for this proactive action. Prior literature has shown that the market in general rewards companies that make decisions of long-term strategic interest to shareholders [74]. In the light of this argument, we conjecture the following hypothesis:

H3. Firms with higher growth potential generate larger increase in market value when they announce the adoption of ITC than <sup>fi</sup>rms with lower growth potential.

Financial liquidity is an important factor in developing dynamic capabilities. To generate dynamic capabilities, <sup>fi</sup>rms need money to acquire human and physical resources. Therefore, commitment of <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources and an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanism to direct <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources to align them with the needs of the market are imperative factors for developing dynamic capabilities [69]. Financial healthiness can be re<sup>fl</sup>ected by third party credit rating [16]. Firms with high credit rating are usually <sup>fi</sup>nancially robust and can repay their debt. On the other hand, <sup>fi</sup>rms with low credit rating usually have some <sup>fi</sup>- nancial problems. As any IT investment inevitably involves risks, the negative impact of such risks on <sup>fi</sup>nancially healthy <sup>fi</sup>rms is perceived to be lower than that on <sup>fi</sup>nancially unhealthy companies. Therefore, status of <sup>fi</sup>nancial health of a <sup>fi</sup>rm in<sup>fl</sup>uences the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of investors to an extent. Prior studies have also found that credit rating announcements have some in<sup>fl</sup>uence on abnormal stock price returns [55]. In view of this, we conceive the following hypothesis:

H4. Firms with higher credit rating will show more positive impact on market value when they announce the adoption of ITC than <sup>fi</sup>rms with lower credit rating.

Prior studies have shown strong correlation between IT capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [7,60]. IT capability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the effective use of information systems that enhance or support core competence of the company [60]. Prior research has shown that competitive IT capabilities such as IT business expertise and relationship infrastructure can generate competitive advantages [9]. Furthermore, implementation of advanced IT applications can help generate operational excellence and enhance dynamic capabilities [65]. Firms with strong IT capability should have greater ability to make the best use of ITC to protect customers against identity theft. The chance of successful implementation is also higher because they have better IT infrastructure and IT expertise than <sup>fi</sup>rms with weaker IT capability. Therefore, this logically leads to the following hypothesis:

H5. Firms with stronger IT capability will show more positive impact on market value when they announce the adoption of ITC than <sup>fi</sup>rms with weaker IT capability.

Prior studies have shown that early adopters of new technology often enjoy several lead time advantages [47]. The lead time advantages allow the adopters to learn and gain more experience than other competitors. Through learning and accumulating experience, <sup>fi</sup>rms can enhance the dynamic capabilities and foster IT capabilities in the area of IT infrastructure, IT business expertise, and relationship infrastructure [9]. Therefore, early adopters of ITC should have some advantages over their competitors. In the US, many anti-identity theft laws were enacted in recent times. These included the Social Security Number Privacy and Identity Theft Prevention Act of 2003, the Amended Identity Theft and Assumption Deterrence Act of 2003, the Anti-Phishing Act of 2005 in California as well as similar anti-phishing laws in Arkansas and Texas in 2005 [41,42]. In Europe, phishing may be subject to the Data Protection Act [41]. In Hong Kong, though there is no explicit law against phishing, such activity may contravene Copyright Ordinance, Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance, Telecommunication Ordinance, Crime Ordinance (Cap. 200), and Theft Ordinance [10]. We consider an adopter of ITC to be an early adopter if such an announcement took place in 2005 or before. This leads to the hypothesis:

H6. Early adopters of ITC (in 2005 or before) generate more positive impact on market value than late adopters.

In the face of budget constraints, different countermeasures with varying level of technical sophistication have been adopted by e-commerce companies. Some sophisticated tools like 2FA are known to provide stronger protection to customers against phishing attacks. Such tools are also known to boost the con<sup>fi</sup>dence of customers when they use the related electronic environment. At the time of research, 2FA, which includes digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cates, hardware tokens, and one-time passwords, is considered the most sophisticated ITC against identity theft. Hong Kong and Singapore government mandated the compulsory use of 2FA, when customers conducted high risk online transactions, starting from 2005 and 2007, respectively [10]. Therefore, we consider companies which adopt 2FA to be adopters of sophisticated ITC. The Overseas Chinese Banking Corporation found that when customers adopted 2FA, the number of transactions increased by 20% and the amount of transactions per customer increased by 50% [19]. Furthermore, the experience gained by the adopter in the implementation process of a sophisticated ITC can enhance the intellectual capital of a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Prior research has shown that the gain in intellectual capital can facilitate knowledge management and dynamic capabilities [38]. In future, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can reuse the implementation experience when they launch tools with similar sophistication. This is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for the long term growth of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H7. When <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt a sophisticated type of ITC the corresponding announcements generate higher market returns than when <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt a less sophisticated type of ITC.

## 4. Research method

An event study that analyzes the immediate impact of events on <sup>fi</sup>rm value is a popular method to analyze the immediate response of the investors to announcements of security breaches [15,18,33,43,46]. However, prior research does not provide a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed approach for conducting an event study. Some variations also exist in prior studies, e.g., difference in the length of confounding window and estimation window, differences in the statistical tests of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of abnormal returns, and differences in conducting subsampling analysis. In this paper, we provide a comprehensive, consistent, and step-by-step description of how to conduct an event study. The details are shown in Fig. 1. This illustration can provide useful guidelines to future researchers on how to conduct an event study.

In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, relevant announcements are retrieved from one or more news databases. The databases commonly used include Factiva, PR Newswire, and Business Newswire. After eliminating duplicate announcements, only the earliest occurring announcement is retained.

In the second step, announcements related to private companies and non-listed public companies are eliminated because we can only measure stock returns for listed companies. Then announcements that are released around the announcement date of some other confounding news are eliminated. Usually a <sup>fi</sup>ltering window of three to <sup>fi</sup>ve days is selected, and confounding events such as announcements of earnings, declaration of dividends, change of senior management, among others, are typically considered. The longer the confounding window, the more accurate are the results generated in the event study. However, the retained sample size becomes smaller due to the stringent <sup>fi</sup>ltering criterion. Nevertheless, in this research, we adopt a <sup>fi</sup>ve day confounding window that extends from two days before the announcement to two days after the announcement.

In the third step, the tickers of the companies making relevant announcements are listed and their stock prices are downloaded. To ensure that reliable results are obtained from the event study, announcements related to thinly traded stocks are eliminated in this step. If the average stock price is less than US \$1 or the average daily trading volume is less than 50,000 shares in the estimation period, then the announcement associated with those stocks is eliminated, as thinly traded markets have limited predictability and can over-react or under-react to announcements [68]. Table 1 shows how the collected data went through the different <sup>fi</sup>ltering processes.

In the fourth step, a model is selected for computation of abnormal stock returns. The most common computation model is the market model [40], which is based on the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) that assumes linear relationship between rate of return and rate of market return. The CAPM is represented by Eq. (1):

$$

where $R _ { i t }$ is the rate of return for announcement i of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>rm on day $t , R _ { m t }$ is the rate of return of market index m on day t, α is the y-intercept, β is the slope that measures the sensitivity of $R _ { m t } ,$ and ε is the disturbance term with Ordinary Least Squares properties. The windows used for estimation of the event also vary from one study to another. The majority of researchers have used 200 or more trading days' data prior to the announcement of the event for obtaining the regression model [27,40]. In order to minimize the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the event on the regression model, it is a common practice to leave a gap of about a month between the regression window and the event window [63].

In the <sup>fi</sup>fth step, the abnormal return of stock prices is computed along with its statistical power using the method described in an earlier study [27]. The abnormal return (AR) of announcement i on day t is given by the formula:

$$
A R _ {i t} = R _ {i t} - (a _ {i} + b _ {i} R _ {m t})\tag{2}
$$

where R is the rate of return, m is the market index, $a _ { i }$ is the intercept, and $b _ { i }$ is slope of the regression model for announcement i. a and b are the sample estimates of population parameters α and β in Eq. (1). We use either S&P 500 index or Nasdaq composite index as $R _ { m t } .$ For stocks listed in NYSE, we use S&P 500 as market index. For stocks listed in Nasdaq, we choose either S&P 500 or Nasdaq composite index that gives the highest adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ in the regression model as shown in Eq. (1). As a robustness test, apart from S&P 500 and Nasdaq composite indices, we also try S&P Banking Index, Nasdaq 100, and Nasdaq Computer. If a <sup>fi</sup>rm belongs to more than one market composite index, we choose the index that gives the highest adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ in the regression model as shown in Eq. (1). The results are qualitatively similar to the ones presented in this paper. Readers may refer to Appendix A for formulae that are used to compute cumulative abnormal returns (CAR), standardized cumulative abnormal returns (CSAR) and parametric test statistics. In this research, due to the small sample size, instead of using Z statistics, we use Student's t statistics in computing the parametric test statistics.

Prior research typically varies the event window from one day up to 21 days around the announcement date of the event. A shorter event window is often preferred because the result is less susceptible to noise [50]. In this research, we used data related to 200 trading days that occurred one month prior to the event for regression. Our event days included the day of the announcement (i.e., $\mathrm { \Delta t = \left[ 0 \right] ) }$ ), as well as the day following the announcement $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ t = [ } 1 ] )$ . Fig. 2 shows the regression window, confounding news window, and the event window that are used in this study.

To further establish the robustness of the result [49], some researchers have proposed the use of nonparametric tests, such as the Corrado's rank test [20,21], the sign Z test [20,58,71], the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [18,45,71], and bootstrapping [20], in addition to the parametric Z test. Among the nonparametric tests, the sign Z test [22] and the Corrado's rank test [24] are popularly used. Corrado's rank test is reported to perform better than other signi<sup>fi</sup>cance tests in the analysis of NASDAQ stock data [14]. Readers may refer to Appendix B for a description and related formulae used to conduct the nonparametric sign and Corrado's rank tests.

In this research, apart from parametric tests, we also conduct nonparametric tests and nonparametric bootstrapping. Bootstrapping is a simulation technique for statistical inference [29]. By resampling the sample data randomly and repeatedly with replacement, nonparametric bootstrap method can determine the empirical distribution of observed data [28]. In this study, we resample the data 5000 times to generate the sample distribution, and determine the sample variance on the basis of the empirical distribution. Subsequently, we use the sample standard deviation to compute the bootstrap test statistics instead of the standard deviation of the original sample. Appendix C shows the steps for computation of the bootstrap test statistic. Nonparametric bootstrapping technique is useful in determining a consistent sample variance when the sample data are small and the underlying data distribution is unknown [29].

In the sixth step, researchers often de<sup>fi</sup>ne appropriate subsamples based on <sup>fi</sup>rm size, different time periods of announcements, various types of IT investments, among others, and analyze the abnormal returns for each subsample. Again, parametric, nonparametric, and bootstrapping tests are used, and hypotheses are tested on the generated subsamples.

We followed the six steps of the event study method as shown in Fig. 1. Announcements were retrieved from the news database Factiva, using keywords such as anti-identity theft, 2FA, digital certificate, dynamic password generator, digital signature, and one-time password. Duplicate announcements and announcements associated with confounding events, such as declaration of dividends, mergers and acquisitions, changes in credit rating, and changes in senior management that occurred between two days before to two days after the date of announcement, were discarded. Finally, 87 announcements related to US listed companies and released from 1995 to 2012 were examined further. These announcements included 46 companies that had one announcement each, eight that contributed two announcements each, and three that released more than three announcements. Nevertheless, for those companies with multiple announcements, the dates of announcement were separated by at least three months and the ITC adopted were totally different. The rate of return of stock price of the announcing <sup>fi</sup>rm on the announcement day and one day after the announcement was retrieved to see if the change was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, with respect to the benchmark S&P 500 index and Nasdaq composite index. A positive return indicated that the immediate impact of the adoption of ITC was positive for the announcing <sup>fi</sup>rm.

To test the hypotheses, we conducted subsampling analysis using subsamples of <sup>fi</sup>rm size, growth potential, credit rating, IT capability, time of adoption, and sophistication of ITC. A company was considered to be big if its total assets at the end of that year prior to the year of the announcement were greater than the average of the entire sample (i.e., US \$142.88 billion for the collected data). Otherwise, it was considered to be small. This approach of <sup>fi</sup>rm size classi<sup>fi</sup>cation has been adopted in prior research [2]. Similarly, if a <sup>fi</sup>rm had a market value to book value ratio greater than average (i.e., 3.92), it was considered to be a <sup>fi</sup>rm with high growth potential. With regard to credit rating, we used Standard and Poor's domestic long term credit rating as the basis for analysis. Firms with ratings ranging from A- to AAA are considered to have high credit rating. Firms that appeared in Information Week 500 in the same year of the announcement or in any of the four years before such announcement are considered to be high in IT capability. Otherwise, they were considered to have low IT capability. Firms that adopted ITC in or before 2005 were considered to be early adopters, whereas those that adopted ITC in and after 2005 were labeled as late adopters. Year 2005 was chosen as the line of division because many identity theft laws (e.g. Social Security Number Privacy and Identity Theft Prevention Act, Identity Theft and Assumption Deterrence Act, and Anti-Phishing Act) had already been enacted in a number of states of the US by 2005 [41,42]. Regarding sophistication of ITC, we considered two-factor authentication (2FA) tools, which included digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cates, hardware tokens, and one-time passwords, as advanced ITC and others as less sophisticated ITC. In our sample data, non-sophisticated ITC included SMS alerts of identity thefts, user access monitoring systems, and platforms for electronic signature. ## 5. Discussion of results

Our data analysis showed that adoption of ITC increased stock prices of <sup>fi</sup>rm by 0.63% over a two day period (on the day of announcement, and one day after the date of announcement). At the same time, this change was found to be statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant when parametric test (10% level of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance), non-parametric sign (1% level) and rank (5% level) tests, and non-parametric bootstrapping test (5% level) were used. Although the sample size was small, this provided evidence that the stock market rewarded companies that adopted ITC favorably. A plausible explanation was that investors generally believed that adoption of ITC secured online transactions conducted on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's website, and also enabled the <sup>fi</sup>rm to improve their future cash <sup>fl</sup>ows by attracting more customers. As the average stock price of the 78 <sup>fi</sup>rms was US \$43.3, and their average number of outstanding shares was approximately 1880 million, a 0.63% increase in stock return resulted in a gain of market capitalization by US\$ 515 million.

## 5.1. Size mattered but not significantly

Big <sup>fi</sup>rms in the sample had total assets greater than US\$ 142.88 billion (average of total assets for all 78 <sup>fi</sup>rms) at the end of the year preceding the date of the announcements, and small <sup>fi</sup>rms had total assets less than that. Fig. 3 shows that big <sup>fi</sup>rms gained US \$1132 million in market capitalization within two days of making the announcement, which was 4.4 times that of the small <sup>fi</sup>rms. With regard to the percentage change in abnormal returns, the gain for big <sup>fi</sup>rms was found to be higher than that for small <sup>fi</sup>rms. As shown in Table 4, large <sup>fi</sup>rms had 1.36% change in CAR whereas small <sup>fi</sup>rms had a change of 0.48%. However, the small <sup>fi</sup>rm subsample was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (10% level) in the nonparametric sign and rank tests, and only marginally insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in the parametric test (with p-value equal to 0.15). The big <sup>fi</sup>rm subsample was only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in one of the nonparametric tests. Moreover, Table 5 shows that small <sup>fi</sup>rms had a positive increase in stock prices on the day after the announcement, and this change was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for all three tests. On the contrary, the CAR of large <sup>fi</sup>rms was insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in all statistical tests. As shown in Table 6, the bootstrapping tests revealed that the CAR of small <sup>fi</sup>rms was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for t = [0,1] at 10% level and marginally insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant for t = [1] (with a p-value of 0.14). In contrast the CAR of large <sup>fi</sup>rms was insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in the bootstrapping tests for both event windows. As a robustness check, we split our sample data according to the median rather than average total assets. The untabulated results were qualitatively similar. Small sample <sup>fi</sup>rms were more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in most of the statistical tests than large sample <sup>fi</sup>rms. Therefore, we concluded that hypothesis H2 is partially supported.

In Table 5, CARs of both high credit rating companies low credit rating <sup>fi</sup>rms were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant only in the nonparametric rank test (and not the parametric test) in t = [1]. The bootstrapping results (Table 6) showed that neither subsamples were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. ## 5.4. IT leaders exhibited less reaction in longer window

Following the example of prior research, we considered a <sup>fi</sup>rm to be an IT leader (with high IT capability) if it was listed in the Information Week 500 in the year of the announcement or in any of the four years preceding that [8]. If the <sup>fi</sup>rm never appeared in the list within this time period, it was considered to be a non-IT leader. As shown in Table 4, the CAR of non-IT leaders (0.97%) was surprisingly higher than that of IT-leaders (0.47%). The CAR of non-IT leaders was signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant in nonparametric tests but insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in the parametric test (as shown in Table 4) and bootstrapping (as shown in Table 6) whereas that of IT leaders was insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in all tests. However, in Table 5, the results were reversed and the CAR of IT leaders (0.49% as shown in Table 5) was higher than that of non-IT leaders (0.27%). Both CARs are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in nonparametric test (as shown in Table 5) but insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in parametric test and bootstrapping (Table 6). The results seemed to suggest that in a longer event window t = [0,1], the positive impact of IT leaders faltered. Therefore, we cannot provide adequate support to H6. The lower than expected CAR of IT leaders might be attributed to the predominant higher expectation of investors towards IT leaders. Investors believed that it was normal (and perhaps expected) for IT leaders to adopt measures to protect customers against identity theft, and so such announcements did not create any impact on the market. On the contrary, when non-IT leaders announced the adoption of ITC, investors generally appreciated their proactive action in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of technology to safeguard the interests of their consumers.

## 5.5. It's all about timing

We split the full sample into two subsamples based on the year of announcement. Year 2005 is important in the history of identity theft, as a number of states in the US, such as Arkansas, California, and Texas, passed stringent laws against phishing, and other identity thefts. All announcements prior to 2005 belonged to the early adopters group, and announcements made in 2006–12 made up the late adopters group. The early adopters group's positive market reaction as well as gain in market capitalization were higher than those of the late adopters group. As shown in Table 4, the CAR of the former group (1.12%) was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 5% level in all parametric and nonparametric tests whereas that of the latter group (0.45%) was insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in all tests. The results were similar when we considered the reaction on the day following the announcement (Table 5) and when we adopted the bootstrapping method (Table 6). This indicated that the market rewarded <sup>fi</sup>rst movers more generously. The positive reaction for the early adopters group was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and it showed that <sup>fi</sup>rst movers enjoyed more advantages as they exceeded investors' expectations. As a robustness test, we split our sample using 2004, 2006, and 2007 as the line of division, respectively, and the results remained qualitatively similar. The early adopters received higher and more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant of CARs than the late adopters. The significant results were not likely to be related to the year that we selected for dividing the sample into two groups. Therefore, hypothesis H7 is supported.

## 5.6. Strong technology preferred

One of the popularly adopted ITCs is 2FA, and governments of some countries have mandated the adoption of this technology for high risk transactions. The analysis showed that announcements related to adoption of sophisticated ITC in the form of 2FA led to a 0.69% change in stock price. The reaction for <sup>fi</sup>rms that adopted 2FA is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 10% level for all parametric and nonparametric tests and bootstrapping in both t = [0,1] and t = [1]. On the contrary, announcements not related to 2FA gave rise to insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant market reaction in all tests and all windows. Indeed, strong and sophisticated ITC that needed more investments and more effort on the part of the adopting <sup>fi</sup>rm was heralded positively by the market because they provided additional layers of protection for the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and re<sup>fl</sup>ected that <sup>fi</sup>rms took security matters seriously.

## 5.7. Changes in ITC adoption

In our sample data, we observed changes in ITC adoption among e-commerce service providers over time. Some <sup>fi</sup>rms were very responsive to the demand of a safe online environment and always adopted sophisticated ITC to safeguard the security of customers. Nevertheless, the frequency of new ITC adoption varied from one <sup>fi</sup>rm to another. Bank of America, for example, adopted new ITC once every 2.8 years based on our sample data. It had 6 ITC announcements (e.g., digital signature, personal digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cate, token-based two-factor authentication and customer-oriented anti-phishing education program) from 1995 to 2011. Gartner also reported that adoption of authentication tools among commercial <sup>fi</sup>rms was on the rise. Nowadays, there were less than 5% of B2B <sup>fi</sup>rms adopting adaptive access control and less than 10% of <sup>fi</sup>rms adopting cloud-based user authentication; however, the former could increase to 30% by 2015 and the latter could increase to 50% by 2017 [1]. In our sample data, we also observed that more <sup>fi</sup>rms adopted non-2FA ITC and emphasized user education and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of fake emails from the customer side. For example, Bank of America collaborated with APWG to launch an anti-phishing awareness program. Microsoft Hotmail, Google Gmail, and Yahoo Mail also joined hands to develop trusted email sender schemes.

## 6. Limitations and future research areas

In this study, we adopted the most stringent steps of <sup>fi</sup>ltering to ensure the reliability of the research results. For example, announcements associated with thinly traded stocks and with confounding events that occurred in a <sup>fi</sup>ve day window were <sup>fi</sup>ltered out. As a result, the sample size of our study was not big. Our data were limited to US listed companies and in some subsample analyses, we had 15 announcements (e.g. ‘big’ subsample). The small sample size might limit the generalizability of the results. Readers are advised to use their discretion when interpreting results related to small samples. Nevertheless, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been validated by various parametric and nonparametric tests conducted on two different event windows. Thus, the reliability of the results is assured.

In future, we may include more announcements from recent years to enlarge the sample size. The expanded dataset can enhance the robustness of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Also, we may conduct a separate study in future to analyze the impact of ITC investments in countries other than the US. The extended scope of the research can further enhance the generalizability of the research results. Furthermore, this research can be applied to other type of information security countermeasure investments such as adoption of brand monitoring service and intrusion detection systems. The perception of investors towards the effectiveness of such measures can be analyzed using the event study method. Another area of extension of this research can be to investigate the impact of ITC adoption on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of the <sup>fi</sup>rm in the long term, since investments in IT sometimes exhibit a lagged reaction.

## 7. Conclusion

Our research provided strong evidence in support of investments in ITC. It showed that the <sup>fi</sup>nancial market reacted positively to such news, and rewarded the adopting <sup>fi</sup>rms signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. The return on stock prices due to such announcements was 0.63% in terms of CAR. This result by itself suggested that <sup>fi</sup>rms should invest in ITC, as the positive reaction from the stock market due to ITC investments was quite signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. With regard to <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics, we found that though smaller in magnitude, small <sup>fi</sup>rms received more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive reaction from the investors. With regard to growth potential and credit rating, investors rewarded the high growth potential <sup>fi</sup>rms and high credit rating <sup>fi</sup>rms that endeavored to improve security by adopting ITC. Furthermore, we found that there was a de<sup>fi</sup>nite <sup>fi</sup>rst mover advantage associated with adoption of ITC, as the announcements in the early period gave rise to a change in stock price, that was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in all statistical tests. Finally, we found that the type of countermeasure that was adopted had a differentiating impact as well. Thus, adoption of 2FA, which included digital certi<sup>fi</sup>cates, hardware tokens and one-time passwords, provided an additional layer of protection to customers apart from username and password based authentication. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and positive market reaction to the adoption of 2FA based ITC showed that investors favored sophisticated security measures as it con<sup>fi</sup>rmed their belief that the <sup>fi</sup>rms really cared about the protection of their customers.

## Appendix A. Formulae for parametric test statistics

The cumulative abnormal return (CAR) is computed using the following formula:

$$
C A R = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {t = S _ {1}} ^ {S _ {2}} A R _ {i t}}{N}\tag{A.1}
$$

where $N$ is the number of announcements, and the event window extends from $S _ { 1 }$ to $S _ { 2 }$ days around the event day. To facilitate the computation of the subsequent statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance test, AR is usually transformed to the standardized abnormal return (SAR) which is calculated using the following formulae:

$$
S A R _ {i t} = \frac {A R _ {i t}}{\sqrt {\operatorname{Var} \left(A R _ {i t}\right)}}\tag{A.2}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var} \left(A R _ {i t}\right) = s _ {i} ^ {2} \left[ 1 + \frac {1}{D _ {i}} + \frac {\left(R _ {m t} - R _ {m}\right) ^ {2}}{\sum_ {j = - T _ {1}} ^ {- T _ {2}} \left(R _ {m j} - R _ {m}\right) ^ {2}} \right].\tag{A.3}
$$

In Eq. (A.3), ${ s _ { i } } ^ { 2 }$ is the residual return variance obtained from the regression model for announcement $i , D _ { i }$ is the number of trading days used in the regression model for announcement i and ranges from $T _ { 1 }$ days to $T _ { 2 }$ days before the date of the event announcement, and $R _ { m }$ is the mean return on market index over the estimation period of $D _ { i }$ trading days. The cumulative standardized abnormal return (CSAR), which aggregates all SARs in the sample, is computed by using the formula:

$$
\mathrm{CSAR} = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {t = S _ {1}} ^ {S _ {2}} \frac {\mathrm{SAR} _ {i t}}{\sqrt {S _ {2} - S _ {1} + 1}}.\tag{A.4}
$$

If CSAR is assumed to follow a normal distribution, a one tail Z-test can be used to test its statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. The formula for the test statistic is given below:

$$
Z = \sqrt {\mathrm{NCSAR}}.\tag{A.5}
$$

## Appendix B. Formulae for nonparametric sign and rank tests

The test statistic of the sign Z test is given by the following formula:

$$
Z = \frac {p _ {t} - N r}{\sqrt {N (1 - r) r}}\tag{B.1}
$$

where $p _ { t }$ is the number of positive abnormal returns of stock price obtained on day t, r is the ratio of positive abnormal returns of stock price to the total number of returns calculated in the estimation period, and N is the total number of announcements.

For Corrado's rank test, the abnormal return of stock price is transformed in both regression and event windows using the following formula:

$$
K _ {i t} = \operatorname{rank} \left(A _ {i t}\right)\tag{B.2}
$$

where $A _ { i t }$ is the abnormal return of stock price of announcement i on day $t , K _ { i t } \in [ 1 , 2 , . . . , D + S _ { 2 } - S _ { 1 } ]$ , D is the number of days used in the estimation window, $[ S _ { 1 } , S _ { 2 } ]$ is the event window, and $K _ { i t } = 1$ corresponds to the smallest $A _ { i t } .$ The test statistic is given by the formula:

$$
R = \frac {\sum_ {s = S _ {1}} ^ {S _ {2}} \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left(K _ {i s} - \overline {{K _ {i}}}\right)}{\sqrt {\sum_ {s = S _ {1}} ^ {S _ {2}} S ^ {2} (\overline {{K}})}}\tag{B.3}
$$

$$
\overline {{{K _ {i}}}} = \frac {D + S _ {2} - S _ {1}}{2} + 0. 5.\tag{B.4}
$$

## Appendix C. Formulae for nonparametric bootstrapping

Nonparametric bootstrapping is a resampling technique to obtain an approximate value of standard errors and test-statistics based on a random sample. Let $A = \{ a _ { 1 } , a _ { 2 } , \dots a _ { N } \}$ be a random sample, where N is the sample size. We treat A as the actual population and draw samples from A repeatedly with replacement. In each bootstrap iteration b, we draw N sample data and form the new sample $A ^ { ( b ) }$ where $A ^ { ( b ) } = \{ a _ { 1 } ^ { ( b ) } , a _ { 2 } ^ { ( b ) } , . . . , \dot { a } _ { N } ^ { ( b ) } \}$ . Based on $A ^ { ( b ) }$ , we compute the sample mean $\overline { { A } } ^ { ( b ) }$

By repeating the sampling procedure, we obtain bootstrap samples $b = 1 , 2 , . . . , B$ and the sample means:

$$
\overline {{A}} ^ {(1)}, \overline {{A}} ^ {(2)}, \dots \overline {{A}} ^ {(B)}.
$$

We determine the bootstrap mean as follows:

$$
\overline {{\hat {A}}} = \frac {1}{B} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {B} \overline {{A}} ^ {(i)}.\tag{C.1}
$$

We use the following formula to compute the bootstrap variance:

$$
\hat {s} ^ {2} = \frac {1}{B - 1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {B} \left(\overline {{A}} ^ {(i)} - \overline {{\hat {A}}}\right) ^ {2}.\tag{C.2}
$$

The test statistic is computed as follows:

$$
T = \frac {\overline {{\hat {A}}}}{\hat {s}}.\tag{C.3}
$$
