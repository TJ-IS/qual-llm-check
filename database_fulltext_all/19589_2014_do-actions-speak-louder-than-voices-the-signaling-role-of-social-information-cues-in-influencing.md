# Do actions speak louder than voices? The signaling role of social information cues in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumer purchase decisions

Christy M.K. Cheung <sup>a,</sup>⁎<sup>,1</sup>, Bo Sophia Xiao <sup>b,1</sup>, Ivy L.B. Liu <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Finance & Decision Sciences, Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong

<sup>b</sup> Information Technology Management Department, University of Hawai'i at Mānoa, USA

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 13 May 2014

Keywords: Social commerce Herding Electronic word of mouth Consumer purchase decision Information signaling theory Dual-process theory

## a b s t r a c t

The rise of social media has facilitated consumer social interactions. Many product-focused online social platforms have included design features that can convey more information about product quality as well as the cred ibility of the members of the social community. Drawing on information signaling theory, we empirically examine how the two social information cues frequently found on online social communities, action-based social information (i.e., peer consumer purchase) and opinion-based social information (i.e., peer consumer review), in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer purchase decisions. We also explore the moderating role of consumer characteristics, consumer engagement and consumer expertise. Analyzing panel data (n = 39,897) collected from a popular online beauty community, we found that consumer purchase decisions are indeed in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the two social information cues and that action-based social information is more in<sup>fl</sup>uential than opinion-based social information. Furthermore, our results show that both consumer engagement and consumer expertise play an important moderating role in consumer purchase decisions, albeit in opposite direction: Whereas consumer engagement exerts a positive moderating effect, consumer expertise is found to have a negative moderating effect. This study contributes to existing literature by providing an evaluation on the signaling role of online social community features in consumer purchase decisions. The results offer important and interesting insights to IS research and practice.

## 1. Introduction

The rise of social media has facilitated consumer social interactions. Consumers use online social platforms, including social networking sites, blogs, social shopping communities, and consumer review sites, to communicate opinions about products and exchange purchase experiences [16,37,41]. These online social communities have become important information sources that initiate or simplify consumer purchase decisions. Many online social communities have developed and included design features, such as the display of social information cues that can convey more information about product quality as well as the credibility of the members of the social community. Peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review are two popular social information cues that are commonly adopted to facilitate consumer purchase decision making [15]. Peer consumer purchase provides an informative signal of quality that encourages consumers to follow actions of their predecessors, leading to what is called herd behavior [3]. Peer consumer review is frequently described as electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) communication. This opinion-based information can contain positive or negative statements made by potential, actual, and former consumers about a product or a company through the Internet [27]. Prior literature has shown the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role peer consumer review plays in shaping a consumer's purchase decision [18,38,45].

With the popularity of social media technologies, consumers increasingly engage in online social communities and actively share their experiences with, evaluations of, and opinions on products and brands with other consumers [37]. This may result in vast amount of user-generated information in online social communities and create information overload on consumers [46]. Hence, research is needed to address this challenge and understand how online social information affects consumer purchase decisions. In particular, researchers should explore the relative impact of the two commonly adopted social information cues (i.e., peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review) on consumer purchase decisions, and investigate which type of social information cue is more effective in driving consumer purchase behaviors. A good understanding of the impact of the two major social information cues is essential for developing e-marketing strategies for effectively managing consumer online social interactions.

In recent years, we have witnessed an explosion of literature focusing on the effectiveness of online peer consumer review (electronic word of mouth, or eWOM) on consumer behavior [24,25,38,40]. Similar to traditional word of mouth studies, research on online consumer reviews usually considers them a type of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence affecting consumer purchase decisions. Existing studies have shown that, as a form of eWOM communication, online consumer reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer product attitude, purchase intention, and actual purchase [34,45]. In contrast, we found that the second type of social information cue, peer consumer purchase, has received limited attention in the Information Systems (IS) literature. The few available studies adopted information cascades theory [7] to explain the herd behavior observed in information technology (IT) adoption [30,36,56]. To the best of our knowledge, little research has empirically investigated how publicly observable online social information about other consumers' actions (i.e., peer consumer purchase) affects a consumer's purchase decision. Furthermore, the relative impact of action-based social information (i.e., peer consumer purchase) and opinion-based social information (i.e., peer consumer review) on consumer purchase decisions remain unknown.

Online social interactions involve two parties: the communicator (sender) and the receiver. The actual impact of the information received through online social interactions indeed may vary from person to person. The same content may engender very different responses in different receivers [58], depending on receivers' experiences, engagement, and expertise. In the eWOM communication literature, researchers have found that consumer engagement and consumer expertise moderate the effect of peer consumer reviews on consumer purchase decisions [34,42,44]. However, how consumer characteristics moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of peer consumer purchase on consumer purchase decisions is not very well understood in the existing literature.

To address the gaps in the literature, we draw on information signaling theory to explain the relative impact of two social information cues, action-based social information (i.e., peer consumer purchase) and opinion-based social information (i.e., peer consumer review) on consumer purchase decisions. In doing so, we hope to shed light on the evaluation of the two different types of social information cues in online social communities. In addition, we examine how consumer characteristics, consumer engagement and consumer expertise, moderate the impact of social information cues on consumer purchase decisions. The research questions for this study are as follows:

1. How will the two social information cues, peer consumer purchase (i.e., action-based social information) and peer consumer review (i.e., opinion-based social information) influence consumer purchase decision?

2. Which type of social information cues will have stronger impact on consumer purchase decision?

3. How will consumer engagement and consumer expertise influence the relationship between the two social information cues and consumer purchase decision?

In the present study, we address these research questions by gathering and analyzing a unique data set from a popular online beauty shopping community in Asia. To summarize, we expect that the current study makes four signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions. First, previous work has primarily focused on the effectiveness of online peer consumer reviews in traditional online communities. Our paper examines consumer purchase decision in a new social media arena, online social communities. Second, existing studies have empirically shown that online consumer reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uenced consumer purchase decisions. However, the second type of social information cue, peer consumer purchase, has received little attention in the IS literature. In this study, we explore the relative impact of the two commonly adopted online social information cues (i.e., peer consumer reviews and peer consumer purchases) on consumer purchase decisions, and investigate which type of social information cue is more effective in driving consumer purchase behaviors. Third, we notice that how consumer characteristics moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online social information cues on consumer purchase decisions is not very well understood in the existing literature. This study attempts to show the moderating role of consumer engagement and consumer expertise in consumer purchase decisions. Finally, the evidence found in previous studies has primarily been derived from data collected through a subjective approach (e.g., via survey or experimental design). In this study, we crawl data from a real social community and investigate the relative impact of online social information cues on consumer purchase decisions.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, we present the theoretical foundation for this study and introduce our research model. We then describe the methodology of the study and present the results of data analysis. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the theoretical and practical contributions of our work. We also address the limitations and future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Online social information

The growing availability and popularity of online social platforms (e.g., social networking sites, online discussion forums, blogs, and online shopping communities) have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased the importance of online social information as a market force [26]. Consumers can easily learn from and be affected by action-based social information (e.g., peer consumer purchase) and/or opinion-based social information (e.g., peer consumer review) from online social platforms [14].

Following others' action (or the so-called herd behavior) has been well-documented in a number of diverse domains [48], including stock market [50], political choice [4], technology adoption [54], and consumer preferences [15]. Information cascades, sanctions on deviants, positive payoff externalities and conformity preference are the primary mechanisms explaining herd behavior in the existing literature [24]. In recent years, herding has also been adopted by IS researchers to explain online auctions and software downloading [24,51]. These studies empirically showed that, given their imperfect knowledge, consumers were strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the information inferred from others' behavior (e.g., bid numbers and download counts).

Opinion-based social information in the form of eWOM communication can take place in various channels. For example, consumers can post their opinions, comments, and reviews of products on weblogs (e.g. xanga.com), discussion forums (e.g. zapak.com), review websites (e.g. Epinions.com), e-bulletin board systems, newsgroups, and social networking sites (e.g. facebook.com). Prior research has demonstrated an association between eWOM and product sales/revenue and mostly explained this association through either awareness effects or persuasive effects [21]. A considerable number of studies have further showed that only the volume of consumer reviews is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with product sales [13,20,24,38].

## 2.2. Information signaling theory

Akerlof [1] argued that quality signaling is a solution for information asymmetry. When facing a market interaction, different parties often have different amount of information regarding the transaction [52]. For example, sellers know the quality of their products or services, but buyers are not fully informed about the true quality of a product prior to purchase. One solution to this problem is for sellers to send prepurchase signals about their quality through brand name, price, advertising expenditures, and warranty [31]. Signaling theory has been applied in consumer research to explain how consumers rely on signals (e.g., informational cues that are actions or artifacts of businesses) to form expectations about quality [9].

In recent years, researchers have begun to apply signaling theory to explore how IT features affect consumer participation in the online environment. For example, Li et al. [35] drew on signaling theory to develop a typology of Internet auction features and examine their signaling roles. They further examined empirically how different types of features (quality indicators) affected consumer participation and bidding decisions. Benlian and Hess [6] used signaling theory as a theoretical foundation to conceptualize the signaling role of IT features and their effects on trust and participation in online communities. Amblee and Bui [2] investigated the effect of eWOM communication among a closed community of book readers. They suggested that, as a collective signal of reputation, eWOM could be used to convey the reputation of products, brands, and complementary goods.

## 2.3. Dual-process theory

Over the years, researchers have used dual-process theory of human information processing, including the Elaboration likelihood model (ELM) [47] and Heuristic–Systematic model (HSM) [10], to study how information processing behavior can lead to decision outcomes [49,53,57]. The two models provide similar mechanisms in explaining information processing strategies through central/systematic route and/or peripheral/heuristic route. The central/systematic processing involves careful examination of the message (e.g., the nature of arguments in the message), whereas the peripheral/heuristic processing uses environmental cues of the message (e.g., the subject matter of the message) to decide whether to accept the message or not.

Cheung and Thadani [16] note that ELM and HSM are the two widely used theoretical perspectives in studies of online social information. In addition, consumer expertise and consumer engagement (involvement) are usually postulated as moderators affecting whether consumers adopt central or peripheral route processing when making purchase decisions. For example, Park and Lee [46] found that when consumers had higher level of engagement, they had greater motivation to engage in effortful cognitive activity through the central route. When consumers had lower level of engagement, they tended to rely on peripheral cues during information processing. Park and Kim [44] found that consumer expertise played an important role in determining the impact of eWOM content (e.g., type and volume of online consumer review) on purchase intention. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they found that the impact of review type on purchase intention was stronger for experts than for novices while the effect of review volume on purchase intention was stronger for novices than experts.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 1 depicts the research model for this study. We posit that online social information cues will have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct effects on consumer purchase decision, with action-based social information (i.e., peer consumer purchase) being a more powerful predictor than opinion-based social information (i.e., peer consumer review). We also expect that consumer characteristics moderate the effect of online social information on consumer purchase decision.

## 3.1. Peer consumer purchase

Park and Lessig [43] posit that consumers usually have imperfect knowledge of the true value of a product and thus they tend to observe and consider others' purchasing behavior in their own buying. Similar to other consumer behavioral information, action-based social information (i.e., peer consumer purchase) usually carries only the discrete signals expressed by actions of others, but not the reasons behind their actions [24]. Traditional consumer behavior research reveals that other consumers' purchasing behavior provides an informative signal of quality that encourages consumers to follow actions of their predecessors and make purchase decisions [8,32,51]. Recent research in IS discipline has also empirically shown the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between product popularity (e.g., the number of product sales, and the number of software download) and consumer purchase decisions [15,24,28], as popularity is believed by consumers to be an indicator of quality. In the current study, we also believe that prior purchases made by peer consumers in online social communities send a strong signal to later consumers about product quality (with greater number of peer consumer purchases indicating better quality), and that this information cue will positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer's purchase decision.

H1. Peer consumer purchase will positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer purchase decision.

## 3.2. Peer consumer review

Peer consumer review on online social platforms represents a form of eWOM communication. The relationship between eWOM and consumer purchase behavior is well-established in prior literature [17, 42,45]. Particularly, the volume of peer consumer reviews is consistently found to be an important factor in affecting consumer purchase decision [2]. For instance, Duan et al. [24] found that the increase in eWOM volume drove product awareness or accessibility and thereby put the product in the choice set of consumers. Amblee and Bui [2] further suggested that the number of eWOM indicates product quality. In the current study, we focus on the impact of the volume of peer consumer reviews (eWOM) on consumer purchase decision in the context of online social communities. This information cue not only conveys the existence of the product (thus creating an awareness effect), but also re<sup>fl</sup>ects certain degree of product quality. Thus, we expect that peer consumer review will enhance consumer purchase decision – the greater the number of product reviews by peer consumers, the more likely a consumer will make a purchase decision.

## 3.3. Action-based versus opinion-based social information

The Internet and social media channels provide plentiful information about other consumers' choices and product popularity. Actionbased social information (i.e., peer consumer purchase) provides a strong signal of product quality that encourages consumers to follow others' purchasing behavior [8,32,51]. Opinion-based social information in the form of eWOM (i.e., peer consumer review) has also been shown to serve as a signal of product quality that drives product sales [2]. While these two social information cues are commonly found in online social communities, their relative impact on product sales remains unknown. For instance, there is no empirical investigation on the effects of the two different types of information cues in online social communities. When facing vast amount of information, individuals tend to base their decision on information in which they have more con<sup>fi</sup>dence [14]. As posited by Nock (2008), actions (rather than words) are frequently used as a means of in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the behavior of others, as actions carry “greater social and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c currency” than do words (p. 5). In everyday life, what people do is of greater concern than what people say (i.e., cheap talk) (Haiman, 1998) – while people are often asked about what they think and feel about different issues, their actions are ultimately held up as the more accurate measure of what they really think or feel (Nock, 2008). By and large, observable actions (i.e., costly signals) are considered a more credible signaling mechanism when compared to pure words (i.e., costless signals) (Lee, 2007). Thus, consistent with Heider's (1958) supposition that actions speak louder than words and the existing empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings (e.g., Inoue, Kilian, & Kiraz, 2009; Wertz & German, 2007), we believe that action-based social information cue carries a stronger signal of product quality than does opinion-based social information, thus exerting a stronger impact on consumer purchase decision.

H3. Peer consumer purchase will exert stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumer purchase decision than will peer consumer review.

## 3.4. Consumer engagement

Consumer engagement refers to the frequency a consumer participates in an online social community (e.g., in the form of sharing product-related experience and providing ratings on products). Results of a meta-analysis has revealed that engagement (involvement) is an important in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factor in information processing [29]. Whereas highly involved individuals are more likely to engage in effortful processing of issue-relevant arguments through the central route, individuals characterized by low involvement tend to rely on peripheral cues (e.g., environmental characteristic of the message, attractiveness of the source, mere number of arguments presented) during information processing. For instance, examining the impact of group membership on persuasion, Mackie and Queller [39] found that individuals who were more involved with a group were more motivated to process group-relevant information carefully and effortfully. In the context of online social communication, prior studies have also demonstrated empirically the role of consumer engagement in moderating the effect of eWOM on consumer decision process [23,33,34]. Accordingly, we believe that highly engaged consumers will be less likely to rely on peripheral cues (i.e., peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review) when making purchase decisions.

H4. Consumer engagement will negatively moderate the relationship between peer consumer purchase and consumer purchase decision.

H5. Consumer engagement will negatively moderate the relationship between peer consumer review and consumer purchase decision.

## 3.5. Consumer expertise

Prior studies found that individuals with different levels of expertise tended to use different information-processing routes to process persuasive information [51,53]. In general, knowledgeable consumers (with high expertise) were less susceptible to interpersonal consumer in<sup>fl</sup>uence and tended to rely on their own experiences for purchase decisions [5]. In the context of online social communication, Park and Kim [44] also found that consumer expertise played an important moderating role in determining the impact of eWOM content on consumer purchase decision. In similar vein, in this study, we believe that consumers with high level of expertise have more con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their own decisions and behavior. As such, they tend to rely on their own knowledge to make purchase decisions and will be less likely to use heuristic cues for information processing. Consequently, for knowledgeable consumers, the impact of online social information (both peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review) on consumer purchase decision will be weakened.

H6. Consumer expertise will negatively moderate the relationship between peer consumer purchase and consumer purchase decision.

H7. Consumer expertise will negatively moderate the relationship between peer consumer review and consumer purchase decision.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Data collection

The data for this study were collected during the month of December 2010 from a popular online beauty forum (hereafter referred to as Forum) in Asia, which provides a platform for members to learn about beauty products, to share their experience related to beauty products, and to interact with other beauty enthusiasts.

The Forum organizes beauty products by brands and provides basic information about each product. Members of the Forum can post the experience that they have had with the use of any product (that is available at the Forum), provide a rating (from 1 to 7) on the product, reply to other members' posts, recommend a member's post to others, and choose to follow other members on the forum. They can also share the products that they have purchased by adding products to their buy-lists.

The present study focuses on the discussion of branded products, which include all products under the same brand name. Individuals who have explicitly added a particular brand as their favorite brand are considered members of that brand community, and hence the unit of analysis for this study is a member of a brand community. We aim to explore how a member's purchase decision is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other members' product reviews and product choices in a brand community and how such in<sup>fl</sup>uence is moderated by the member's level of engagement and expertise in this brand community.

## 4.2. Operationalization of constructs

The constructs included in the research model are operationalized as follows.

## 4.2.1. Peer consumer review

A member of a brand community can rate a product (of a particular brand) while sharing her or his experience about the product. S/he can also follow other community members whose posts or ratings s/he <sup>fi</sup>nds useful. As the provision of rating is optional, not everyone that a member follows would have ratings on products in a particular brand. Therefore, opinion-based social information, peer consumer review, is operationalized as the total number of ratings<sup>2</sup> (on products in a particular brand) provided by individuals who are followed by a member of the brand community, or the member's reference persons. To provide support for the hypothesized causal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of peer consumer review on consumer purchase decision, we only included ratings that had been provided by a member's reference persons before a product of this brand was added to the member's buy-list (see Table 1 for an illustration).

4.2.2. Peer consumer purchase

A community member can add a product to her or his buy-list to show that s/he has already bought the product. S/he can also learn the past purchase actions of her or his reference persons by viewing the products included in their buy-lists. Thus, action-based social information, peer consumer purchase, is operationalized as the total number of products (of a particular brand) in the buy-lists of a community member's reference persons. Similar care was taken to ensure that we took into account only products of a particular brand added to the reference persons' buy-lists before a product of this brand was added to the member's own buy-list (see Table 1 for illustration).

## 4.2.3. Consumer engagement

The Forum displays statistics on the number of experience-sharing messages that a member has posted to the Forum. As a member who shares her or his experience about products in a particular brand fre quently is likely to be an active member of the brand community, the moderator, consumer engagement, is operationalized as the number of experience-sharing messages that a member has posted to the Forum about products in a particular brand.

## 4.2.4. Consumer expertise

The Forum also provides statistics on the number of recommenda tions that a member receives on the experience-sharing messages s/he has posted to the Forum. As a member whose posts receive a large number of recommendations from peer members in a particular brand is likely to be an expert of the brand, the moderator, consumer expertise, is operationalized as the total number of recommendations a member has received on the experience-sharing messages s/he has posted to the Forum about products in a particular brand.

## 5. Data analysis

Table 2 summarizes the descriptive statistics for each variable. In this study, we adopted an empirical analysis approach and collected objective usage data from 39,897 members in a social community.

Consumer purchase decision is represented by using a count variable summing up all the products (of the same brand) that are added to a buy-list. Poisson regression and negative binomial regression are often used to analyze count data. Poisson regression model makes the very restrictive assumption that the mean of the dependent variable equals the variance [19]. If the variance is greater than the mean, the data is said to be over-dispersed, which can lead to in<sup>fl</sup>ation of the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t chisquare test and the overestimation of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of predictors [19]. An approach to over-dispersion is the use of the negative binomial regression model [19]. As shown in Table 2, for the num of products in a member's buy-list, the variance (2.604) is greater than the mean (0.35), suggesting that the data is over-dispersed. Therefore, in this study, negative binomial regression (using SPSS) was adopted to test the hypothesized relationships.

A negative binomial regression analysis was performed with consumer purchase decision as dependent variable, peer consumer review and peer consumer purchase as independent variables, and consumer engagement and consumer expertise as moderator variables. A comparison of the two β coef<sup>fi</sup>cients reveals that peer consumer purchase is the more in<sup>fl</sup>uential predictor than peer consumer review (Wald t = 4.87, $\mathrm { d f } = 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 \AA )$ ), suggesting that the greater the number of product ratings provided and prior purchases made by a member's reference persons in a brand community, the more likely the member will purchase products of that brand. Moreover, a member will refer more to her or his reference persons' prior purchase behavior than their opinions when making her or his own purchase decisions. Thus, H1, H2, and H3 are supported.

In addition, Table 3 reveals that, as hypothesized, consumer expertise exerts a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative moderating effect on the relationship between the two predictors (i.e., peer consumer review and peer consumer purchase) and consumer purchase decision $( \beta = - 0 . 0 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 ; \beta =$ −0.006, p b 0.01). The results thus support H6 and H7.

Further, the results of our analysis reveal that, contrary to expectation, consumer engagement exerts a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive moderating effect on the relationship between peer consumer review and consumer purchase decision $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ as well as that between peer consumer purchase and consumer purchase decision $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , suggesting that, as members become more actively involved in a brand community (indicated by the number of experience-sharing messages they have posted on products of the brand), their purchase decisions will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their reference persons' product ratings and product purchases to a greater extent. Thus, the results do not support H4 or H5.

## 6. Discussion and implications

In this study, we seek to examine the relative effects of the two social information cues (i.e., peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review) on consumer purchase decisions and the potential moderating role of consumer engagement and consumer expertise. The results of this study provide notable insights for both academic researchers and practitioners.

## 6.1. Major findings

By analyzing our panel data collected from a popular online beauty community, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that both action-based social information and opinion-based social information have a strong impact on consumer purchase decision, with peer consumer purchase being a more important predictor than peer consumer review. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with the conventional thinking that “actions speak louder than words”. Other consumers' previous purchase actions tend to be more in<sup>fl</sup>uential than their eWOM. This study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that people tend to make a decision on the basis of information in which they have more con<sup>fi</sup>dence [14], and that action-based information (i.e., peer consumer purchase), which carries a stronger signal about the product quality, exerts a significantly stronger impact on consumer purchase decision than does opinion-based information (i.e., peer consumer review).

Our results also shed light on the moderating role of consumer characteristics in the relationship between social information cues and consumer purchase decisions. Both consumer engagement and consumer expertise are found to be statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, though they work in opposite directions. Consumer expertise has a negative moderating effect on the relationship between the two social information cues and consumer purchase decision. In other words, consumers with a higher level of expertise in a particular brand are less likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by others' opinions or actions. Our study thus con<sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>ndings from earlier work on information processing that receivers' ability determines whether they prefer to reduce mental processing effort. For instance, Chaiken et al. [11] showed that consumers who did not have much experience and knowledge about a brand were less motivated to process additional information and that they tended to simplify decision heuristics by following others' actions and opinions in their purchase decision.

Conversely, consumer engagement is found to have a positive moderating effect, suggesting that, the more engaging consumers are in an online social community, the more likely it is for them to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by peer consumers' opinions and actions. A potential explanation of the unexpected positive moderating effect of consumer engagement is that highly involved members tend to spend a large amount time sharing product-related experience in an online social community. Such engagement facilitates the internalization of an identity as a member of the online social community. According to social identity theory [55], individuals who categorize themselves as members of a group and perceive that the group is important to them are more likely to internalize group attributes and behave in line with group norms. Through frequent social interaction with other members in an online social community, highly involved members have greater exposure to shared social comparative information from other fellow members. They also have more chances to learn about the group and appropriate ways of behavior. This exposure can cultivate a sense of psychological belonging, motivating the members to adopt the norms of the community as relevant standards for their own behavior. As a result, compared to those less engaged in activities of an online social community, highly involved members are more likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other members' opinion and actions.

## 6.2. Research implications

This study makes several important contributions to future research. First, this study provides strong empirical support for existing <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the impact that online social information has on consumers' purchase decisions [14]. In addition, we draw from information signaling theory to explicitly compare the relative power of action-based social information cue and opinion-based information cue in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumer decision-making. The results empirically establish the former as the more important predictor, suggesting that actions do speak louder than words. This study has also extended information signaling theory to online social communities.

Second, whereas prior research in online social information has generally focused on the effects of the stimulus (e.g., review ratings, review volume, quality), this study contributes to this body of literature by integrating dual-process theory in the investigation into the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of consumer characteristics in moderating the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review.

Finally, our study extends existing research in terms of the method used. In the last decade, there has been an extensive body of research conducted on the power of eWOM, and a majority of these studies are survey/experiment-based, in which respondents were asked to report the impact of eWOM on their purchase decisions [17,42,45]. In recent years, there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase in the number of studies using secondary panel data collected from the Internet and drawing inferences from online product reviews, product characteristics, and consumers' actual behaviors [16,24,25,58]. In cases where panel data sets are used, the focal dependent variable is usually product sales/revenue [12,14], rather than individual consumers' purchase decision. To our knowledge, this is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical studies to use panel data set to evaluate the causal in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online social information cues (peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review) on individual consumers' purchase decision-making. The present study thus enhances our understanding of the impact of online social interactions at a micro level, <sup>fi</sup>lling a gap in existing research to date.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

This study has important managerial implications for online social communities, which can use these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to improve online social community designs and to develop online marketing strategies for effectively managing consumer online social interactions.

With the rapid development and penetration of social media technologies, consumers increasingly rely on online social information to make purchase decisions. Understanding how online social information affects consumer purchase decisions is vitally important to <sup>fi</sup>rms that use information about consumers' prior purchases and reviews to promote their products and services. The results of this study can be used to develop guidelines for creating more effective online marketing strategies. For instance, the <sup>fi</sup>nding that peer consumer purchase is more in-<sup>fl</sup>uential than peer consumer review suggests that action-based information cue might be perceived by individual consumers as more credible than opinion-based information cue. Therefore, in addition to facilitating eWOM communication by allowing consumers to post product reviews/ratings online, <sup>fi</sup>rms are also advised to facilitate consumers to report past purchase behavior or usage activity on their websites.

With different types of online social information (e.g., reviews, ratings, recommendations, and prior purchases) becoming increasingly available to consumers, such information may impose a heavy cognitive burden on consumers, resulting in a decrease in perceived informativeness of online social information [45]. To prevent consumers from experiencing information overload, <sup>fi</sup>rms are encouraged to provide consumers personalized social information based on consumer characteristics (e.g., personal information consumers input when registering membership, statistics on consumer activities at the website). The results of this study show that, whereas peer consumer purchase and peer consumer review are more in<sup>fl</sup>uential when consumers have relatively little product-related expertise, the two social information cues are more powerful in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumers who are active participants of a brand community. A strategy then would be to expose those consumers who are more active in a brand community and/or those who are less experienced in a brand to other consumers' product reviews/ ratings as well as reports of past buyers' purchase actions. Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that these are where this information will be most effective.

## 6.4. Limitations

There are a number of limitations to this research. First, our data comes from an online beauty community, which focuses on experience products and has primariy females as members. Thus, caution is advised in generalizing the results of this study to other settings. Second, we sampled only 8 brands of beauty products in our data analysis. Future studies could sample a larger set of products in order to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that our results hold. Third, this study examines the moderating role of two receiver characteristics, consumer engagement and consumer expertise. Future study could explore how other communicator and receiver characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence the impact of online social information cues on consumer purchase behaviors. Similarly, the total number of ratings and the total number of products purchased are used to measure peer consumer review and peer consumer purchase representatively. Future study could use other variables, such as the quality and valence of consumer reviews, to measure the two social information cues. Finally, in this study, the measures for peer consumer purchase, peer consumer review, consumer expertise, consumer engagement and consumer purchase behavior are quantitative surrogates and not direct measures of these constructs. Future studies could complement such objective approach with qualitative analysis, which may provide additional insights on how consumer purchase decisions are in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced by different types of online social information cues.
