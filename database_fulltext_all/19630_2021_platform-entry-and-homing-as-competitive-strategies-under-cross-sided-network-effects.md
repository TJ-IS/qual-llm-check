Cheng-Han Wu $^{a,*}$ wuchan@mail.ncku.edu.tw, Netnapha Chamnisampan $^{a}$

$^{a}$ Department of Industrial and Information Management, National Cheng Kung University,

## Abstract

In a competitive environment, determining when to enter the market and what homing policies should be adopted are crucial strategic decisions for platform-based businesses. In this study, we investigate the interactions between two platforms competing in two-sided markets with cross-sided network effects and determine their prices under different entry and homing strategies. We derive the equilibrium demands of two-sided markets for platforms and the equilibrium prices for two competing platforms. We further conduct a comparative analysis among scenarios with different combinations of homing and entry strategies. We find that early-entry platforms will preserve high margins in large early markets, where fierce price competition occurs once their rivals enter these markets. However, early entry is not always beneficial for platforms because the market-cannibalization effect emerges in the subsequent period. Furthermore, this effect will be amplified by the platforms' homing and entry strategies. In addition, cross-sided network effects lead buyers, sellers, and platforms to prefer a multi-homing policy, which is beneficial for society as a whole. Managerial insights are also provided for platform-based businesses.

Keywords: platform competition; market entry; platform homing; two-sided markets; cross-sided network effects

## 1. Introduction

Modern businesses, such as Alibaba, Facebook, Google, eBay, Microsoft, and Visa, are currently engaging in “platform economy.” Schenker [54] reported that seven companies out of 10 had begun to focus on adopting platform business strategies in 2018. Unlike traditional business models, platform-based businesses are commonly linked in two-sided markets with different customers, where one side is composed of sellers, and the other side is composed of buyers. The interest of customers on one side of a platform grows as the market size of customers on the other side grows, which is called the “cross-sided network effects” [19, 66]. In addition, sellers pursue long-term profitability and thus behave more strategically than buyers [2]. These two types of customers are inherently different but mutually beneficial to each other.

The homing policy is a strategic means by which platforms compete [6, 8]. Two homing policies are commonly applied in practice: a “single-homing” policy under which customers are restricted from joining one platform only and a “multi-homing” policy under which customers can join more than one platform. In practice, a buyer can use available platforms to acquire information and then make a single purchase through a platform. It is plausible that a buyer has no incentive to repeatedly purchase from different platforms at any time [e.g., 5, 43, 47], especially when a consumer’s budget is limited for any purchase [64, 48] or when repeated purchases do not generate cumulative utility. According to a market survey of food delivery platforms reported by McKinsey & Company [28], consumers are strictly to new-delivery platforms: “Once customers sign up, 80 percent never or rarely leave for another platform, creating a strong winner-take-all dynamic.” This result provides evidence of the plausibility of single-homing on the consumer side and also supports the motivation of platform competition. As a result, we consider a common scenario wherein a buyer makes a single purchase from one platform, and platforms may opt to adopt a single- or multi-homing policy on the seller side.

In terms of market-entry issues, order-of-entry choices have been shown to have significant impacts on firm profits [17, 63] and also to affect the decisions of an entrant made in the early- and the post-entry periods [16, 34]. There are two possible scenarios: One is synchronous entry, under which platforms enter markets at the same time. For example, two leading dating platforms, Tinder and Coffee Meets Bagel, were both launched in 2012 and competed with each other [46]. The other is asynchronous entry, under which a platform enters the market earlier than its rivals to capture early adopters. For example, Swiggy, an online catering platform founded in 2014 in India [59], was challenged by Uber Eats, which accessed the Indian market in 2017 [1]. Uber Eats started with free deliveries for new customers. At the same time, Swiggy cut delivery rates for everyone, and these two online delivery food companies increased discounts to win the market share [55]. However, this action may intensify the strength of competition in the subsequent period and thus may not necessarily be beneficial for these platforms $[56, 66]$ . Staykova and Damsgaard $[57]$ empirically studied the entry strategies of digital payment platforms and found that an entrant platform, “Paii,” had to differentiate its strategies to gain market leadership.

Parker et al. [45] pointed out that both Uber and Lyft seek to discourage multi-homing as a cardinal competitive tactic for platforms, in that they can exclusively access essential assets and generate profits from their core business. Zhu and Iansiti [66] indicated that the negative effect of multi-homing might worsen as more rivals enter the markets. Thus, Amazon incentivizes sellers to sell exclusively on its platform by adjusting fees and services. A conventional view is that new entrant platforms can benefit by utilizing multi-homing to grab the markets from incumbent platforms. In Table 1, we summarize some practical examples and the corresponding homing and entry scenarios, thus revealing the practical values of this study. These examples abound, and the platform market entry strategy and homing policy are closely related, yet a limited number of studies have investigated these two strategies simultaneously. Hence, this study applies these strategies to B2C platforms who compete on their pricing decisions and face both seller- and buyer-type customers. The buyers use available platforms and make a single purchase through a platform. The sellers behave more strategically than the buyers. Under the single-homing policy, the sellers have to decide which platform to join; however, under the multi-homing policy, the sellers can join the platforms that yield positive utilities. For clarity, we show our research framework and the issues addressed in this work in Figure 1. Under this research framework, we propose to answer the following research questions: (i) Should a platform enter the markets at the same time or earlier than a rival when expecting price competition and market cannibalization? (ii) What homing policy is preferred by platforms under different entry scenarios? (iii) How do the cross-sided network effects impact platform strategies and prices?

## Figure 1. Research framework and the issues addressed in this work

To address these questions, we develop a stylized model wherein two platforms compete in terms of pricing in two-sided (sells and buyers) markets with cross-sided network effects, where sellers behave more strategically than buyers by considering possible future platform options. The key findings of this work are summarized as follows: (1) Asynchronous entry can alleviate the intensity of price competition raised by the single-homing policy. However, under the multi-homing policy, asynchronous entry causes the first-mover platform to behave more aggressively by lowering its prices. (2) Counter to the conventional view that platforms seek to discourage multi-homing in order to exclusively access their essential assets [45, 8, 66], we find that asynchronous entry causes multi-homing more likely to be beneficial to both platforms, especially when their competitiveness decreases (e.g., under a strong market cannibalization effect for the first-mover platform) or when price competition is too intense (e.g., under strong cross-sided network effects). (3) When cross-sided network effects increase, asynchronous entry is preferred by the first-mover platform regardless of homing policy. However, it is not preferred by the late-entrant platform under the multi-homing policy. Contrary to conventional wisdom, this result indicates that multi-homing does not necessarily benefit new entrant platforms. (4) Cross-sided network effects on both buyer- and seller-sided markets can increase surpluses.

Asynchronous entry under the multi-homing policy is commonly beneficial to buyers and sellers but not to platforms, which is consistent with the findings of Zhu and Iansiti [66] and Belleflamme and Peitz [8]. However, when cross-sided network effects are significant, the multi-homing policy is likely to be preferred by buyers, sellers, and platforms and thus is advantageous to society as a whole.

## 2. Literature review

Our study is primarily associated with three streams in the existing literature: (i) pricing in two-sided markets, (ii) platform competition, and (iii) strategic choices for platform-based markets. Previous works in the first stream on two-sided markets have focused on pricing strategies related to platform-based markets [e.g., 44, 57, 29, 13]. Parker and Van Alstyne [44] studied platform pricing for two groups of customers (end consumers and content providers) in regard to the benefits of network externality. At a lower benefit of externality, platforms may have positive prices in both groups of customers, at a high benefit of externality, platforms may allow one side access through low charges. Similarly, Liu and Serfes [39] examined price discrimination related to joining fees for customers in two-sided markets and derived the discriminatory price. They found that price discrimination yields high profits when the marginal cost is weakly associated with network externality. Cachon et al. [13] discussed the pricing policies of a service platform (e.g., the Uber platform) and compared pricing schemes by setting provider wages and buyer prices. They found that dynamic pricing increases the benefits to both riders and drivers as well as the profit for the platform. Similarly, Afeche et al. [2] examined strategic behaviors in a ride-sharing platform composed of both riders and drivers. They found that location-specific or origin-destination pricing can affect the drivers' choices. However, these previous works mainly focused on pricing issues in monopolistic models, and only a few analytical studies have explored the strategic decisions made by platforms. Hence, our study is aimed toward bridging this gap by proposing a game-theoretic model featuring platform preferences for entry and homing strategies.

The second stream of extant literature investigated platform competition in two-sided markets, where some of them considered homing policies. Rochet and Tirole [50] investigated pricing strategies in two-sided markets in terms of platform competition. They found that competitive prices depend on the extent of multi-homing on the seller side, i.e., payment card platforms prefer to serve cardholders who are loyal to the merchants, leading platforms to offer lower transaction fees to merchants in order to attract them. Armstrong [6] examined different two-sided market structures under platform competition conditions with network externality and generated insights into the fees of two group agents under different homing strategies, i.e., where both sides were single-homing and in a case where one side was single homing and another one was multi-homing. We follow the works of Rochet and Tirole [50] and Armstrong [6] by considering the concept of cross-sided network effects in which the utilities of either group vary depending on the market size of the other group. Economides and Katsamakas [18] investigated competition between two different operation-system platforms; specifically, they compared different pricing strategies for a proprietary and an open-source platform. Li et al. [37] investigated two platforms competing for both sellers and buyers. They showed that if the platforms are perfectly substituted, they should achieve differentiation levels, which allows them to charge joining fees to the sellers with higher prices than their rival platforms. Anderson Jr et al. [5] formulated strategic platform choices related to technology investment in three distinct settings: monopoly, price-setting duopoly, and pricing duopoly. They focused on the trade-offs between improving a platform's core technology and stimulating cross-sided network effects. We follow Anderson Jr et al. [5] by considering a common case where each member in the buyer market makes a single purchase, indicating that any buyer is single-homing. Kim and Tse [32] examined the strategic choices of two competing search platforms and indicated that the equilibrium strategies for search platforms depend on the information available on the Internet, the searchers' degree of patience related to waiting for information, and the search quality.

Li et al. [38] developed and analyzed a platform ecosystem to examine the impacts of platform performance investments and found that platform decisions are determined by the investment efficiency and royalty rate. Mantena et al. [41] considered a situation where a developer provides valued complements for platforms and examined an exclusive contract between developers and platforms. They showed that the likelihood of an exclusive contract differs at various levels of platform market maturity. Halaburda and Yehezkel [27] discussed homing strategies and asymmetric information under competition between an incumbent and an entrant platform and found that the multi-homing strategy is likely to be beneficial to the incumbent platform. However, they did not endogenously examine the entry strategy. Recently, Zhu and Iansiti [66] specified the critical drivers of platform success to include the strength of the network effect and platform strategies intended to prevent disintermediation. We include these two key factors in our models by investigating the impacts of cross-sided network effects on strategies. Notably, only a few studies $[18, 38]$ endogenously examine the price decisions on the customer side, but all of these studies limit the single-merchant customer setting. Most of the relevant studies overlook endogenous transaction prices on the customer side to highlight the platform pricing decisions for a mass of customers, similar to this study. Moreover, we follow studies considering the plausibility of the assumption that platforms endogenously determine joining fees for sellers but allow buyers to use the platforms for free or with an exogenous cost $[37, 5, 2, 43]$ .

In the last stream of literature, we focused on market-entry choices and homing policies. Market-entry strategies have been discussed for non-platform-based markets [e.g., 3, 29, 10], but only a few analytical studies have discussed entry sequences for platform-based markets [e.g., 14, 20, 57, 65]. Katz and Shapiro [29] and Anderson [3] revealed that network effects increase the difficulties associated with the ability of a second-mover to take over market leadership. Staykova and Damsgaard [57] quantitatively analyzed expansion and entry strategies for digital payment platforms, and their empirical results revealed that the timing of expansion and entry are equally crucial for platform development. Caillaud and Jullien [14] investigated different applications of two-sided markets and generated the optimal fees levied on the two sides of a platform by constructing a model with cross-sided network externalities. Caillaud and Jullien [14] discussed price competition between an incumbent and entry platform on the seller side, but the endogenous entry sequence was overlooked. Eisenmann et al. [20] introduced “platform envelopment” by considering market entry issues and the interactions between different platform markets, where they showed that late entrance platforms may overcome early-entry platforms because of the indirect network effects from other platform markets. Zhu and Iansiti [65] studied platform competition between incumbent and entrant platforms and indicated that the entrant platform’s success depends on buyers’ evaluations of future applications and the strength of indirect network effects. More specifically, they discovered that an entrant game console platform could overcome the incumbent platform with a technology installed-base advantage. Corresponding to the findings of Zhu and Iansiti [65], our study also reveals that early entry is not necessarily a dominant strategy for platforms. However, Zhu and Iansiti [65] and Halaburda and Yehezkel [27] mainly focused on the interaction between incumbent and entrant platforms, where the entry sequence of platforms was exogenous; by contrast, we argue that a platform can endogenously determine their entry sequence. Belleflamme and Peitz [8] studied the influences of homing policies on platforms, buyers, and sellers and revealed that the single-homing policy is not necessarily more profitable for platforms than the multi-homing policy.

We summarize the models and issues discussed in the relevant literature in Table 2 to highlight the contribution of this study. To the best of our knowledge, entry-timing taking homing policies into consideration has not undergone analytical research. Therefore, the current study contributes to the literature by examining endogenous entry and homing strategies in two competing platforms, such that platforms not only compete with regard to their decisions but also interact in terms of their strategic choices. Furthermore, a two-period model emerges when the platforms enter the markets asynchronously, and we derive the equilibrium under two periods through utilizing a dynamic programming approach. In addition, we consider a plausible setting where seller members behave more strategically (i.e. are more forward-looking) than buyer members.

## 3. Model

Consider a set that consists of two competing platforms, denoted as Platforms 1 and 2, in a two-sided market, in which platforms cater to the tastes of customers in a two-sided market. We establish a typical setting in which one market is composed of sellers, and the other market is composed of buyers. Sellers refer to firms that provide services or products, and buyers refer to end consumers who purchase from firms. We examine the sellers' platform joining choices and the buyers' purchase choices. Because sellers have more market information than buyers, sellers select platforms from a long-term perspective by considering possible future options. Hence, sellers are more forward-looking than buyers, who make their purchase decisions based on the current options available to them, revealing the inherent asymmetry between these two types of customers. The platforms and the customers interact in a two-stage process. In the first stage, the platforms announce their service fees on the seller side, and both types of customers select the platforms based on their utilities. Note that we follow a common practice wherein platforms do not charge joining fees from the buyer side to stimulate demands $[26]$ . The market share of the other side will increase the utility of the customers on either side; in other words, cross-sided network externalities $[50, 6, 8]$ exist. To affect the size of the two-sided markets, the platforms make the following strategic decisions:

1. Homing strategy. The platforms can choose to adopt a single- or multi-homing policy for the seller side. Sellers are restricted from joining a single platform when the single-homing policy is adopted; otherwise, they can join any platforms that provide positive utilities under the multi-homing policy. Note that because there are no joining fees or usage limitations on the buyer side, any buyer can join both platforms to acquire information but can be satisfied by purchasing from either platform at any time. This situation is very common in reality, such as catering platforms like UberEats and FoodPanda, or B2C shopping platforms like Amazon and eBay. Therefore, in line with reality, we consider that buyers make one purchase at a time.

2. Entry strategy. Two market-entry strategies are depicted in Figure 2. When platforms enter the market synchronously, the platforms interact in one period, and the network effects are associated with the market sizes of the two-sided market, denoted by $NS_{i}$ and $NB_{i}$ , in the same period. When platforms enter the market asynchronously, Platform 1 enters the market earlier than Platform 2 to stimulate early adopters, denoted by $NS_{F}$ and $NB_{F}$ . The timeline can be divided into two periods: In the first period, Platform 1 behaves as a monopoly before the entry of Platform 2; in the second period, they compete in a duopoly. In such a two-period model, Platform 1 determines its pricing decisions dynamically. In addition, because market cannibalization may occur in the seller-side market for Platform 1, early entry is not necessarily a profitable strategy and thus deserves further discussion.

As a result, four possible scenarios, denoted as $k \in \{SS, SA, MS, MA\}$ , emerge: (i) a single-homing policy with synchronous entry (Scenario SS), (ii) a multi-homing policy with synchronous entry (Scenario MS), (iii) a single-homing policy with asynchronous entry (Scenario SA), and (iv) a multi-homing policy with asynchronous entry (Scenario MA). For the scenario index k, the first letter takes the value of S or M, respectively representing the single- and multi-homing policy, and the second letter takes the value of S or A, respectively indicating synchronous and asynchronous entry. To ease exposition, we let $k_{S}=\{SS,MS\}$ and $k_{A}=\{SA,MA\}$ be the scenarios with synchronous and asynchronous strategies, respectively. Throughout the paper, we use the index $i\in\{1,2\}$ to denote the platforms, $j\in\{s,b\}$ to denote the customer types (i.e., seller and buyer), and $o\in\{F,1,2\}$ denotes the options for sellers and buyers to join the platforms. Specifically, F indicates the option of Platform 1 in the first period, and 1, 2 indicate the options for Platforms 1 and 2 in the second period, respectively. We summarize the notation in Table 3.

## 1.4.13.1. Customer decisions

We follow existing works [50, 6, 18, 8] to formulate a customer's utility in a two-sided market based on location models. A type- $j$ ( $j \in \{s, b\}$ ) customer's utility for a platform is formulated as follows:

$$
U = \lambda_ {j} - \mathcal {D} + \mathcal {N F} - \mathcal {P}.\tag{1}
$$

Under this location model, $\lambda_{j}$ is the primary valuation of a type- $j$ customer. $\mathcal{D}$ represents the distance between the platform location and the customer position, indicating perception gaps on the part of the customer purchasing from the platform. To capture the heterogeneity of customers, customers are uniformly distributed in the model. Hence, $\mathcal{D}$ consists of a random variable, i.e., the customer's position, representing customers' heterogeneous tastes. $\mathcal{NF}$ is the overall impact of network effects and is associated with the strength of cross-sided network effects and the market size of the other side in terms of enhancing the customer's preference for the platform. $\mathcal{P}$ is the service fee paid by the customer when selecting the platform. On the seller side, $\mathcal{P}$ is endogenously determined by the platforms; however, on the buyer side, the platforms are free of charge to join, and thus each buyer incurs a fixed $\mathcal{P}$ , such as a transaction or delivery fee for a buyer when a purchase occurs on the platform. Customers perceive different values of $\mathcal{NF}$ , $\mathcal{D}$ , and $\mathcal{P}$ for different platform choices, and thus each customer selects the choice that gives the highest utility. Because a customer has more than two choices when the platforms enter asynchronously, it is difficult to formulate the locations of the available choices for the Hotelling-type models. Following Salop [52], Salop and Scheffman [53], Gupta et al. [24] and

Cricelli et al. [15], we adopt Salop's circular city model, which maintains simplicity when a customer on one side has more than two choices, as depicted in Figure 3. For simplicity, the circumference is 1, indicating that the market size is normalized to one [37]. The type of Salop model adopted in this study applies to a case where different consumers have distinct preferences for the platforms regardless of features and prices, i.e., “horizontal differentiation.” More specifically, there is no inherently superior choice for customers [22, 40, 33]; thus, a consumer makes a choice depending on the distance from the platforms and the corresponding price/cost.

## 3.1.1. Buyer purchase choice

We focus on the buyers' purchase choices from either platform, rather than based on their platform joining choices. Following a basic premise that any buyer makes a single purchase at a time via the platform that currently provides her/hirm with the highest utility [5, 11, 47, 8], we can formulate the direct interaction between the platforms on the buyer side. Note that the buyer demand can represent the total amount of purchases on the buyer side in a period, and thus the platforms compete for the share of their buyer demand. In the following, we derive the market size of the buyer demand by establishing the purchase model of the buyer at each purchase.

We let $\chi \sim$ Uniform[0,1] denote each buyer's location in a Salop's circular city model to formulate the buyers' heterogeneous tastes and let $h_b(\chi)$ be the probability density function of $\chi$ . Platform $i$ is located at point $x_i$ ; then, $|\chi - x_i|$ denotes the minimal distance between a taste- $\chi$ buyer's location and Platform $i$ 's location, i.e., the closer arc length around the circle. As shown in Figure 3 (a), the platforms are uniformly distributed in the Salop circle, i.e., $x_1$ and $x_2$ are at points 0 and 1/2, respectively. Thus, $\mathcal{D} = |\chi - x_i|$ . Let $\alpha_b$ be the strength of cross-sided network effects on the buyer side, and let $\mathbb{NS}_i^k$ be the market size of the sellers on Platform $i$ under Scenario $k$ . We thus have $\mathcal{NF} = \alpha_b \times \mathbb{NS}_i^k$ . Furthermore, $\mathcal{P} = m_i$ means the fee for a buyer when purchasing through Platform $i$ . Following the formula expressed in Eq. (1), we have the utility of a buyer for Platform $i$ under Scenario $k$ , as follows:

$$
U B _ {i} ^ {k} (\chi) = \lambda_ {b} + \alpha_ {b} \mathbb {N S} _ {i} ^ {k} - | \chi - x _ {i} | - m _ {i}, \forall k.\tag{2}
$$

To avoid trivial cases, we assume that $\lambda_{b}>1/4+m_{i}$ , as specified in the Appendix. As a result, when both platforms exist, each buyer can obtain a positive utility from either platform and thus has sufficient motivation to make purchase [6, 37, 8]; however, when only Platform 1 exists, each buyer decides to purchase only when obtaining a positive utility. Solving for the equality of the utilities between two platforms gives the indifference points $\overline{x}_{12}$ and $\overline{x}_{21}$ . The buyers located on $\Omega_{i}\equiv[\overline{x}_{21},1-x_{i}]\cup[x_{i},\overline{x}_{12}]$ join Platform i. Then, taking the integral of $\chi$ over the interval $\Omega_{i}$ gives the numbers of buyers for each platform, i.e., $NB_{i}^{k}$ for $k\in k_{s}$ .

Under Scenarios SS and MS, buyers experience the numbers of the sellers who join in the same single period; hence, $NS_{1}^{k}=NS_{1}^{k}$ and $NS_{2}^{k}=NS_{2}^{k}, k\in k$ . Under Scenarios SA and MA, we assume that the total demand for early adopters, denoted by $\theta$ , is smaller than the total demand for regular buyers, i.e., $0\leq\theta<1/2$ . When Platform 1 enters the markets earlier, early adopters choose to purchase through Platform 1 when their utility is non-negative: $UB_{F}^{k}(\chi)=\lambda_{b}+\alpha_{b}NS_{F}^{k}-|\chi-x_{1}|-m_{1}\geq0\Leftrightarrow\chi\in\Omega_{F}, k\in k_{A}$ . In line with reality, the early-entry platform will not necessarily grab the entire market, and thus, the asynchronous-entry strategy is not inherently superior to the synchronous-entry strategy. Taking the integral of $\chi$ over the interval $\Omega_{F}$ gives the numbers of early adopters, i.e., $NB_{F}^{k}$ for $k\in k_{A}$ . After Platform 2 enters the market, the market size of the sellers joining Platform 1 is the sum of the sellers joining over the two periods; hence, $NS_{1}^{k}=NS_{1}^{k}+NS_{1}^{k}$ and $NS_{2}^{k}=NS_{2}^{k}; k\in k_{A}$ .

## 3.1.2. Seller selection choice

Regarding the sellers' platform joining choices, we let $\psi$ and $y_{i}$ denote each seller's and Platform $i$ 's locations in the Salop circular model, respectively. In addition, $\psi \sim$ Uniform[0,1] and $h_s(\psi)$ is the probability density function of $\psi$ . Following Eq. (1), the utility of a taste- $\psi$ seller for Platform $i$ is given by $US_i^k (\psi) = \lambda_s + \alpha_s\mathbb{NB}_i^k -|\psi -y_i| - p_i$ , $\forall k$ . Similar to Eq. (2), $\alpha_{s}$ represents the strength of cross-sided network effects on the seller side, and $\mathbb{NB}_i^k$ is the market size of the buyers on Platform i under Scenario k, and $p_{i}$ is the price endogenously determined by Platform i.

We now specify the location of $y_{i}$ and the values of $\mathbb{NB}$ . When the platforms enter the market synchronously, sellers choose either Platform 1 or 2, and thus $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$ are at points 0 and 1/2, as illustrated in Figure 3 (a). Moreover, $\mathbb{NB}_1^k = NB_1^k$ and $\mathbb{NB}_2^k = NB_2^k$ ; $k \in k_S$ . When the platforms enter the market asynchronously, sellers are forward-looking and have correct second-period information in the first period. Thus, sellers join one platform based on three options: (1) Platform 1 in the first period ( $o = F$ ), (2) Platform 2 in the second period ( $o = 1$ ), and (3) Platform 2 in the second period ( $o = 2$ ). Because options (1) and (2) involve joining the same platform in different periods, the sellers perceive a level of similarity, leading to the market-cannibalization effect, denoted by $z$ . As shown in Figure 3 (b), the locations of the options are adjusted to $y_{F} = z$ , $y_{1} = 1/3$ , and $y_{2} = 2/3$ ; and thus $|y_{F} - y_{1}| = 1/3 - z$ , $|y_{1} - y_{2}| = 1/3$ , and $|y_{2} - y_{F}| = 1/3 + z$ , where $0 \leq z < 1/3$ . This formulation indicates that sellers perceive the smallest differentiation between options (1) and (2) because they are the same platform. By contrast, the largest perceived difference is between options (1) and (3) because of the different platforms and entry periods. Furthermore, $\mathbb{NB}_1^k = NB_F^k + NB_1^k$ , and $\mathbb{NB}_2^k = NB_2^k$ , $k \in \{SA, MA\}$ .

Under the single-homing policy, taking the integral of $\psi$ between the indifference points, we can derive the number of sellers for each platform, i.e., $NS_{o}^{k}$ for $k \in \{SS, SA\}$ . Under the multi-homing policy, the sellers can join the platforms that provide them with non-negative utilities; therefore, integrating $\psi$ for $US_{i}^{k}(\psi) \geq 0$ gives the number of sellers, $NS_{o}^{k}$ for $k \in \{MS, MA\}$ .

## 1.8.1

## 1.8.25.1. Analysis of the parametric impacts on platform strategies

To compare the performance of the homing and entry strategies on the platforms' equilibrium profits, we define $\Phi_i$ ( $\Psi_i$ ) to be the comparative index of Platform $i$ 's profit compared between the asynchronous- and synchronous-entry strategies (single-homing and multi-homing policies). For example, $\Phi_1 \equiv (\pi_F^{SA} + \pi_1^{SA}) / \pi_1^{SS}$ , and $\Psi_1 \equiv (\pi_F^{SA} + \pi_1^{SA}) / (\pi_F^{MA} + \pi_1^{MA})$ . The base parameter setting is as follows: $\alpha_b = \alpha_s = 0.2$ , $\delta = 0.7$ , $\theta = 0.2$ , $z = 0.2$ , $m_1 = 0.3$ , $m_2 = m_1 \times \Delta_m$ , $\Delta_m = 0.8$ , and $\lambda_s = \lambda_b = 0.55$ , where $\Delta_m$ represents the difference between $m_1$ and $m_2$ . In Figure 4, we show the impacts of $\alpha_b$ and $\alpha_s$ on $\Phi_i$ and $\Psi_i$ . In addition, when $\Phi_i$ ( $\Psi_i$ ) is above the baseline at $\Phi_i = 1$ ( $\Psi_i = 1$ ), this indicates that the asynchronous-entry (single-homing) strategy is more beneficial to Platform $i$ than the synchronous-entry (multi-homing) strategy; otherwise, the results are contrary.

## Figure 4. Cross-sided network effects on the performance of the homing and entry strategies

Figure 4 reveals that the cross-sided network effects on the homing and entry strategy performance are similar for both platforms. (1) In most cases, the values of $\Phi_{i}$ are greater than 1; i.e., asynchronous entry is more beneficial for both the platforms than synchronous entry, especially under the single-homing policy. In addition, greater cross-sided network effects will improve of asynchronous entry performance on both platforms. For instance, in the game-console market, which inherently has strong cross-sided network effects, Playstation 2 (the early-entry platform) competes with Xbox (the post-entry platform). In such a situation, both of these game-console platforms offer exclusive contracts by which game developers can execute the single-homing strategy 65, 36]. However, when platform competition on the seller side is absent under the multi-homing policy, the network effects on asynchronous entry performance become insignificant. (2) Just as cross-sided network effects on the buyer side increase, asynchronous entry benefits Platform 1 but hurts Platform 2, indicating that cross-sided network effects on the buyer side cause early-entry platforms to be more competitive. For example, strong cross-sided network effects on the buyer side allowed early movers, such as YouTube and eBay, to be successfully defended from late entrants, e.g., Google video and Yahoo auction [65]. (3) Greater cross-sided network effects decrease the values of $\Psi_{i}$ ; thus, when cross-sided network effects increase, the multi-homing policy with asynchronous entry is more profitable to platforms. Otherwise, the single-homing policy is preferred by platforms. This result is different from the conventional view suggesting that platforms seek to discourage multi-homing to exclusively access their essential assets [45, 8, 66]. Our results reveal that asynchronous entry causes multi-homing to be beneficial to both platforms, and we also complement the work of Halaburda and Yehezkel [27], who states that the multi-homing policy yields more benefits to first-mover platforms.

Figure 5. Impacts of seller perceived similarity and early-market size on the performance of various entry strategies

We then focus on the impacts of the parameters associated with asynchronous entry in Figure 5, which depicts the changes in $\varphi_{i}$ concerning the market-cannibalization effect on Platform 1's seller market $z$ and the early-market size $\theta$ . The increase in the market-cannibalization effect on increases in Platform 1's seller market makes asynchronous entry less profitable for Platform 1 but more profitable for Platform 2. Interestingly, this negative (positive) impact is more (less) significant for Platform 1 (Platform 2) under the multi-homing policy than under the single-homing policy. Hence, the single-homing policy can mitigate the negative impact of market cannibalization on the asynchronous entry of Platform 1. Thus, Platform 1 prefers the single-homing policy when the market cannibalization effect is significant. When the size of the early market ( $\theta$ ) increases, asynchronous entry is more profitable for the platform, indicating that a greater early-market size mitigates the negative impacts of platform competition on asynchronous entry performance. Under the multi-homing policy, this impact becomes insignificant because there is no direct between-platform competition.

## 1.8.3

## 1.8.45.2. Analysis of platform preferences for the scenarios

Figure 6 shows the platform preferences for the scenarios that lead to the highest profits. Figure 6 (a) and (b) reveal that when the cross-sided network effects are stronger, price competition is more intensive, where both platforms prefer to adopt the multi-homing policy to avoid direct competition. This result corroborates the argument of an empirical study [58], which stated that when cross-sided network effects are weak, platforms may position themselves as niche players and likely take the entire market by adopting the single-homing policy. However, when cross-sided network effects are significant, the multi-homing policy may be more preferred by platforms than the single-homing policy. Consistent with a case of mobile operating system platforms, Bresnahan et al. [11] revealed that iOS and Android allow developers to be multi-homing when cross-sided network effects increase. In these cases, Platform 1 prefers to enter the market asynchronously, unlike Platform 2, because asynchronous entry causes Platform 1 to decrease its price, which is less profitable for Platform 2. This result indicates that the advantage (disadvantage) of asynchronous entry is more significant for the early-entry (post-entry) platform. When cross-sided network effects are weak, the advantage of asynchronous entry for Platform 1 is less significant. Thus, its profit from the early market cannot compensate for the losses caused by market cannibalization, leading it to choose synchronous entry, whereas Platform 2 behaves oppositely. Hence, this result verifies why start-up platforms that generally have low cross-sided network effects at the initiation stage generally enter markets synchronously, for example, sharing short-form video platforms, Tik Tok and Lasso, simultaneously released in 2018 [7]. Notably, when the network effects are moderately low, asynchronous entry under the single-homing policy is preferred by both platforms. Hence, the single-homing policy induces platforms to choose Scenario SA at equilibrium, such that platform competition can be mitigated. However, under the multi-homing policy, the platforms have different preferences for their entry strategies, and thus are unable to achieve the equilibrium choice in the scenarios.

Figure 6 (c) and (d) illustrate that when asynchronous entry leads to a greater market cannibalization effect for Platform 1, both platforms are still more likely to choose Scenario SA. Interestingly, when the market cannibalization effect is significant, Platform 1 will still not adopt the synchronous-entry strategy but will rather launch direct competition with Platform 2 by adopting the single-homing policy, even when the early market is small. With the aid of Figure 6 (a), we infer that the reason for this is that cross-sided network effects lead Scenario MS to be less profitable for Platform 1. Intuitively, because Platform 1's competitiveness decreases due to the market cannibalization effect under asynchronous entry, Platform 2 prefers Scenario SA to preserve higher margins when the market cannibalization effect increases. For example, Xbox, a post-entry platform, adopts single-homing with game developers through exclusive contracts [66]. In addition, when the scales of the early buyer market are greater, the market cannibalization effect can be mitigated. Thus, both platforms are less likely to change their preferences for the multi-homing policy.

## 1.8.55.3. Analysis of buyer and seller surpluses

Next, we examine the buyer and seller surpluses under the different scenarios. The buyer and seller surpluses under Scenario k can be respectively represented by $SP_{b}^{k} = \sum_{i} \left( \int_{\chi \in \Omega_{i}^{k}} UB_{i}^{k}(\chi) \times h_{b}(\chi) \chi \right)$ and $SP_{b}^{k} = \sum_{i} \left( \int_{\psi \in \Lambda_{i}^{k}} US_{i}^{k}(\psi) \times h_{s}(\psi) \psi \right)$ , where $\Omega_{i}^{k}$ and $\Lambda_{i}^{k}$ denote the sets of $\phi$ and $\psi$ selecting choice i under Scenario k. Figure 7 shows the influences of cross-sided network effects on buyer and seller surpluses.

## Figure 7 Cross-sided network effects on buyer and seller surpluses

Several insights can be obtained from Figure 7. (1) Cross-sided network effects in both the buyer- and seller-sided markets can increase the surpluses of buyers and sellers. Generally, Scenario MA is most beneficial to buyers and sellers. (2) Focusing on the buyer surplus $SP_{b}$ , the scenarios with asynchronous entry lead to the higher $SP_{b}$ . In addition, the multi-homing policy amplifies the positive impacts of cross-sided network effects on $SP_{b}$ ; thus, if cross-sided network effects on both sides are great, the scenarios with the multi-homing policy may contribute more to $SP_{b}$ . (3) Regarding the seller surplus $SP_{s}$ , the scenarios with the multi-homing policy, lead to higher $SP_{s}$ . Notably, the single-homing policy amplifies the positive impacts of the network effects on $SP_{s}$ , especially Scenario SS. Thus, when cross-sided network effects are significant, Scenario SS may lead platforms to make decisions that benefit sellers, thus increasing their surpluses. For instance, in 2009, BigCommerce and Magento entered the e-commerce platforms market. Magento offered cheap joining fees for sellers, and meanwhile BigCommerce provided free functions that allowed sellers to design their own online shops, so single-homing was encouraged on the seller side [23]. However, Scenario SS was less preferred by buyers, indicating that there was an existing conflict between buyer and seller preferences.

(4) Comparing the platform preferences depicted in Figure 6, it can be observed that Scenario SA is possibly preferred by both platforms when network effects are somewhat weak but is always the most unfavorable scenario for sellers. It is common to speculate that price competition between platforms is advantageous in terms of seller surplus. However, this is only true when platforms enter the market synchronously. When platforms enter the market asynchronously, it seems that the negative impact of price competition is passed to the seller side, which hurts sellers but benefits platforms. When cross-sided network effects are weak, the platform preferences (i.e., single-homing policy) are different from those of buyers and sellers (i.e., multi-homing policy). Consistently with the empirical findings of Staykova and Damsgaard [57], when cross-sided network effects are high, both sellers and buyers are more likely to prefer the multi-homing policy. We further indicate that when cross-sided network effects increase, the multi-homing policy is more likely to be preferred by buyers, sellers, and platforms, which is more advantageous to society as a whole.

## 6. Conclusion and discussion

The number of platform-based businesses is continuously increasing. However, platform-based businesses often struggle when entering markets with keeping their business ahead of the competition. In such circumstances, platforms need to determine the most efficient entry-timing and homing choices in order to attract prospective new customers while maintaining their original customer base. Therefore, we formulate game-theoretic models featuring entry and homing strategies to examine platform competition in a two-sided market with cross-sided network effects. We derived the equilibrium demands for a two-sided market for platforms and the equilibrium prices of two competing platforms using a dynamic approach. We then examined four different scenarios to investigate the impact of entry strategies, homing strategies, cross-sided network effects, platform preferences for the strategies, and customer surpluses. Based on the findings, the advantageous scenarios and strategies for the platforms, sellers, and buyers are summarized in Table 7.

Impacts on the equilibrium demands and prices. When platforms enter the market synchronously, the multi-homing policy makes it possible to mitigate price competition, and thus such platforms can preserve higher margins, as discussed in previous studies [4]. Under the single-homing policy, asynchronous entry can alleviate the intensity of price competition. However, under the multi-homing policy, asynchronous entry causes the first-mover platform to behave more aggressively in terms of price competition by lowering its price. For instance, Groupon, an early-entry platform, was more aggressive in terms of price competition by providing daily deal promotion when the entrants LivingSocial, came into the market [30]. These results complement those found by Halberda and Yehezkel [27] and Biglaiser et al. [9], indicating that the interaction between the entry and homing strategies changes the pricing behavior of platforms.

Platform preferences for entry and homing strategies. When a strong market cannibalization effect is harmful to an early-entry platform, asynchronous entry is preferred by this platform because it is possible to preserve higher margins in the first period and stabilize prices in the second period, thus alleviating price competition. If this is the case, the multi-homing policy with asynchronous entry causes an early-entry platform to behave more aggressively in terms of price competition. Hence, early- and post-entry platforms will prefer the single-homing policy, which is similar to the findings of Budzinski and Köhler [12]. For example, to avoid price competition with rivals, Foodpanda prevents restaurants from partnering with other food delivery platforms, e.g., Uber Eats, by offering exclusive contracts for its restaurants [42]. Similarly, Uber Eats, a post-entry platform, improves restaurant loyalty by launching daily promotions or zero delivery fees to restaurants [51]. When the size of the early buyer market is greater, the market cannibalization effect can be alleviated. Therefore, both platforms prefer the single-homing policy. Under strong cross-sided network effects, asynchronous entry is preferred as opposed to the early-entry platform but less preferred than the post-entry platform. Otherwise, the multi-homing is more preferred by the platforms.

Impacts on seller and buyer surpluses. Cross-sided network effects can increase the surpluses of both buyers and sellers. In most cases, the multi-homing policy with asynchronous entry of platforms is more beneficial to both buyers and sellers. However, the single-homing policy amplifies the positive impacts of network effects on seller surpluses. When cross-sided network effects are weak, platform preferences (single-homing policy) are different from those of buyers and sellers who prefer the multi-homing policy. However, when cross-sided network effects increase, the multi-homing policy is likely to be preferred by buyers, sellers, and platforms. Ride-hailing platforms, i.e., Uber and Lyft, adopted the multi-homing policy on the seller side to increase the mass of customers, such that the cross-sided network effects were enhanced [66].

There are several possible extensions of our study that can be explored to achieve a better understanding of the competitive dynamics of the platform economy. First, it is plausible that platforms financially intervene in markets, e.g., platforms subsidize shipping fees or engage in cooperative promotional activities. It would be insightful to investigate how the strategic interaction between platforms and sellers affects buyer purchasing behavior, platform equilibrium decisions, and the dynamics of platform competition. Second, we consider platform competition in a two-sided market. However, a multi-sided market, i.e., a three-sided market (drivers, restaurants, and buyers) for food-delivery platforms (Foodpanda, UberEats), is worth exploring since there are more complex interactions between platforms in such a system. However, this extension direction will inevitably increase analytical complexity, so a theoretical analysis may be intractable. Third, a worthwhile direction would be to relax the assumption by considering that buyers can make multiple purchases from platforms that deliver positive utilities. This scenario generally holds when the platforms are complementary [60]; thus, it would be interesting to examine co-opetitive interactions between platforms.

## Explanation of the assumption of $\lambda_{j}>1/4+m_{i}$

For simplicity, we use $UB_{i}$ to represent $UB_{i}^{k}(\chi)$ . If $UB_{1} + UB_{2} > 0$ , we can confirm that at least one of $UB_{1}$ and $UB_{2}$ is positive. From Eq. (2), $UB_{1} + UB_{2} = 2\lambda_{b} + \alpha_{b}(\mathbb{NS}_{1}^{k} + \mathbb{NS}_{2}^{k}) - 1/2 - m_{1} - m_{2}$ . Hence, we have $\lambda_{b} - 1/4 - m_{i} > 0 \Rightarrow 2\lambda_{b} - 1/2 - m_{1} - m_{2} > 0 \Rightarrow 2\lambda_{b} + \alpha_{b}(\mathbb{NS}_{1}^{k} + \mathbb{NS}_{2}^{k}) - 1/2 - m_{1} - m_{2} = UB_{1} + UB_{2} > 0$ . Therefore, $\lambda_{b} > 1/4 + m_{i}$ is a sufficient condition for the buyer to purchase through either platform.
