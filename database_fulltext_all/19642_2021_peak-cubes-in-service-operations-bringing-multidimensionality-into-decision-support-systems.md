## A B S T R A C T

Companies like Ritz Carlton, Disney and Verizon are among many who have invested in analytics to improve their customers’ service experiences with the firms. Extensive data are collected on all aspects of how customers interact or experience the products or services. Research has shown the importance of the “peak-end” rule in service design: that is. providing a customer with good “peak" service levels and “ending” the service experience with high quality can enhance customer satisfaction and build loyalty. However, previous studies have examined this phenomenon only in contexts with unidimensional service levels, We introduce peak cubes, which enable service designers and scholars to pinpoint prominent service levels in multidimensional service experience pro files—thereby extending current research on behavioral economics and service design to more general settings. Results indicate the potential of multidimensional peak-end models to better predict customer satisfaction in various service scenarios. Using Shapley values in coalitional game theory, the resulting models can also inform service designers about the quality dimensions that are critical from the perspective of multidimensional peakend heuristic and customer satisfaction. Our research contributions and proposed methodology will enhance decision support systems with multidimensional capabilities and have applications to fields as diverse as service operations and healthcare.

## 1. Introduction

In service operations, “perception is reality” ([7], p. 78). A cus tomer’s interpretation of a service experience determines her loyalty to the service provider, which builds to a profitable customer relationship. Research on behavioral science and behavioral economics has led to the effective design of services to enhance customer satisfaction [10]. Indeed, in the last decade firms have started taking small steps to actualize this mindset. One early example was how an insurance com pany helped nurses manage patients more effectively by structuring conversations to get over bad news early, focusing on positives, and ending well [13].

The identification of the psychology of experience as a missing link in building decision support for service science [8] raises the potential of new research streams that draw on behavioral economics to provide guidelines for how to build decision support systems for organizations that seek to maximize customer service experiences (of which are many, ranging from B2B firms such as FedEx, UPS, or DHL and B2C firms such as Ritz Carlton or Disney).

Recent research in this realm (e.g., [11,15]) highlight the importance of the “peak-end” rule in which a sequence of events is evaluated based on its maximum (peak) and final (end) utilities [16]. As a natural consequence of this, Das Gupta et al.’s [11] findings indicate that where possible it is optimal to position the highest (peak) service level near the end of the sequence, thereby “finishing strong.” However, most of the previous studies are conducted in unidimensional service settings; that is, they apply the peak-end rule to designing services on a single vari able. As a consequence, this suggests looking at multiple single variable service experience time series data streams separately, in isolation of the others, rather than in coniunction. Hence. if customers actually derive most utility from some combination of factors rather than a single factor, the unidimensional perspective will not help discover this fact. Recent work in the DSS literature [23] shows the value of multidimensionality. Our research takes this perspective and shows how firms can use the “peak-end rule” in a multidimensional manner; when organizations have access to multiple time series data on different variables that matter to a customer (§2.2 provides a specific example).

We propose a methodology for computing the “peak cube,” a construct that contains all salient service levels from different perspec tives corresponding to specific subsets of service quality variables. To examine the potential contributions of the peak cube, we incorporate the resulting features into a decision support system whose focus is moni toring customer (dis)satisfaction in multidimensional settings, which in turn helps prevent customer churn in the absence of a service contract. The results indicate that in several service operations scenarios, peak cubes substantially improve early detection of defection due to the peakend rule in service settings with multidimensional service levels. In the proposed methodology, we also demonstrate how Shapley values in cooperative game theory can be applied to the resulting defection detection models to identify the service quality dimensions that are critical from the perspective of multidimensional peak-end heuristic and customer (dis)satisfaction.

We use logistics as the application in this paper because this is an area where we have applied this work. We have worked closely over several years with a leading logistics company, widely celebrated for its operational excellence. In our previous research [4], we built defection detection models using multi-dimensional (B2B) customer data for \~200,000 Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises (SMEs) and small of fices. Associated with each customer were many service-related time series data (e.g. number of late packages each week, where the time series is of weekly granularity). The dependent variable was package shipping volume (also a weekly time series) and our models in part used unidimensional peak-end features extracted from service quality data to predict when shipping volume decayed (i.e. the customer churned). Due to data confidentiality agreements we cannot provide details of the specific company that include these data analyses. Therefore, for this paper, we use simulations of comparable data to highlight what peak cubes can provide to this domain. Nonetheless, the methodology and insights provided here are generic and apply to multiple domains wherever service quality data are tracked over time.

We make several key contributions in our research. First, building on work on Pareto frontiers in the database literature we present a meth odology for implementing the peak-cube idea in multidimensional ser vice operations scenarios. Second, we show how peak cubes can be learned from multidimensional time series data in service operations, and further explain several feature sets that can be computed from the resulting peak cubes. We also make the code for constructing peak cubes and computing various feature sets available to service designers and researchers in the GitHub repository.<sup>1</sup> Third, we demonstrate how pre dictive modeling using multidimensional peak-cube models can outperform traditional (unidimensional) models in different service operations scenarios. Fourth, the proposed methodology applies Shapley values (in coalitional game theory) to the models to inform service de signers about the quality dimensions that are critical from the perspec tive of multidimensional peak-end heuristic and customer satisfaction. Finally, we show how ideas in behavioral economics, database, predic tive modeling, and service science can be integrated in order to build a new research stream in the area of customer experience analytics with applications to firms in a wide range of industries like UPS, FedEx, Disney, Ritz Carlton, and Verizon.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In §2, we provide the theo retical background from behavioral economics, service design, and database literature, and motivate the research with a service operations example. In §3, we introduce the peak cube model, present an algorithm for its computation, and provide examples for various classes of features that can be computed from peak cubes. In §4, we provide a computa tional model via a set of algorithms for simulating thousands of customer bases with millions of customers along with their multidimensional service events. We run the simulations under various configurations of service operations scenarios. In §5, we construct 24,000 predictive models with different techniques to examine the application of peak cubes in monitoring customer dissatisfaction due to the peak-end rule in multidimensional service settings. A thorough discussion of the impli cations of the simulation results appears in §6. In $^ { \mathrm { 9 7 , } }$ we discuss model deployment with peak cubes in real settings, and show how in addition to defection detection, the resulting models can inform service designers about the quality subspaces that are critical from the perspective of peak-end rule. Finally, in §8 we present our concluding remarks and potential applications of peak cubes in other disciplines such as healthcare. ## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Behavioral economics and service design

Behavioral economics views humans as “boundedly rational” agents [33] who tend to employ heuristics [36] and make “satisficing” rather than “optimizing” decisions [32]. This perspective is corroborated by how individuals seem to evaluate a sequence of events. Were they rational, individuals would carry out the evaluation using a normative measure like the total experienced utility (i.e., sum of all events’ utili ties). However, behavioral economics research (e.g., [16,26]) suggests that humans evaluate a sequence of events based on heuristics – such as its highest (peak) and final (end) utilities—a finding celebrated as the “peak-end” rule.

Most service experiences can indeed be viewed as a sequence of events over time. Scholars accordingly started drawing on the “peakend” rule and related findings in behavioral economics (e.g., the slope of the experience profile; see [3]) to provide guidelines for service design. For example, Chase and Dasu [7] suggest a set of service design prin ciples including getting the unpleasant experiences out of the way early and finishing strong, given how crucial the end event is. Subsequent empirical findings (e.g., [37]) corroborate the importance of peak ex periences on customer satisfaction formation

Drawing on the behavioral economics literature, Dixon and Verma [14] outline four “sequence effects” that service designers should consider for customer satisfaction. In summary, these effects concern the most salient point (peak effect), the timings of the salient points (spread effect), the events’ utilities slope (trend effect), and the experience profile final state (end effect). Their findings provide empirical support for the significant role of the sequence effects in determining customer loyalty indicated by the repurchasing of season subscriptions.

Recent research examines the sequence effects within a single (continuous) service experience and across discrete service events (which can span over years). For example, Dixon and Thompson [15] suggest a meta-heuristic to maximize the four sequence effects in the context of service scheduling and event bundling for season subscription sales. Their findings indicate that service designers can achieve rela tively efficient sequences through the simplified optimization problem, which only contains the sequence effects that are most important to their customers. Das Gupta et al. [11], on the other hand, examine these ef fects in the problem of maximizing customer satisfaction from a single (continuous) service encounter. Their findings suggest positioning the peak service level near the end.

The key limitation of this research stream is its focus on unidimen sional service levels (e.g., [11]) or aggregate utility measures (e.g., [15]). We extend this research direction to more general service contexts with multidimensional service levels. Accordingly, service designers and scholars should be able to pinpoint salient points in multidimensional service experience profiles as illustrated in the following example.

## 2.2. Motivating example

To motivate the problem of peak-end effects in a multidimensional service experience profile, we consider business-to-business (B2B) courier service operations in which the service relationship between the logistics company and an SME or small/home office can span over several months. Every service relationship can be viewed as a sequence of discrete experiences (see [14,15]) in which a service event concerns a consignment containing several packages delivered (by the service company) to different destinations. In this application context, too, previous research (e.g., [25]) confirms that the perceived quality of service plays an important role in determining customer loyalty.

For the moment, we assume that in accordance with Simon’s [34] perspective to administrative decision-making processes, the peak-end heuristic plays a role in the “satisficing” administrator’s perception of service quality (we will explain this further at the end of this section). Given the significant impact of the peak of service level (utility) on satisfaction, we also assume that the peak of service failure (disutility) determines customer dissatisfaction, which might in turn cause the customer to switch services in the absence of a service contract. In other words, while service design research mainly concerns applying the peak end rule to maximizing customer satisfaction and its impact on loyalty (e.g., measured by subscription repurchase; see [14]), in this study we assume that the peak-end rule (if applied to service disutility) could also maximize customer dissatisfaction, which could subsequently lead to service churn. Over many years of collaboration with a logistics com pany, we drew on these assumptions to build defection detection models that in part employed unidimensional peak-end features. In this section, we explain the contribution of incorporating multidimensional peak-end features in decision support systems that monitor customer dissatisfac tion, which could in turn help prevent churn in non-contractual service settings.

In the logistics service operations, a service event (i.e., a consignment that contains packages delivered to different destinations) is subject to several types of failure. Specifically, some packages in the consignment might be picked up late, some might not be picked up at all, some might be delivered to their destinations late, some might get damaged in the process, and some might get lost. To simplify the illustration, for the moment, we consider only two types of failure, namely damaged packages and lost packages. The time series in Fig. 1 concern the two types of service failure that a customer could experience during the first twelve service events. More precisely, the time series show the numbers of damaged and lost packages per service event. For example, one (out of several) packages in the third service event is damaged in the process. It is important to note that service failures might have different units in other service operations settings.

Viewing the first three service events as the calibration window, the customer in the above example experienced peak(damaged) and peak (lost) at t=5 and t=4 respectively. These critical service events could be captured through the traditional (i.e., unidimensional) perspective; that is, in every unidimensional time series one could search for the point at which the corresponding service failure is at its maximum.

Viewing the two service failure time series from a multidimensional perspective, however, makes the last service event significant given that the customer experienced peak(damaged, lost). In other words, t=12 is the first service event in which the customer experienced one damaged and one lost package concurrently. In the absence of a service contract, if the customer in the above example switches at t=12, one could poten tially attribute the churn to multidimensional peak-end effects (i.e., experiencing peak(damaged, lost))—an observation that would be over looked through the traditional (unidimensional) analysis of the peakend rule, which is common practice in behavioral economics and ser vice design research. The last service event (t=12) in the above example is located on the two-dimensional Pareto frontier of the above cus tomer’s time series, which relates this study to Pareto frontiers query processing in database research.

It is important to note that by using B2B service operations as a motivating example for multidimensional peak-end heuristic we are not positing that the administrators of SMEs or small/home offices are in fact employing such decision-making shortcuts. This issue refers to the great debate in organizational decision making where conventional economics assumes that “rational” administrators do behave in a manner consistent with neoclassical norms for optimal behavior, whereas behavioral economics maintains that “boundedly rational” administrators adopt heuristics in their decision making (for a recent review of this debate and heuristics in organizational decision making see [1]). We rather select B2B logistics service operations given our experience with building defection detection models for \~200,000 SMEs and small/home offices in the field based on how they evaluate the service quality provided by a Fortune 500 company. To that end, we draw on Simon’s perspective (1997b) [35] that there is a continuum of decision making styles in organizations that involves both analytical and heuristic techniques, where a primary heuristic to evaluate past episodes of experience is the peak-end rule. If administrators employ the peakend heuristic in evaluating multidimensional service profiles, then multidimensional peak-end features can potentially contribute to pre dicting their subsequent decisions manifested in defection from the service company. In other words, we are suggesting that multidimen sional peak-end features (along with other feature sets) can contribute to predicting (individual or organizational) decisions that result from a combination of heuristic and analytical evaluations of multidimensional service profiles.

It can also be argued that an SME or small office might transform service failures (of different types) into a common unit (e.g., dollar value) to evaluate the service quality. In such cases, too, “boundedly rational” administrators might apply the peak-end heuristic to the combined measure, which could be captured with traditional (unidi mensional) peak features. This, however, does not diminish the potential contribution of multidimensional peak-end features for two reasons. First, it is not always feasible for the service company to quantify the loss (e.g., in dollars) that a customer (e.g., SME or small office) incurs due to a service failure. For example, the service company cannot estimate the damage to a small business reputation due to late delivery of packages (from the customer’s point of view). Of note, this limitation also holds in the case of employing multidimensional peak features. The limitation, however, makes multidimensional peaks of service failures (of different types) a more comprehensive proxy to customer dissatisfaction if the peak-end heuristic is employed as a decision-making shortcut.

Second, transforming different units into a common unit is not al ways possible, especially in non-business domains like healthcare (in which multidimensional peaks are still potentially relevant). As an extreme case, consider the problem of predicting patient deterioration with temporally extended vital signs (e.g., blood pressure and body temperature), which cannot be transformed into a common unit. ${ \mathrm { I f } } ,$ along with other features, peaks of vital signs potentially matter in predicting patient deterioration, multidimensional peak features can contribute to the corresponding decision support systems.

## 2.3. Pareto frontiers in databases

Named after the economist and engineer Vilfredo Pareto, an object is considered as Pareto optimal if there is no other object in the data that dominates it. In a multidimensional space, specifically, object $s _ { l }$ is said to “dominate” object $s _ { 2 }$ if and only $\mathrm { i f } s _ { 1 }$ is as good as or better than $s _ { 2 }$ on all dimensions and is strictly better than $\scriptstyle { s _ { 2 } }$ on at least one dimension.

Borzsonyi, Kossmann, and Stocker [6] extend SQL (Structured Query Language) with a “skyline” operator to capture the Pareto frontiers in multidimensional databases. They motivate the operator with the problem of a trip to Bahamas in which the traveler is looking for hotels that are both cheap and close to the beach. From the traveler’s twodimensional perspective, hotel $\mathbf { \epsilon } ^ { \prime } \mathbf { A } ^ { \prime }$ would be a better choice than hotel ‘B’ iff (A.Distance ≤ B.Distance ∧ A.Price ≤ B.Price) ∧ (A.Distance < B. By definition, the hotels skyline (Fig. 2.a) is defined by the interesting (Pareto optimal) hotels (denoted by solid points) that are not dominated by any other hotel in the dataset. The traveler could then make the final decision by selecting from the set of skyline hotels. Several algorithms have been proposed for efficient (e.g., parallel and distributed) skyline query processing (for a recent survey, see [19]).

## 3. Peak cube: multidimensional peaks of experience

In this section, we explain why neither the full-space skyline in Fig. 2. b nor the data skycube (which will be further explained in 3.1) fully captures multidimensional peak effects in service operations. We sub sequently introduce a novel model of the peak cube that effectively pinpoints prominent service events in multidimensional service experiences.

## 3.1. A peak cube model

## 3.2. Computing the peak cube of service experience

Consider a service transactions database that provides a set of <customer ID, event sequence, $S F _ { 1 } , . . . , S F _ { n } { > }$ tuples for each customer, where n denotes the number of service quality (success or failure) di mensions. In the case of a churner, specifically, we would know about service failure levels of different types that the customer experienced per service event until the churn. To examine any relationship between the (multidimensional) peak effects and customer dissatisfaction (man ifested in churn), we first need to compute the peak cube—that we define here as the complete set of multidimensional peaks of service failures on all possible subspaces of the failures space for each customer. More precisely, for every customer the algorithm receives the corre sponding multidimensional service failure time series and returns the service events in which the customer experienced service failure peaks with respect to different subspaces of the failures space. The output will enable service designers and scholars to examine the peak effect on service (dis)satisfaction; e.g., if the number of multidimensional service failure peaks (or simply multidimensional peaks) is a useful predictor of switching behavior, which pertains to multidimensional peak-end effects.

Inspired by the previous algorithms for precomputing skycube [24,38] and to construct service failure peak cubes, we propose a bottom-up algorithm that directly benefits from the relational and algebraic calculi for database preferences [28] and the query language in its implemented package in CRAN [29,30]. For every customer, the PeakCube algorithm (Fig. 4) finds the service events in which a customer experienced failure peaks on any subspace of service failures space. The bottom-up algorithm constructs the peak cube for each customer in a stepwise manner from level one (unidimensional peaks) to level n (fulldimensional peaks).

In the next section, we will use this algorithm to construct service failure peak cubes for millions of simulated customers in the context of B2B logistics industry with five known types of service failures, namely late pickups, missed pickups, late delivery, damaged packages, and lost packages. Of note, the number of relevant dimensions in most Pareto optima use cases in large databases normally ranges from two to five [28] given how the computational cost exponentially increases with dimensionality. Also of note, the focus of the present study is more on the application of the suggested peak cube in service (rather than al gorithm) design; nonetheless, with multithreading we are able to use the PeakCube algorithm to construct the five-dimensional peak cubes for four million customers on a desktop computer. The code for constructing peak cubes from multidimensional service level time series appears in the electronic companion to this paper.

To facilitate the execution of the PeakCube algorithm in Fig. 4, we code the subspaces using a binary-coded decimal (BCD) system. In a three-dimensional service failure space, for example, the bit sequences 0, 1, and 2 are assigned to $S F _ { 1 } , \ S F _ { 2 }$ and $S F _ { 3 }$ respectively. Within this schema, subspace ${ < S F _ { 2 } > }$ is represented by $2 ( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , 2 1 ) ,$ , while subspace ${ < S F _ { 1 } , S F _ { 3 } > }$ is represented by $5 \ ( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } 2 0 + 2 2 )$ . More precisely, M⊂N iff the bitwise and operator (&) of subspace N and a lower-level subspace M equals M. For example, $< S F _ { 1 } , S F _ { 3 } > C < S F _ { 1 } , S F _ { 2 } , S F _ { 3 } >$ and $( 7 \& 5 ) = 5 ,$ , whereas $< S F _ { 2 } > / \subset < S F _ { 1 } ,$ $S F _ { 3 } >$ and $( 5 \& 2 ) \neq 2 .$ This allows us to simply construct a filter set (FS) that contains {s | s∈peak(T, M) for every M⊂N} during runtime; the set of peak(T, N) will then be equal to Skyline(T, N)-FS(T, N).

## 3.3. Feature engineering with peak cubes

The algorithm in Fig. 4 is executed for every customer to establish her service failure peak cube in the form of <customer ID, event ID, subspace, subspace binary code, subspace level>. After defining the “end” period (i.e., the number of last service events considered as the “end”), the peak cube data are used to compute various feature sets that can be employed in prediction models. For exposition, here we briefly explain a few classes of features that can be constructed from the resulting customer peak cubes.

The first class of features concerns whether a customer experienced a failure peak on a certain service quality subspace, or the number of times that she experienced peaks of failure on that subspace during the end period (if the end period contains two or more service events). ## 4. Simulating customer bases and service failure time series

As proof of concept for the peak cube model and algorithm, we simulate thousands of customer bases and their multidimensional ser vice event time series to illustrate potential applications of peak cubes in service design. At a high level we first simulate customer bases, then build peak cubes, and finally predictive models. The simulation itself is comprised of three base modules, namely CreateEmbeddedPatterns, NonChurnServiceFailures, and CreateCustomers. Every iteration of the simulation generates a customer base containing thousands of service customers. To address different service operations scenarios, we run thousands of iterations of the simulation with randomly selected pa rameters as explained in detail below.

As noted in the introduction, we have worked closely with a worl. d‑leader in the logistics industry on this problem. Due to data confi dentiality issues we cannot present those results but present these simulations as a proximate substitute that closely mirrors the reality of the data. Simulations are also appropriate as a research method here since our goal is to examine the potential advantages of basing the design of service operations on the richness of multidimensional data rather than on the limited perspective of unidimensional data. A more important question we argue is whether the method works; and this can also be demonstrated with carefully constructed simulations as we do here.

We begin by presenting a high-level outline of the simulation design:

1. We create scenarios where combinations of different service failure levels result in “patterns” that probabilistically cause a customer to churn. We might, for instance, create 100 such “patterns” and simulate multivariate customer time series data that reflect the notion that when any such pattern involving multiple time series occurs, the customer is (more) likely to churn. Our goal here is to show that the “peak cube” methodology can identify these patterns even in the presence of noise.

2. To make this more realistic, we simulate different “failure regimes” where some service factors fail more often than others. For example, in the courier delivery context “late packages” are more likely than “lost packages.” Hence the time series comprising of how many packages are late each week may be modeled in a relatively higher failure regime than the time series corresponding to lost packages.

3. In order to show the value of the peak cube methodology, we will create a large number of customers along with their multidimensional time series of service failures, and use these data to show that predictive models for customer churn that use features identified by the peak cube algorithm will outperform models which only consider unidimensional peaks in failures. While we expect this to be the case given how we are simulating customers, the results will demonstrate a few important things: (1) the PeakCube algorithm works and we are able to discover the peak cubes that were “embedded” when we simulated the data, (2) the algorithm is effective in the presence of many different sources of noise, (3) the method is robust to various key inputs in the process, and (4) the predictive accuracy gains can be significant if there are indeed such multidimensional peak-end effects.

In addition to sharing the code to construct peak cubes from multi variate time series data, we also share the code for the simulation to enable researchers to build extended versions of this to test other sce narios beyond the context of the present study.

## 4.1. The CreateEmbeddedPatterns module

The CreateEmbeddedPatterns algorithm (Fig. 5) serves two goals in every iteration of the simulation. First, it sets different probability ranges called “service failure regimes,” which are inspired by the observation that some aspects of service quality have more frequent failures. In the context of shipping service operations, for example, the number of late deliveries per service event (consignment) is usually greater than the number of lost packages. As a specific type of failure, late delivery therefore belongs to a higher failure regime compared to lost packages. In each iteration of the simulation, the Crea teEmbeddedPatterns algorithm establishes three failure regimes, namely low, medium, and high failure regimes—which in turn determines the non-zero failure probabilities of the randomly assigned service quality aspects. Lines (1) to (8) in the algorithm concern creating the service failure regimes and randomly assigning different service quality aspects to them. Each service failure regime spans a 20% none-zero failure probability range. (See Fig. 5.)

When the CreateEmbeddedPatterns module is invoked in every itera tion (see §4.3), the upper bound (UB) of the high failure regime is randomly selected from the set of {50%, 55%...70%}. If the high failure regime upper bound is set to 60%, for example, the high, medium, and low failure regimes will be accordingly set to [40%, 60%], [20%, 40%], and [0%, 20%] respectively. For each service quality dimension that is (randomly) assigned to the medium failure regime, for example, a fail ure probability (i.e., probability of a non-zero failure) will be randomly selected from the corresponding range of [20%, 40%]. In other words, if three service failure dimensions are assigned to this medium failure regime, each of them will be given a different (randomly selected) nonzero failure probability from the range of [20%, 40%]. These failure probabilities will later be used to simulate the multidimensional service failure time series for every customer in the customer base generated by the corresponding iteration.

In every round of the simulation, the CreateEmbeddedPatterns module also constructs several multidimensional service failure patterns that could potentially cause a customer to churn. Three parameters in the algorithm determine the embedded service failure churn patterns. ## 4.2. The NonChurnServiceFailures module

Using the service failures probabilities in EP (generated by the CreateEmbeddedPatterns module), the NonChurnServiceFailures module (Fig. 6) simulates the service failures that a customer experiences in a regular service event (which is not necessarily a precursor to churn). The module also ensures that the service failure dimensionality of the simulated service event is less than the dimensionality of service failure patterns that could lead to churn (i.e., minPatternSize); otherwise, the module randomly sets the difference to zero.

For every customer base (generated by an iteration of the simula tion), the NonChurnServiceFailures module will be invoked for every customer to simulate a single multidimensional service experience. For a non-churning service customer with fifty service events in the moti vating example, the NonChurnServiceFailures module is called fifty times to simulate fifty different five-dimensional service events, which in turn generates a five-dimensional service failures time series that is comprised of fifty service events for that customer.

We apply logistic regression, gradient boosting trees, and support vector machines (SVM) as three well-established techniques in defection detection research and practice (see [22] for example). Of the three methods, gradient boosting trees [17] has also become the de facto choice among ensemble methods and is frequently used in machine learning competitions like the Netflix prize [5]. Conventional gradient boosting decision tree algorithms need to scan all the instances to compute the information gain of all the possible split points for every feature. Accordingly, its performance becomes very time consuming as the data size and feature dimension grow. Although dimensionality is not a concern in building our first set of prediction models with gradient boosting trees (as the data only contains high-level features), it will be an issue in later stages given the exponential number of features that can be extracted from a peak cube (see §3.3).

Thus, for the gradient boosting tree models we apply LightGBM [20] that has improved the efficiency and scalability by employing two techniques. With Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS), LightGBM excludes a significant portion of data with small gradients, given that the data instances with larger gradients contribute more to the information gain. Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB), on the other hand, helps reduce the number of features through bundling mutually exclusive features. While achieving the same performance, LightGBM significantly out performs other implementations of gradient boosting decision trees (i.e., Stochastic Gradient Boosting by [18]; XGBoost by [9]) in terms of memory consumption and computational speed.

For every customer base (4000 customer bases total), we train two model sets (each set contains a logistic regression, a LightGBM, and an SVM model) with a random 70% of the data. In summary, we build 24000 prediction models (i.e., 4000 customer bases, three techniques with single versus multidimensional features).

We let each model compute the churn likelihood of the remaining 30% of customers in the corresponding customer base and subsequently compute the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) curve to measure each model’s performance on the holdout data. ## 6.4. Minimum cardinality of embedded churn patterns

To further examine the application of multidimensional peak-end effects in predicting customer dissatisfaction, we incorporate min PatternSize in the simulation to set the minimum cardinality (i.e., the number of non-zero service failures) of embedded churn patterns con structed by CreateEmbeddedPatterns in each simulation iteration. For example, if the number of churn patterns (nPattern) is set to 100 and minPatternSize is set to 3, the CreateEmbeddedPatterns module will generate 100 failure patterns with the minimum cardinality of 3 that could potentially lead to churn in the corresponding simulation iteration.

In the five-dimensional service operations scenario, specifically, we ran 1000 iterations of the simulation per minPatternSize while all other simulation parameters were set randomly. In each iteration, 100 embedded service failure churn patterns were generated. For each minimum cardinality of churn patterns, we subsequently trained 1000 unidimensional peak-end and 1000 multidimensional peak-end models to predict customer dissatisfaction. Fig. 15 indicates while the multidi mensional peak-end models’ predictive power is insensitive to the minimum cardinality of embedded churn patterns, the predictive per formance of unidimensional peak-end models drops after min PatternSize=2. Still, the difference in the performance of single and multidimensional peak-end models is nearly 15% at minPatternSize=2.

The drop in the predictive performance of unidimensional peak-end models can be explained intuitively. In the scenario corresponding to minPatternSize=2, the maximum cardinality of non-zero service failures in regular service events is mostly one (the probability of which is determined by nonChurnerSeesPatternP in the simulation). Except for the churn pattern cardinality of 5 in this scenario, it is still possible that some of the service failures in the encountered churn pattern are non zero for the first time. In this case the churn event is easily captured by the corresponding unidimensional peaks, thereby improving the predictive performance of unidimensional peak-end models. ## 7. Model deployment and practical implications

Service companies can incorporate peak cubes and the resulting feature sets (§3.3) in their decision support systems for defection detection. Using a random set of customers, their churn decisions, and peak cube features (along with other potentially informative features), a defection detection model is first constructed with a prediction algo rithm like LightGBM (see §5). The model is then tested on the remaining (unseen) customers to examine its predictive performance in compari son to alternate models. The service company may then deploy the defection detection system with peak cube feature sets. After every service encounter, the updated customer’s peak cube features are used in the prediction model to compute the churn likelihood. The prediction model should itself be calibrated periodically as customer peak cubes update through service transactions.

In addition to monitoring customer dissatisfaction and early detec tion of churn, can such defection detection models inform service de signers about the quality subspaces that are critical from the perspective of the peak-end heuristic and customer (dis)satisfaction? For example, which combinations of service failures should be averted given their potential for customer churn? To answer this and similar questions, one needs to first understand which features are comparatively important in the prediction model and how they contribute to churn likelihood.

## 7.1. Multidimensional peaks importance

There has been a de facto trade-off between model accuracy and interpretability in predictive analytics. While sophisticated models like gradient boosting trees achieve state-of-the-art predictive performance, interpretation and explanation of their predictions become more chal lenging. Motivated by this problem, various methods have been pro posed to explain the (black box) models’ predictions. Ribeiro, Singh, and Guestrin [27], for example, propose LIME (Local Interpretable Modelagnostic Explanations), which provides local model interpretability by tweaking the feature values of a single data instance and tracking the resulting impact on the output. It should be noted, however, that LIME provides interpretability through local linear explanation, which might not be powerful enough to explain the behavior of more sophisticated data like shown in the present study. The provided explanations are not always stable either, e.g., Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola [2] demonstrate that LIME explanations of two close points can vary greatly in a simu lated setting.

A more recent approach to interpreting and explaining model pre dictions has roots in coalitional game theory [21]. Viewing the predic tion task as a cooperative game, every decision made by the model corresponds to a payout, which is defined as the difference between the model’s prediction for a specific instance and the average prediction for the whole sample. In this setting, each feature is then viewed as a player who collaborates in the game to receive some payout. The Shapley value in coalitional games helps determine the gain distribution between the players that satisfies all four desirable properties of fairness [31]. Lundberg and Lee [21] draw on this to propose an approach to explaining what drives a decision made by a prediction model with a high-dimensional feature vector (which is the case of the present study). They particularly view each feature in the model as a force, whose magnitude of impact on a prediction is determined by its Shapley value in the corresponding coalitional game. In the context of the present study, particularly, the Shapley value of a peak feature $p \ ( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } \phi _ { p } )$ to a

## 8. Research contributions and future directions

In this research, we build and implement a peak cube model, which captures all salient service levels in multidimensional service experience profiles. To investigate its potential contributions to research and practice, we simulate 4000 customer bases (n = 1000) along with their multidimensional service experience profiles and subsequently construct a service failure (disutility) peak cube for each customer. We next apply the features extracted from these peak cubes to building customer dissatisfaction models with various techniques (namely lo gistic regression, LightGBM, and SVM) based on the assumption that service failure peaks maximize customer dissatisfaction, which could in turn lead to service churn in non-contractual settings. Compared to unidimensional models, the results point to the value of multidimen sional peak-end models in predicting customer dissatisfaction (man ifested in service churn). More precisely, the results indicate that multidimensional peak-end models significantly outperform unidimen sional models in all configurations of service operations (captured by several parameters in the simulation and in the presence of noise). Peak cubes are particularly useful in predicting customer dissatisfaction due to the peak-end rule in service operations scenarios with higher proba bilities of failures in which customers are sensitive to multidimensional service failures (i.e., multiple non-zero service failures of different types) even with the smallest magnitudes. Using Shapley values in coalitional game theory, the resulting models can also identify the combinations of service failures that should be averted given their potential for customer defection.

This paper makes several important contributions at the intersection of behavioral economics, service science, and computational analysis. First, we present a methodology for implementing the peak-cube idea in multidimensional service operations scenarios. Second, we present an algorithm to learn peak cubes from multidimensional time series data in service operations. Third, we show how predictive models using multi dimensional peak-cube models significantly outperform traditional models in different service operations scenarios. Fourth, our method ology applies Shapley values to the defection detection models to inform service designers about the quality dimensions that are critical from the perspective of multidimensional peak-end heuristic and customer satisfaction. Finally, we show how ideas in behavioral economics, database, and service science can be integrated to building a new research stream in the area of customer experience analytics with ap plications to firms in a wide range of industries.

Research limitations include the use of simulations to present the results of our peak cube methodology with detailed algorithms. Due to confidentiality issues, we are unable to report on the data obtained while working with a major logistics company. The simulated data are a proximate replica of the real data. Also, while we have worked specif ically in the logistics industry, similar scenarios exist in entertainment, telecommunications, hospitality and healthcare—all significant service industries that are moving toward enhancing customer experiences with better use of data analytics. As an additional contribution to the com munity we will make openly available the entire code base for both constructing peak cubes and generating various simulation scenarios to enable the testing of multidimensional vs unidimensional peak-end rule manifestations in a variety of customer data so that other researchers can build on our work and adapt it to different industries.

The peak cube model affords many exciting new research directions. In addition to building models that help monitor customer dissatisfaction due to multidimensional peak-end effects (which is this paper’s focus), service science studies can use the peak cube construct to apply the multidimensional peak-end rule to designing both continuous and discrete service experiences to maximize customer satisfaction. Peak cubes also allow service scholars to extend relevant research questions from unidimensional to multidimensional service experience profiles. For example, researchers can use service level peak cubes to examine the sequence effects (i.e., peak, end, trend, and spread effects) on customer satisfaction in service settings with multiple service levels. As an illus tration for two-dimensional service sequences, we could replace service failures $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , S F _ { 1 }$ and SF ) with service levels $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , S L _ { 1 }$ and SL ) in Fig. 3. Following Dixon & Verma [14], researchers can examine if service se quences that end with a two-dimensional service level peak (denoted by solid rectangles in Fig. 3) yield better customer satisfaction, or if the slope of the least squares regression line through the number of events two-dimensional service level peaks has any impact on customer satisfaction.

In addition to behavioral economics and service design, the sug gested peak cube construct can potentially contribute to other disciplines. Broadly, the application of peak cubes will support rigorous analyses of any setting with multidimensional time series data accom panied by a dependent variable of interest.
