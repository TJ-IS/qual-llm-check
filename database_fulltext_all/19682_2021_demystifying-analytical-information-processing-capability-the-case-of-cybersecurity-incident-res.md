## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Analytical information processing capability Incident response Business analytics Information processing theory Cybersecurity Information security management

## A B S T R A C T

Little is known about how organizations leverage business analytics (BA) to develop, process, and exploit analytical information in cybersecurity incident response (CSIR). Drawing on information processing theory (IPT), we conducted a field study using a multiple case study design to answer the following research question: How do organizations exploit analytical information in the process of cybersecurity incident response by using business analytics? We developed a theoretical framework that explains how organizations respond to the dy namic cyber threat environment by exploiting analytical information processing capability in the CSIR process. This, in turn, leads to positive outcomes in enterprise security performance, delivering both strategic and financial benefits. Our findings contribute to the BA and cybersecurity literature by providing useful insights into BA applications and the facilitation of analytics-driven decision making in CSIR. Further, they contribute to IPT by providing new insights about analytical information needs, mechanisms to seek analytical information, and analytical information use in the process of CSIR.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations are leveraging business analytics (BA) capabilities to exploit data and secure competitive advantage [1,2]. Analytical infor mation refers to the insights (quantitative or qualitative) used by busi. ness executives in their analysis and decision-making tasks [3]. The predominant use of analytical information has been to understand customer behaviors [4], design better marketing campaigns [5] and improve supply chain performance [6]. To date, there is limited research on exploitation of analytical information for cybersecurity incident response (CSIR). According to Kavanagh et al. [7,p.35], “The greatest area of unmet need is effective targeted attack and breach detection. Orga nizations are failing at early breach detection, with more than 92% of breaches undetected by the breached organization. The situation can be improved with better threat intelligence, the addition of behavior profiling and better analytics". CSIR is an information intensive process which requires organizations to tap into all available information to help them discover the potential of a threat, detect the actual attack quickly, gather intel ligence about the attack, and then execute an enterprise-wide response before the attack becomes significant.

Evidence of analytical information use in CSIR is mostly anecdotal [8] and application specific (see for example, studies of analytics use in developing situation awareness of cybersecurity incidents [9], moni toring cybersecurity alerts for anomaly detection [10], analyzing network traffic for detecting advanced persistent threats [11], and analysis of server logs for intrusion detection [12]). It is not vet suffi. ciently clear what analytical information is needed, which mechanisms should be used to seek analytical information for CSIR and how com panies should evolve to develop and implement skills and procedures to leverage the analytical information and thereby compete in the evolving cyber threat environment.

Given this current state of analytical information use in CSIR, our study investigates the factors associated with a firm’s exploitation of an overall analytical information processing capability (AIPC) for decision making in CSIR. Using information processing theory (IPT), we examine the role of BA capabilities in the exploitation of analytical information in CSIR. Our research question is How do organizations exploit analytical information in the process of cybersecurity incident response by using business analytics?

To answer this question, we compared in-depth data from three large global financial organizations as they used BA to exploit analytical in formation in their CSIR process. Integrating insights from this data with extant literature informed a framework that explains how strong BA capabilities enhance firms’ AIPC which, in turn, improves overall en terprise security performance by delivering strategic and financial benefits.

In the next section of this paper, we discuss the theoretical back ground and develop a conceptualization of AIPC in CSIR. Then we illustrate our case study by describing details of the study design and the data analysis procedure. Subsequently, we present the results and a discussion of the research findings and limitations, as well as directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

This section provides a review of the literature pertaining to the IPT as well as AIPC and CSIR. First, we explain the key concepts of IPT linking them with BA. Then we discuss the intersection of AIPC and BA as a research gap. Finally, we conceptualize AIPC in CSIR which pro vides the theoretical basis for our theoretical framework.

## 2.1. Information processing theory

IPT considers information as one of the most significant enterprise resources. IPT has three important concepts - information processing needs, information processing capability, and the fit between the two to obtain optimal performance. Organizations need quality information to deal with uncertainty and to improve their decision making [13,14]. Uncertainty is conceptualized as the absence of information [15] or “the difference between the amount of information required to perform the task and the amount of information already possessed by the organization” [13, p.28]. The key goal for organizations is to manage uncertainty (such as task complexity, dynamism, and the rate of environmental change) through deploying mechanisms of information processing [13,16]. Or ganizations confronted with high levels of uncertainty are assumed to acquire more information to reduce uncertainty [15]. That is why IPT emphasizes the importance of matching information processing needs (the amount of information about organizational activities needed to address uncertainty) with information processing capabilities (the ability to capture, integrate, and analyze data and information, and use the insights gained in the context of organizational decision-making): the greater the task uncertainty, the greater the amount of informa tion to be processed [17].

IPT posits that organizations should design their structures, mecha nisms and business processes in such a manner that facilitates infor mation processing and thereby enables informed decision-making, cost reduction, and improved organizational performance [13,16]. Infor mation processing mechanisms are effective if they can manage the amount and type of information required in a given problem context. Information processing capabilities are created through a combination of organizational and technological resources, and effective mechanism are associated with high performance levels [15]. Mechanisms that address uncertainty are supposed to optimize the amount of information available to the decision maker [15]. Hence. different combinations of information processing mechanisms are needed for different decision process contexts. For instance, Premkumar et al. [18] showed that the interactive effect of information processing needs and information processing capabilities has a significant effect on performance in an interorganizational supply chain context. Wang et al. [19] demonstrated that there is a positive relationship between inter-firm information processing capability and supply chain company performance. An or ganization is expected to be more effective when its information pro cessing capabilities are matched to the amount of uncertainty they face [16]. Extending these arguments to matching analytical information processing needs with AIPC to deal with uncertain cyber threat envi ronments, our research seeks to explore how organizations exploit analytical information in the process of CSIR using BA.

## 2.2. Intersection of AIPC and business analytics

BA is a core capability that most organizations are investing heavily in today to exploit analytical information and gain competitive advan tage [1]. Analytical information includes all the information an orga nization can use comprehensively to help its business executives in their analysis and decision-making tasks [4]. AIPC, in particular, refers to the extent to which firms use BA practices and applications that analyze critical business data to gain a better understanding of the business and to make timely business decisions [3]. Building on the concept of developing and exploiting AIPC using BA, it can be broken down into four distinct types: (1) descriptive analytics; (2) predictive analytics; (3) prescriptive analytics and (4) real-time analytics [14,20]. Each type offers different insights that can help managers to make important business decisions [21]. It is useful to differentiate between these types as there are implications for the people, process, technology, and ar chitecture required to exploit AIPC.

Descriptive analytics uses historical data to provide trending infor mation on past or current business events to answer “What has happened?” questions [22,23]. Predictive analytics provides insight that exceed beyond using historical data as the principal basis for business decisions and answers “What could happen?” questions. Pre scriptive analytics explores a set of possible actions and suggests a reliable path to an optimal solution based on descriptive and predictive analyses of complex data and answers “What should we do?” questions [22,23]. Though the final decision is up to business managers, pre scriptive analytics solutions provide the most reliable path to an optimal solution for business needs or resolution of operational problems. Finally, real-time analytics allows the analysis and monitoring of busi ness events as they happen and answers “What is happening right now?” questions [24].

Researchers largely agree on what BA is and how its different types enable organizations to better understand the business environment and improve their strategic decision-making. However, we do not know how BA can be employed strategically to exploit AIPC. Nor do we understand how organizations leverage AIPC in CSIR to deal with dynamic and uncertain cyber threat environments.

## 2.3. Conceptualizing AIPC in cybersecurity incident response

CSIR is a continuous process that enables organizations to detect, analyze, eradicate, and recover from potential cybersecurity incidents in a timely and cost-effective manner [25]. Organizations usually build a Security Operations Center (SOC) which is a centralized location where CSIR teams monitor, detect, analyze and respond to cybersecurity in cidents, typically on a 24/7/365 basis [25].

The process of responding to cybersecurity incidents is a complex, nuanced activity carried out with high levels of uncertainty caused by the dynamic nature of cybersecurity threats that are unpredictable, complex and evolving in nature [26,27]. Therefore, the access to analytical information and actionable insights related to cyber threats that have happened or are happening, has a direct impact on how the incident unfolds and ultimately its impact on the organization. Orga nizations that perceive their cyber threat environment to be uncertain will tend to scan more for analytical information using BA to make sense of the environment, to create new knowledge, and to make informed decisions [14,16]. Organizations need analytical information about cybersecurity threats to address uncertainty in their cyber threat land scape. To fulfill their analytical information needs, organizations leverage their analytical information seeking methods and processes such as Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) to identify, monitor, analyze and record cybersecurity events or incidents within their cyber threat environment [15]. Finally, organizations must leverage the insights that emerge from analytical information processing in the context of CSIR decision-making [3,17]. Effective organizations are those that fit their analytical information processing needs, seeking mechanisms, and usage to the amount of uncertainty they face in their cyber threat environment [14].

Evidence of analytical information use in CSIR is mostly anecdotal and application specific. For example, Ahmad et al. [9] highlighted the role of analytics-driven reporting in developing situation awareness of cybersecurity incidents; Pierazzi et al. [10] explained the use of ana lytics in monitoring cybersecurity alerts for anomaly detection; Mar chetti et al. [11] described the use of analytics in analyzing high volumes of network data for detecting advanced persistent threats; and Juvonen et al. [12] noted the critical role of analytics in analyzing server logs for intrusion detection. This stream of research has generated valuable in sights on the CSIR process offering significant implications for practi tioners. However, the theoretical foundations underpinning the exploitation of an overall AIPC in CSIR and its impact on overall en terprise security performance remain unexamined. Very little is known about the needs, seeking mechanisms and usage of analytical informa tion in the CSIR process. To address this gap, based on IPT, we propose a theoretical framework (see Fig. 1) that links analytical information processing needs, seeking mechanisms, and usage in the CSIR process to address the dynamic cyber threat environment with the enterprise se curity performance. ## 3. Research methodology

Given the nascent nature of research on exploiting analytical infor mation in CSIR, we chose to use a multiple case study methodology based on the case study protocols and guidelines specified in [28,29]. The unit of analysis is the incident response function of each organiza tion. We considered each case as a distinct analytical unit and built a theoretical framework from the case studies by recursive cycling among the case data, emerging theory, and extant literature [29,30].

According to Gioia et al. [28,p.24], during an inductive theory building process ‘existing ideas or theories’ may be used to explain some of the findings. We subscribed to Gioia et al.’s [28,p.22] view that using elements of existing theory might be of significant help in examining ‘not only all the major emergent concepts, themes, and dimensions, but also in explaining their dynamic inter-relationships’. Therefore, we used seed concepts from the literature on IPT and BA capabilities, which provided the theoretical framing necessary for developing an under standing of how organizations exploit AIPC in the CSIR process using BA. Although, IPT was used as the sensitizing tool, we inferred our claims and findings from, and ground them in, the empirical data.

## 3.1. Research context and case selection

Theoretical sampling [28] was used to select organizations with the most sophisticated cybersecurity and BA solutions such as: SIEM, reporting and analysis tools. We sought participants who possessed a wide repertoire of in-depth knowledge and experiences in using BA in the CSIR process. Twenty-seven participants, drawn from three financial sector organizations, took part in our study (see Appendix A). Each case organization was a successful company with a high market share, employing more than 25,000 employees. The financial sector organi zations were selected as they face a rapidly changing cyber threat landscape. Each organization developed a BA capability to exploit analytical information in their CSIR process. In addition, all the orga nizations were at high levels of maturity in both their CSIR process and in their practice of BA. To ensure the confidentiality of the case partic ipants, we used A, B, and C to label each firm.

## 3.2. Data collection

Data was collected by conducting semi-structured interviews in each organization. Relevant background information documents (including strategic progress reports, organizational charts, PowerPoint pre sentations and meeting agendas) were also made available and served a complementary material. In addition, information from the organiza tions’ websites and industry analyses reports was also extracted which allowed us to triangulate the understanding of each organization's CSIR function context, strategy, practices, and outcomes. In total, 27 in terviews were conducted. To triangulate insights from different sources [30], participants at various levels in the cybersecurity unit were interviewed including: 1) strategic top-level managers, 2) middle-level senior managers, 3) cybersecurity analysts, and 4) data scientists working with CSIR function.

Data collection and analysis was conducted progressively in an iterative fashion. We discussed the research objectives with the top-level managers who were interviewed first. They then helped to identify additional participants (see Appendix B for participant details) who were selected for their extensive experience in both the cybersecurity and analytics domains with sufficient knowledge to provide insights on behalf of their organization. Interviews were recorded and transcribed with the consent of the participants and each lasted between one and two hours. At the end of the interviews, the participants were asked for further reflections on examples they gave during the interview. Exten sive notes were made during the interviews for reference during data analysis. Data collection and analysis were undertaken until ‘theoretical saturation’ [30] was reached, wherein further data provided no new insights.

## 3.3. Data analysis

Guidelines from Gioia et al. [29] were used to seek qualitative rigor and systematically transfer raw data into theoretical interpretations. The process of data analysis was iterative, to improve insights and general izability [28]. We initially reviewed the background materials, inter view transcripts and field notes to look for indicators of how CSIR teams exploit analytical information using BA to deal with the dynamic cyber threat landscape in their everyday practices. We employed constant comparative techniques and the combination of open, axial and selec tive coding [31] to analyze interview data.

In the first phase, we used “induction” as the logical data analysis process and did initial coding to develop first-order concepts from the data by adhering to participants’ wording and terms [29]. The codes captured concepts such as ‘continuous monitoring’, ‘threat hunting’, ‘cybersecurity awareness’, and ‘threat intelligence’. These codes were assigned to words, sentences or even paragraphs in the margins of the interview transcripts. The process of interviewing and coding data continued until no further distinct, shared patterns among the data were found; i.e. theoretical saturation was reached [30]. Overall, we assigned 150 codes to describe the role of BA in exploiting AIPC in the CSIR process.

In the next phase, we used “abduction”; that is, we relied on the existing literature and our knowledge to analyze and develop concepts that explained the data. We reanalyzed the data by acting as knowl edgeable agents and using researcher-centric concepts. Focusing on the deep structure underlying the first-order concepts and the similarities and differences between them, we reduced the first-order concepts to more abstract second-order themes. In total, we identified 13 secondorder themes. During the final phase, the data was further analyzed by investigating the possibility of aggregating the concepts identified in second-order themes to form higher level more abstract concepts (aggregate dimensions). By doing so, the 13 second-order themes were combined into five aggregate dimensions that captured the overarching concepts relevant for understanding the role of BA in exploiting AIPC in the CSIR process.

Appendix C provides an example of the data structure that was developed after several iterations of analyzing the data from first-order concepts to second-order themes and then to final aggregate dimensions. Using the data structure, we revalidated the final concepts back to the underlying data and established a clear connection between data, the emerging concepts, and the aggregate dimensions. By keeping the voices of both informants and researchers, we could rigorously develop detailed and accurate definitions of concepts from the data.

## 4. Backgrounds of case organizations

We describe the background on the development and exploitation of AIPC in each firm.

Firm A: Firm A employs more than 60,000 employees worldwide and is in the top 50 banks in the world. Approximately ten years ago, Firm A established a dedicated BA center that works collaboratively with the business functions. Additionally, Firm A also understands the importance of CSIR, with top management committed to achieving a distinctive analytical and CSIR capability that enables business units to perform their daily operations securely whils effectively meeting performance objectives.

At Firm A, analytical practices were integrated into the cybersecurity function to nurture a data-driven decision-making culture in cyberse curity management. Firm A’s cybersecurity strategy includes SOC, SIEM and a custom-built advanced cyber analytics solution that helps to generate insights based on security data and take informed actions. Firm A exploits analytical information in all the phases of their CSIR process (preparation, response, and follow-up). Some example usage cases include: access management (e.g. in blocking and logging of unautho rized access); forensic analysis (e.g. analyzing network data to monitor network activity in real-time); and analysis of logs (e.g. service and application logs, operating system logs, network device logs and network flows).

Firm B: Firm B is a large multinational insurance company, employing over 25,000 personnel. Eight years ago, Firm B imple mented its first corporate data warehouse. Since 2014, Firm B has invested heavily in building a data-driven decision-making capa bility within their cybersecurity function.

Firm B has a dedicated analytics team that built their security ana lytics capability. Firm B’s cybersecurity strategy includes SOC, SIEM, and a managed security service provider. Firm B also exploits analytical information using BA in all phases of the CSIR process. Some examples where Firm B’s CSIR team uses BA include security log analysis; access management (e.g., managing access across all its systems and assets in real-time); and malware analysis.

Firm C: Firm C is a multinational financial services company that has branches worldwide and has more than 35,000 employees. Firm C’s cybersecurity division supports multiple business domains with a dedicated SOC and analytics team that works collaboratively with each business function. The cybersecurity analytics division was established in 2014 primarily to address the insider threat and data leakage issues of the organization.

Firm C’s cybersecurity strategy includes a combination of SOC and SIEM systems. Firm C has developed an advanced cyber analytics capability that delivers insights to security executives so that they can take informed actions. Firm C also uses BA to exploit analytical infor mation in all phases of the CSIR process. Some example usage cases include: insider threat detection; employee activity monitoring; and analysis and monitoring emerging cyber threats.

## 5.1. Dynamic cyber threat environment

The cyber threat landscape in which the case organizations operated shaped their analytical information needs, seeking mechanisms and usage. The dynamic, complex, and evolving nature of cyber threats, in turn, compelled these organizations to develop AIPC in their CSIR pro cess. AIPC can be developed through a combination of people, process, and technology to help turn data into insights which in turn drive informed business decisions and actions. The Director of Global Cyber Forensics in Firm C explained this challenge as follows: Sophisticated, well-resourced advanced persistent threat actors are impossible to stop from intruding. Having advanced analytics capability means that we have the ability to capture and use far more data than we could before and therefore understand these types of threats in a better way.

Firm A’s Head of the Cybersecurity Incident Response Team high lighted that advanced attackers use sophisticated analytical technolo gies and dealing with them requires an advanced analytics capability: Advanced attackers are using sophisticated analytical technologies such as artificial intelligence and machine learning. Not going down the same path in [cyber] defense potentially puts our organization further behind the at tackers. The case organizations responded to the dynamic cyber threat environment, which offered uncertainty and dynamism characterized by both predictable and unpredictable threats, by exploiting AIPC in the CSIR process using BA.

## 5.2. Analytical information processing capability in incident response

Most changes in the cyber threat landscape originate from two sources: 1) from the ways in which organizations create, collect, store, and share information and 2) from the innovative ways attackers exploit enterprise systems and informational assets. The exposure of this in formation across multiple targets (e.g. networks, data, and physical components) makes a tempting target for hackers and criminals. The General Manager of Cybersecurity Risk Management in Firm A noted that AIPC enabled analysis of cybersecurity threats and incidents from multiple sources and found patterns between different threats and in cidents that would otherwise remain undetected: Advanced cyber ana lytics helps us in collection, analysis and correlation of information from multiple data sources such as IPS, IDS, SIEM, spam and antivirus software, and network and application logs so that we can gain visibility into the most pertinent insights related to cybersecurity risks and can make informed ac tions accordingly.

Firm C’s Chief Security Architect elaborated on how AIPC enables them to analyze cybersecurity events as they occur and to determine which events require immediate attention and what assets are impacted by the incident: We continuously analyze events and logs to detect something potentially that is suspicious. Something that may or may not be an incident. In case of an incident, we are talking about something that has happened or is happening. So, we want to look at all types of events and rule out the false positives. And then determine which ones are real incidents or breaches and being able to address those through the incident response process.

## 5.2.1. Analytical information needs

Cybersecurity managers need actionable insights faster than ever before to reduce risk, to meet compliance requirements, and to detect and respond to cybersecurity threats in an efficient and timely manner. The General Manager of Cybersecurity Risk Management in Firm A explained that they needed different types of analytical insights (e.g. descriptive, forensic, predictive, and real-time) to analyze cybersecurity events: We have moved from basically a historical-oriented analytical capability in which we analyzed the past events and were reactive to a more proactive real-time analysis of data in which we are managing the perimeter at real-time based on the information that we see…we now have the ability to consume the historical insights, analyze them, and then try to use that in formation on a real-time basis and separately determine how best to manage for the future.

Analytical information is required in almost every part of the CSIR process with most stakeholders (e.g. chief information security officer, cybersecurity architects, analysts, and managers) needing to collect, use, and analyze data to shape their roles and strategies. That is why the key decision makers in the case organizations used BA to analyze streaming data from various sources to detect malicious activities. The General Manager of Data and Analytics in Firm A noted that: With analytics, we are focused on incident detection. For example, using stream processing of data from network flows, sensors, and meta data we look for suspicious or anomalous network activities that strongly indicate a security incident is in progress.

The Director of Global Cyber Forensics in Firm C explained that each decision maker in the CSIR process needs different analytical informa tion depending on what their role and goals are: From managing compliance from a specific set of cybersecurity applications and detecting insider threats to calculating the asset values that are at risk, different stakeholders in the cybersecurity incident response process will be looking for different outcomes when they leverage analytics.

When asked about how they used descriptive and forensic insights in the CSIR process a Data Scientist in Firm B noted that: Using descriptive analytics, we integrate raw data from multiple sources to understand what has happened and get valuable insights about the events that happened in the past. However, with forensic analytics, we are less interested in knowing what has happened in the past in general but rather more focused on understanding and discovering why something happened. So, we are looking for processes and causes, instead of the results. Thus, cybersecurity managers and an alysts involved in CSIR decision-making require all four types of analytical insights (descriptive, forensic, predictive, and real-time). In the next section, we explain the mechanisms employed to seek these analytical insights.

## 5.2.2. Mechanisms for analytical information seeking

Four key mechanisms were identified in the CISR process when stakeholders were seeking analytical information: dynamic risk assess ment, threat hunting, anomaly detection and continuous monitoring.

Dynamic risk assessment: The case organizations were increasingly looking for proactive methods to respond to cybersecurity incidents dynamically and to gain more visibility into their cybersecurity threat environment. The General Manager of Cybersecurity Risk Management in Firm A explained that tracking the key risk in dicators through dynamic risk assessment is one of the key mecha nisms to seek analytical information: In my own team’s roadmap, the priority is to improve the measurement of metrics and report the coverage and effectiveness of our security controls and do dynamic risk assessment by looking at what we call as Key Risk Indicators.

The case organizations discussed two types of key risk indicators: lag indicators and lead indicators. The Manager of Cybersecurity Strategy and Governance in Firm B explained each: Lag indicators provide statistic around (a) this is how much spam we received last month, (b) this is the number of desktops that have been affected by malware, (c) and this is how many malicious URLs that were accessed. But they are after the event and those are called lag indicators. They tell us something bad that happened which we detected. What lead indicators tell us is how things are working and can give us some information about the future so that we can take pre-emptive actions. So, for example, the lead indicator for us can be the number of people that requested a security exemption to formally be non-compliant with a security policy or standard.

The case organizations also measured and reviewed their risk man agement and analytical capabilities against standard frameworks and industry peers to proactively improve their risk assessment process. As the General Manager of Cybersecurity Strategy and Governance in Firm B noted: So, by virtue of our teams periodically reviewing and measuring our capabilities against the framework and against our industry peers, we are demonstrating that we are proactively trying to continue to improve our ca pabilities to stay one step ahead of the threats. Thus, BA enabled the case organizations to undertake risk assessment dynamically by helping them analyze and monitor the cybersecurity risks and threats using key risk indicators that provide them with timely information regarding risk exposure, emerging risk trends, and changes in risk profile of the organization.

Threat hunting: The use of BA in the process of cyber threat hunting has become incredibly important in the case organizations as it helped in executing rapid response against complex threats and in mitigating potential damage resulting from cyberattacks. The Head of the Cybersecurity Incident Response Team in Firm A noted that: Threat hunting is the process of proactive and iterative detection, isola tion, and mitigation of advanced threats that are not detected by tradi tional security controls.

When asked about what type of data sources, analytical tools and solutions are used in the process of cyber threat hunting, a Senior Cybersecurity Analyst in Firm A stated that: We use the combination of security monitoring tools including data loss prevention, network security monitoring, network intrusion detection, antivirus software, firewalls and insider threat detection…We also use SIEM solutions and analytics solutions for statistical and intelligence analysis to identify malicious activities and track down adversaries.

The Manager of IT and Information Security in Firm B highlighted the role of descriptive and real-time analytics in the threat hunting process. Analytical applications that process data in batches deliver historical insights based on lagging indicators, whilst the insights generated by real-time analytical platforms deliver insights that are more current and predictive in nature as they are based on leading in dicators: If I make a comparison of using batch style analytics with real-time analytics, it may take hours or even days to yield results using batch style analytics. So, our batch analytical applications are very good in delivering “after the fact” insights [lag indicators]. In contrast, the insights that we get from analyzing lead key risk indicators allow us to get ahead of the curve.

Anomaly detection: In addition to using automated BA decisionmaking features in CSIR, it is also used in other subject areas (e.g. anomaly and fraud detection), as these demand a low latency response. The General Manager of Cybersecurity Risk Management in Firm A noted that: Recently, we have added the automated response capability in our security event processing solution. It can execute auto mated response or actions like flagging, filtering, transforming, and alerting of cybersecurity events as they arrive. A response might be executed based on sophisticated criteria, such as anomaly detection models. We not only use this in our cybersecurity incident detection and response, but also in fraud detection and recommendation, as these de mand lowest latency response.

The Manager of IT and Information Security in Firm B explained the goal of cybersecurity solutions that can make automated decisions for anomaly detection using BA as follows: The goal is to create an analytics solution that can screen and identify threats and anomalies, and when a threat is detected trigger automated corrective actions. For example, our advanced threat detection systems automatically generate threat intelligence alerts that are passed to our threat prevention systems for immediate blocking. … This kind of actionable intelligence needed rethinking and even retooling of our cybersecurity infrastructure.

The Head of Global Insider Threat Detection in Firm C noted that the CSIR process cannot be fully automated and requires the involvement of cybersecurity managers to investigate, analyze, and confirm the threats, particularly when the threats are dynamic and unpredictable such as a malicious insider theat. Given the sensitive nature of cybersecurity issues, the incident detection and response process cannot be fully automated and security managers still need to investigate and confirm threats, particularly when the threats are internal.

Thus, automated decision-making is one of the key features of BA used in the CSIR process for anomaly detection. The goal of using automation in the CSIR process is to swiftly detect and respond to cybersecurity incidents. However, the involvement of humans (cyber security managers) is critical as the complexity of CSIR requires human input during the process of assessing the impact, scope, and severity of the cyberattacks.

Continuous monitoring: The case organizations used two specific types of real-time analytics (on demand and continuous) in their CSIR process for continuous monitoring and analysis of cybersecurity events from disparate sources. The General Manager of Data and Analytics in Firm A differentiated between these two types as fol lows: Analysis on demand is a reactive approach in which a user or system requests a query to further investigate and understand what is happening right now…whereas continuous data analysis is a proactive approach that alerts users or triggers actions based on the events as they occur.

Similarly, the Manager of Cybersecurity Strategy and Governance in Firm B explained that continuous monitoring and data analysis helps to analyze cybersecurity events as they occur and proactively detects and prevents incidents before they happen: We use continuous monitoring systems (SIEM) to analyze cybersecurity events in which we do event and log collection and correlation. This helps us to separate real events [incidents] from nonimpact events. In addition, this also helps us to locate and contain cybersecurity incidents.

The Director of Global Cyber Forensics in Firm C elaborated that they use both on demand and continuous data analysis to combat advanced cybersecurity threats by analyzing log data from security systems and integrating it with external threat intelligence feeds to generate comprehensive cybersecurity insights. To combat advanced cybersecurity threats, such as sensitive data exfiltration and zero-day malware, we use both on demand and continuous real-time analytics to analyze all network traffic, log data from applications, security systems, and network data and also fuse external threat intelligence in real-time. Therefore, both on demand and continuous real-time analytics have their time and place within CSIR. Continuous data analysis is more proactive as it alerts the incident response teams with continuous updates in real-time. On demand data analysis is reactive as it waits for cybersecurity managers to request a query and then delivers insights.

## 5.3. Analytical information use

The four key mechanisms for seeking analytical information through BA helped the case organizations to enhance their cybersecurity awareness, understand adversary behavior, generate cyber threat in telligence, and enable proactive CSIR strategies. We explain each in detail below.

Enhance cybersecurity awareness: The integration and consolidation of information from the different cybersecurity systems (such as SIEM, log management, intrusion detection, and prevention systems) with threat intelligence feeds and its analysis using monitoring dash boards helped the case organizations to improve cybersecurity awareness of their cybersecurity managers. As the General Manager of Data and Analytics in Firm A noted: We have deployed intrusion prevention, log management and advanced SIEM systems with correlation capabilities to consolidate threat intelligence feeds into monitoring dash boards and alerting systems. The effort that we have put in consolidating this information has resulted in improved situational awareness of ac tivities, users, and systems as well as awareness of the attacks being attempted on our networks.

A Cybersecurity Senior Manager in Firm A highlighted the impor tance of sharing cybersecurity related information in a structured way to develop cybersecurity awareness as follows: Being a Senior Manager, I need to be aware of not only any particular cybersecurity incident but also of the whole cyber threat landscape. In this aspect, my cybersecurity awareness is both global and local. This can only happen if we share cybersecurity related information in a structured way.

Finally, the analytics-driven decision-making culture in the case or ganizations has resulted in improved cybersecurity awareness which has empowered cybersecurity managers to make timely and productive decisions. The Director of Global Cyber Forensics in Firm C stated: So, the analytics driven decision making has developed a culture in such a way that we are able to establish a much firmer tone of management intent, much clearer guidelines around what our risk tolerance or risk appetite is, and also being able to deliver meaningful insights that can empower the decision makers to take the appropriate timely actions. Thus, the key mechanisms for analytical information seeking such as continuous monitoring, dy namic risk assessment and timely integrated and consolidated infor mation sharing among cybersecurity resources help organizations improve awareness in CSIR.

Generate cyber threat intelligence: Organizations cannot successfully respond to cybersecurity attacks unless they develop a comprehen sive understanding of their cybersecurity threat landscape. BA hel ped the case organizations generate cyber threat intelligence to better understand what threats are coming their way and how they should respond to them. The Director of Global Cyber Forensics in Firm C explained this as follows: Cyber threat intelligence is the analyzed information that helps us understand the capability, opportunity and intent of malicious actors…Intent reflects the desire of a malicious actor in targeting our assets, capability is the means used in the malicious activity, and opportunity is the vulnerabilities that a malicious actor can exploit…analyzing this information is extremely important and cyber threat intelligence gives us this information.

The General Manager of Cybersecurity Strategy and Governance in Firm B explained that the ability to analyze data and extract anomalies that may indicate an active threat in the network is another example of identifying cyber threats using real-time threat intelligence: On an operational point of view, we also have processes where we are proactive in addressing the [cyber] threat landscape. We have continuous threat intelli gence feeds from networks in which we use analytics to analyze what is happening and detect anomalies. Thus, the case organizations can use BA capabilities to generate cyber threat intelligence (on both external and internal cyber threats) helping them to detect cyberattacks as they happen and to respond to them in a timely manner.

Enable proactive incident response: The most challenging part of the CSIR process in the case organizations was the timely and accurate detection and assessment of possible cybersecurity incidents, and then the selection and execution of the most appropriate response strategy. The General Manager of Cybersecurity Strategy and Governance in Firm B noted that analytical information helps them to detect cybersecurity incidents during the reconnaissance phase (before the organization is attacked): Our incident response team uses intelligence feeds to understand the procedures, tactics and techniques of the attackers and can stop some attacks by degrading or disrupting their efforts. In this way, analytics helps us in detecting an incident during the reconnaissance phase, that is, before we have actually been attacked.

The Head of the Cybersecurity Incident Response Team in Firm A explained how they have designed their CSIR strategy around the cyber kill chain model that helps them to focus on the different stages of an attack and thereby execute a proactive response strategy: We have designed our cybersecurity incident monitoring and response plan based on the cyber kill chain model that identifies the sequence of stages which an attacker must pass through to reach the desired goal. The incident response type and execution of recommended actions from the playbook are dependent on the stage where the attack was detected. They further elaborated with examples of different response types and the importance of cyber threat intelligence in cyberattack investigation as follows: Sometimes denial of service attacks can be a diversion from another serious attack, and they are hard to detect. Our SOC team actively monitors and investigates all the relevant activity and applies threat intelligence before taking any counter attacking actions…similarly multistage attacks like APT’s are the most difficult to defend against. Therefore, it is crucial for them [SOC team] to analyze the events in a larger context by incorporating the latest threat in telligence updates.

Understand adversary behavior: As cybersecurity threats evolve rapidly, the case organizations increasingly shifted from a reactive approach to a proactive approach using user behavior analytics to better understand cyber threats before an attacker can cause damage. The Head of Global Insider Threat Detection in Firm C noted that: Behavioral analytics requires integration of data from a variety of different sources and using this we are interested in extracting anomalies that may indicate an active threat within the network. The types of behavior being analyzed include user behavior, data behavior, and application behavior. The Chief Security Architect in Firm C explained: User behavior is about how the users are operating within the network, where they are located, the devices they are accessing, and the sequence of their data access points throughout their operation…data behavior is about the behavior of data itself, for example, how the data is manipulated or moved or transferred within the network…Application behavior is about the behavior of an application both during its normal operations and the behavior impact it may be having on other applications in the enterprise ecosystem.

## 5.4. Enterprise security performance

This section examines how the case organizations reaped strategic and financial benefits and improved their overall enterprise security performance as a result of exploiting AIPC in the CSIR.

The Manager of Cybersecurity Strategy and Governance in Firm B stated that: The concept of cybersecurity risk management is so that we can operate our business processes in a manner with some assurance that we can be very confident in doing our daily business operations. The analogy is like where you have brakes on a car, it is the brakes that are there to allow you to go faster, it is not to slow you down. So, the benefit of using analytics is that we can manage our risks appropriately and we can have the confidence to innovate and operate our business processes in a manner that they can be very effective and efficient.

Similarly, the Manager of Cybersecurity Strategy and Governance in Firm B highlighted that using analytics helped them to develop more efficient and robust user access models and thereby improved their ac cess management: I think the real benefits that we are seeing from cyber security analytics right now in terms of the immediate future is helping our access management. So, our immediate opportunity that we see in applying more advanced analytics techniques is to try and develop more efficient and robust access models. That will then help us progressively reduce our risk of things like fraud or accidental disclosure of information.

The Head of the Cybersecurity Incident Response Team at Firm A raised two critical questions that drive the overall cost in the CSIR process and explained that: If we analyze the cybersecurity incident response process carefully, there are two questions that drive overall cost in the incident response process, (1) once the attacker first gains access to the network, how long does it take to detect the intrusion?; and (2) once we have detected the intrusion or incident, how quickly can we execute a response and remediate the incident? Advanced analytics helps us in addressing both questions by reducing the time it takes to detect and respond to cybersecurity incidents and that can also lead to cost savings and better protection of data.

Finally, the Chief Security Architect of Firm C provided an example of how the use of real-time analytics in the right manner can help or ganizations to gain financial benefits as follows: Organizations who invest in the analytics and do it the right way, of course they can lower their cost. So, if an organization is spending 25% of their budget in dealing with security incidents, breaches and penalties versus another organization that is only spending 10% because they are using the right tools and analytics to prevent incidents, breaches and not paying penalties, that means they already have an economic benefit over their competitors.

In summary, the use of BA to exploit AIPC in the CSIR process in the case organizations has helped them to improve their overall enterprise security performance in two ways: financially, by reducing the cost and time to detect and respond to cybersecurity incidents and strategically, building customer trust, improving cybersecurity awareness and handling cybersecurity incidents proactively.

## 5.5. Illustrating pathways to exploit analytical information processing capability

This section provides the details of the pathways the case organiza tions leveraged to exploit AIPC in CSIR using BA (see Table 2).

The case organizations experienced an uncertain cyber threat land scape characterized by the complex, sophisticated, and dynamic nature of cybersecurity threats that include both predictable threats (e.g. distributed denial of service, trojan attacks, and phishing attacks) and unpredictable threats (e.g. advanced persistent threats, insider data theft, and zero-day attacks). The case organizations needed four specific types of analytical insights to deal with both predictable and unpre dictable threats.

Descriptive insights enabled the case organizations to build an un derstanding of what happened in their cyber threat environment in the past. They were developed by integrating and analyzing historical data from various source systems such as: intrusion detection systems, intrusion prevention systems, network logs, firewall logs, spam, and antivirus logs. The case organizations employed threat hunting and anomaly detection mechanisms to seek the descriptive insights. Security managers used these insights to generate intelligence about cyberse curity threats to improve awareness about their current cyber threat environment in terms of the kinds of cyber threats they are getting (and comparing this to the past), and to understand adversary behavior.

Forensic insights were used to extend the descriptive insights and develop an in-depth understanding of why something specific happened in their cyber threat environment. Anomaly detection and threat hunt ing mechanisms were used to generate forensic insights. These insights helped the case organizations to discover anomalies, their causes, and causal relationships among different cybersecurity threats. They used these insights to generate intelligence about dependencies and patterns among different threats, to improve their cybersecurity awareness regarding predictable cyber threats, and to build profiles of adversaries based on their specific behaviors.

To understand what is happening right now in the cyber threat environment, the case organizations generated real-time insights. They employed a combination of continuous monitoring, anomaly detection, threat hunting, and dynamic risk assessment to seek real-time insights. These insights helped to separate real and serious cybersecurity threats from false positives as well as to find unpredictable and new threats so that they can respond proactively. The cybersecurity analysts and managers used the real-time insights to develop real-time awareness about what is happening on the network, generate real-time intelligence about unpredictable threats, and to execute proactive CSIR.

Predictive insights were used to understand what was likely to happen in the cyber threat environment in the future. A combination of anomaly detection, threat hunting, continuous monitoring, dynamic risk assessment mechanisms, and the findings from descriptive, forensic, and real-time insights were used to generate predictive insights. Cyberse curity managers used these insights to enhance their cybersecurity awareness, to anticipate and forecast future trends, and to craft the “ideal course of action” to proactively deal with unknown and unpre dictable cybersecurity threats. The General Manager of Data and Ana lytics in Firm A explained that predictive insights require high quality data and continuous optimization to make them effective: There are many advantages of predictive insights…but it is essential to understand that forecasting using predictive analytics is just an estimate. The accuracy of the estimate is highly dependent on data quality and modelling technique and that is why predictive models require careful treatment and continuous optimization.

## 6. Discussion of results and conclusion

Cybersecurity has become one of the top issues at the forefront of every executive’s mind. In this paper, we have investigated the under explored perspective of exploiting AIPC using BA in the process of CSIR to address the dynamic and uncertain cybersecurity threat environment. Employing IPT as a lens, we conducted a multiple case study to gain a better understanding of the needs, seeking mechanisms and usage of analytical information in the CSIR process, the role that BA capabilities play in exploiting AIPC and their impact on enterprise security perfor mance. We found that organizations exploit analytical information to generate four distinct types of analytical insights (descriptive, forensic, predictive, and real-time) to meet their analytical information needs. The specific mechanisms that organizations use to seek analytical in formation include threat hunting, anomaly detection, continuous monitoring, and dynamic risk assessment. Lastly, the studied organiza tions use this analytical information to enhance their cybersecurity awareness, enable proactive CSIR, understand adversary behavior, and generate cyber threat intelligence. The exploitation of AIPC in CSIR, in turn, leads to positive outcomes in their overall enterprise security performance by delivering strategic and financial benefits. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our research results in the following subsections.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

Our study advances current knowledge by developing a conceptual understanding underpinned by relevant theories and empirical evidence. A theoretical framework is developed, and it shows that strong BA capabilities help organizations to exploit AIPC which, in turn, im proves overall enterprise security performance by delivering strategic and financial benefits. We have also provided empirical evidence to support the conceptualization.

Based on IPT, we developed a theoretical framework that considers the composition of analytical information processing needs, analytical information seeking mechanisms and analytical information use for the context of CSIR. Using this, we identified specific pathways for exploiting AIPC to address uncertainty and dynamism in CSIR. To our knowledge, this is the first study that applies IPT to conceptualize AIPC and investigates its role in enabling analytics-driven decision-making in incident response using a multiple case study approach. In contrast to previous conceptualizations $[ 1 3 , 1 6 , 1 8 ]$ and single case approaches $[ 9 , 1 5 ]$ to information processing, the conceptualization we use has allowed us to infer empirically grounded insights for exploiting AIPC in CSIR using BA.

We contribute to the IPT view by providing empirical evidence to support the key idea that in order to deal with uncertainty, organizations need to increase their capabilities in gathering, processing, and acting on information from the environment [13,16] in such a manner that fa cilitates information processing and thereby enables informed decisionmaking, subsequently improving enterprise performance [18]. When an organization develops specific strategies, mechanisms, and processes to exploit APIC using BA, it deals with an uncertain cyber threat environ ment in a proactive manner and thereby improves its overall enterprise security performance.

Finally, the current conceptualization of IPT does not specifically deal with analytical information, nor does it break it into distinct types (e.g. descriptive, forensic, predictive, and real-time) and study their roles in addressing dynamic cyber threat environment. We provide new insights about the specific types of analytical information needs, their seeking mechanisms, and use in the CSIR process. Organizations exploit AIPC to generate four distinct types of analytical insights to meet their analytical information needs. The mechanisms organizations use to seek the analytical information include threat hunting, anomaly detection, dynamic risk assessment, and continuous monitoring. Organizations use analytical information to enhance their cybersecurity awareness, un derstand adversary behavior, generate cyber threat intelligence, and enable proactive CSIR. Our findings can form a foundation for future research on the information processing view.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Our implications for incident response teams include the nuanced view of the role of AIPC. Rather than using AIPC at an operational level only to deal with uncertainty in the cyber threat environment, incident response teams need to recognize the more innovative role of AIPC at the strategic level in implementing both protective and detective response strategies. The utilization of the range of capabilities that are enabled by AIPC will help incident response teams implement the combination of prevention, detection and response strategies that can help them to better cope with uncertainty in the dynamic cyber threat landscape.

Cybersecurity and analytics vendors should recognize the potentially far-reaching innovative role that their cybersecurity and analytical so lutions may provide to enterprises. Creating analytical applications that can integrate threat intelligence data, automate investigations and forensic analysis, and apply complex algorithms and behavior analytics to discover potential threats will help their clients implement innovative CSIR strategies that can deal with the dynamic threat environment. The far-reaching use of analytical information in the BA enabled CSIR pro cess (such as enhancing cybersecurity awareness, understanding ad versary behavior, generating cyber threat intelligence, and enabling proactive CSIR) will also increase the demand for data integration, automation, visualization, and analytics. Therefore, vendors who develop cybersecurity solutions need to consider these requirements

when developing their solutions.

## 6.3. Limitations and directions for future research

We designed our case studies, focusing on financial firms so that we could have more generalizable results within the financial sector. However, there is a need for further discussion and validation of our findings. Our results, whilst we think will be generalizable across all organizations, need to be investigated in other contexts involving BA adoption to exploit AIPC as they may only be applicable to the specific characteristics of the CSIR process at the sites we studied. For example, given that we purposely selected financial organizations with exemplar use of BA and CSIR, then that may limit the generalizability of our findings. Organizations that are less mature in BA or CSIR may have different outcomes than reported in our study, and thus, further research into these types of organizations to ascertain if they support, extend, or reject our findings.

Finally, in our study we identified several novel concepts around the analytical information needs, seeking mechanisms and usage of BA in CSIR (e.g. dynamic risk assessment, threat hunting, user behavior ana lytics and proactive CSIR). Whilst these have been initially defined and described, the measurement of these concepts is missing in this study. Operationalizing these concepts in future research and collecting survey data to empirically validate our findings will be important.
