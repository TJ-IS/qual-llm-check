## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Search advertising Keyword selection Broad match decision Keyword specificity Position Search engines

## A B S T R A C T

In paid search advertising firms need to make decisions on a matching strategy between broad match and exact match to target their current and prospective customers. Broad match broadens the scope of targeting while exact match provides more accurate targeting. This paper investigates the relative effectiveness of matching decisions using both secondary data and a field experiment data. Both studies show that compared to exact match, broad match leads to lower advertising performance. Moreover, the negative effect of broad match on CTR is more prominent for more specific or lower (better)-position keywords, and the effects of broad match on CR, revenue and profits are less negative for higher (worse)-position keywords. These results suggest that managers of paid search advertising campaigns should take into account keyword specificity and position in making optimal matching strategy decisions for their keyword portfolios.

## 1. Introduction

Search advertisers use two major matching strategies to match key words that they bid on to search terms that are used by consumers to search for information. The first method is exact match where the keyword and the search term are identical. The second method is broad match where search engines target search firms’ advertisements to consumers whose search terms are different from the keyword bid for by the firm, thus widening the reach of the audience for the ad (Fig. 1 il lustrates the decisions involved in the process of paid search advertising from the advertisers’ perspective). Broad match is somewhat more popular than exact match in the industry. For example, 56% and 70% of clicks on Google and Bing respectively are generated through broad match, while those numbers through exact match are only 33% and 20% [1].

This growing popularity of broad match is due to two reasons. First, it enables advertisers to account for uncertainty in consumer search terms. 40% of searches on search engines are too refined for advertisers to predict using only exact match [2]. Second, it enables search adver tisers to reduce keyword management costs [3,4]. For example, consider an example of the online marketplace LivingSocial. When LivingSocial buys the generic keyword “group deals” and uses broad match, it can potentially attract those using the generic search term “group deals”, those searching for “Livingsocial deals” as well as those customers who are searching for “groupon deals”. Broad match is the default match type in Google Keyword Matching Options, where the Google AdWords sys tem automatically runs search ads on relevant variations of the key words, even if these search terms are not in firms’ keyword lists. As indicated in Google’s Adwords website, broad match can benefit ad vertisers as it can “attract more visitors to your website, spend less time building keyword lists, and focus your spending on keywords that work”.<sup>1</sup> One third of Google’s clicks and conversions are contributed by broad match keywords [5].

Making keyword matching strategy decisions involves intrinsic tradeoffs. While “broad match” enables advertising firms to extend their reach, we argue that broad match is potentially less targeted than exact match. This is exacerbated by the fact that the algorithm used by the search firm is a black box, and there is very little information available to advertisers about the actual search profile (in terms of search terms used) of the consumers who will be shown the ad. Thus, broad match could lead to possible mismatch between the keyword and the search term. The consequent mismatch between the ad and the search term could substantially waste ad impressions and worsen advertising per formance (e.g., click-through rate, conversion rate). A clear understanding of firms’ tradeoffs in choosing matching options is important for formulating optimal search advertising strategies.

A growing stream of literature has been studying the impact of keyword matching strategies on the performance of search ads. For example, Amaldoss et al. [4] use analytical methods to study exact and broad match as competitive strategies, and derive implications for the search engine and for competing search advertisers. Narayanan and Kalyanam [5] argue that broad match is less targeted and find that broad match delivers significantly lower click-through rate (CTR) as compared to exact match. Du et al. [6] find that broad match can increase CTR but decrease conversion rate of competitor keywords, relative to generic keywords. Taken as a whole these studies have shown that matching strategies are an important area of study, and that search advertisers need to understand how to use them to effectively balance traffic and targeting.

But one of the aspects of matching strategies that the literature has not explored is the role of keyword characteristics. One of the most important keyword characteristics for search advertising that previous literature has established is keyword specificity. Specificity measures how specific a keyword is within a product category [7], e.g., a less specific keyword such as “hotel” as contrasted with a more specific keyword such as “Shanghai economic hotel”, which has been found to affect the effectiveness of search advertising campaigns. For example, Agrawal et al. [8] find that specificity strengthens the positive impact of position on conversion. Ghose and Yang [9] show significant main ef fects of keyword specificity but having no role in moderating rank’s influence on keyword performance. Rutz et al. [10] find that more specific keywords tend to be more effective for click throughs rather than for conversion. Li et al. [7] find that revenue is higher for more specific keywords when the firm uses “last-click” attribution.

In this study, we argue that the impact of broad match on advertising performance varies across different levels of keyword specificity and position. We draw on the activity theory in information retrieval to theoretically motivate how keyword specificity and position moderate the effect of broad match on advertising performance. The activity theory in information retrieval proposes that users break down their information need into sub-needs, which are scheduled one by one in the search process [11]. The theory suggests that users’ sub-needs on in formation tend to be more specific over time in a search task, going from more generic to more specific topic searches [11]. For example, when consumers are in the early stage of a search task, they have only a broad idea on what they want, and thus search for terms with a low level of specificity. When consumers are in the late stage, on the contrary, they usually know their needs better, and will search for terms with a high level of specificity.

Web search, as a modern example of information retrieval, displays the information that a user needs in the form of a search query via keywords specified by the user [12]. The search engine attempts to satisfy this information need by presenting the search engine ads, with user response constituting a measure of the relevance of the search ads [13]. A broad match strategy expands the scope of the topics and pre sents the ads to more users, including those who are still in the early stage of the search task (and decision-making) and have not formulated any specific idea of the market. According to the activity theory in in formation retrieval, consumers who search for higher-specificity key words are more likely to know their needs better and have developed narrowly construed preferences [11]. They may thus be less likely to respond to the unexpected ads from broad match. Therefore, we would expect the moderating effect of keyword specificity to be negative on consumers’ response to broad match ads. Similarly, when consumers in the early stage of the search task conduct the search and view ads served up by broad match, they are likely to respond to more ads so as to find out more about what they want. In this scenario, the effect of topposition (i.e., low-position) advertising to attract consumers’ attention is weakened. Thus, we expect the interaction effect between broad match and top-position keywords on advertising performance would be negative.

While previous studies have made important progress in under standing the effectiveness of broad match, they have not examined how keyword specificity and position influence the performance of matching strategies (see Table 1). This is an important question since measuring the moderating effects of keyword specificity and position allows search advertisers to investigate match strategy effectiveness and optimize the match strategy based on keyword specificity and position to improve advertising performance. Thus, this leads the research question of this study: How do matching strategies, especially broad match, perform with respect to keyword specificity and position?

To study this research question, we would require data on paid search advertising across various types of keywords, as well as variation in the firm strategies in using matching options over time. In this paper we address the important research question by studying how different types of keyword attributes influence response to broad match using secondary panel data of paid search advertising from a Chinese hotel, as well as a field experiment for the same company. The results show that (1) compared to exact match, broad match leads to lower advertising performance; (2) the negative effect of broad match on CTR is more prominent for more specific or lower (better)-position; and (3) the negative effects of broad match on conversion rate, revenue, and profits are attenuated for higher (worse)-position keywords.

## 2. Literature review

Our study is related to four streams of literature. The first is a nascent stream of literature on Google match [4]–[6]. While advertisers need to use Google Keyword Matching Options to target their ads to potential search terms of consumers, they can use three types of matching on Google, from broad to narrow: broad match, phrase match and exact match.<sup>2</sup> With exact match, the firm shows its ads to consumers searching for the exact keyword; phrase match shows ads to the exact keyword with additional words before or after; while broad match shows ads on relevant variations of the keyword, such as synonyms, singular and plural forms, possible misspellings, and phrases containing the keyword. A growing stream of literature has examined the effects of matching strategies on the advertising performances. For example, Narayanan and Kalyanam [5] find that broad match is associated with a lower CTR as compared to exact match. Klapdor et al. [17] show that keyword matching options moderate the effects of advertiser name in the keyword and query variation on advertising performance. Consistent with Narayanan and Kalyanam [5], we find that broad match decreases CTR as compared to exact match. Moreover, our results show that broad match lowers conversion rate of advertisements.

The second stream of literature that is related to our study is that on the effect of keyword specificity on the effectiveness of paid search advertising [7,8,10]. Keyword specificity is one keyword characteristic that can be used to classify keywords. Most previous studies have operationalized keyword specificity using comparatively simpler mea sures of specificity. Agarwal et al. [8] operationalize specificity as the level of product hierarchy of the advertiser’s landing page (for example, “casual shirts” has a specificity value of 2 while “shirts” has a specificity value of 1). Ghose and Yang [9] use simple proxies for keyword speci ficity in the form of three variables: dummy variables for mentions of brand names, retailer names and a continuous variable for length of the keyword. Rutz et al. [10] also use length of keywords, as well as a number of dummy variables to account for brand names, locations (country, state and city), and category. Li et al. [7] have developed a more sophisticated measure of specificity, a judgment-based measure of keyword specificity. Specificity has been found to affect the effective ness of search advertising campaigns. For instance, Lu and Zhao [19] find that indirect sales or cross-selling is more effective when less spe cific keywords are used while direct sales of the advertised products are more effective when specific keywords are used. Im et al. [25] suggest that users who are searching for more specific terms are more likely to make a purchase. Consistent with Rutz et al. [10], we find that keyword specificity helps increase CTR only. However, previous research has not examined how keyword specificity influences the performance of matching strategies. The study of this moderating effect of keyword specificity on keyword matching is important since understanding this effect will enable managers to more effectively manage their keyword bidding based on keyword specificity. Our study finds that keyword specificity strengthens the negative effect of broad match on CTR.

The third stream of literature that is related to our study is that on the effect of position on the effectiveness of paid search advertising [8,9,22,26]. Position refers to the rank of an advertisement in response to a query for a keyword on search results, which has also been found as an important predictor of advertising performance [8,9,21,26]. Specif ically, position is associated with CTRs, conversion rates, and revenue.

For example, Ashkan and Clarke [14] find that context-based factors such as keyword position displayed on the search engine results page (SERP) impact CTR. Consistent with previous studies [9], we find that position is negatively associated with advertising performance (e.g., CTR, conversion rate, revenue, and profits). However, previous research has not examined the moderating effect of position on keyword matching strategies. We do not yet understand how the effects of broad match on advertising performance differ between high-position and low-position keywords. Through using both secondary data and a field experiment data, we find that the negative effects of broad match on advertising performance (e.g., CTR, conversion rate, revenue, profits) are reduced for high-position (worse-position) keywords.

The last stream of literature we relate to is that on online targeting relevance [27]. The literature of paid search advertising has examined several factors that influence advertisement performance, such as position [8,28,29], consumer types [30–34] and keyword characteristics [9,26,35–37]. Recent studies have examined the effect of targeting strat egy on performance of paid search advertising. For example, Rutz and Bucklin [20] find evidence for spillover of generic search to branded search but not the reverse, thus showing that this spillover is asymmetric. In addition to these two targeting strategies, advertisers can use a third targeting strategy, targeting competitors’ customers [38]. Desai et al. [39] develop a theoretical model and find that the focal brand should buy the own branded kevword to defend from threats from its competitor but cannot succeed in driving out the competitor if the exposure effect (i.e., consumers’ perception in brand quality will change after they are exposed to the advertisement) and contrast effect are weak (i.e., consumers perceive two different brands even more different after they see the brands together); but if both effects are strong, the brand owner can preclude the competitor successfully from buying its own branded keywords. Sayedi et al. [3] study the tradeoffs on two competing firms’ strategies of tar geting each other’ customers using a game theory model. They find that symmetric firms might use asymmetric bidding strategies, whereas one firm focuses on creating awareness through traditional advertising chan nel while the other mainly targets on the keywords of the former firm through paid search advertising. Chiou and Tucker [40] use field studie to examine the effects of targeting competitors’ customers by a third-party reseller using a trademark in its paid search advertising. They show tha this strategy reduces the click-through rate of the trademark holder’s paid search ads, but it benefits the trademark holder as customers will buy from the direct channel. Zhang et al. [411 develop a new method to enhance bidding for search ad bidding firms by suggesting relevant but less competitive keywords, which can increase ad impressions, clicks and revenue. We build on this line of work by investigating the performance of different targeting strategies in terms of a broader keyword characteristic of keyword relevance such as bidding on generic keywords and own branded keywords. Consistent with previous literature [7] we find that own branded keywords lead to better advertising performance such as conversion rate, revenue, and profits, as compared to generic and competitor keywords.

## 3. Exploratory study

## 3.1. Background and data

To explore the effects of broad match on advertising performance, we collected secondary panel data from a value-priced hotel chain in China. The hotel provided us a dataset containing daily information on their paid search advertising on Google’s AdWords platform. The dataset spanned all keyword advertisements from the hotel from September 9, 2011 to February 29, 2012. The dataset contained 1651 unique key words with 56,199 observations. Each observation included date, keyword, match type, quality score (calculated by the search engine as a function of the past CTR of the keyword and the quality of the landing page), keyword specificity, impressions, clicks, cost per click, average position, conversions, and revenue. We compute the click-through rate for each observation as the ratio of the number of clicks to the number of impressions and the conversion rate as the ratio of the number of con versions to the number of clicks. Table 2 displays the descriptive sta tistics and the correlations.

The hotel used two match strategies to trigger the ads, i.e. exact match (49.561%) and broad match (50.439%). In general, broad match has a higher level of revenue than exact match. However, this comes at a cost since broad match generates a higher level of average CPC than exact match. Moreover, the average CTR and conversion rate are quite similar between two match strategies.

We follow the procedure outlined in Li et al. [7] to determine keyword specificity. Three judges who are proficient in the Chinese language were invited to evaluate the specificity level of each keyword in the dataset. The judges were first asked to rate the specificity of each keyword independently. The specificity score was in the range between 1 and 5, where a higher score indicated a higher level of specificity. If two or more judges’ ratings on a keyword were the same, the keyword’s specificity score was the same rating; otherwise, if none of the judges had the same rating on a keyword, they discussed the results further and made a final decision.

## 3.2. Model and empirical results

In this subsection, we simultaneously model the advertisers keyword bidding behavior, the search engine’s keyword position deci sion, and consumers’ click-through and conversion rates.

## 3.2.1. Advertisers’ bidding decision

First, we model the advertisers’ keyword bidding behavior. The advertiser needs to decide on the bidding price (CPC is a proxy for the bidding price) for keyword i on day t. The advertiser may consider a keyword’s bidding price based on its past performance. Specifically, a keyword’s current bidding price is a function of the position of the keyword on the previous day [9]. In addition, the extent of competi tiveness in the bidding process for a keyword could be influenced by keyword specificity. Another variable used by search engines to decide on the minimum bid of keywords is the quality score, and we therefore control for the landing page quality in the bidding price function. We therefore model the advertiser’s bidding decision as follows:

$$

$$

where $C P C _ { i t }$ represents the cost per click of keyword i at time t decided by the advertiser. Position represents the rank of keyword i in the search results at time t. The lower the value of the position, the higher the ranking in the search results. Broad represents a broad match strategy dummy, which equals 0 if the advertiser uses exact match for keyword i at time t and equals 1 if the advertiser uses broad match. Specificity represents the specificity of a keyword. A greater value for specificity suggests that the keyword is more specific. As indicated above, the specificity score for each keyword in this study was rated by three in dependent judges, and can take values ranging from 1 to 5. Quality represents the quality score of the keyword, which is calculated by the search engine as a function of the past CTR of the keyword and the quality of the landing page (e.g., relevancy, transparency, and naviga bility) [9]. Focal represents an own branded keyword dummy, which equals 1 if the keyword is an own branded keyword and 0 otherwise. ## 3.2.2. The search engine’s Ad position decision

The search engine makes the ad position decision by using the bid ding price multiplied by a quality score. Following previous literature $[ 9 , 4 2 ]$ , we use a log-log model to describe this process that the position is affected by the keyword’s bidding price and quality score. We also include the broad match and keyword specificity in the position model,<sup>4</sup> since different levels of these variables will capture varying levels of competition. Thus, the search engines’ decision on determining the positions for paid search advertisements for each keyword is modeled as follows.

$$

## 4. Field experiment

## 4.1. Data collection and model-free results

Model estimates from our secondary panel data can help advertisers understand the effect of broad match on ad performance. However, there may be some limitations to secondary data model examination. For example, broad match may not be an exogenous predictor of ad performance, and it is unclear whether the host of covariates we include as control variables eliminate the problem. Another potential limitation to our analysis is that the hotel did not intentionally control the usage of broad match across keywords and ads in the above dataset.

To address these possible limitations and further validate our earlier results, we conducted a field experiment in which the hotel experi mented with changing broad match strategy for their paid search ads through Google’s AdWords platform in Google.hk. The hotel ran the experiment for six months from November 1, 2012 to April 30, 2013; and data from the experiment is aggregated to the campaign-day level. During this period the hotel varied only one dimension of the adver tising, the keyword match options. The hotel randomly selected a group of keywords (588 keywords) as the treatment group, while the control group consisted of the other 556 keywords.<sup>7</sup> Before conducting the field experiment, we first did randomization checks for the treatment group and the control group. Table 4 confirms that the assignment of keywords was random across the treatment and control groups. As shown in Table 4, there were no significant differences in the pre-treatment per formance (i.e., impressions, click-through rate, conversion rate, CPC, revenue, profits, and position) and keyword characteristics (i.e., quality score, specificity, percentage of generic keywords, percentage of own branded keywords, and percentage of competitor keywords) between the treatment group and the control group.<sup>8</sup>

In the first three months (pre-treatment period), the hotel used exact match for all the keywords. In the last three months (post-treatment period), the hotel used exact match only for the control group and broad match for the treatment group. The hotel tracked both clicks on the search ads and following conversions $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ booking a hotel on the website). We estimate the revenue and profits of each observation based on clicks, conversions and the costs of clicks. Fig. 2 shows that compared to the control group, keywords in the treatment group have a relatively lower CTR, lower conversion rate, less revenue, and less profits in the post-treatment period when broad match was used than in the pretreatment period when exact match was used.

To test the real treatment effect of broad match on advertising per formance and how the effect varies across different levels of position, quality score, and keyword specificity, we follow previous studies [45,46] by using a difference-in-difference-in-difference (DDD) approach:

$$

## 5. Conclusions and implications

This paper explores the question of when firms should use keyword match options in paid search advertising, with a focus on the effec tiveness of broad match as compared to exact match. There can be ar guments both for and against broad match; the primary argument for broad match is that it can leverage lower cost keywords to achieve ob jectives such as targeting a wider prospective audience. However, the inexact nature of broad match’s targeting will mean that there is possible wastage of ad dollars. For example, broad match could display advertising to an audience who may not be really interested in firms product. Such an audience would be resistant to conversion to purchase, in which case broad match will not increase revenue for search firms.

We focus on the impact of broad match (versus exact match) strategy on the performance on keywords in paid search advertising. In contrast with the earlier literature that only examine the main effect of broad match [6], we examine a detailed set of interactions between keyword specificity, position, and broad match, which, to our knowledge, have not been addressed in the earlier empirical literature. We investigate this question by both using a secondary data from and conducting a field experiment with a value-priced hotel chain. The results are summarized in Table 8.

In general, Table 8 shows that in general, broad match leads to worse performance compared to exact match, and that this negative impact varies across keywords. In particular, the negative effect of broad match on click-through rate (CTR) is strengthened for lower (better)-position or more specific keywords, and the negative effects of broad match on conversion rate, revenue, and profits are weakened for higher (worse)- position keywords.

This is an important and interesting finding, and our study is the first to comprehensively investigate the differences between the effects of broad match and exact match with respect to keyword specificity and quality. Contrary to conventional wisdom of advertisers, our results suggest that broad match might be an unprofitable search advertising strategy, whose effectiveness crucially depends on the keyword speci ficity and position. Thus, our study contributes to the literature on keyword matching strategy. Extant literature on matching strategies has focused on implications for the search engine and competition. For example, Amaldoss et al. [4] theoretically show that advertisers would use broad match if the accuracy of broad match is high but would not use it if the accuracy is low. Narayanan and Kalyanam [5] find that broad match delivers greater impressions than exact match and receives less clicks and conversions. In this study, we have explored comprehensively the effect of broad match on ad CTR, CR, revenue, and profits.

Our findings have implications for firms that try to approach a wide range of customers in paid search advertising. They suggest that at tempts to use broad match to expose advertising to a wider swathe of customers may backfire, as it can lead the firm to adopt unprofitable search advertising strategies. If their strategies have the objective of increasing customer attention (through greater impressions) and thus increasing the brand awareness, using broad match in advertising might be an effective strategy. By contrast, if search firms’ strategies focus on revenue and profits, they should use broad match carefully only for higher (worse)-position keywords.

The results were validated by a field experiment conducted with the same hotel through Google’s AdWords platform in China, where the hotel experimented with changing broad match strategy for their paid search ads after an initial three-month period. Thus, our results are largely robust across both the secondary data analyses and the field experiment. Our field experiment methodology provides implications for advertisers to test the effectiveness of match strategies. It may be difficult to generalize our findings to recommendations on which match strategies should be used across all products and all companies, since customers’ decisions and behaviors are different across different prod ucts and industries. But our field experiment shows that advertisers could randomly select some keywords to experiment with on match strategy for several weeks and then change to the other match strategy. Advertisers can rely on field experiments to examine the impact of match strategy on revenue outcome and develop a better keyword matching strategy to maximize their revenue.

## 6. Limitations and future research

Our study has a few limitations that may yield opportunities for future research. First, we collect data only from the Google search en gine. Data from other search engines such as Yahoo! and Bing can be studied to generalize these findings. Second, our data is at the campaign level, which means that we do not track individual consumers explicitly. Third, studies have indicated that consumers’ search and conversion activities vary across different types of consumers [49] and product categories [47]. Future research could extend this study to different consumer segments or product categories or product life stages to investigate the differentiated effects of broad match in different con texts. Despite these limitations, we believe that this research provides useful evidence about the need for firms to optimize their keyword match options across broad match and exact match when using paid search advertising to target customers.

This suggests that advertisers decide on the bidding price for a keyword based on its previous position (better previous position, higher bidding price). This shows that branded keywords (including both focal-brand and competitor keywords) will lead to a lower bid, and therefore a better chance to for the ad to secure a good position.
