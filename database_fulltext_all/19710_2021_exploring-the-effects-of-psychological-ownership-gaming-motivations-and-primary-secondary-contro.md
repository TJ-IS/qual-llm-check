## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online game addiction Psychological ownership Gaming motivations Primary control Secondary control

## A B S T R A C T

Online gaming has grown to be a very popular electronic entertainment for people throughout the world. However, the burgeoning popularity of online games in some cases leads to addiction, a phenomenon that has received considerable attention. The purpose of this study is to examine the effects of psychological ownership, gaming motivation, and primary–secondary control on online game addiction (OGA). Based on 436 valid re sponses collected from online questionnaires, the partial least squares structural equation modeling approach was employed to test the research model. Findings show that the motivations of achievement and escapism are positively associated with psychological ownership toward a virtual gaming environment. Of particular interest is the inverted-U relationship between psychological ownership and OGA, suggesting that too little or too much psychological ownership is associated with less OGA. Finally, individuals with high levels of primary (secondary) control are more (less) inclined to be addicted to online games.

This study provides practical implications and strategies to address the alarming increase in OGA. First, families, communities, and universities should encourage outdoor activities, such as sports competitions, and offline cosplay games to address the need for achievement and escapism. Second, individuals who play online games for relaxation should choose a relatively easy or hard mode to avoid game addiction. Finally, parents, teachers, and employers are encouraged to pay attention to individuals who have a high level of primary control since they can be susceptible to OGA. This study provides several theoretical contributions. First, the study enriches OGA and the gaming motivation literature by exploring the roles of gaming motivation, psychological ownership, and personal control strategies as they can be a factor in OGA. In addition, this study is the first to investigate the dark side of psychological ownership in terms of negative outcomes for OGA. This study also advances the personal control strategy literature by understanding the roles of primary and secondary control on OGA.

## 1. Introduction

Video games have become a prevalent form of electronic entertain ment, as evidenced by approximately 2.2 billion active gamers world wide [1]. The video games market is expected to be worth over US\$90 billion by 2020,<sup>1</sup> with online games making up a large portion of growth and revenues (e.g., Epic Games’ popular online game Fortnite has an estimated 125 million players [2]). A recent report forecasts that 2020’s global games market will generate revenues of \$159.3 billion because of the COVID-19 lockdowns.

Gaming in various incarnations can be traced throughout human history [3] as a way to help people relax and retreat from daily routines. In addition to providing entertainment, playing games helps to develop communitywide social capital [3], which encourages shared group values, increases intergroup trust, and promotes teamwork. Social cap ital relationships are essential and useful for the individual, commu nities, and business [4,5].

Despite the positive aspects of playing games, the burgeoning popularity of online games has led to some negative effects [6] as the number of reported cases of online gaming addiction (OGA) has increased, with numerous negative consequences, such as ignoring family responsibilities, job dereliction, health problems, crime, and even deaths [7]. These concerns have been growing, and the medical com munity has identified compulsive gaming as a disorder. Indeed, in June of 2018, the 11th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases defined a gaming disorder as the impaired control over gaming behavior.<sup>3</sup>

OGA is defined as a subtype of Internet addiction and implies the maladaptive psychological dependency on a specific subset of infor mation technology artifacts, namely, online games [8]. OGA is also known as problematic or pathological online game use [9]. Many studies have examined OGA along a continuum, from etiology and risk factors to the pathology of OGA, to the ramifications in terms of negative out comes, to the identification of potential treatments [3].

Our primary focus is on the antecedents that lead to OGA. Prior research has attributed gaming addiction to external factors (i.e., the game’s structural characteristics, such as the relationship between player and his or her character [10]), internal factors (i.e., a gamer’s personality characteristics [11,12]), and motivations related to playing games [13,14].

One of the intriguing phenomena of the virtual gaming world is that gamers, compared to other technology users (such as website surfers), have a stronger feeling of ownership of the virtual environment. Gamers have shown great interest in occupying the virtual world and interacting with the entities within that space [15]. This phenomenon of possession is called psychological ownership. It is a psychological state in which an individual feels he or she possesses ownership of a focal target [16,17].

Previous literature has applied the psychological ownership construct to a variety of contexts. For example, Van Dyne and Pierce [18] find a positive link between employees’ psychological ownership of the organization and their attitudes and work behavior. Fuchs, Prandelli [19] show that consumers who assume more psychological ownership of a product exhibit a stronger desire for that product. However, very few studies have examined the role of psychological ownership to virtual worlds [15], and virtually no research examines the role of psycholog ical ownership on OGA.

To advance our understanding of the role of psychological ownership in OGA, we build on the game playing motivations research of Yee [20]. Yee argues that although people play games for various reasons, they are particularly interested in or motivated by achievement, social interaction, and escapism. Recent research suggests that gaming motivation is one of the most important predictors of OGA [8,21,22]. That is, the desire for escapism, advancement, and socializing motivate gamers [3], and these motivations can become problematic, even addicting, when coupled with excessive playing time [8].

Another critical predictor of OGA is the concept of control [8]. Control is a driving force in behavior, and individuals exert a varying amount of control over their environment [23]. Previous research has shown that low levels of self-control are positively related to OGA [3,24]. We delve deeper into the domain of control theory by drawing from similar, yet distinct bodies of work on primary control and sec ondary control. As first delineated by Rothbaum, Weisz [25], primary control refers to a person’s desire and behavior to change the environ ment and the people around him or her to achieve a sense of control.

In contrast, secondary control refers to an individual’s attempt to gain a sense of control by adapting himself or herself, by fitting into the environment and “flowing with the current” [25]. Although numerous studies have investigated primary–secondary control in the context of education and healthcare [26–28], this construct has received limited empirical attention in the gaming literature. We, therefore, examine how the dual processes of primary and secondary control can help to predict the formation of OGA.

In sum, this study examines the following research issues:

(a) How do gaming motivations, achievement, desire for social, and escapism relate to the individuals’ perception of their psycho logical ownership of the virtual gaming world?

(b) How does psychological ownership of the virtual gaming world lead to the formation of OGA?

(c) How does primary–secondary control affect the formation of OGA?

We integrate the literature from OGA, psychological ownership, and gaming motivation with the emerging literature on primary–secondary control to create a model that guides our empirical analysis and allows us to investigate these research questions. We test our model using survey data collected from 436 undergraduate students from a north eastern United States university.

This study makes three main theoretical contributions. First, unlike prior works that study the direct relationship between OGA and gaming motivations, this research advances the online gaming literature by bridging these two topics under the novel construct of psychological ownership. Second, we extend the social psychology and management literature by evaluating the negative effect of psychological ownership on the formation of OGA. Finally, we contribute to the emerging liter ature on primary–secondary control by investigating the varying impact on OGA. This study has societal and educational implications. Under standing the etiology and risk factors of OGA can address the social, psychological, and physical health issues related to OGA and also guide policymaking for educational entities.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 pro vides a review of the literature on OGA and gaming motivations (achievement, social, and escapism), psychological ownership, and primary and secondary control. Section 3 discusses the hypotheses, and Section 4 describes the research method, including data, measurement, and modeling. Section 5 reports our results. Finally, Section 6 discusses implications, limitations, and potential avenues for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Online game addiction and gaming motivation

OGA has been recently investigated across various disciplines, including social psychology, education, information systems, and psy chiatry [6,8,24,29,30]. In a large-scale longitudinal study of 3034 elementary students in Singapore, pathological gaming, defined as gaming that resulted in a dysfunctional family, friends, and school re lationships. It was also related to depression, social phobias, anxiety, and lower grades in 9% of the study participants [31]. Based on these estimates, the recent, significant increases in online gaming is alarming. For example, an estimated 125 million gamers play Epic Game’s Fort nite. Based on the 9% estimate, over 11 million Fortnite players may exhibit a harmful gaming pathology. Given the prevalence of online gaming with an estimated 2.2 billion active gamers worldwide [1], the problem may be even more substantial.

The literature of OGA can be classified into two broad categories. One body of work focuses on the predictors or antecedents of OGA [3,6,21,24], and the other stream of research examines the conse quences of OGA [32,33]. We extend the literature of the first category by considering the influence of gaming motivations using the novel psy chological construct of psychological ownership.

Past work has suggested that specific psychological characteristics drive OGA. Zhu, Zhang [34] found that a low-quality parent-child relationship predicts adolescent OGA and is linked to the decreasing quality of the school affiliation and an increase in the connectedness with deviant peers. Tone, Zhao [35] showed that personality, family function, life events, and social support are related to OGA and that these associations are mediated by attraction to online gaming. Also,

Yang and Tung [36] reported that Taiwanese high school students with personalities characterized by shyness, dependence, depression, and low self-esteem have a higher tendency to be addicted to online games.

Within the stream of research focused on the relationship between gaming motivation and OGA, prior literature has considered motivation based on categorizing patterns of player behavior [37]. Bartle [38], a developer of multiplayer computer games, was among the first to pro pose a widely discussed taxonomy of game players. Specifically, Bartle suggested four types of players: Killers, Achievers, Socializers, and Ex plorers [38]. He developed a four-quadrant map that was defined by two dimensions of player behavior: (a) acting versus interacting and (b) focusing on the virtual world versus focusing on other players [37,38]. He then projected each player type into this map and found that (a) Killers are motivated to act against players; (b) Socializers are motivated to interact with other players; (c) Achievers are motivated to master the virtual world; and (d) Explorers are motivated to interact with the vir tual world [37,38]. Rooted in self-determination theory, Ryan, Rigby [37] offered a motivation model based on the psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Przybylski, Rigby [39] used this model to evaluate how video game engagement shapes psycholog ical processes and, in turn, influences people’s well-being. Hsu and Lu [40] apply a technology acceptance model, combined with social in fluences concepts and flow experience, to check gamers’ motivations for playing online games. They found that social norms, attitudes, and flow experience explain approximately 80% of the variance in gaming motivations.

Yee [20] empirically identified player motivations using factor analysis. His work revealed ten sub-motives for gaming that fell into three overarching motivations: achievement, social, and escapism. On line gamers who are focused on achievement want to achieve game mastery, desire competition, and seek power within the game. Those who are focused on social objectives want to interact with other players and develop in-game ties. Escapism players seek relief from real-life problems and thus fully engage in the virtual gaming environment.

Following this framework, Xu, Turel [8] employed a survey-based method and identified several functional needs (e.g., the need for rela tionship, the need for escapism) that can predict OGA. Kuss, Louws [3] showed similar evidence of the role of escapism and other such mech anisms in excessive gaming behavior. In a more recent study, Chang, Hsieh [21] examined the mediational effects of multiple gaming mo tives, from online game involvement to problematic Internet use. They proposed that escapism and advancement motives were positively related to problematic Internet use, but socializing motives were not.

We adapt Yee [20] framework for gaming motivations: achievement, social, and escapism. Based on this framework, we advance the gaming motivation literature and game addiction literature by introducing the novel construct of psychological ownership as an intermediary variable between gaming motivation and OGA.

## 2.2. Psychological ownership

Psychological ownership, as differentiated from legal ownership, is generally defined as the state in which an individual feels as though the target of ownership is “mine” [41]. Feelings of possessiveness are ubiquitous and can refer to physical (e.g., car, house, etc.) or nonphys ical objects (e.g., ideas, virtual world, etc.; [15]). A large body of research examines psychological ownership in an array of contexts, including organizational behavior [16,18], marketing [19], information systems [15,42], and healthcare [43].

Prior work has found that employees with psychological ownership of the organization have positive attitudes regarding their work, including organizational commitment, job satisfaction, and organization-based self-esteem, all of which lead to high levels of job performance [18,44]. In the healthcare domain, Par´e, Sicotte [43] explored the effects of psychological ownership on physicians’ accep tance of technology. The results show that a physician’s psychological ownership of the clinical technology system is positively related to his or her perception of the system’s utility. Along the same lines, the literature on cybersecurity suggests that psychological ownership positively in fluences security compliance intention [42,45]. Research in marketing has found that when customers are authorized to select the products to be marketed (i.e., selecting products of which they had stronger psy chological ownership), they showed higher demand for these products, even when identical products were offered [19]. The emerging literature on virtual worlds suggests that a world design that induces psychological ownership is critical to attracting users to spend more time in the virtual world [15].

Research thus far has tended to examine the positive aspects of psychological ownership. However, Pierce, Kostova [41] reminded scholars that extreme psychological ownership could have negative consequences. In particular, the authors argued that individuals can become preoccupied with their increasing psychological ownership and can even become obsessed, causing a detrimental effect on their re lationships with their family, friends, and community [41]. Nonetheless, research on the negative effects of psychological ownership remains lacking. In line with Moon, Hossain [23], who show that psychological ownership facilitates player loyalty to online games. We speculate that psychological ownership may, in turn, affect the formation of OGA. Thus, we extend the prior literature by exploring whether and, if so, to what extent psychological ownership relates to OGA.

## 2.3. Primary and secondary control

Control is an important concept across many academic fields [46]. Several experimental and theoretical research studies in sociology and psychology have shown that across a lifespan, individual differences in perceived control are associated with different consequences in a variety of life domains, including achievement, motivation, self-esteem, and mental/physical well-being [47]. Theories about control generally as sume that individuals actively manage their development [27]. In particular, people set goals, seek their attainment, and deal with the consequences over their entire lifetimes [26].

Rothbaum, Weisz [25] proposed a dual-process conceptualization of control behavior, delineating primary and secondary control. According to this innovative framework, people attempt to gain control not only by bringing the environment into line with their wishes (primary control) but also by bringing themselves into line with environmental forces (secondary control). Subsequent research has adapted and extended this concept by proposing that primary control consists of goal-directed persistence and effort, and secondary control involves a variety of be haviors, including the downgrading of expectations of performance, accepting limitations, and perceiving benefits from an unfavorable outcome [28,48].

Research on primary and secondary control has been undertaking in the health, educational, and coping domains. A study examining the benefits of primary and secondary control for self-rated health in stu dents showed that while both primary and secondary control are related to lower illness-related behaviors for males, only secondary control corresponded to better overall health and fewer illness symptoms for females [28]. Weisz, McCabe [49] studied children who were under going uncontrollable stressors and found that secondary-control chil dren exhibited better behavioral adjustment than primary-control children. In another study that investigated the use of primary- and secondary-control strategies among men and women who had healthrelated restrictions on everyday tasks, Chipperfield, Perry [50] found that women’s use of primary-control strategies was related to past health events. They also found that women adopt secondary-control strategies more than men.

Despite the conceptual application of primary and secondary control to other fields of inquiry, very little of this research has focused on OGA. One study investigated the predictors of player commitment to online games and found that the perceived control over the character, in term of primary control and secondary control, is positively related to the psychological ownership of the character, which, in turn, predicts the player’s commitment to online games [23]. To the best of our knowl edge, our study is the first to examine the relationship between primary and secondary control and OGA.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. Gaming motivations and psychological ownership

As previously noted, we adopt the Yee [20] framework of online gaming motivation. In a more recent study that explored the relation ship between flow and psychological ownership in the context of secu rity, Yoo, Sanders [45] disentangled flow, which is a second-order construct, into five separate first-order dimensions: feedback, challenge, autonomy, immersion, and social interaction. They argued that each of the five dimensions predicts psychological ownership, which indicated that the flow is also positively associated with psychological ownership. Three of the five subdimensions of flow (i.e., challenge, social interac tion, and immersion) align with the three gaming motivations we apply in this study: achievement, social, and escapism. We propose that all three gaming motivations are positively related to psychological ownership. Note here, Yoo, Sanders, and Cerveny [45] is the paper we mainly refer to in supporting our hypotheses. That paper investigated the impact of flow and psychological ownership on security education, training, and awareness (SETA) effectiveness, self-efficacy, and security compliance intention. The authors detangled flow into five sub dimensions to argue how flow relates to psychological ownership in SETA. While we use this paper as a supporting reference, there are at least two notable differences between our paper and Yoo, Sanders, and Cerveny [45].

First, as a relatively new but important theoretical concept in psy chology, psychological ownership has started gaining attention from business domains such as marketing [19] and organizational behavior [41]. Yoo, Sanders, and Cerveny [45] is among the first to bring this concept to Information Systems (IS) area, but their research domain is on security. To our knowledge, our paper is the first to study psycho logical ownership in the gaming addiction context. While prior studies use psychological ownership to theorize workplace issues, we extend this theory’s boundary to the non-work environment. Will gamers develop psychological ownership in the gaming environment in a way that workers do in their workplaces? If so, what factors can contribute to the generation of this sense? Motivated by these questions, we explore the antecedents of gaming-related psychological ownership in the cur rent study. In essence, the primary difference between our study and Yoo, Sanders, and Cerveny [45] is the research domain or context.

Second, while the research model presented in Yoo, Sanders, and Cerveny [45] shows that the focal variable predicting psychological ownership is flow, our variables of interests predicting psychological ownership are gaming motivations. Those are distinct concepts. While flow is a highly focused psychological state where an individual is completely absorbed and engaged in an activity [51], gaming motiva tions are particular functional needs people seek in the gaming envi ronment [52]. We would like to clarify that our purpose here is to use flow theory to rationalize the hypothesized relationships between the three gaming motivations and psychological ownership rather than replicating or reconfirming the finding outlined in Yoo, Sanders, and Cerveny [45]. In sum, in additional to the different research context, our research model is distinct from Yoo, Sanders, and Cerveny [45].

## 3.1.1. Achievement motivation

According to self-determination theory, competence (i.e., the desire to feel capable of performing a task) is one of three basic innate needs of human beings [53]. In line with this notion, Yee [20] argues that in gaming, a sense of achievement comes from three components: advancement, mechanics, and competition. Advancement refers to the players’ interests in gaining power and accumulating in-game wealth. Mechanics refers to players’ interests in analysing the underlying rules, leveling up characters, and optimizing character performance. Compe tition refers to the desire to challenge and compete with other players.

Within a gaming environment, a player with the need for increased competence tends to seek more power, higher-performance characters, and rare items to outperform others; these behaviors help generate feelings of capability. Other studies show that the desire for advance ment encourages players to stay in the game [21]. Sepehr and Head [54] also suggest that the perception of video game competitiveness has a strong predicting effect on video gameplay satisfaction. Therefore, we propose that the motivation for achievement keeps gamers engaged in the gaming environment, which, in turn, facilitates psychological ownership. Based on these arguments, we form the following hypothesis (see Fig. 1).

H1. The motivation for achievement is positively associated with psycho logical ownership.

## 3.1.2. Social motivation

Yee [20] identifies three components of social motivation: socializ ing, relationships, and teamwork. Thus, social motivation relates to developing and maintaining relationships with others, including help ing, chatting, and progressing the storyline as a group effort [39]. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs suggests that social interaction and collaboration between individuals can help fulfill needs and provides a sense of belonging and love [55]. Fang, Lee [56] found that in the setting of the organization, moderate levels of cross-group social interactions enable ideas and knowledge to diffuse across groups and lead to the highest job performance. Similarly, Chang, Hsieh [21] showed that gamers tend to use in-game social capital, which allows them to collaborate closely within a game’s community to overcome difficulties. Also, Yoo, Sanders [45] reported that social interactions among mem bers—made easy by recent technology and apps—are essential to the formation of affective emotions that eventually lead to psychological ownership. Based on the previous discussion, we form the following hypothesis (see Fig. 1).

H2. The motivation for socializing is positively associated with psycholog ical ownership.

## 3.1.3. Escapism motivation

Yee [20] argues that escapism motivation is one of the four components of immersion (along with discovery, role-playing, and customization). Escapism refers to a person’s attempt to avoid thinking about or to run away from real-life problems by engaging in an online experience [20]. Of the four dimensions of immersion, escapism has drawn the most attention from research on gaming motivation [8,21]. A handful of research projects have suggested that the escapism motiva tion increases the extent of online game playing [3,45,57]. We argue that higher levels of engagement and more time devoted to the game can afford players the opportunity to gain more familiarity with the gaming environment and to acquire a sense of belonging and closeness, which, in turn, can lead to psychological ownership. Additionally, an in dividual’s desire to escape from the world often relates to real struggles and challenging experiences, such as the failure to secure feelings of ownership toward their family, the workplace, and the community. In contrast, these individuals may find it substantially easier to gain psy chological ownership in a virtual gaming world. Based on the previou arguments, we formulate the following hypothesis (see Fig. 1).

H3. The motivation for escapism is positively associated with psychological ownership.

## 3.2. Psychological ownership and OGA

Like any other psychological state, psychological ownership can be a double-edged sword in certain circumstances [41]. In the context of online gaming, psychological ownership can refer to the feeling of “This is my virtual world” or “I sense that I own this virtual world.” Mehroof and Griffiths [58] found that aggression and hostility are significantly related to OGA and that these conditions can be produced via psycho logical ownership [59]. For instance, within the societal context of the transition from totalitarian to democratic regimes, a high degree of ownership felt by the leaders of a totalitarian state can inhibit the implementation of public demands for a democratic society [411. Other work has shown that the pursuit of ownership of extrinsic objects may result in a diminished self-concept [60]; accordingly, loneliness, intro version, and social inhibition strongly predict OGA [3].

However, when psychological ownership reaches very high levels, its impact on OGA may decrease. According to flow theory, people may gain a sense of flow if they have a high level of skill in a challenging environment, and some early evidence suggests that being in a flow state does not lead to addiction [61]. However, highly skilled individuals tend to become bored and apathetic when facing low levels of challenge [51]. For online gamers, conquering and accomplishing most tasks (or prog ress) as dictated by the game often leads to psychological ownership of this virtual gaming world [45]. Conversely, some highly skilled players may lose interest in the game because they have achieved all game objectives and have explored most of the areas in the virtual world. Since flow experience plays an essential role in online game playing [40], the absence of flow experience may correspond to less addiction to such online games. Thus, based on the previous discussion, we state the following hypothesis (see Fig. 1).

H4. Psychological ownership has an inverted U-shaped association with OGA, such that the effect of psychological ownership is positive at low levels but becomes negative at high levels.

## 3.3. Primary and secondary control and OGA

Previous studies have suggested that a low level of self-control is related to a variety of addictive behaviors, including Internet addiction [24,62]. Prior research finds a significant negative association between self-control and OGA; that is, the more self-control individuals have, the less likely they will become addicted to online games [24,58]. Primary and secondary control, on the other hand, are conceptualized in a slightly different way.

While self-control is generally defined as an ability to resist an im pulse or temptation to perform a behavior [24], primary and secondary control is often used to describe the relationship between an individual and an outside environment or entity [25]. So, how do primary and secondary control relate to OGA? Individuals who pursue primary control attempt to change the situation and/or people around them to align with their personal views and experience. In other words, these individuals do not pursue self-change; in this sense, they are perceived to be more narcissistic, a characteristic previously linked to game addiction [24]. Furthermore, those who usually seek to change others are likely to be more aggressive, which is another characteristic previously associ ated with game addiction [63]. Rhee, Sanders [64] found that in dividuals with mature egos have relatively less mature virtual egos, which suggests that they can act with less maturity in a virtual gaming world in order to acquire a sense of control. Taking all these charac teristics into consideration, an individual who pursues primary control likely enjoys competing and attacking, which can all be fulfilled by the design of an online game. Thus, we posit that primary-control type of people may show a higher tendency to be addicted to online games and, accordingly, state the following hypothesis (see Fig. 1).

H5. Individuals who seek primary control are more likely to be addicted to online gaming.

Secondary control, on the other hand, refers to the strategies in dividuals use to adjust themselves to fit into their environment and with those around them and an acceptance of the consequences when they fail to achieve their goals [25,65]. Compared to primary control, sec ondary control aligns more closely with self-control, because it requires self-adjustment. In line with this discussion, we posit that people who seek to change themselves rather than the environment may be less aggressive toward the environment and the people around them and therefore are less likely to experience OGA. Hence, we state the following hypothesis (see Fig. 1).

H6. Individuals who seek secondary control are less likely to be addicted to online gaming.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data

The first phase of the data collection consisted of a pilot study. The objective of this phase was to focus on measurement issues and to refine the research instruments before the final data collection. The pilot sur vey questionnaire was distributed through Qualtrics to 71 undergradu ate business majors at a university in the northeastern United States. Since the the gamers usually use electronic devices and in many cases internet connection, an online survey is more appropriate compared to traditional paper based surveys.

To test the primary study model, we used an online questionnaire. We emailed a link to 587 students enrolled in an undergraduate statistics class at the same university as in the first phase. Young adults represent the most active group in gaming communities and thus a sample of college students represent a relevant group of the population [8].

The students were informed that their involvement in the study was voluntary and anonymous. The number of students participating in the survey was 541, for a participation rate of 92%. After scrutinizing the responses, we eliminated responses with no variance, responses where the participant has no experience playing online games,<sup>4</sup> and responses where the participant finished the survey in less than two minutes. Valid responses totaled 436.

## 4.2. Measurement tools

We adapted items from the social psychology and the gaming liter ature for the latent variable constructs. We use Lemmens, Valkenburg [66] OGA scale, which is one of the most widely used game addiction scales. We adapted Yee [20] ‘s gaming motivation items to measure achievement motivation, social motivation, and escapism motivation. We used the psychological ownership scale from Lee and Chen [15], which they adapted from Van Dyne and Pierce [18]. The primary control scale was derived from Dan, Sagi-Schwartz [67]. We adapted the sec ondary control construct from both Wrosch, Heckhausen [27] and Dan, Sagi-Schwartz [67]. Prior research suggests that the characteristics of male gender and older age can predict more severe addictions to online games [68]; therefore, we included gender and age as control variables in our model. We also control for the gamer’s activity level to take into 5 account the extent to which he or she plays online games.

## 5. Analysis and results

We use SmartPLS 3.0 to analyze our survey data. Partial least squares (PLS) employs a component-based structural equation modeling approach for estimation, which imposes minimal restrictions on data distribution. Previous studies have argued that PLS has advantages over covariance-based structural equation modeling when the research study is focused more on the nature of the relationships rather than the magnitude of those relationships [70,71]. Also, if the measurement model has more than four indicators, and the loadings meet recom mended standards, estimating common factor models results in very little bias [72]. SmartPLS 3.0 also allows us to test the quadratic effect [73], which relates to H4.

Following prior research using PLS models, we test our model using a two-step approach [71,74,75]. We assess the reliability and the validity of the measurement model in the first step and then test the structural model in the second step [76].

## 5.1. Measurement model

We examine both convergent and discriminant validity. We confirm convergent validity based on three criteria: (a) The composite reliability of all constructs is above 0.70; (b) all item loadings are higher than 0.70 and statistically significant; and (c) the average variance extracted (AVE) of all constructs are larger than 0.50. ## 6. Discussion

The objective of this study is to explore the impacts of gaming mo tivations (i.e., achievement motivation, social motivation, and escapism) on the psychological ownership of the virtual gaming world to determine how psychological ownership is related to addiction to online games. We also investigate the role of primary and secondary control in the formation of OGA.

## 6.1. Key findings

Our results show that motivations for achievement and escapism predict a gamer’s psychological ownership of the virtual world. How ever, social motivation does not relate to psychological ownership. We postulate that individuals who wish to socialize take advantage of various other options (e.g., in-person friendships or via social media platforms [85]) and thus do not rely predominately on the virtual world and gaming to interact with others. Our findings are in line with those of Chang, Hsieh [21], who investigated the bridging role of three gaming motivations—advancement, socializing, and escapism—between game involvement and problematic Internet use. They found that advancement and escapism are positively significant mediators and that socializing is not a significant factor. Our results also corroborate pre vious motivation studies [57,86].

Last, we find an inverted U-shaped relationship between psycho logical ownership and online gaming addiction. As we predicted, at low levels of psychological ownership, OGA increases as the level of psy chological ownership increases; however, beyond a certain level of psychological ownership, OGA decreases. A possible explanation is that high levels of psychological ownership may be related to boredom and game fatigue [87,88]. In other words, although players may feel a high level of psychological ownership, their interest wanes after meeting all in-game challenges exploring most of the map. Finally, we find that primary (secondary) control is positively (negatively) related to OGA, which suggests that those who seek to control or influence the outside environment rather than themselves are more prone to OGA.

## 6.2. Theory contribution

This study provides several contributions to the literature. First, our study adds to the long and critical discussion of the predictors of OGA $[ 3 , 6 , 8 , 9 , 2 4 ]$ . While a handful of studies have examined the influence of gaming motivations on game addiction [8,21], the addiction literature has yet to explore the role of psychological ownership in OGA. This study fills that gap by developing a novel approach that investigates how gaming motivations influence psychological ownership. In addition, prior studies on OGA have primarily emphasized linear relations be tween predictors and addictions [6,63]; we show that psychological ownership, in fact, has a curvilinear relation to OGA.

Among all the hypotheses, the relationship between social and psy chological ownership is not significant. Despite the non-significant result, this finding advances our understanding of online game addic tion. In terms of theory contribution, perhaps the most closely related work is that of Xu, Turel, and Yuan [8], who study the antecedents of online game addiction among adolescents and find several functional needs such as need for relationship and need for escapism drive online game playing and addiction. Our research extends this work in several ways: 1) whereas Xu, Turel, and Yuan [8] theorize direct relationships between functional needs and addiction, we bridge the gaming moti vations and gaming motivation with psychological ownership. 2) both Xu, Turel, and Yuan [8] and our study find a positive relationship be tween need for escapism and our respective outcome of interest. How ever, their results do not support a hypothesis from the need for advancement to addiction, while our study finds a positive relationship between achievement motivation and psychological ownership. In other words, our study advances their work by identifying a way to connect achievement/advancement with game addiction. 3) the non-significant result between socializing and psychological ownership is setting a boundary of the theory outlined by Xu, Turel, and Yuan [8]. When considering other variables in the antecedent’s model of gaming moti vation, one should interpret the socializing motivation more conservatively.

Second, our study extends the literature on psychological ownership. Whereas the research literature has predominantly focused on the pos itive outcomes of psychological ownership, such as enhanced job performance [18], greater acceptance of Internet technology by physi cians [43], and improved perception of cybersecurity [42,45], we show that psychological ownership has detrimental effects [41]. Our study unravels this potential negative effect of psychological ownership by empirically showing that, under certain circumstances, it is positively associated with OGA—a definite negative consequence. Given the widely expanding use of the Internet and video gaming, this is concerning.

Finally, although the emerging literature on primary and secondary control has gained attention in the field of psychology [28], medical research [50], and information systems [23], research has yet to apply these concepts to gaming addiction. Thus, we advance the literature by using this dual-process construct to OGA and provide the first empirical evidence that the disposition toward primary (secondary) control posi tively (negatively) predicts OGA. That is, individuals seeking primary control are more likely to become addicted to online games than those seeking secondary control. This finding corroborates previous studies that investigate the influence of primary control and secondary control on senior citizens and life stage changes [27,89].

## 6.3. Practical implications

This research provides implications for multiple parties, including users/gamers, publishers/game designers, and policymakers. For users, our findings allow users to make an informed decision in regards to gaming activity. In particular, this paper highlights the significance of game motivations related to psychological ownership, which in turn relates to game addiction. Specifically, those motivated by achievement and escapism are very likely to develop a sense of psychological ownership toward the virtual gaming world. Gaining this feeling may not be detrimental when the goal is to entertain. However, they should be cautious that certain levels of psychological ownership may lead to gaming addiction. On the other hand, if one is motivated by socializing within games, s/he is not likely to be addicted to the game, at least not via the development of psychological ownership. The paper also high lights the effect of primary/secondary control on online gaming addic tion, which helps raise awareness of users regarding the consequences of control.

For publishers and game designers, this paper offers several impli cations. First, the study sheds light on the positive relationship between achievement/escapism motivations and psychological ownership. We posit that game designers keep investing in developing achievement and escapism elements in a gaming environment. Most importantly, our study finds a non-significant relationship between socializing motiva tion and psychological ownership. This result reflects the availability and advancement of social media platforms in recent years, which mitigates the need to socialize in an online gaming environment. Social functionalities in online gaming environments and platforms are not on par with social media platforms. Based on this result, we suggest that game designers should modify their strategy to deal with increasing needs of social interaction. Game publishers have the advantage of having a large customer base who are mostly social media users. The implication is that publishers who develop a robust and attractive gaming platform and provide a rich social media platform will increase their market share as gamers will be drawn to platforms that achieve both capabilities.

Second, while publishers may be motivated by revenue and profit, publishers have a social responsibility to mitigate online gaming’s negative impacts, including addressing the addiction issue. A strong social media platform capability allows publishers to mitigate gaming addiction while at the same time, maintaining profits.

Third, the inverted-U relationship between psychological ownership and online game addiction may be attributed to the lack of “replay ability” in many popular games. This is particularly true in the context of achievement games. The concept of game “replayability” refers to gamers’ perception of the ability to continue playing and enjoying a game despite its completion [90]. These findings provide significant implications for game designers. In essence, publishers and game de signers need to invest more in gaming experiences that last longer and prolong the gaming experience.

This research also offers significant implications for policymakers. The tech industry has witnessed several new policies motivated by the negative consequences of platforms on game players. For example, the California Consumer Privacy Act of 2018 (CCPA) granted more control to California users over their personal information.<sup>7</sup> In addition, there have been legislative initiatives related to the age of gamers and the digital content of the games. This research provides insight into the dynamics of game playing and can attenuate the negative consequences.

Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic has disrupted the education sector and boosted online learning. Many predict a dramatic increase in institutional online learning after the pandemic.<sup>8</sup> Therefore, we strongly recommend incorporating virtual educational games in the online learning environment that focusing on achievement and escapism mo tivations. As noted in our study, games focusing on achievement and escapism can predict higher levels of psychological ownership. In the online learning context, we need platforms that will help the learner focus on content. Getting learners to focus on content is a perennial problem faced by educators in online learning environments.<sup>9</sup> Psycho logical ownership provides a novel angle for educators to engage learners better.

Online gaming is a rapidly changing industry. There have been major evolutionary changes in technology in recent years. In particular, there are significant advances in augmented reality, virtual reality, artificial intelligence, and voice controls,<sup>10</sup> as well as the emergence of streaming platforms. Those disruptive technologies are changing the way gamers approach games.<sup>11</sup> Our results can be generalized to these contexts and can guide the developers of these technologies regarding how to better engage users and at the same time, how to prevent addictions effectively.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

Our work is subject to several limitations. First, we address the broad context of online gaming, covering those launched on all devices, including mobile phones and consoles (Xbox®, PlayStation®, and PCs). However, the likelihood of addiction to games may vary according to the device (e.g., portability). Future research should examine our model for different gaming devices; for example, do players get addicted to mobile online games easier because they spend more time on their mobile de vices? Second, gender differences may have a role in gaming motiva tions and game addiction [91]. Although we include gender as a control variable in the model, future research may explore the moderating effect of gender on the relationship between gaming motivations and psy chological ownership, as well as the relation between psychological ownership and OGA. Finally, our findings are based on a sample collected in one country. To increase the generalizability of the findings, future studies are encouraged to replicate the model in different cultural settings.
