# Effect of data privacy and security investment on the value of big data firms

Yueyue Zhang , Cheng Zhang <sup>\*</sup>, Yunjie Xu (Calvin)

School of Management, Fudan University, Shanghai 200433, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT investment IT risk Big data Data Privacy Data Security Event study

## A B S T R A C T

The use of big data analytics (BDA) and related technologies by firms can raise concerns regarding data privacy and security (DPS). In this study, with stock return and risk as indicators of firm performance, we investigate how DPS investment affects the financial performance of BDA firms (firms with BDA investment) and non-BDA firms (firms without BDA investment). We use a difference-in-differences approach and apply the propensity score matching to 1400 DPS announcements made by 228 US firms whose stocks are publicly traded on US stock markets from 2004 to 2018. The main findings are as follows: (1) On average, DPS investment significantly reduces firms’ systematic risk. (2) The risk reduction effects are greater for non-BDA firms than for BDA firms. Our results indicate that DPS investment reduces firm risk and that the business value of specific information technology (IT) investment (e.g., DPS) is influenced by firms’ other IT assets (e.g., BDA). The present finding advance the literature on the business value of IT and firms’ IT portfolio management in digitized business environments.

## 1. Introduction

With the accumulation of abundant raw data in daily operations and the availability of powerful and sophisticated analytical tools, organi zations today can improve their decision-making and create new market opportunities [1]. For instance, the private cloud computing of the American retailer Walmart processes 2.5 petabytes of customer data every hour [2]. Many organizations have made considerable investment in business intelligence and analytics to exploit their data resources [3]. Researchers and practitioners have used terms such as “big data,” “big data analytics,” “data analytics,” and “business analytics” interchange ably to describe the data-driven decision-making process [4,5]. Ac cording to the studies by Gartner [6] and IBM [7], business intelligence and analytics constitute the top technological priority for chief infor mation officers and chief financial officers nowadays.

The use of big data analytics (BDA) and its related technologies to better understand user intentions and behaviors [8] and to facilitate operational decision-making [4,9] has attracted a wealth of attention in the field of business. Studies have directly or indirectly validated the market recognition of the value of BDA on the enhancement of firms operations, marketing, customer service, value chain operations, organizational agility, and knowledge management [4,8,9,10].

Although BDA is believed to provide a valuable opportunity for firms to improve decision-making capabilities through the mining of users demographic, behavioral, psychological, and other information, firms face multiple risks in the use of big data such as privacy intrusion [11]. The security and privacy threats of big data are obvious. In 2018, the Commission Nationale Informatique & Libert´es (CNIL), a French data protection authority, fined Google approximately \$57 million for failing to properly disclose to its users how data are collected in its services [12]. Investing in data privacy and security (DPS)<sup>1</sup> may help allay such concerns. However, whether DPS investment is more conducive to BDA firms (i.e., firms with BDA investment), as compared to non-BDA firms (i.e., firms without BDA investment), is unclear. The value of a single type of information technology (IT) investment (e.g., e-commerce and social media) has been investigated in research [13–15]. However, the interdependence between various types of IT resources remains unex plored [16]. In the present study, we use an event study approach in concert with a matching design to estimate the wealth and risk effects of DPS investment on firms’ equity performance. In addition, we investi gate whether these effects differed between BDA and non-BDA firms. As we explore the impacts of DPS investment on firm value, our focus is on investors’ expectations of firms’ underlying equity return and risk which are assessed with the abnormal return as well as idiosyncratic (i.e., risks associated with a firm’s volatility and other firm-specific factors) and systematic risks (i.e., firm exposure to macro-level economic risks) [17]. We adopt the perspective of a trader or investor in analyzing the con sequences of DPS investment on firms’ future performance.

Our results reveal that the effects of DPS investment vary by condi tions. Specifically, firms with DPS investment have a lower systematic risk than firms without such investment. The risk reduction effects of DPS investment are greater for non-BDA firms than for BDA firms. The varying risk reduction effects suggest that investors are more uncertain regarding DPS investment made by BDA firms than those made by non BDA firms. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical study to examine the intertwined effects of two types of IT (i.e., DPS and BDA). The interaction between the two - specifically, the contingent value of DPS investment with regard to firms’ other IT assets (i.e., BDA) - adds to the understanding of IT asset portfolio management.

This study makes several contributions. First, we investigate the business value of DPS investment through the lens of IT investment portfolios. Research on IT value has mostly focused on a single type of IT investment [13–15]. We extend the literature by going beyond this scope and considering more than one type of IT resource. By investi gating the varying effects of DPS investment on equity performance (i.e., the stock return and risk) of BDA and non-BDA firms, the symbiotic relationship between various types of IT resources is revealed [16,20]. This study also deepens the understanding of IT asset portfolio man agement by showing that the value of a specific type of IT investment (i. e., DPS) made by a firm can be influenced by BDA, another type of IT endowment in their IT portfolio. These findings may serve as a reference for organizational decision-making on how to improve firms’ IT in vestment portfolio performance by balancing risk and return through different ITs [21]. Second, prior studies have suggested that information security investment constitutes a vital decision for firms [22,23]. However, many other firms that have not immediately obtained the expected gains from information security investment have sometimes hesitated to make relevant decisions [24]. This study indicates that DPS investment may serve more as a risk moderator [15]. However, DPs investment reduces equity risk more for non-BDA firms than for BDA firms. This may be attributed to the fact that BDA firms usually incur more serious privacy concerns and greater distrust in customers than do non-BDA firms [25,26]. Although DPS investment signals a firm's commitment to data safety to investors, it is less persuasive for BDA firms than for non-BDA firms. Decision-makers and technology leaders in BDA firms may need to proactively communicate their commitment to DPS to investors [23]. For example, they could disclose their firms’ DPS investment on social media platforms more frequently to trigger user engagement and gain more institutional legitimacy, thereby reducing market uncertainty regarding the effectiveness of DPS investment in their organizations [27].

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review the literature on the business value of IT investment and how to measure its impact on firm performance. Next, we develop our hypotheses and describe our research design in detail. Finally, we present our empirical findings and discuss their implications as well as their limitations.

## 2. Theory and hypothesis development

## 2.1. Value of IT investment

The business value of IT investment has received considerable attention in the information systems literature [13,14,28,29]. The impact of IT (as an input or independent variable) on firm performance (as an output or dependent variable) has been widely studied through various methodological approaches. Announcements of IT investment can help firms effectively communicate with stakeholders and investors about their commitment to generating resources and building competitive advantage. Thus, an event study is suitable for investigating investors’ expectations regarding the consequences of a firm’s current actions on its future performance. Researchers on firm valuation often adopt two common measures: stock return and risk [13,15]. The stock return, also termed abnormal return, measures a firm’s profitability in relation to its equity. A firm’s equity risk is closely linked to investors expectations of the volatility of its cash flows [15,17].

Many studies have examined the positive relationship between general IT investment (e.g., investment in IT infrastructure, IT budgets, and IT labor) or specific IT investment (e.g., investment in e-commerce, social media platforms, and information security) and abnormal returns on stock prices [13–15,23]. A small but emerging body of literature in this research stream has investigated the impact of BDA as a specific type of IT investment on firm performance. It is reported that firms’ big data strategies and data-driven decision-making can have positive effects on their return [4,30,31].

Another strand of literature has investigated the relationship be tween IT investment (e.g., investment in enterprise resource planning systems and e-commerce) and firms’ equity risk [13,15]. Dewan and Ren [15] found that firms’ announcements of e-commerce adoption (i.e., ecommerce strategy implementation) are associated with increased firm risk as indicated by stock price volatility. In the present context, firms adoption of BDA enables them to access large data sets to make de cisions, which may involve confidential and privacy-sensitive data [32,33]. With the development of big data and related technologies, information about individuals’ personal habits, interests, and lifestyles can be collected on web browsing (on devices such as smartphones) and social media platforms [34]. Personal data have a high leakage risk in the process of data storage, transmission, and usage [35]. Such risk has led to widespread social concerns on data security and privacy. It has become a new risk for firms that invest in BDA [32,33]. In essence, firms BDA investments may involve more salient risk than do other types of IT investment.

## 2.2. IT investment portfolio

Prior studies on the business value and risk of IT investment have mostly focused on the direct effect of a single type of IT investment on firm performance. Also, some studies have examined the interactions between general IT investment and R&D investment [16]. However, firms rarely make IT investments independent of other IT resources. Similar to the management of financial portfolios, firms often manage IT as a portfolio of assets. Firms identify potential IT projects on the basis of their interdependencies, and balance risk and return to improve per formance [21,36]. Although the literature has demonstrated that it is economically justifiable for firms to invest in BDA or DPS on a stand alone basis [22,23,30], how the performance of one IT is contingent on the existence of other ITs remains unclear.

The present study investigates whether DPS investment significantly affects a firm’s market performance and whether this effect varies be tween BDA and non-BDA firms. Our hypotheses on the impact of DPS investment are based on the resource-based view and institutional the ory. According to the resource-based view, investors may consider a collection of data as a valuable, rare, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable resource that can generate a competitive advantage [37]. However, data (especially big data) pose considerable risks to information privacy and security at the societal level [32,35]. How firms address and manage these risks can be understood through the lens of institutional theory [38,43,44]. External demand for DPS pushes firms to legitimize their data-driven business practices [39]. Those societal concerns can be eased through relevant DPS investment; however, the extent to which this can be accomplished may vary among firms. For instance, BDA firms usually arouse greater societal concerns and thus experience more dif ficulty in gaining legitimacy for their data-based business [33,35]. Given this background, the present study explores the potential interactions between BDA and DPS

## 3. Hypothesis development

According to the resource-based view [37], data assets become competitive resources when data are valuable, inimitable, rare, and nonsubstitutable. When firms fail to ensure DPS, these attributes are compromised and the value of data as a resource may no longer be guaranteed. Therefore, DPS investment can help increase firm value. The following hypothesis is posited:

Hypothesis 1. Firms’ abnormal return increases after DPS investment.

The idiosyncratic equity risk reflects investors’ expectations of a firm’s equity volatility that are due to firm-specific factors unrelated to the financial market [17]. The adoption of DPS technology can mitigate operational risks by reducing security threats [14]. Therefore, DPS in vestment can ensure operational stability and thus the stability of a firm’s future cash flows. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 2. Firms’ idiosyncratic risk decreases after DPS investment.

Systematic risk refers to the firm’s exposure to macro-level economic risks, such as fluctuations in market demand, that are borne by the entire industry [17]. Given the widespread societal concerns over DPS, firms face customer distrust and legal regulations with regard to their data practices. As mentioned, firms that operate a data-related business are exposed to greater economic risks [32,33]. DPS investment can help guard against the potential downside in the firm’s economic footprint in data-related businesses. Disclosure of security-related information serves as an indication of firms’ commitment to addressing relevant risks and protecting customers’ personal information [40], thereby allevi ating external concerns regarding information security. Consequently, DPS investment can help firms cope with macroeconomic vulnerabilities with enhanced legitimacy and brand equity in the eyes of stakeholders. Firms with DPS investment are thus able to buffer themselves from market shocks, and endure less systematic risk. The following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 3. Firms’ systematic risk decreases after DPS investment.

Big data investment may only benefit firms with large volumes of data [41]. The term big data refers to massive and complex datasets generated in real-time. The extraction of patterns, trends, and associa tions from these datasets requires the use of sophisticated management, analytical, and processing techniques [42]. The four defining charac teristics of big data—beyond its size—are variety (i.e., data type di versity), velocity (i.e., the speed at which data are generated and used), variability (i.e., how the data are captured from different spatial and temporal contexts), and value (i.e., measurable and often previously unknown improvements) [43]. By taking into account these four Vs, firms can build and use endogenous and exogenous knowledge from the big data. Sufficient protection on datasets via DPS investment could thus help BDA firms guard their datasets against attacks and maintain their competitive advantage.

Conversely, anecdotal evidence suggests that DPS investment may impede the operation of data-related businesses by lowering the preci sion of data, thus resulting in higher data processing costs. For instance, advertisers lost on average at least 52% of their revenue once Google allowed its users to disable cookie tracking in browsers [44]. The Chi nese online gaming firm NetEase reported that “compliance with any additional [privacy] laws could be expensive and may place restrictions on the conduct of our business and the manner in which we interact with our customers” [45]. However, a firm that has invested in BDA can search for alternative sources of data to reduce these side effects of DPS investment. In Google’s case, it could use search keywords, user profiles, and social connections to complement the blocked data source.<sup>2</sup> Hence, we argue that DPS poses less hindrance to BDA firms than non-BDA firms because the latter uses a less variety of data and fewer sources of data, and BDA firms can realize more potential value from DPS investment. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 4. BDA firms’ abnormal return increases more than do non-BDA firms after DPS investment.

Regarding firm risks, equity risk, which measures the variation in the firm’s stock performance, theoretically reflects the investors’ perception of the firm’s future cash flow based on information from public an nouncements (e.g., those about DPS investment). Specifically, the simultaneous implementation of DPS and BDA and the realization of their potential synergistic effects may affect the uncertainty of firms operational costs. IT implementation is inherently risky because of technological complexity and implementation challenges [46]. Whether firms adopting both technologies face greater risks than do those adopting only one (e.g., whether 1 + 1 > 2) is unclear. Therefore, DPS investment by BDA firms is associated with higher uncertainty in implementation risk compared to non-BDA firms. Higher uncertainty leads to higher volatility of stock returns from DPS investment. There fore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 5. BDA firms’ idiosyncratic risk decreases less than do non-BDA firms after DPS investment.

As indicated in Hypothesis 3, DPS investment helps offset the po tential downside risks concerning a firm’s economic footprint in datarelated businesses. However, big data often requires sources of data drawn from various technical environments, such as the Internet of Things, cloud computing, and social networking [26]. Privacy leakage through BDA has become a common concern. Even the anonymity of users cannot be guaranteed. For instance, in a study of 3-month credit card records of 1.1 million users, it was found that four spatiotemporal points are sufficient to uniquely reidentify 90% of users. Adding one dimension of user information (i.e., price in the context) increases the risk of reidentification by 22% on average [25]. This indicates that anonymous personal information can be easily deanonymized through the collection. transmission, and analysis of BDA. Although the disclosure of DPS investment information may suggest to investors about firms’ commitment to addressing security-related risks and thus help firms better establish consumer trust, brand equity, and institutional legitimacy for their business [40], market concerns regarding data se curity and privacy remain more serious for BDA firms than for non-BDA firms. In other words, the risk mitigation effects of DPS investment are lower for BDA firms than for non-BDA firms.

In addition, because BDA firms have larger economic footprints in data-related businesses, their DPS systems may incur more fixed costs than do non-BDA firms. Such cost difference is reflected as the degree of operational leverage, which is one of the drivers of systematic risk [15]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 6. BDA firms’ systematic risk decreases less than do non-BDA firms after DPS investment.

## 4. Data and methodology

An event study methodology that entails analysis of the impact of an event on firm value is a popular method for assessing the immediate response of investors to announcements. This methodology is exten sively used in the fields of information systems, finance, marketing, and management to measure the reaction of the stock market to a firm’s announcements [13–15,22,23]. In the present study, we used an event study approach to examine the return and risk effects of DPS an nouncements. Because a firm’s decision to make DPS investment is self selected, a strong selection bias potentially makes these firms system atically different from other firms without DPS investment. To address this self-selection bias, we emploved a matching technique to define more closely aligned treatment and control samples. Our data setup enabled us to make better causal inferences with minimal selection bias to estimate the treatment effect of DPS investment [47].

We chose 2001 as the starting year because the Key Development section of SPCI database was less complete prior to that year. ## 4.2. Method and measures

We exploited three dependent variables of interest as $D V _ { i t } .$ The dependent variables were derived from the event study approach to capture the market reaction to these announcements. Researchers sug gest two common measures of firm equity value: stock return and risk [13,15]. We collected firms’ daily stock returns from the University of Chicago’s Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP). The CRSP US Stock Database contains end-of-day and month-end prices of all stocks listed at NASDAQ, NYSE, and AMEX, along with basic market indices.

Equity Return. We used cumulative standardized abnormal return (CSAR) to assess the impact of an event on stock prices. We adopted the Fama–French–Carhart model [50,51] (Eq. (1)) to compute abnormal stock returns. The model incorporates the market return factor, size factor, book-to-market factor, and momentum factor [50,51]. It posits a linear relationship between the stock return and the four factors over a given time period.

$$
R _ {i t} - R _ {f t} = \beta_ {0 i} + \beta_ {1 i} (R _ {m t} - R _ {f t}) + \beta_ {2 i} S M B _ {t} + \beta_ {3 i} H M L _ {t} + \beta_ {4 i} M O M _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where $R _ { i t }$ is the return of firm i on day $t , R _ { m t }$ is the return of market portfolio m on day $t , R _ { f t }$ is the risk-free return on day t, SMB is the dif ference between the rates of return of small and big stock firms, HML is the difference in returns between high and low book-to-market ratio stocks, MOM is the Carhart’s momentum effects, $\beta _ { O i }$ is the intercept, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the model residual. Data for Fama–French–Carhart factors and momentum $( R _ { m } , R _ { f } , S M B _ { t } , H M L _ { b }$ and MOM ) were obtained from htt p://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data\_library.ht ml.

We used a three-day window around the event date [− 1,1] to ac count for possible information leakage on the day before the announcement and for possible delays in the dissemination of news on the day after the announcement [48]. We estimated all parameters of the model using ordinary least squares regression over an estimation period of 180 trading days. We ended the estimation period 10 days prior to the event date to shield the estimates from possible effects related to the announcement and to avoid non-stationarities in the estimates. We also required that a firm must have a minimum of 50 days of stock return observations during the estimation period. A value-weighted average return of all stocks in our sample was used as the market return. Event study differentiates returns that would have been expected if the analyzed event (i.e., the DPS announcement) had not taken place. We thus calculated abnormal returns $( A R _ { i t } )$ for firm i on Day t as the differ ence between the actual and the expected returns using Eq. (2) as shown below:

$$
A R _ {i t} = \left(R _ {i t} - R _ {f t}\right) - \left[ \widehat {\beta} _ {0 i} + \widehat {\beta} _ {1 i} \left(R _ {m t} - R _ {f t}\right) + \widehat {\beta} _ {2 i} S M B _ {t} + \widehat {\beta} _ {3 i} H M L _ {t} + \widehat {\beta} _ {4 i} M O M _ {t} \right]\tag{2}
$$

We then calculated the cumulative standardized abnormal returns (CSAR) over the three-day [− 1,1] event window around the announcement date for each firm as our first dependent variable [14,23].

Equity Risk. Firms’ risk includes systematic risk and idiosyncratic risk. Event study compares risks before and after a significant event. Taking the variance of both sides of the Fama–French–Carhart mode (Eq. (1)):

$$
\operatorname{Var} \left(R _ {i} - R _ {f}\right) = \beta_ {i} ^ {2} \operatorname{Var} \left(R _ {m} - R _ {f}\right) + \operatorname{Var} \left(\varepsilon_ {i}\right)\tag{3}
$$

where Var $( R _ { i } \cdot R _ { f } )$ is the total risk of a firm, $\beta _ { i }$ characterizes the sys tematic risk in the total risk, and Var (ε ) is the idiosyncratic risk. We calculated the change in systematic risk following the model in Dewan and Ren [15] before and after the announcement event. A key feature of this model is that it extends the estimation window to include both pre event and post-event data and it allows for the model parameters β to change after the event. Specifically, in this study, before an event, the estimation window spans from -190th trading day to -10th trading day. After an event, the estimation window spans from +10th trading day to +190th trading day.

We calculated the second component of equity risk, idiosyncratic risk, by taking the standard deviation of the residual of the Eq. (1). To measure the change in firm idiosyncratic risk, we first calculated the firm’s pre-event idiosyncratic risk in the estimation window from -190th trading day to -10th trading day. Similarly, the firm’s post-event idio syncratic risk is based on the estimation window from +10th trading day to +190th trading day. The timeline for computing firm risk is illus trated in Fig. 1. We measured the change in firms’ risk, i.e., by sub tracting pre-event risk from post-event risk. Noteworthy, we used the difference between the pre-event and post-event firm risks rather than the absolute value. The negative value means that firms’ risk decreases after DPS adoption, and the positive value means that firms’ risk in creases after DPS adoption.

## 4.3. Matching design

Our goal is to verify whether there is a causal effect of DPS invest ment on financial dependent variables and whether the effect varies between BDA and non-BDA companies. Using the matching method, we could ensure that the changes in market performance are indeed due to the DPS announcement rather than to some unobservable factors that correlate with the stock market.

Having identified firms with DPS investments, we constructed a matching sample of firms that had the same propensity to make DPS investments but did not. The matching approach identified a set of benchmark firms that did not make an announcement of DPS investment but were similar to the sample firms in main aspects of interest such as industry, BDA background, stock size, and performance [15,52].

The initial pool of control group included all firms listed in three US stock markets (i.e., NASDAQ, NYSE, and AMEX) that did not make any DPS announcement during our research period. 3797 public firms entered the candidate pool. We paired each firm-event observation in our sample with a control observation in the same year and with similar stock performance and firm attributes: (1) the average daily stock return over the two years prior to the DPS event, (2) the average daily trading volume over the two years prior to the DPS event, (3) whether the firm is a BDA firm, and (4) the primary 2-digit standard industrial classification (SIC) code of the firm as industry fixed effect.

We adopted propensity score matching (PSM) [53] with the number of nearest neighbors set to 1 and a caliper of 0.05 standard deviation of the propensity score. If no suitable benchmark firm existed with the matching criteria, the observation was discarded [52]. This was to ensure there was no inadequate sample to contaminate the results of the comparison. 60 events were thus excluded. The application of these criteria produced a sample that contained 2680 firm-event observations (1340 treated and 1340 control). Eventually, there were 225 unique firms in the treatment group and 317 unique firms in the benchmark group that met all the matching criteria.

The calculation of DVs for the firms in the control group was exactly the same as it was for the treatment sample. We conducted a balance test comparing the treatment and the matched records. Fig. 2 shows that the PSM technique ensured the balance between the two groups by the key variables.

To control for other factors, we included firm-level and environ mental variables. Firms’ accounting and financial factors were extracted from the Compustat database. Factors included annual R&D, advertising expense, firm size, financial performance, and DPS experience. Prior studies considered R&D and advertising as important inputs along with IT investment [16]. Firms’ technology capabilities (including those related to hardware and software) could be reflected in their R&D expense [59]. Advertising boosts firm value by making firms more visible to individual and institutional investors [54]. Firm size wa measured by the natural logarithm of the total assets [14]. Firm per formance was measured by the return on assets (ROA) [17]. These control variables were measured according to the fiscal year immedi ately preceding the year of the DPS event. The firm’s DPS experience was measured by the number of DPS announcements by the firm before the focal DPS event. For environmental variables, we included the public concerns on privacy using Google Trends Index. Google search trends provide a normalized measure of the increase or decrease in Internet searches associated with the specified terms over time. It has been used to capture trends in consumer confidence, real estate prices, and trading behavior on stock exchanges [55]. We recorded the Google trends in each month from 2004 to 2018 with “privacy” as the keyword. We also classified firms that were located in data-intensive industries such as the standard industrial classification (SIC) code 6000–6999 (Finance, In surance, and Real Estate) and 7000–7999 (Services) [56] to control for industry-specific characteristics. The results are robust when we used 2- digit SIC codes as the control for industry fixed effect. We summarize the definition of all variables in Table 2.

## 5.2. DiD analysis with a matched sample

Since the unit of analysis was an announcement, one firm could make multiple DPS announcements. Accordingly, we estimated all models using feasible generalized least squares (FGLS). The FGLS estimator is considered more efficient than the OLS estimator because of its correc tion for heteroskedasticity, serial correlation, and cross-sectional cor relation. We used ‘xtgls’ command with ‘corr(ar1)’ and ‘panels(het) options in Stata 15. This command allows estimation in the presence of AR (1) autocorrelation within panels and cross-sectional correlation and heteroskedasticity across panels. We controlled for time-related dummy variables in the full models. We then calculated variance inflation fac tors (VIFs) for all models in our analyses. Results showed that the average VIF value for the three models (4), (5), and (6) was 2.72, indicating that the multicollinearity problem was not a concern in the estimation [57]. Due to missing values on some variables, the final size of the sample is 2376. The estimation results of the treatment effect (i.e., DPS investment) are shown in columns (1)–(3). The full models with interaction effects of BDA and DPS are displayed in columns (4)–(6).

According to the base models and full models in Table 6, the effect of DPS investment on equity return was on average insignificant, which was consistent with the result shown in Table 3. As such, hypothesis 1 was not supported. Besides, the coefficient of Treatment was not significant in model (2), but was significantly negative in model (3) (β = $- 0 . 0 3 1 , p = . 0 1 1 )$ , indicating that DPS investment on average signifi cantly reduced a firm’s systematic risk more than idiosyncratic risk. Therefore, hypothesis 2 was not supported while hypothesis 3 was supported.

Considering the interaction terms of DPS and BDA, we observed no significant DiD coefficient in model (4). Therefore, hypothesis 4 was not supported. Since the main effect of DPS on idiosyncratic risk was not significant, hypothesis 5 was not supported. In the contrast, the strongly significant interaction effect in model (6) $( \beta = 0 . 1 3 5 , p = . 0 0 0 )$ suggested that non-BDA firms were more likely to benefit from DPS in vestment than BDA firms in terms of systematic risk mitigation, thus supporting our hypothesis 6. In other words, the result suggested that being a BDA firm incurs greater uncertainty among investors with re gard to their DPS investment.

## 5.3. Robustness check

We ensured the robustness of our results by performing several additional analyses, including using alternative event windows, using an alternative market model, verifying our BDA measure, missing data fulfillment, addressing self-selection bias of BDA/non-BDA firms, and alternative setup of the regression model. The various checks lend further credence to our findings regarding the heterogeneous impacts of DPS investment on BDA and non-BDA firms

## 6. Discussions

We utilize a difference-in-differences (DiD) approach and event study method to examine how DPS investment impacted the value of firms and generated different effects between BDA and non-BDA firms. We conclude from the results that DPS investment was overall associated with a significant decrease in firms’ systematic risk. The risk reduction effects were greater for non-BDA firms than for BDA firms.

The present study makes several contributions to the literature. First, it complements the strand of research on the wealth effect of securityrelated investment on firm value [14,22,23]. From our findings, it can be inferred that DPS also serves as a risk changer [15]. Firms may adopt DPS technologies in their IT portfolio to enhance their market value through risk mitigation. Reliable data security safeguards such as deidentification, encryption, rotating and random identifiers, decentral ized identities, and similar measures can protect users’ data from harmful exposure and hacking attempts. For example, firms can prevent data breaches by securing user credentials—specifically, by stream lining the user experience associated with two-factor authentication [14]. To strengthen data privacy, firms can also consider ensuring that users are informed when their data are collected and that users are aware of the purpose of the data collection procedure [58]. Second, the information systems literature usually focuses on the value of a single IT investment. The present study provides a better understanding of the interdependencies of various IT resources and demonstrates how the value of a specific type of IT investment (i.e., DPS) made by a firm can be influenced by BDA, another type of IT endowment. Specifically, DPS investment reduces equity risk more for non-BDA firms than for BDA firms. Although DPS investment signals a firm’s commitment to data safety to investors, the market reaction varies. Notably, the BDA back ground of a firm may generate greater uncertainty among investors with regard to DPS investment.

Our study also yields two managerial implications for decisionmaking in matters related to information security. First, our findings provide managers with a comprehensive understanding of the real market value of firms’ DPS activities. As mentioned, many firms may hesitate to make security investment decisions when they do not immediately see expected gains from such investment [24]. The study suggests that DPS investment may serve more as a risk changer than wealth changer for firms. Second, our study illuminates a key aspect (i. e., firms’ BDA background) that has been overlooked in the evaluation of return on IT security–related investment. According to one study, se curity investment and external security environment are interdependent [24]. We suggest that technology leaders also consider the interdepen dence between security investment and internal IT resources. Regarding financial decision-making, we found that the market value of DPS may be contingent on firms’ BDA background. Risk reduction effects are not as substantial for BDA firms as for non-BDA firms. Executive decision makers in BDA firms may need to more effectively communicate their efforts in reinforcing DPS to investors (e.g., through greater disclosure on social media platforms) to mitigate market concerns on the effec tiveness of DPS investment in their organizations.

Several future extensions of the scope of the present study are pre sented as follows. First, we focused on the relationship between DPS and BDA. Combinations of other types of IT resources may produce different outcomes of stock return and risk. Second, because we could not identify the exact date when firms became categorized as using BDA in the SPCI database, we defined BDA as a firm type as long as the historical key announcements of the firm contained references to activities associated with big data. Future research can use alternative data sources to address this issue and provide more nuanced evidence on the relation ship between DPS and BDA investment; for instance, whether the DPS value changes before and after a firm becomes a BDA firm merits investigation. Third, market reaction to DPS investment may vary on technology sophistication [14], which may further influence the inter action effects of BDA and DPS investments. Identifying the technology sophistication of DPS technology can provide a more granular test of our theoretical arguments. Last, we used market reaction as a measure of financial performance; possible extensions include using accountingbased indicators. Future research can also explore the long-term ef fects of DPS investment on the business value of BDA firms and non-BDA firms.
