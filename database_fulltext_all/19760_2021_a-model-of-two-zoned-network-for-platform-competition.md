# A model of two-zoned network for platform competition

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Platform competition Two-zoned network Two-sided network Zone parallelism Latent payment Service diversity

## A B S T R A C T

Platform-mediated network has received increasing attention from researchers and practitioners in recent years. The literature on this topic has been mainly derived from the model of two-sided network (2SN) where producers and consumers comprise the two sides and are considered the main user groups. Inadequacy, however, has been noticed where the two-sided network model cannot fully explicate the dynamics of platform competition. A more comprehensive model can help firms better understand the nature of platform competition so as to make informed decisions in this evolving landscape. By adding a higher dimension of “zone” to the existing “side” dimension, we propose a new two-zoned network (2ZN) model. A 2ZN consists of two 2SNs and the platform at the core functionally serves as a nexus connecting the two networks. In addition, we propose three properties of 2ZN (namely, zone parallelism, service diversity in the pay zone, and latent payment in the free zone) to help further explore two-zoned networks. For illustration purposes, four real business cases are identified, analyzed, and discussed to exhibit the applicability of the proposed 2ZN model. With additional insights into the structure and inner working of platform-mediated networks through the new 2ZN model, managers can make betterinformed decisions to cope with platform competition. The case analysis and related discussion suggest prom ising opportunities for future research.

## 1. Introduction

In the last two decades, the business landscape has experienced a massive transformation by digital platforms. This force has led to an emerging market structure, the so-called networked markets [1]. Many industries ranging from transportation, hospitality and healthcare to financial service, software and media have all been significantly impacted. To study this emerging market structure, researchers and practitioners have mainly employed the model of two-sided network (2SN) [1–3]. In a 2SN, the core platform plays a key role in providing the infrastructure, services, and rules designed by the platform owners and sponsors. Moreover, myriad user groups such as customers, producers, complementors, and intermediaries connect, communicate and transact with one another [4–8]. Examples of 2SN include HMOs that link pa: tients with healthcare providers; Google that directs Web users to ad vertisers; eBay that brings together buyers and sellers in its online auction market; Uber that connects riders with drivers for ride-sharing; Airbnb that offers traveling guests short-term lodging services provided by local hosts; Upwork that matches freelancers with clients through its online labor market; and online gaming that helps game designers to attract gamers [1,2,9–11].

The structure of two-sided network markets is fundamentally different from that of traditional ones, and their distinct features war rant elaboration. The following issues are typical for two-sided network markets whose structure is subject to unique network effects. First, the interactions within and between user groups often lead to direct (sameside) and indirect (cross-side) network effects [1]. Due to many possible interplays of such network effects, pricing for 2SN can be challenging. For example, permanent subsidies offered by one side to the other side are a practice frequently adopted as a catalyst for cross-side network effects in creating a positive feedback loop to foster a virtuous cycle. Pricing issues in two-sided networks have long been a focal topic in the literature [4,12]. Second, for monetization, network effects in 2SN are closely related to users' willingness to pay. and researchers have explored strategies for platforms to maximize this willingness to pay [13]. Third, issues such as the “chicken or egg” problem at launch, managing expectations, addressing externalities, considering disruption to existing industries, and better understanding demand- vs. supply-side scale economies are all important topics and need to be examined. Once a platform owner gains a first-mover advantage, the “winner-take-all” (WTA) phenomenon is likely to occur, as the dominant leader enjoys tremendous returns to scale with little left for competitors. Fourth, the organizational capabilities of platform sponsors and the selection of platform development strategies are crucial to the success of 2SN and hence ought to be carefully studied [9,14,15]. Finally, the roles of various participants and the identification of a useful platform taxonomy both are matters of importance for market structuring [2]. All these areas can be advanced through enhanced structural lenses that delve into broader platform competition issues regarding the nature and dy namics of 2SN.

A new model called the two-zoned network (2ZN) is developed to enhance the current prevailing 2SN model. A key difference in our model is that we advance the notion by adding one more dimension (referred to as “zone”) to the existing dimension of “side.” A separate 2SN operates within each zone, and the nexus platform connects the two 2SNs across the two zones. A 2ZN platform provides free and paid ser vices in the two zones, which are labeled as the free and pay zones, correspondingly. However, it needs to be pointed out that payment is just one of several marked differences that differentiate the zones. They are called the free zone and pay zone primarily for ease of reference in the paper. At least two other substantial differences also exist: (1) the function of the network likely differs for uses in each zone, and (2) user interactions within each zone can be dissimilar. These differences may result in varied 2SNs of different nature, even though the core platform of the 2ZN remains the same and is shared.

Aside from the conceptual model, three essential properties of 2ZN are also proposed: zone parallelism, service diversity in the pay zone, and latent payment in the free zone. One specific 2ZN case can reside anywhere in the three dimensional space constructed by these proper ties. We then analyze four business cases by applying this new 2ZN framework for comparison and contrast. Our study contributes to the literature by offering a new model of two-zoned network to help analyze emerging types of platform-mediated markets. We demonstrate the 2ZN model’s usefulness and applicability through the analysis of cases where the existing 2SN model seems to fall short. While significant work has been done on platform management and competition for 2SN and its participants [16], previous findings are not directly generalizable to the cases considered in the paper. Through the proposed 2ZN model, new insights are gained for exploring broader and more complicated issues of platform-mediated markets.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature of platform-mediated networks and network effects. Sec tion 3 develops and elaborates the two-zoned network model. Section 4 presents case analysis of two-zoned networks to explicate the new fea tures of the 2ZN model compared to the traditional 2SN model. Section 5 proposes three essential properties of two-zoned networks to facilitate in-depth analysis. Finally, Section 6 discusses the strategic implications and legal ramifications of the 2ZN model before we conclude in Section 7.

At the core of a traditional two-sided network lies a platform that links the two user sides of the network and facilitates their interactions. The core platform thus influences pricing, value creation, architectural design, and other essential aspects of a platform business. How a user group uses the network is called the function of the platform. Eisenmann [2] summarizes four functions of 2SN as connectivity, variety, matching, and price-setting. For instance, online auction house eBay is considered a price-setting platform as it helps buyers with needs to find sellers with offerings so they can reach a fair price through bidding. Facebook, on the contrary, is a variety social network platform where one side of in dividual users is subsidized by another side of advertisers that accounts for 85% of its revenue. Users find Facebook valuable because it allows them to share and view a variety of content with friends. Marketers also find Facebook valuable because its large and diverse user base helps a firm reach targeted consumers. Due to the strong network effect, both direct and indirect, Facebook soon dominated the social media market once the virtuous cycle was set in motion [1].

## 3.1. Essentials of 2ZN

While the 2SN model has been shown useful in analyzing platform competition, it could not fully capture the competitive dynamics of some new network platforms. For instance, platforms like LinkedIn and YouTube offer free services and paid services through different accounts. The difference between these accounts and their functions motivates our study. As an example, while paid users on LinkedIn mainly make use of its hiring and job features based on the platform’s matching function, free users primarily use it as a social network site to make connections with other users (connectivity) and to consume content (variety). The platform provides distinctive values for the two types of users in its role of enabling interactions both inside and across groups. The platform achieves this not through one but different mechanisms. It is thus both necessary and compelling to further improve the 2SN model so that these new platform networks can be fully understood.

A key differentiating factor of 2ZNs from the traditional 2SNs is whether services of different value are offered in the two zones through the different functions performed by the platform. If so, platform owners may implement a unique competitive strategy for paid users and free users so as to create distinct values in the two zones. In essence, we further enhance the 2SN model by creating an additional “zone” dimension beyond the “side” dimension. Users in each zone leverage or derive value from the platform and its provided services in a variety of ways. We present our two-zoned network model in Fig. 2. As can be seen, a two-zoned network consists of one free zone and one pay zone, each comprising a two-sided network of its own. It is noted that in each zone, the 2SNs could be three-sided or multiple-sided depending on the actual market structure (as several of the cases to be discussed will show). In the middle, the platform serves as an interface nexus that connects different user groups by performing various functions in each zone.

Following Eisenmann’s definitions [2], we describe the possible participants in a 2ZN. Platform owners (or sponsors) specify the rules, guidelines, and standards for a network platform. Accordingly, they decide the underlying technologies, the participants’ roles, and the interaction mechanisms. Platform providers are the interfaces that link user groups together as mediation for their interactions. Suppliers of platform components offer additional goods and services to enhance user experience and facilitate user interactions. Demand-side users, also referred to as end users, are the main consumers. The platform does not charge them for using the services of the free zone since the platform owner intends to grow the number of end users in the free zone as quickly as possible. End users in the pay zone make either one-time or recurring payments to enjoy extra services in terms of better quality or more variety. End users in both free and pay zones are often subject to the direct (same-side) or indirect (cross-side) network effects. Due to these positive network effects, platform providers may obtain economies of scale on the demand side that can be leveraged for monetization either directly or indirectly. Supply-side users (also called producers) deliver content to be consumed by users on the demand side of the platform; that is, they interact or transact with end users in both free and pay zones. The priority of the free zone is to obtain new users quickly and achieve a critical mass level necessary to trigger positive network effects. In the pay zone, platform operators aim to gain profit and the platform offers an assortment of services to achieve this goal. The supply-side users in the two zones can be of the same group since many platforms offer the same service in both zones from the same group of suppliers but with varied quality.

In Fig. 2, we depict direct and indirect network effects in purple and blue arrows, respectively. Each of these network effects can be positive (denoted by the green plus sign) or negative (denoted by the red minus sign) depending on the situation. The definitions and explanations of network effects are consistent with the literature [38,45]. Positive (negative) direct network effect means an increase in the number of users leads to an increase (decrease) in the value for users on the same side. For example, the users of Facebook enjoy a positive direct network effect since the more people use Facebook, the higher utility they obtain in communicating with others. Positive (negative) indirect network ef fect means an increase (decrease) in the number of users on one side spawns the offering of complementary goods from the other side, in turn resulting in an increase (decrease) in the value of the original product. For example, users and advertisers of Facebook have indirect network effects. The effect from users to advertisers is positive since more users mean more potentials buyers and higher values to advertisers whereas the effect from advertisers to users is negative since more advertisers mean users would be exposed to more advertisements which decreasing their utility. The yellow arrow denotes cross-zone interactions between free and paid users, indicating a potential cross-zone network effect that has yet been studied in the extant literature of 2SN. The green dash line with arrow represents transitions from free users group to paid user groups. The platform connects more than one user group in each zone, oftentimes to fulfill different functions. It is noted that the users of a group do not have to perform an identical role in both zones. Adver tisers, for instance typically communicate with free users who are ofter shown a video commercial before accessing free services. In contrast, paid users, once signing up for the premier accounts, would not have to interact with advertisers.

## 3.2. Properties of 2ZN

We propose three properties to help analyze the platform ecosystem of a 2ZN. These properties are proposed because they are the most distinctive 2ZN features compared with 2SN and they can crucial and helpful in guiding strategic decisions. We follow Davis and Cosenza’s [46] four major levels of measurement (i.e., nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio) to specify the indicator evaluation process for the three properties. The first creation of 2ZN is the consideration of “zone.” With the additional zone, the immediate feature would be whether these two zones are heterogeneous or homogeneous. The first property differen tiates these two zones based on functionality. Using a functional clas sification, Eisenmann [2] categorizes 2SNs into four types: connectivity, matching, price-setting, and variety. We first define zone parallelism of a 2ZN as the similarity level of the two zones in terms of their functions, and it is an inter-zone property. A 2ZN exhibits high zone parallelism when its two 2SNs perform identical functions in the two zones, while it is said to show low zone parallelism if they perform dissimilar functions in the two zones. Specifically, zone parallelism is decided by comparing the functions of services in the two zones and counting how many of them are different. If the services in the two zones are functionally and numerically the same, then the 2ZN has high zone parallelism; other wise, it is said to have low zone parallelism. In other words, the extent of zone parallelism is ordinal and can be calculated by comparing how many different services the two zones have or counting how many same services the two zones have.

Next, end users in the free zone are oftentimes expected of a latent payment in exchange for the free services. For example, users may be asked to watch a video commercial or share personal information like phone numbers, email addresses, physical locations, etc. Thus, the sec ond essential property of two-zoned networks relates to the latent pay ment in the free zone. In other words, whether or not the service is really free to end-users in the free zone is a characteristic that can distinguish one 2ZN platform from another. This is a binary nominal indicator with 1 meaning there is latent payment in the free zone and 0 indicating no latent payment. If the latent payment is required, then the services in the free zone are not truly free since it incurs opportunity costs for endusers. Companies should take different strategies to different cus tomers depending on whether they pay nothing or pay latently. For free users without latent payment, the goal of the company is to grow the user base as much as possible. For users that pay the platform latently, except accumulate user base, an additional goal is to match potential users (buyers) with advertisers.

Finally, a 2ZN platform may provide paid users with services of assorted qualities and varieties. In some cases, the additional service is an extension of the free service with greater capacity or longer duration. In other cases, to satisfy unique user needs, the platform provides totally new services in the pay zone that are different from the service offered in the free zone. Thus, we propose the third property as the service diversity in the pay zone. This interval property is measured by the number of unique services this platform provides in the pay zone.

It is noted that service diversity is not the same with zone paral lelism, as the former is an attribute of the pay zone while the latter is decided by comparing the free zone and pay zone. Sometimes, a plat form has high service diversity in the pay zone and this diversity likely makes paid services quite different from the free services offered in the free zone, leading to low zone parallelism. However, in other cases, both properties could be low as the pay services fulfill the homogenous equivalent functions (i.e., low service diversity) and yet are very different from the free services (i.e., low zone parallelism). In other words, service diversity and zone parallelism do not necessarily corre late with each other.

Moreover, high service diversity means a 2ZN performs myriad varied functions, leading to complicated technical requirements that may need extensive development efforts from designers and developers. Low service diversity, on the other hand, means a 2ZN likely can share the infrastructure for similar functions across the two zones. The im plications of service diversity for technical requirements, infrastructure deployment, and development costs correspond to the logic of service computing where more complex IT services translate into more complicated technical design and coding as well as greater deployment and maintenance efforts. Service diversity thus relates to internal infrastructure cost and represents a key factor for platform design. Specifically, service diversity implies different infrastructure costs to support different services across zones. These costs can impact technical choices where some architectural approaches are more fitting than others. Managers to whom this construct matter the most are likely from the computer science side of platform design. Meanwhile service di versity can also influence the ability of platform to bundle services to stave off competition or perform other improper conducts. The design of underlying platform architecture may demonstrate the intent of de signers to evolve the platform in anticipation of increasing competition. As such, service diversity can provide important insights into platform market issues and can be used as a relevant metric in the deliberation of antitrust policy.

In summary, in the new 2ZN model we introduce a new dimension of zone to differentiate free services and paid services. We also propose zone parallelism as an inter-zone property and service diversity in the pay zone and latent payment in the free zone as two intra-zone prop erties to assist further analysis. The 2ZN model is associated with, but different from, the traditional 2SN model. With the aid of zone, more specific mechanisms can be leveraged to explore matters of importance, ranging from pricing and budgeting to platform design and resource allocation. The three properties help platform managers to recognize competitive dynamics and make better decisions. Using the 2ZN model, other stakeholders can articulate when there exist monopolistic threats or legitimate disputes concerning antitrust debates. In the following two sections, we demonstrate the applicability of the 2ZN model by analyzing four real platforms and discussing how they contrast with each other using the three properties.

## 4. Case studies

## 4.1. Boingo

Boingo Wireless provides mobile internet services and it has over 1 million hotspots worldwide in places like airports, hotels, caf´es, and restaurants. Boingo has acquired several companies in its expansion history. Many customers have used free Boingo Wi-Fi services but are required to watch a short commercial video before doing so. In addition, before one chooses the free Wi-Fi service, Boingo always recommends a free user to sign up for its premium services. There are several choices with varying membership fees. The platform generates revenues from two user groups with two different strategies: advertising fees from advertisers to subsidize free users, and subscription fees from paid users. As depicted in Fig. 3, we apply the proposed 2ZN model to the case of Boingo.

The core platform of Boingo plays a similar role in each zone, as both free users and paid users consume the Wi-Fi service. However, the platform subsidizes free users with revenues generated through charging the advertisers. A positive one-way cross-side network effect exists from the user side to the advertiser side, i.e., the more users there are, the more likely advertisers would run their ads on the platform. Free users also make latent payments with their time watching commercial videos prior to being allowed to access the free Wi-Fi for a limited period. Within each group, there is a negative same-side network effect. The Wi-Fi bandwidth is limited, so a large number of free users can cause a decrease in the speed for each user trying to access the Internet. Fo advertisers, the more companies that run campaigns on the platform, the higher price they likely have to pay for their advertising. In the free zone, users are mainly concerned with the convenience of free Wi-Fi, and they do not have control over the locations in which they can use Boingo’s free access. When a user responds to the commercial video with a click-through, sign-up, or purchase, the platform matches advertisers with potential customers.

In the pay zone, after paying a fee for the subscription to an upgraded service, users are not required to view any commercials. The upgraded plan also involves more locations and longer service time. To make the subscription plan attractive, Boingo has to provide enough hotspots covering sufficiently widespread places, so the pay zone falls into a variety function in terms of locations. Moreover, the services in the pay zone are merely the upgrade of basic free Wi-Fi service with more lo cations, longer use period, and additional devices.

Because of the limited bandwidth, the same-side network effect (SSNE) among free users is negative, as a greater number of users results in slower Wi-Fi access. The SSNE is also negative for advertisers because there exists strong competition for ad campaigns. Users are asked to watch one or two commercials but advertisers prefer a larger viewer base, so a positive cross-side network effect (CSNE) exists. In the pay zone, the network effect within the user group remains the same as in the free zone. There are also positive cross-side and cross-zone network ef fects, as paid customers value the variety of locations where they can access Boingo’s Wi-Fi, and locations also value a platform with more end users (both paid and free) in order to attract more customers into their places. The 2ZN model provides insights into critical issues such as pricing, tipping, and antitrust, among others.

Using the 2ZN model, Boingo managers can get a clearer picture of different interacting groups through different mechanisms between the two zones. In the free zone, they could pay more attention to the interaction between free users and advertisers since this is the major source of revenues for Boingo from the free zone. In the pay zone, the company should pay more attention to the interaction between paid users and locations. Paid users are looking for a variety of locations with Boingo Wi-Fi and the more locations install Boingo Wi-Fi, the higher utility for its paid users.

## 4.2. YouTube

YouTube is an online video-sharing platform that connects users/ viewers, video providers, and advertisers. Since its launch in 2005, the company has been a service provider of free video consumption with revenue generated through advertisement. On October 28, 2015, You Tube launched “YouTube Red” – a premium subscription service that offers ad-free streaming of all videos, access to exclusive content, background offline video playback on mobile devices, and access to the “All Access” service of Google Play Music. The main function of the platform is to provide end users in both zones with a variety of interesting and informative content. ## 4.4. LinkedIn

LinkedIn represents the most complex case of two-zoned network in our analysis. The two zones of LinkedIn are shown in Fig. 6. LinkedIn serves as a social network platform in the free zone, which is similar to Facebook but more professionally oriented. Professionals use LinkedIn to maintain their social network and not necessarily to seek jobs. Moreover, users also post pictures, share news or insights, and learn from other’s experiences on this platform. Therefore, from an end user’s viewpoint, the platform functions of LinkedIn are connectivity and va riety. The social network function of the platform in the free zone is sufficient for end users to network and consume content.

However, the free basic services of the free zone do not have enough functionalities to enable paid users to complete advanced activities (e.g., employment recruitment, business opportunity pursuing, and job search). Paid users on LinkedIn have specific needs that are more diverse in scope. For individual paid users, LinkedIn provides four premium accounts: Job Seekers, Recruiter Lite, Business Plus, and Sales Navigator Professional, which aim at job-seeking users, recruiters, businesses, and sales representatives, respectively. These premium accounts offer addi tional features and functionalities. The monthly fees charged for these premium accounts are substantial (ranging from \$29.99 to \$99.95) and they target users of special needs with advanced communication, connection, and matching capabilities to help perform these tasks. LinkedIn also offers two organization plans to enterprise clients: Hiring Solutions and Marketing Solutions. Organizations use Hiring Solutions to identify potential job candidates using the qualifiers like industry, job function, geography, experience, education, and other filters. There are more than 30 million companies with a presence on LinkedIn and the platform lists more than 20 million open jobs on LinkedIn Jobs.

There is a range of factors to be considered, including user groups and network effects as previ ously discussed. More notably, cross-zone network effects also exist across the two zones of LinkedIn. However, typical monopoly tests such as SSNIP/HM only consider a single service offered within one user group and thus are likely to be insufficient for complicated acquisition cases. The new constructs of the 2ZN model can complement existing monopoly tests for complex platforms like LinkedIn. For instance, one may categorize a mobile app platform that offers music, video, and podcast contents as anticompetitive with the belief that the platform tries to leverage its cross-zone network effects to hinder competition and gain enormous market power. Separating its services into several distinct 2ZNs can help mitigate the risk of antitrust actions.

## 5. Measurement criteria and application of 2ZN properties

We use the three 2ZN properties proposed in Section 3.2 to further explore the four platform cases. First, zone parallelism assesses cross-zone functional similarity. Second, service diversity in the pay zone reflects the variety of paid services in extent and types. Third, latent payment in the free zone indicates whether the free services incur implicit costs for end users in any non-monetary form of payment such as time, information, and other items of value.

## 6. Practical relevance

## 6.1. Strategic implications

The proposed 2ZN model has strategic implications for business operations, especially for the design and development of IT infrastructure. For a 2ZN, dividing it into two zones is crucial since the focal platform performs different functions to serve end users in each zone. Considering the zone dimension reveals several critical underlying issues. To obtain competitive advantages, platform owners need to fully understand their business models and the services they provide. Applying the 2ZN model, the two zones can be analyzed separately and the rules and strategies be developed accordingly. Platform owners must carefully reevaluate several strategic questions including service pric ing, platform openness, the ways to circumvent the “winner-take-all” consequence, and the actions taken to cope with the potential threat of envelopment by adjacent platforms. Nowadays, market competition is much more intense, and an immediate response is needed to cope with market changes. A clear understanding of the constantly evolving structures of network markets and the underlying mechanisms is the first step toward platform success. Our 2ZN model can help a platform business gain a better glimpse of its ecosystem.

Moreover, the two-zoned network model has implications for service computing, which has become the dominant form of distributed computing with “service” representing the most important core component. In this respect, the motivation for proposing a new 2ZN model beyond the traditional 2SN is that the services each zone provides can be composed of a different nature. Since the development of computing infrastructure is now service-oriented, our 2ZN model can guide platform designers to identify requirements and then develop the fitting infrastructure for the two zones accordingly [13]. Considering zone parallelism and service variety, a platform business can decide if a common infrastructure or multiple infrastructures should be developed for the two zones. The 2ZN model also can inform how the platform should allocate its resources between the two zones in order to share costs in developing infrastructures and managing IT expertise.

## 6.2. Legal ramifications

The 2ZN model also can provide insights into the legal domain, especially on the matter of antitrust disputes and adjudications. Market structure is a key point to consider in a typical antitrust deliberation [17,50,51]. The 2ZN model can inform the regulatory bodies by providing a more detailed zone-level analysis of the underlying market structure of platform networks with new features and properties. For example, the tests of SSNIP/HM (i.e., small but significant and nontransitory increase in price and hypothetical monopolist) are the stan dard methods of assessing market power and monopoly [52]. As Hoppner [53] points out, when these tests are applied to anti-trust cases involving two-sided networks, some modifications or re-interpretations are needed since their cross-side network effects, if they exist, are crucial but often overlooked. It is known that the SSNIP/HM tests cannot be directly applied to a two-sided network, especially if one side charges zero price to attract many free users so that a cross-side network effect can be exploited. As Katz and Sallet [54] note, regulators do not really need to develop new antitrust laws for multi-sided platforms; instead, they are advised to reapply existing regulations with new interpretations and insights to evaluate if a platform business has possessed consider able monopoly power that can eliminate competition before they take any countervailing actions. In this regard, our 2ZN model can enhance the power of these monopoly tests by proposing a “zone” dimension beyond the “side” dimension to incorporate more complicated network structures.

Besides, when using any approach for analyzing antitrust, legal practitioners and researchers need to consider the overall profits generated at the platform level, not just at the side level [48]. The capability of a platform owner to exercise monopoly power in one zone may very much hinge on the configuration of the other zone. As an example, when the seldom studied cross-zone network effect exists in a 2ZN, end users in one zone can be regarded as a key deciding factor for the service providers in the other zone. Thus, while the market power may not appear sufficient to demand a monopolistic position in one zone, it could still significantly enhance a platform owner’s ability to raise the price in the other zone if the cross-zone network effect is to be exploited. The proposed 2ZN model provides insights into this type of competition for legitimate determinants. This legal relevance has become increasingly important, as stakeholders are demanding regula tory scrutiny of digital platforms for possible violations of antitrust. For acquisition, the model demonstrates the potential to help inform both regulators and executives alike. Aided by these insights, both defendants and plaintiffs involved in an antitrust case related to platform compe tition are advised to examine distinct user groups and services in each zone for making persuasive arguments.

In a milestone report by the Committee for the Study of Digital Platforms, Morton et al. [55] propose the creation of a new govern mental platform market regulator in the U.S., the so-called ‘Digital Au thority.’ The authors point out that these new markets may result from a “unique combination of economic forces that requires new analysis and new public policy” (page 57). Apart from the platforms themselves, our 2ZN model can also help this new Digital Authority (if created) first assess each player’s position and then monitor their evolvement (e.g., when a platform’s zone parallelism changes from low to high) that can affect the dynamics of “competition for the market.” Morton et al.’s [55] report and our framework also echo the remarks of U.S. Assistant At torney General [56]: “So when we talk about digital platforms providing ‘free’ services, we are really talking about business strategies where zero is the chosen profit maximizing price…As in all enforcement matters, we should engage in careful case-by-case analyses in which we look closely at the evidence. When appropriate we can tailor our approach to consider unique characteristics of a zero-price market.” Our 2ZN model provides a specific way to look into and understand these new zero-price markets. The regulatory need espoused in Morton et al.’s report and the U.S. Assistant Attorney General’s speech provide evidence of the need for new constructs and approaches like our 2ZN model. In other words, Morton et al.’s [55] report and Delrahim’s [56] call for new approaches reinforce the need to study what we refer to as the free zone in our paper.

## 7. Conclusion

The digitization of products and services over the past two decades has dramatically changed the economics of many industries, shifting the source of competitive advantages to the platforms that control the interface. In this study, we develop a new 2ZN model to enhance and complement the existing 2SN model. A new “zone” dimension is intro duced to look beyond the “side” dimension. Within each zone, there is a separate two-sided market. The core platform is both strategically and operationally critical as it connects the two zones and links the two sides of end users in each zone. We propose three properties of 2ZN – zone parallelism, service diversity in the pay zone, and latent payment in the free zone – to shed light on the platform structure. Four examples (i.e., Boingo, YouTube, GitHub and LinkedIn) are analyzed and discussed to showcase the applicability. The four platforms are found to display notable variations along the three dimensions. YouTube, for example, has the highest zone parallelism, while LinkedIn has the lowest zone parallelism. For YouTube and Boingo, free services are not truly free, as they require latent payment from users. As for the services in the pay zone, LinkedIn has the highest diversity and YouTube has the lowest diversity.

Theoretically, our proposed 2ZN model contributes to the extant literature on platform-mediated markets, and the three properties shed new light on the emerging types of platform-mediated networks. This new model also provides illuminating insights into relevant antitrust issues. We emphasize the necessity of considering the “zone” dimension, as the conventional tools (like the SSNIP/HM tests that assess the mo nopoly position of a firm) should be adapted so that they can take into account the additional zone dimension beyond the side dimension. Zone parallelism, in this case, can be particularly beneficial in devising new approaches to complement or enhance these test tools.

Our two-zoned network model carries practical implications. To day’s platform market is unpredictably volatile and intensely competi tive. A single mistake can doom a platform business. Managing twozoned networks is more challenging than managing two-sided net works, as more diverse participants are involved and more complex interactions need to be monitored and scrutinized. Knowing the differ ences between the structure of a platform and those of its competitors is the first step in formulating winning competitive strategies. Zone parallelism can inform if a homogeneous or heterogeneous approach should be adopted for service provisioning in the two zones. Moreover, network effects can be quite distinct in each zone, requiring the platform owner to design different pricing menus, take appropriate steps to address the winner-take-all dynamics, and develop effective safeguards against the potential threat of platform envelopment. Finally, the new model has a two-tiered structure, with the lower level as “side” and the additional upper level as “zone.” In the free zone, the platform owner must decide whether to latently charge users and, if so, how to address the concomitant risk of alienating them. In the pay zone, the platform owner needs to consider the extent and diversity of extra services being offered, and how the decisions on service design, development, and deployment would impact cost and profitability. With additional in sights into the structure and inner working of platform-mediated net works, executives can make better-informed decisions on platform strategy. This paper shows strong theoretical and technical relevance in the support of enhanced decision making.

There are promising potential opportunities for future research. First, using econometric models, researchers can empirically study the related issues to validate the 2ZN model with archival data. Second, incorporating the concept of zone, analytical models can be developed to study the behaviors of various stakeholders involved. This analytical approach can look at network effects, price discrimination, and business conditions for insights into various types of platform-mediated network markets. Third, the 2ZN model can be extended to broader applications beyond the four cases we discussed in the paper to generalize the find ings. Next, the four measurement criteria (1)–(4) proposed in Section 5 for the three properties are practical and yet rudimentary. Future research can extend the 2ZN model through other scientific research methods, such as analytical models, simulation, and empirical validation.

Finally, there could be a third zone or another dimension beyond the “zone” as the underlying technologies for platform-based networks advance. Our study serves only as a starting point to call for future studies to develop more comprehensive and state-of-the-art analytical frameworks to extend the current work. In conclusion, this work rep resents a first but significant step taken toward a better understanding of the structure of two-zoned networks and the associated platform competition.
