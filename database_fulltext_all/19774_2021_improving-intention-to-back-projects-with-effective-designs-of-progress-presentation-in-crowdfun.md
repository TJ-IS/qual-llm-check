## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Crowdfunding Progress cue Motion effect Color Information diagnosticity

## A B S T R A C T

Despite the crucial role a crowdfunding campaign webpage can play in attracting potential backers, there has been a lack of theory-driven studies to investigate how to design effective campaign webpages for successful campaigns. Based on the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) framework, HCI theories, and literature on in formation diagnosticity, this study investigates how the effects of progress presentation designs can improve intention to back a crowdfunding campaign project. A total of 463 subjects participated in two controlled ex periments. MANCOVA and subsequent ANCOVA tests were conducted. We find significant effects of progress cue designs on backers’ perceptions, in turn inducing their intention to back projects.

## 1. Introduction

“Design elements on your digital fundraising page can be the split second difference between someone donating to your campaign or not. While design elements alone won’t do your fundraising for you, these user interface elements will increase your chances of success.” [36].

“Just by showing a progress bar and informing the user that the end is near, people are much more likely to complete a series of actions.” [25].

Crowdfunding has blossomed as a fundraising alternative because securing financing from venture capitalists, angel investors, or other financial institutions can be a great challenge for entrepreneurs to fund new projects. Crowdfunding refers to “informal venture financing allowing entrepreneurs to directly appeal to the general public (i.e., the ‘crowd’) for help in getting their innovative ideas off the ground” [47], p. 2. Crowdfunding platforms such as Kickstarter and Indiegogo allow new ventures to raise hundreds of millions of dollars by launching campaigns on them [24]. As crowdfunding has developed into a viable option for investors and project developers alike, research on this kind of fundraising emerges in different areas including entrepreneurship [58], marketing [84], information systems [83], operations [14] and eco nomics [64]. Unsurprisingly, much of this work focuses on what motivates and deters project investors from participating in crowd funding. For example, Etter et al., [24] find that the trajectory of money pledged and social media communication about the crowdfunding project influence project success. Zhou et al. [83] point out the signifi cant influence of the length, readability, and tone of the content of crowdfunding project descriptions on funding success. Despite previous research efforts, only about 40% of campaigns reach their desired goal amount [53,67,82], suggesting the need for further investigation on how to enhance crowdfunding campaign performance.

Entrepreneurs can easily create a campaign webpage at a crowd funding site, where their projects compete with other ventures to attract backers [74]. A crowdfunding campaign webpage can play a crucial role in sharing/communicating successful information about project prog ress and the quality of the offering with potential investors [68]. There have been a few attempts to investigate the effects of crowdfunding site design components, including images [11,45,52,65,83], textual content [45,56,69,74,78], and videos [7,15,52], showing significant impact on crowdfunding success. However, these past studies only looked at the presence effects of design elements commonly used in commercial crowdfunding sites (e.g., number of images and videos, use of image, depth and appeal of project description) or simply treated overall visual design quality as a single composite factor. Thus, there is still ample room for designing various interface elements and cues at a crowdfunding site and examining each design component’s unique ef fect separately leading to project success. In addition, to avoid the fal lacy where campaign providers design webpages solely based on their intuition and experiences, a theory-based investigation is warranted to explore design components to attract potential backers and persuade them to support the campaign.

Progress cues are used prolifically in crowdfunding campaign sites and show proven effectiveness as one of the most influential design factors in various online contexts through past studies in the HCI field [32,35]. However, to our knowledge, there have been no attempts to examine the effects of progress cues in the context of crowdfunding site design. Thus, we propose and empirically test the influences of design alternatives on crowdfunding backers’ perceptions and behaviors. Spe cifically, by manipulating progress bar design characteristics including progress bar motion, color, and presentation of extra progress infor mation, this study investigates how such cues influence potential backers’ perceptions of positive affect, trust, and information diag nosticity, triggering their intention to back a project. To examine the nomological network, we develop a research model by applying the stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) framework [57] as an overarching theoretical base. We also adopt two HCI theories, motion effect theory and visual search theory, to develop relevant hypotheses. We conjecture that well-designed progress elements on crowdfunding site should not only be necessary, communicative, and attractive but also economical, effective, and powerful solutions for improving campaign success.

For the remainder of the paper, we first review the literature to develop a theoretical model and hypotheses of how progress bar designs can influence perceptions and behaviors of potential crowdfunding backers. The experimental designs are described, results are presented, and findings are deliberated. We then offer theoretical and practical implications. Lastly, we discuss limitations and provide future research suggestions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Stimulus-organism-response (S-O-R) framework

The main theoretical foundation of this study is the S-O-R frame. work, which postulates that environmental cues influence an in dividual’s cognitive and affective reactions, which in turn trigger his/ her behavior [57]. The framework suggests that a stimulus can be conceptualized as an environmental cue that influences our organismic reactions [4]. In an online environment, design components in a website can serve as the stimuli. Organism represents the “internal processes and structures intervening between stimuli external to the person and the final actions, reactions, or responses emitted” [4], p. 46. Online users perceptions toward design cues, such as attitude, positive affect, trust, usefulness, usability, and information diagnosticity, can be examples of an organism. Finally, response is defined as the consumer outcome, including psychological and behavioral reactions. The S-O-R framework has been widely adopted for examining effects of website design alter natives on consumers’ perceptions and purchases (e.g., [17,40,62]). For example, using the S-O-R framework, Peng and Kim [62] examine how perceptions of the online shopping environment and shopping value influence attitudes toward online shopping, emotional purchase, and repurchase intention. In our study, researchers frame perceptions of the online shopping environment and shopping values as the stimuli, atti tudes toward online shopping and emotional purchase as the organism, and repurchase intentions as the response. As shown in Fig. 1, the S-O-R framework provides support for categorizing a crowdfunding campaign environment into three layers of variables - stimuli (progress bars with different motion, color, and extra information), organism (the mediating process or variables that represent perceptions toward a project including positive affect, trust, and information diagnosticity), and response (intention to back a project).

## 2.1.1. Stimuli (progress bar) and organism (perceptions)

This study specially focuses on progress bar designs for several rea sons. First, as a visual cue, various designs of progress bars have been known to influence cognitive and affective appraisals and behaviors [48,50]. For example, Lee et al. [48] find that a moving progress bar is more effective in drawing attention than a static bar while waiting for downloading music. Second, progress bars as visual stimuli [21] tend to convey progress information better and more effectively than textual descriptions. Third, a variety of progress bars have commonly been used in most crowdfunding webpages. Finally, a progress bar is an efficient progress presentation that can be easily designed and implemented without much cost. We specifically select the motion, color and extra progress status information of progress cues as three stimuli and examine their effects on positive affect, trust and information diagnosticity.

2.1.1.1. Motion of a progress bar. Motion effect theory [6], rooted in neurological science and biological mechanisms of information pro cessing, posits that moving objects capture more attentional and emotional resources than static objects [19,26,49]. Moving objects present people with new information, causing greater emotional re sponses. including self-reported arousal (or excitement) [66.70]. In the HCI area, researchers have adopted this theory to assess the effects of moving (or animated) objects on interfaces (e.g., [34]). For instance, Guido et al. [31] find that moving logos are more preferred by mobile device users. Lee et al. [48] find that moving images on web interfaces induce more focused immersion, temporal dissociation and heightened enjoyment than those with static images in an online wait context. Hong et al. [34] find that animation in non-banner-ads tends to attract users attention and animated items are more likely to be purchased. Phillips and Lee [63] investigate the effect of using animated spokes-characters (animated beings or objects used to promote a product or service) and find that animation increases character liking and perceived entertain ment, and fosters positive attitudes toward the website. In this study, we apply motion effect theory to investigate the effect of progress bar mo tion in crowdfunding webpage design. Based on the theory, we expect that a campaign webpage with a moving progress bar induces more positive affect than one with a static progress bar. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1. A crowdfunding campaign webpage with a moving progress bar

2.1.1.2. Color of a progress bar. Color is a ubiquitous part of our lives and can stimulate us in some way every day. In brick-and-mortar retailing, color is considered a powerful tool to effectively communi cate and influence consumption behavior [22,42]. Each color carries a different meaning. Some colors tend to excite people, while others tend to have a relaxing effect. Red color tends to arouse people to perceive excitement and pleasantness (e.g., [5,9]), whereas blue color is regarded as people’s favorite color, inducing high levels of trust, relaxation, and comfort (e.g., [1,51]).

In online retailing, designers use color to influence online con sumers’ perceptions toward webpages and products or services pre sented on them (e.g., [16,30]). In the context of online processing, Gorn et al. [30] find that a red webpage background color tends to arouse and excite people, while Dijkstra et al. [20] find that the blue color increases website trust. Compared to the color blue, online ads with a red color scheme elicit higher click-through rates, likely due to increased emotional arousal [71]. Cyr et al. [16] find color can influence online shoppers’ trust or satisfaction toward websites and enhance their eloyalty. Considering that blue and red are two of the most frequently adopted colors in e-business websites, this study chose them as progress bar colors and examined their effect on positive affect and trust. Based on the findings of previous color studies, we expect that people seeing a red progress bar on a crowdfunding campaign webpage will experience more excitement while those seeing a blue progress bar will perceive higher trust. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2. A crowdfunding campaign webpage with a blue progress bar will induce less positive affect (H2a) and more trust (H2b) than one with a red progress bar.

In addition, we can expect a synergy effect between motion and color to induce positive affect. Motion effect theory and color theory posit that moving objects and red color are effective in boosting fun, excitement, and enjoyment. Thus, we hypothesize that a moving red-colored prog ress bar can be more effective in producing positive affect than a static blue-colored one.

H3. The effect of a moving progress bar on positive affect is greater for a red-colored one than for a blue-colored one.

2.1.1.3. Extra information of a progress bar. Visual search theory [79] posits that people tend to allocate different proportions of their attention to process information in their visual field depending not only on the salience of the cues presented to them but most importantly on their perceived relevance to a user’s goal and task [3,28]. In the HCI area, studies focus on designing task-relevant visual cues on web interfaces to draw users’ attention to more valuable information and help their decision-making. Lee et al. [48] find that relevant designs of text and image elements on web interfaces can induce more focused immersion, temporal dissociation and heightened enjoyment.

Studies find that consumers show positive attitudes toward websites with sufficient amounts of valuable information, triggering increased purchases of products or services. Filieri et al. [27] find that adding online reviews to a website leads to increased trust. Similarly, other studies find that people perceive more trust and information diagnosticity at a website that includes components rendering extra valuable information such as recommendation agents [76], videos (as opposed to static pictures), tangible product presentation formats [73], and virtual product experi ences [39]. In this study, we expect that project backers want to evaluate the goal of a campaign, the status of funding progress, and the likelihood of success from its campaign webpage, and if they observe such progress information on the webpage, they could develop positive attitudes toward the project and project owners, and develop stronger intentions to invest. For example, Lu et al. [54] find that people perceive high information diagnosticity if consumers believe website information helps them make purchase decisions. Specifically we predict people perceive extra infor mation accompanying a progress bar (e.g., % goal completion, amount pledged, # of backers, days remaining) as helpful and useful (i.e., diag nostic) in determining the potential success of a crowdfunding project, and express more trust in the project. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4. A crowdfunding campaign webpage with extra campaign progress information will elicit more trust (H4a) and lead to stronger perceptions of information diagnosticity (H4b) than a campaign webpage without extra campaign progress information.

Information diagnosticity can be described as individuals’ percep tions of relevant information that can assist them in understanding and evaluating a target (e.g., [38]). In general, quantity and quality of informational cues and the helpfulness of the information can contribute to its diagnosticity [2]. Therefore, extra information (i.e., a higher quantity of information cues) provided on a crowdfunding campaign webpage with trust-inducing design cues should lead to greater infor mation diagnosticity. Information presented with blue color tends to be perceived as more reliable and trustworthy. Thus, we conjecture that there is a synergy effect of these two design cues on information diagnosticity.

H5. The effect of a progress bar on information diagnosticity is greater for a blue-colored one than for a red-colored one.

## 2.1.2. Moderators (project type) between stimuli (progress bars) and organism (perceptions)

One way to categorize crowdfunding campaigns is based on whether a project’s products or services are primarily instrumental and functional or provide more experiential consumption, fun, pleasure, and excite ment. The former is called a utilitarian project while the latter is called a hedonic one. For example, a project that introduces a professional dive watch can be considered a utilitarian project, while one that asks for funding an app-controlled paper airplane a hedonic one. Meanwhile, a web page can be designed using utilitarian and hedonic design compo nents [48], and previous studies find that consumers engage with and react differently to those design components (e.g., [13,18]). Cober et al. [13] find high-affect design components are likely to provide fun, excitement, and enjoyment. The effect of those design components be comes greater when it matches project type (hedonic vs utilitarian), because of a cognitive fit between them (e.g., hedonic design components for a hedonic project and utilitarian design components for a utilitarian project) [77]. In this study, we focus on the interaction between progress bar color as a design component and project type.

As indicated earlier, color researchers find that red color is associated with fun and excitement, and thus people perceive more excitement, fun, and enjoyment when they see red objects than other-colored objects. Similarly we expect that potential backers perceive greater fun and enjoyment when they see a high-affect progress bar (e.g., red one) in a hedonic project campaign webpage than when they see a high-affect progress bar in a utilitarian project campaign webpage or a low-affect progress bar (i.e., blue one) in a hedonic project webpage. Thus, we hypothesize:

H6. Progress bar color and project type interact such that people viewing a crowdfunding campaign webpage with a red progress bar will perceive greater difference in positive affect between utilitarian and hedonic projects than those viewing a blue progress bar.

## 2.2. Organism (perceptions) and response (intention)

In this study, we use intention to back a project as a response variable that has been used as a surrogate measure of actual sponsorship of a project in previous studies (e.g., [8,72]). Positive affect refers to a “pleasant feeling, state or good mood,” [23], p. 286, and influences our thoughts, behaviors and decision making. Researchers find that positive affect enhances expectations about the likely outcome of anticipated events or activities and promotes engagement in things of interest [41,55]. Positive affect is known to significantly affect various online behaviors. For example, Yen and Wu [80] indicate that positive affect successfully predicts the adoption of mobile financial services. Karson and Fisher [43] find a significant relationship between positive attitude toward the site and intention to return to the site. Further Konietzny et al., [46] find that positive affect increases online users’ intention to engage in online gambling. In our study, we expect that potential backers who experience positive affect at a crowdfunding webpage will positively evaluate the likelihood of project success and thus react to an important crowdfunding call-to-action: backing intention. Thus, we hypothesize:

H7. Perceived positive affect generated from a crowdfunding project webpage will be positively related to intention to back the project.

Trust is “a consumer’s subjective belief that the selling party or entity will fulfill its transactional obligations as the consumer understands them and has been known to significantly influence online user behav iors” [44]. Due to the nature of Internet shopping, online users may experience some risk. Trust becomes a key way of dealing with these somewhat risky situations [29]. Yoon [81] finds that website trust positively influences purchase intention when there are security and privacy concerns. Kim et al. [44] also identify a positive relationship between trust and intention to buy in a business-to-consumer web environment. Chandra et al. [12] reveal that individuals’ trust in a virtual world is positively related to their intention to use it for work place collaboration. Given uncertainty for crowdfunding project success, if potential backers perceive trust in a crowdfunding project webpage where campaign progress information informs their investment de cisions or design components make them feel calm and relaxed, they are more willing to support the project. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H8. Perceived trust generated from a crowdfunding project webpage will be positively related to intention to back the project.

Website information diagnosticity is “the extent to which consumers believe the information of a website is helpful for them to understand products” [39], p. 475. Prior research finds that useful website infor mation helps online users’ decision making. Pavlou et al. [61] find that detailed products or service-related information in a website mitigates perceived uncertainty in online shopping, which leads to increased purchase intentions. Jeong and Lambert [37] identify perceived use fulness of information at lodging web sites as a significant predictor of willingness to book reservations at these sites. In the context of crowd funding projects, the quality of information describing a project be comes crucial when potential backers evaluate its merits, and perceptions of higher quality projects positively influence investment decisions [8]. Therefore, we expect that perceived usefulness of project status information rendered through a crowdfunding progress bar itself as well as text attached to it will increase the likelihood of a person backing the project. Thus, we hypothesize:

H9. Perceived usefulness of information provided in a crowdfunding project webpage will be positively related to intention to back the project.

In sum, in addition to referring to the S-O-R framework as our overarching theoretical base, motion effect theory (progress bar motion) and visual search theory (extra information) are two HCI theories we adopted for developing relevant hypotheses.

## 3. Research methodology and results

We conducted two controlled experiments to (1) investigate effects of progress bar design cues on positive affect, trust, and information diagnosticity across different types of crowdfunding projects, and (2) examine the nomological networks between positive affect, trust, and information diagnosticity and intention to back a project. The purpose of Study 1 is to establish how progress bar color and animation influence positive affect and trust toward a crowdfunding project. In addition, we examine how project type, hedonic or utilitarian, moderates the effect of progress bar color on positive affect. After confirming these effects, Study 2 is conducted to determine how extra progress information and color influence trust and information diagnosticity. Fig. 2 depicts the research model guiding our research hypotheses.

## 3.1. Design

Study 1 and Study 2 are designed to examine the effects of progress bar design cues – Study 1: motion (static vs. animated) x color (blue vs. red) and Study 2: information-level (high vs. low-level extra informa tion) x color (blue vs. red) – on positive affect, trust, and information diagnosticity, and to test the nomological networks between those three constructs and intention to back a project. Data were analyzed using MANCOVA and subsequent ANCOVA tests and regression analyses. Age and crowdfunding project familiarity<sup>1</sup> were used as control variables. To test our research model and hypotheses, we developed a simulated, online crowdfunding webpage by mimicking popular crowdfunding sites such as Kickstarter and GoFundMe.

Then 263 subject signed up for Study 2 one month after Study 1 was completed. Subjects were randomly assigned to one treatment condition to investigate the effects of different progress bar design cues. After removing no shows, incomplete, and wrong responses,<sup>2</sup> 228 subjects for Study 1 (90.1% response rate) and 235 for Study 2 (89% response rate) were included. ## 3.2. Methods and results

3.2.1. Study 1: impact of motion, color, and project type on positive affect and trust

3.2.1.1. Study 1: method. We designed eight different crowdfunding webpages, randomly assigned subjects to one of the conditions of a two (progress bar motion: static vs. animated) x two (color: blue vs. red) x two (project type: utilitarian vs. hedonic) between-subjects design, and examined how the manipulations influence trust and positive affect while visiting the site (see Table 2)

The independent variables were motion (static vs. animated) and color (blue vs. red). For the animated condition, we used a moving ripple effect in a rectangle accumulated progress bar while there was no movement in the static accumulated progress bar. For color, two of the most frequently used colors for progress bars in commercial websites, blue and red, were selected.

Three dependent variables were measured: positive affect (five items; e.g., The crowdfunding campaign page is interesting/enjoyable), trust (three items; e.g., The crowdfunding campaign page was trust worthy), and intention to back a project (three items; e.g., It is likely that I would be a backer of this crowdfunding campaign). They were adopted from previous studies and edited to fit the crowdfunding context. An exploratory factor analysis was conducted, and confirmed that the items grouped into three factors (minimum Eigenvalue = 1.226 > 1, cumu lative percent of variance explained is 76.9%, all factor loadings >0.734, all cross-loadings <0.224, and Cronbach’s Alpha >0.881), and that convergent and discriminant validity, and reliability was high (see Ta bles 3 and 4).

3.2.1.2. Study 1: results. We conducted a manipulation check to examine whether subjects correctly recognized the progress bars with animation and color in a webpage. We asked subjects two questions (i.e., What kind of progress bar (static, animated, none) and what color of progress bar (red, blue, yellow) did you see at the campaign page?”). We found that 95.4% of the subjects correctly identified the progress bar type and color. Thus, we concluded the manipulation was successful.

We then analyzed the effect of animation and color of a progress bar on trust and positive affect. 3.2.1.3. Study 1: discussion. As expected, an animated progress bar leads to more positive affect than a static bar, and a blue progress bar leads to more trust but less positive affect than a red bar. This is in line with motion effect theory, which predicts more fun and excitement when a moving object is present, and color theory, which expects that the color red is associated with excitement while the color blue is associated with trust. However, the interaction between motion and color on positive affect was not significant, although we see that a red animated progress cue $( \mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { R e d A n i m a t e d } } = 4 . 7 6 0 )$ induces more affect than a blue static progress cue $( \mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { B l u e S t a t i c } } = 3 . 7 2 7 )$ . One possible interpretation for the insignificant finding is that a more interestinglyshaped progress bar with more novel or active animation than a plain rectangular progress bar with typical animation is likely to draw more attention and increase the level of fun in participants. On the other hand, our result indicates that a red progress bar leads to greater differences in positive affect between utilitarian and hedonic projects than a blue bar. This suggests that potential backers are more responsive to the color red when it is paired with a hedonic project, which, like the color red, is likely to create excitement. Lastly, progress bars that increase positive affect and trust make potential backers more inclined to back a project.

3.2.2. Study 2: impact of level of extra information and color on trust and information Diagnosticity

3.2.2.1. Study 2: method. After confirming the salient effects of two design components $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ motion and color) of a progress bar on positive affect and trust in Study 1, we conducted Study 2 to further investigate the effect of extra information of a progress bar (high vs. low) and color on trust and information diagnosticity, which in turn induces intention to back a project. A total of 235 subjects participated, as shown in Table 7, and were recruited using the same method as Study 1. The experiment was conducted using the same simulated crowdfunding site and following the same procedure of Study 1, but with crowdfunding webpages in which we manipulated two levels of extra information of a progress bar (high vs. low) and color (blue vs. red) across projects (utilitarian vs. hedonic) (see Table 7). Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the conditions of a two (extra information level: high vs. low) x two (color: blue vs. red) x two (project type: utilitarian vs. hedonic) between-subjects design.

For extra information, we used a percent funded (i.e., 45%) message on top of a rectangular cumulative progress bar for the low extra in formation condition, while additional numeric and text information, including percent funded, money pledged, goal amount, number of backers, and days to go, was used for the high extra information con dition. We chose five types of extra information most commonly used in top-ranked crowdfunding sites. We also selected blue and red color not only because they are often used in crowdfunding sites but also their significant effects on positive affect and trust were confirmed in Study 1. Study 2’s dependent variables are positive affect and trust as well as information diagnosticity. Two information diagnosticity items, “The information provided helped me get a better understanding for this crowdfunding campaign,” and “Information provided helped me carefully evaluate the crowdfunding campaign,” were adopted from Pavlou and Fygenson [60]. An exploratory factor analysis (see Table 8) confirmed high convergent and discriminant validity and reliability (min eigenvalue of five factors is 1.015 > 1, cumulative percent of variance explained is 82.6%, all factor loading scores >0.845, all crossloadings <0.244, and min Cronbach’s Alpha >0.826) (See Tables 8 and 9.).

## 4. Discussion

Although crowdfunding sites continue to grow as a viable alternative to entrepreneurs securing funding [24], research on how potential backers react to crowdfunding webpage design elements is limited. The current study aims to contribute to existing research on effective crowdfunding webpage design, especially focusing on the design of a progress bar. We looked at how animation, color and extra information of a progress bar influence potential backers’ positive affect, trust, in formation diagnosticity, and, in turn, intention to back a crowdfunding project. In addition, we considered the moderating effect of project type. These hypotheses were tested in two controlled experiments. We found when people are considering whether or not to back a crowdfunding venture, progress bar design characteristics can influence that decision. In summary, the findings of our experimental studies validated the proposed model and explained substantial variance in willingness to back a project.

Three main conclusions regarding the effects of progress bars can be drawn. First, we find that a moving rather than a static progress bar influences potential investors’ positive affect. This confirms that when a clearly recognizable, highly salient progress bar with motion is pre sented on a crowdfunding webpage, people perceive more fun and enjoyment while seeing it. This is consistent with findings of previous studies (e.g., [75]) using motion effect theory that predict individuals orientation to a moving object on a webpage. In addition, a blue progress bar was found to elicit greater trust toward the crowdfunding project, similar to how it increases trust in websites where the color blue pre dominates [22], while red progress bars generate more excitement. Similar effects have been found in previous studies (e.g., [30,71]). In addition, the significant interaction effect between color and project type suggests that the type of product or service featured in crowd funding projects activates different amounts of enjoyment and fun for differently colored progress bars shown in those projects. That $\mathbf { i } s ,$ the finding that a red progress bar in a utilitarian project could lead to more positive attitudes toward a project than a blue one used in a hedonic project provides us with useful information about the careful choice of color across different types of crowdfunding projects to maximize the synergy effect between them.

Second, we find people trust and appreciate extra information that appears around a progress bar. This is consistent with findings of pre vious studies noting the positive influence of extra diagnostic informa tion on online users’ attitude and perceptions [27,54]. The diagnostic information around a progress bar builds trust in and develops stronger positive attitudes toward crowdfunding sponsors.

Finally, this study finds that three organismic variables (positive affect, trust, and information diagnosticity) significantly induce inten tion to back a project. That is, we empirically validate the salience of the nomological networks, which address how to (a) elicit potential backers enjoyment, (b) enhance their trust, and (c) increase their perceived in formation diagnosticity while visiting a crowdfunding site, confirming the significant influence of a progress bar in a crowdfunding webpage on potential backers’ perceptions toward the project and willingness to back a project. Of particular note, trust has the strongest effect on intention to back a project, confirming the importance of combining trust-inducing progress bar design features. Given that various types of trust-invoking design components (e.g. trustful human faces, color, and information sources and up-to-date information) are utilized in crowd funding campaign webpages, future studies examining the relative impact of different types of trust-generating design cues are recommended.

## 5. Theoretical and practical implications

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

First, this study contributes to expand the theoretical boundary of the S-O-R framework by empirically confirming its validity in the context of effective crowdfunding site design. The S-O-R framework has been widely used in examining online user behaviors. This study enriches and validates the S-O-R framework by investigating factors influencing users’ intentions in the context of crowdfunding campaign projects. In other words, using S-O-R framework as an overarching theoretical base, our findings contribute to the literature on exploring the antecedents, mediating effects, and outcomes of potential investors’ intention to back crowdfunding campaigns. We therefore conclude and provide reliable confirmation that S-O-R is a robust framework that can explain user behaviors in the context of crowdfunding campaigns. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first scientific study to apply the framework in a crowdfunding context.

Second, despite a great deal of attention by researchers and practi tioners on investigating facilitators and inhibitors for successful crowdfunding and despite the pervasiveness of crowdfunding platforms to enable campaigns, there is a lack of HCI studies on effective crowd funding site design. By proposing and testing design alternatives of progress bars in a website based on motion effect theory and color studies in the online crowdfunding context, we further extend these theoretical applications. With this, our study provides a strong founda tion for HCI researchers to further investigate a better way of imple menting interface components for designing an effective crowdfunding site that increases the likelihood to back crowdfunding projects.

Third, this study reconfirms the significant influence of color on online users’ perceptions. In a recent review of color and psychological functioning, Elliot and Maier [22] note that existing theoretical and empirical work on the topic remains in an early stage of development. In particular, while several studies have explored color effects in adver tising, the majority of this work lacks a theoretical foundation to account for the observed phenomena. Many disciplines, such as neuroscience, interactive media, and marketing have used new technologies (e.g., facial expression analysis, fMRI, eye tracking) to better understand the complexities of color perception, yet relatively few research efforts in the information systems field have been reported. By empirically vali dating the critical role of color in online users’ perceptions in a crowd funding site design context, this study contributes by fueling future investigation of color in various online contexts using advanced technologies.

Fourth, this study contributes to the study of information diag nosticity. While the significant influence of information diagnosticity has been reported in various web environments (e.g., online reviews [27] and product information at websites [54]), there have been few studies regarding how information and website design components interact to influence individuals’ perceived positive affect, trust, and information diagnosticity. By confirming its significant main and interaction effect, this study paves the way to further explore the impact of information (e.g., continuous update of project status for better communication, consistent communication of relevant content for un derlying project) on potential investors’ perceptions and behaviors.

Finally, from a methodological perspective, we found that most previous studies on crowdfunding site design relied on secondary data analysis or content analysis. In this study, we conduct controlled ex periments by carefully designing simulated crowdfunding project pages and progress cue components and analyzing the unique variance explained by each design component. Given the significant results of various progress cue design alternatives, this study paves the way for researchers to utilize a controlled experiment as a research method to further investigate different type of crowdfunding site design cues.

## 5.2. Practical implication

The study also holds important implications for entrepreneurs and digital marketers. Crowdfunding sites make it easy for potential backers to quickly evaluate a wide range of projects. Entrepreneurs who un derstand how design elements like progress bars can influence consumer engagement have a competitive advantage. The design of progress bars can be tailored in ways that will be more persuasive than a one-size-fitsall approach. Similarly, marketers should pay attention to the ways in which the effects of progress bar design cues can vary depending on the type of product or service being sold. In addition, more information surrounding a progress bar may be more appreciated by potential backers seeing a campaign webpage with utilitarian products than he donic ones.

Specifically, while our study confirms that motion progress bars tend to induce positive affect for users visiting a crowdfunding site, more vivid animated progress cues are also recommended as they are more likely to be noticed, paid attention to, and liked than static bars, which are still predominantly used in crowdfunding campaign webpages. In addition, adding visual cues and information to enhance users’ trust toward a crowdfunding site can also increase their willingness to back a project. Lastly, the influence of extra information calls out the impor tance of content strategists in the design of websites and apps, especially those that are part of crowdfunding platforms. Although further inves tigation is warranted, our findings provide content strategists with evi dence regarding the significant effect of information on crowdfunding success that may persuade funding campaign managers to invest more resources to improve the quality of information.

## 6. Limitations and future research

As with any research, this study has limitations. First, this study’s external validity is limited in regard to participants and the experi mental website. We recruited B-school students from one large U.S. university. While student subjects can successfully represent the target population of our study (i.e., people who are familiar with crowdfunding and have experience backing projects), future research would benefit by incorporating more traditional adult samples with having experiences in backing crowdfunding projects to determine whether our findings can extend beyond college students. This would also give researchers the opportunity to examine other demographic factors, like gender and socio-economic status for testing the generalizability of our findings. The experimental campaign webpage is also limited in precisely repre senting a real-world crowdfunding site. Although we were able to carefully control manipulation of progress bar design elements, experi mental settings may discourage realistic evaluation of stimuli compared with more natural settings [59]. Thus, future researchers might consider A/B manipulation and testing of design elements in field experiments where attitudes and behaviors can be captured in a more realistic setting.

Second, since it is an exploratory study, we manipulated only prog ress bar motion, color, and extra information. There are other progress bar components such as size, type, shape, and location. We suggest future work incorporate such design components to further validate our findings. In addition, we did not examine how people react differently to the five types of extra information we introduced. We recommend a future study looking at the differential impact of types of extra infor mation on potential backers’ perceptions. Third, while this study finds significant difference between moving and static progress bar with respect to inducing perceived affect, further design efforts are warranted for evaluating the motion effect, for instance by adding more visually interesting animation effects (e.g., float-in, fade-out) to the progress status information attached to a moving progress bar. Fourth, this study considers how one product typology, hedonic or utilitarian, interacts with progress bar design elements. While this typology is commonly used by researchers, there are other typologies (e.g., six drivers of con sumer benefit [10]) that might provide additional insights into how these design elements persuade. Finally, although we successfully tested and validated the significant effects of progress cues in controlled experiment, it is recommended to future studies to test the generaliz ability of our findings using mixed methodologies such as neuroscience techniques including eye tracking and electroencephalogram (EEG).
