## A B S T R A C T

Most bankruptcy prediction models used by financial institutions rely on single-period data, that is to say data that characterize firms at a given moment of their life. However, the financial literature devoted to bankruptcy considers this phenomenon to be a protracted process and time to be a fundamental explanatory variable of firm failure. The few studies that attempted to incorporate a temporal dimension into a forecasting model using multi period data often vielded results that did not really improve model accuracy. One may then suppose that the principles that ground the historical dimension of bankruptcy are less in question to explain the poor difference between the results estimated with these two types of data than the way time is modeled into prediction rules. This is why we propose a method that relies on a particular modeling of firm history using self-organizing neural networks and a segmentation of the data space, and which makes it possible to typify subsets of firms that share a common evolution of their financial situation over time. This method leads to models that are substantially more accurate than traditional ones, especially when it comes to forecasting the fate of firms the cost of misclassifi cation of which is the highest for any financial institution.

## 1. Introduction

Most scoring models analyzed in the literature, and that are used to estimate a probability of bankruptcy, rely on financial variables that are often measured over a single year and, more rarely, over several years. Although it has long been known that an analysis which relies on trend data is often more revealing of bankruptcy risk than the same analysis that relies solely on data estimated at a given moment in time [4,17], no one has really succeeded so far in building models using historical data that are more accurate than those that rely on single-period data. One may then conclude that important parameters that explain corporate failure, and that are time-dependent, fail to be embodied properly by data that are supposed to capture firm history. In fact, little is known about how a temporal dimension could be implemented into a model that would make it possible to achieve reliable and robust forecasts. Indeed, many models that rely on historical data do not necessarily lead to better predictions than those that rely on single-period data [7,15,44]. While the latter are grounded in a flawed conceptual basis of bankruptcy. they very often achieve similar results as those calculated with the former. Such situation is a constant throughout the literature. So there appears to be a contradiction: bankruptcy is a process and time is a fundamental explanatory variable, but the measures of this process do not make it possible to account for it properly. However, this contradiction is ultimately only apparent. The idea that bankruptcy is a process seems to be less in question to explain this fact than the way this process has been modeled so far.

Firm history has long mainly been taken into account using data that were almost always the same: they correspond either to financial in dicators estimated over several periods [2,20], or simple variations over time of the latter indicators [7], or sometimes to complex, synthetic measures [48]. Nevertheless, even if these data offer a partial solution to represent the main processes that explain how the financial situation of firms may change over time, they still come up against the impossibility of differentiating those that can lead firms to very different fates. For instance, a symptom of good health, such as a high level of cash, may sometimes reveal something completely different. A high level of liquidity can be the consequence of the fact that a firm is desperately trying to delay the onset of bankruptcy, urging its clients to pay their debts while delaying payments to its suppliers, but ultimately without success. What is true of liquidity is also true of the other dimensions that explain a firm’s failure, such as profitability or solvency.

In order to overcome this problem, some algorithmic solutions have been studied which complement the use of historical data, but they are quite rare. One may find some studies where this type of data is used in conjunction with ensemble techniques [19,24]. These techniques make it possible to take into account subtle variations that may exist between firms sharing the same profile and thus provide the beginning of a so lution. One may also find other studies where historical data are used with segmentation techniques. In such situations, the initial variable space is first divided into subsets, and then each subset is used to design a particular model [23]. This time, the division of the data space makes it possible to model some variations that single models cannot represent.

Four conclusions can be drawn from these research works. First, the sole use of historical data, and especially measures of variations, does not necessarily lead to more accurate results than those achieved with single-period data, because traditional modeling methods fail to take advantage of the informational content of such data. Second, there does not exist comparative studies showing to what extent ensemble-based models used in conjunction with data incorporating or not a temporal dimension may improve forecasts. Third, ensemble-based models esti mated using a prior segmentation of data can sometimes improve shortterm forecasts, but no one knows whether these two techniques used in conjunction with historical data achieve better results than without. Fourth, no one knows either to what extent the use of historical data with ensemble-based models and a prior segmentation of data may be of interest.

These are the reasons why we propose a method that is structured around three components. It relies on an ensemble of self-organizing feature maps [28] estimated with a set of variables measured over three consecutive years, and a division of the data space that makes it possible to typify the evolution of firm financial health using six finan cial dimensions that explain bankruptcy. On the one hand, the self organizing feature maps are used to embody typical financial situa tions that characterize both firms that will go bankrupt and those that will survive. On the other hand, the segmentation of the data space prior to the modeling process is intended to enhance the ability of each model to capture subtle variations that may exist between failed and non-failed firms.

The current study has three objectives. First, we propose a new class of ensemble techniques that relies on a fine-tuned modeling of the processes that govern the evolution of firm financial situation over time. Second, we analyze an issue that has not yet been addressed and that deals with the contribution of historical data and data segmentation to the accuracy of ensemble-based models. Third, we reinforce the existing literature which shows that model accuracy can be improved not only through algorithmic sophistication but also by the use of techniques that are likely to account for the different processes that lead firms to failure and where bankruptcy is no longer considered a sudden event but a dynamic process [16,30,37,48].

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a literature review that explains the issue addressed in this article. Sections 3 and 4 present the data and the different methods that were used for our ex perimentations. Section 5 explains the experimental settings. Section 6 presents and comments the results, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Literature review

Researchers interested in bankruptcy prediction very quickly intuited that models should incorporate a temporal dimension and that this was likely to improve results. Edmister, in 1972, barely a few years after Altman’s seminal work in 1968, suggested that measures of vari ations of certain ratios could represent good predictors of failure. Conceptually, he was right. Indeed, bankruptcy is the result of a dy namic process that can be long [30] and temporal measures attempting to embody the evolution of a firm’s financial health could be likely to capture some phenomena that snapshot measures, which characterize firms at a single moment in their history, could not. Empirically, Edmister was also right. Many research works that followed his own work relied on indicators estimated over time, showing that their indi vidual discrimination power was real. These works used variables that represent simple variations of ratios or financial variables [32] over several years. They also used statistical measures calculated with his torical data such as standard errors of estimate [2], standard deviations [7], coefficients of variation [15], etc. However, when one looks closely at the rare studies that have compared the performance of models designed with such measures with those of models estimated with single-period data, one observes that, sometimes, there is absolutely no difference between the results [44] and, sometimes, the results are very close to each other [7,15]. In fact, these models rely on data that are not very robust [4]; their individual discrimination ability is often con junctural and does not remain stable over time. One may then conclude that in such a case, the principles that ground the interest of historical data are less in question than the way models manage to capture their informational content.

A first response to this drawback was provided by ensemble tech niques, even if these techniques were not explicitly designed to address this issue. An ensemble makes it possible to estimate variations around a state of bankruptcy and is likely to embody certain subtleties that single models cannot, provided the different components of an ensemble are sufficiently diverse and their number sufficiently large. Nevertheless, here as well the results are not always convincing, because ensemblebased models often yield forecasts that are only slightly more accurate than those achieved with single models [1,19,45], both using historical or single period data. In fact, ensembles present, to a certain extent, the same weakness as that of traditional classification rules. Within an ensemble, each rule represents a global boundary between two groups, whereas one might think that a rule should rather embody a local boundary, if one expected to capture subtle variations that may affect the evolution of firms’ financial health.

A second response was also provided by the use of a segmentation process applied to data prior to the estimation of a model. Data are first divided into groups using a clustering technique and then each group is used to estimate a given model [23]. To forecast a company’s fate, the company is first assigned to a particular group and then the forecast is made using the model specially designed for that group. This segmen tation therefore represents a good means to model some specific aspects of firms’ financial situation that traditional models fail to embody. However, it still presents at least two drawbacks. First, it must rely on a small number of groups, since too many groups would lead to fewer firms belonging to each group and would then make the estimations not very robust. Second, here as well, the results are mitigated [8] since segmenting the data space does not always improve forecasts. This suggests that trying to fit models to different subgroups of firms may sometimes be of real interest, but the way the fit is performed presum ably requires some tuning to become truly efficient. This is precisely why we propose a method that attempts to take advantage of the different factors presented above while trying to circumvent their major drawbacks. It does not directly rely on the estimation of boundaries between firms but on that of prototypes typifying subsets of firms, and ensembles of prototypes that are estimated using self-organizing feature maps. These prototypes represent to a certain extent a series of local boundaries that make it possible to cluster subsets of firms sharing a common history and at the same time experiencing different fates. More specifically, they make it possible to model small groups of firms that present rare or atypical financial profiles, as well as large groups that correspond to very general and common profiles.

The method we present in this article relies on historical data and on a particular segmentation of the data space. We use financial ratios measured over several years instead of measures of variations since such indicators are not that robust. The segmentation process is not per formed to calculate and apply models that fit a particular group of firms, but rather used to estimate a small number of groups of firms that share a common evolution of their financial profile, where each group is used to calculate an ensemble of models and where the size of each ensemble reflects the size of the group from which it stems, within the sample it belongs to. ## 3. Samples and variables

## 3.1. Samples

Samples were extracted from the Diane database managed by Bureau van Dijk, which provides financial data for over 1.4 million firms that filed their annual accounts with French commercial courts. Table 1 presents the main characteristics of firms that can be found in this database and that are rather close to those of the entire population of French firms. Data used to estimate and test the different models were calculated using balance sheets and income statements, as is tradition ally done.

We collected data over a long period, from 2000 to 2016, in order to control for the influence of the economic environment on model accu racy [25]. These data were divided into 8 samples that corresponded to 4 learning samples and 4 test samples. Each sample was made up of data that characterized firms that either went bankrupt or managed to sur vive over a period of three consecutive years prior to the year when their status was assessed. Thus, for a given sample, the status was estimated during year t, and the data characterizing companies were collected during the years t− 1, t− 2 and t− 3. We chose a three-year period because it has long been known, and at least since Beaver [6], that the discrep ancy between the financial situation of failed and non-failed firms be gins to widen significantly three years prior to the bankruptcy of some of them. This period, though short, is therefore sufficient to model a period in the firms’ recent history that largely explains their future, while making it possible not to exclude from the samples all young firms. Test samples were chosen in order to estimate an out-of-sample and out-oftime error; they were all made up of firms that did not belong to their corresponding learning sample, and these two samples were collected with a one-year lag. Within each sample, all companies that met these requirements were selected at random.

The characteristics of the samples are presented in Table 2. This table shows, for each period over which a pair of learning and test samples was collected, the economic cycle that occurred each year, the years where data were collected to estimate model parameters and test their performance, and the number of firms that were chosen. This table also shows that the different samples are made up of as many failed firms as non-failed ones, though the ratio between these two figures, when one looks closely at the real bankruptcy rate in any developed country, is in general around 2%. This raises the question as to what extent a sample that does not reflect the true proportion of these two groups of firms within the population from which they have been extracted may lead to a statistical bias. Zmijewski [54], Wilson and Sharda [52] and Platt and Platt [43] demonstrated that such a situation may lead to a choice-based sample bias, even if this bias is more or less pronounced depending on the modeling method [42]. Indeed, when the proportion of firms that will go bankrupt increases in a learning sample, the rate of correct classification of these firms increases at the same time whereas the overall correct rate does not change. If the minority class is overrepresented. models will tend to better forecast the fate of the members of this group than if it is not. But is this a true drawback? We know at least two things about this issue. First, a model that tends to correctly predict the fate of firms which will go bankrupt is a rather good model. As the costs of misclassification are strongly asymmetric, it is preferable that a model should be likely to better predict the fate of failed firms than that of the others. when the overall correct classification rate re: mains constant [13]. Second, attempting to balance the two groups of firms within a sample leads to putting aside a portion of data that could be useful to a proper estimation of model parameters, because it is easier to collect data that characterize non-failed firms than data that char acterize failed firms. This is why, for example, Banque de France, the French central bank, uses samples that are made up of 1/3 failed firms and 2/3 non-failed ones [3,5]. With this example, one observes that the 1/3 proportion of failed firms is quite different from the true proportion of 1/50, because banks have a propensity to favor misclassification costs over other parameters when it comes to estimating their own forecasting models. To choose the proportion of failed firms used to design our models, we ran a set of experiments using different proportions. Since there was very little difference between the results achieved with a proportion of 1/3 and that of 1/2, we finally selected 1/2 because a high proportion of failed firms was likely to simplify the segmentation pro cess of learning data. In contrast, we used test samples where the pro portion of bankrupt firms was the same as that in the French population of firms. To estimate model performance, we drew at random, from each test sample, 100 bootstrap samples made up of 2% of failed firms. Each bootstrap sample consisted of 29,400 non-failed companies and 600 failed ones, and results presented in this paper were averaged over all bootstrap samples.

## 3.2. Variables

We estimated, using balance sheets and income statements collected in the Diane database, a set of financial ratios that were used to estimate model parameters. The choice of financial ratios was made for three reasons. First, ratios rely on a theoretical foundation (financial analysis), and are homogeneous, hence easily comparable, because they are calculated using the same method, within a given regulatory framework. Second, they are more robust than many other qualitative variables that might have been used to characterize firms [5], such as those that make it possible to account for their organization, their strategy, their prod ucts or markets, and that can be found in the literature [46,50]. Third, they can be estimated for any company, which is not the case for certain variables, such as financial market variables, that are solely available for listed firms. Ratios were chosen using two criteria to make the selection as independent as possible from any arbitrary choice: we selected those that belong to the main financial dimensions that explain bankruptcy (profitability, liquidity, solvency, activity, financial structure, turnover) and that have already demonstrated their discrimination abilities in previous studies [23,24]. ## 4. Modeling methods

To design models used as a benchmark, we selected the modeling methods commonly used in the literature dealing with bankruptcy prediction. We first chose three methods to design single models, that is, models that rely on a sole classification rule: a survival method with Cox’s proportional hazard model [14], a support vector machine [47], and an extreme learning machine with multiple hidden lavers [53]. We then selected five ensemble methods: bagging [10], boosting with AdaBoost [18], random subspace [22], random forest [11] and XGBoost [12]. With each method, we used a decision tree as a base classifier. Finally, to segment the different samples as well as to design “dynamic models”, we used self-organizing feature maps [28]. Since all these techniques have been widely presented in the literature, it is not necessary to discuss them once more here; we will simply present the algorithm used to segment data and that used to estimate the “dynamic models”.

## 5.2. Segmentation process

Dynamic models rely on a segmentation of the data space that makes it possible to find a fit between classification rules and firms’ financial situation measured over three years of their life, and for some of them, over the very last years. More precisely, this segmentation is used for two purposes. First, it is used to cluster subgroups of firms that share a common profile within each financial dimension that explains failure, presented above. Second, it is used to design subsets of models where each of them fit a given subgroup of firms while ensuring that the number of models in a given subset is proportional to the number of firms in the subgroup that was used for its estimation.

To perform this segmentation, we first quantified each learning sample using six self-organizing feature maps, one per financial dimension. We therefore designed one map with the variables selected for this task and that belong to the “profitability” dimension, then another map with variables from the “solvency” dimension, and so on for the four remaining dimensions. To estimate the size of a map, we used a criterion defined by [9]. For this purpose, we estimated a set of maps ranging in size from 50 to 500 neurons, and for each map we calculated the coefficient of variation of the quantification error esti mated over 100 learning phases.

Once this was done, we analyzed the variations of the error with respect to the number of neurons while looking for the map size that led to a sudden increase in this error. When such a phenomenon occurs, it means that neurons switch from one group to another in the variable space, and that their weights are no longer only influenced by small variations due to their initialization, and hence their estimation becomes unstable. We then selected the map having a size just below the one leading to this sudden increase. This procedure yielded three maps per dimension, or a total of 18 maps for each learning sample.

Next, we clustered the neurons of each map into a small number of meta-classes to easily subsequently split the data space and estimate the dynamic models. For this purpose, neurons were clustered using a hi erarchical ascendant classification and several aggregation criteria (Ward. average linkage and complete linkage) while testing different partitions made up of a limited number of classes, between 2 and 10. For each partition, we assigned the label of the majority class to each neuron, if it existed. If it did not, we assigned the label of the majority class of its immediate neighbors. When this was not possible, we assigned the label of a class at random.

At this stage of the calculation process, we had nine partitions for each map. To choose the partition to be finally selected. we looked for the most homogenous one using three indexes recommended by [40]: Point-Biserial Correlation, C-Index of Hubert and Levin and Gamma of Baker and Hubert. Once the indexes were estimated, for each index we assigned a rank to each partition. The ranks were then summed and the partition that led to the lowest sum was chosen. In case of a tie, we chose the smallest partition. Then, the labels of the groups of each selected partition were recoded using a financial health scale ranging from 1 to the maximum number of groups within a given partition: the mean of each variable within each group was estimated, the different means were ranked in ascending order of financial health, the ranks were summed and sorted in a final list, and each group was assigned a label that corresponded to its rank in the final list. This recoding was carried out in order to obtain a variable that could be considered a quantitative variable and thus could be quantified during the remaining calculations. Each map was therefore split into a certain number of meta-classes.

Finally, we assessed the different groups to be used to segment each learning sample. For this purpose, we projected all firms onto each map while locating those neurons closest to their financial situation using a distance calculation. Once the neurons were found, we assigned to each firm the label of the meta-classes to which these neurons belonged. As we had estimated one map per year and per financial dimension, or 18 maps in total, each firm could now be represented with a sequence of 18 figures. To estimate the groups, all individual sequences were clustered into meta-classes or prototype sequences that characterized different subgroups of firms that shared a common evolution of their financial situation. Here as well, we searched for a partition that was made up of a small number of meta-sequences, between 2 and 10, and that was as homogeneous as possible. To cluster sequences, we used the same pro cedure as that used to cluster neurons of each map. We finally chose the partition of meta-sequences that was the most homogeneous and we used the labels of the meta-sequences to estimate the different groups of firms and then segment each learning sample. The algorithm used to segment data is presented in Appendix 1. At the end of this process, each sample is divided into a few groups made up of firms that share a common financial profile estimated over three consecutive years and across six dimensions. These groups will serve to estimate dynamic models.

## 5.3. Model design

We estimated different types of models that depend on the modeling method as well as on the data collected. To analyze the contributions of historical data and data segmentation to model accuracy, we estimated three sets of results. The first one corresponds to results calculated with all models (single, ensemble and dynamic models) and relies only on single-period data, that is, data measured during the year prior to that when firm status was assessed. The second one corresponds to results also estimated with all models but using historical data, that is, data collected over three years. The third one corresponds to results esti mated only with ensemble-based models or dynamic models, and aims to analyze to what extent the scheme that has been designed to estimate dynamic models, and that relies on a prior segmentation of data, could be used efficiently in conjunction with traditional ensemble techniques. We present, in the following sections, the methodological choices we made to design the models and to set up the model parameters.

## 5.3.1. Design of single models

The estimation of single models was performed using a variable se lection technique that fit each modeling method. The selections pre sented in Section 5.1 (Explanatory variable selection) were only used to discard highly correlated variables and those that presented a low in dividual discrimination power and were not intended to produce the final sets of variables that would be used to design models. We thus chose different techniques depending on the modeling method in use in order to find a true fit especially between the evaluation criterion of different subsets of variables and the modeling method that would use these subsets. Indeed, certain criteria, such as those that rely on linear assumptions between the inputs and outputs of a model, are ill-suited to non-linear models [33]. It is therefore wise to properly choose the components of a variable selection technique depending on the char acteristics of the modeling method that is intended to be used in conjunction with it.

Choosing a variable selection technique comes down to selecting three components: a method that makes it possible to explore the vari able space (backward, forward, stepwise), if exploring the whole space is deemed too expensive, an evaluation criterion to assess the performance of different solutions and that will be used to select the final one, and a stopping criterion to interrupt the search. We chose such components with each modeling method.

With Cox’s method, we used a backward search and an evaluation criterion based on the estimation of an error. The estimation was per formed by dividing each learning sample into two parts: 50% of data were used to design models with the different sets of variables to be evaluated, and 50% to test their forecasting error. Over each step, we removed one variable, we estimated a model with the remaining vari ables and selected the subset that led to the best classification rate. The process was repeated until all variables were discarded. Then, we looked for the subset that led to the lowest error, as well as the subsets that led to an error not statistically different from the lowest error at a threshold of 1% using a two-sided test for differences between proportions. We then selected, from among these subsets, the set made up of the smallest number of variables.

With the extreme learning machine, network architecture was esti mated a priori [33], and we used the algorithm presented in [53] that makes it possible to design multiple-hidden layer networks. The number of layers and the size of each hidden layer were assessed experimentally using a number of layers ranging from 2 to $^ { 8 , }$ a number of neurons ranging from 5 to 50, and subsets of variables drawn at random. We used one bias per layer and the hyperbolic tangent as an activation function of neurons. Each time, calculations were performed 100 times, using 50% of a learning sample to estimate network parameters and 50% to esti mate the error calculated with them. Results were then averaged and we selected the architecture that led to an error statistically close to the lowest error and that was made up of the smallest number of neurons. Once the architecture was determined, we selected variables. We tested different selection criteria that rely on zero, first and second order techniques [33] as well as on the estimation of an out-of-sample error, and we chose the latter technique since it led, on average, to the best results. Each time, we used a backward search. we discarded variables one at a time until all variables were removed, retrained the network after each removal and chose the subset that led to an error statistically close to the lowest error and made up of the smallest number of vari ables. Here as well, each time, the learning process was performed using 50% of a learning sample, and the error was estimated with the remaining 50%.

With the support vector machine, we tested three kernel functions (linear, polynomial and radial basis) and estimated several parameters using 100 subsamples drawn at random from each learning sample. With each subsample, 50% of data were used to assess the different param eters and 50% to test the error. We first estimated the parameter C that serves to penalize the error with the three functions, using values ranging from $1 0 ^ { - 5 } , 1 0 ^ { - 4 } { \mathrm { ~ t o ~ } } 1 0 ^ { 5 }$ . We then estimated parameter $\gamma ,$ that controls the area of influence of the support vectors, in conjunction with the radial basis and polynomial functions, using the same range of values as that used to calculate C. Lastly, we calculated the degree d of the polynomial function with values ranging from 1, 2 to 10, and set the constant term r of this function to 0. The error achieved with each combination of parameters was averaged over the 100 subsamples and we chose the function and its corresponding parameters that led to the lowest error. Once this was done, variables were selected using a backward search and the estimation of an error as an evaluation criterion.

## 5.3.2. Design of ensemble-based models

To design ensemble-based models, we first assessed several param eters using 50% of a learning sample and the combination of parameters that led to the lowest error achieved with the remaining 50% was finally chosen. The number of iterations required to estimate each ensemble was determined using different values ranging from 10 to 500, with a step size of 10. The number of variables that served to design classifiers with random subspace and random forest was determined using subsets ranging from 2 to 10 variables, with a step size of one. Several other parameters were also determined with XGBoost: the learning rate was estimated with values that ranged from 0.05 to 0.5, with a step size of 0.05, the maximum depth of each tree was assessed using values ranging from 2 to 10, with a step size of one, and finally, to avoid overfitting, we used an early stopping technique. The base classifiers used with bagging and boosting were estimated with CART: variables were chosen during the pruning sequences of the various trees. In this case, we also used 50% of a learning sample to grow the classification trees and 50% to prune them using a Gini heterogeneity index. The base classifiers used with random subspace, random forest and XGBoost were calculated with a random number of variables. To complete the estimation of ensemblebased models, we defined their size experimentally. With each tech nique, we built an initial ensemble made up of 500 models using 50% of a learning sample, then we pruned these ensembles by discarding, during each step, one model based on its error produced using the remaining 50%, until all models were removed. Finally, we selected the ensemble made up of the smallest number of classifiers and that led to an error statistically close to the lowest. The resulting ensembles, calcu lated with each modeling method, were used to make forecasts.

## 5.3.3. Design of dynamic models

Dynamic models were designed with one-dimension self-organizing feature maps. Each map was assessed using two main steps. Over the first one, the growing sequence, maps were added one at a time, sequentially, to the ensemble being estimated using 50% of a learning sample. Over the second step, the previous ensemble was pruned using the remaining 50%. Over the first step, each model was designed with a map the size n of which was randomly drawn from a range of values between 50 and N neurons, and using a subset of k variables also drawn at random among the variables chosen to design models. The estimation of each map was performed with half of the 50% of a learning sample used over the first step. The addition of a model to the ensemble being estimated was carried out using an error calculation performed with the other half of the 50%. For this purpose, we calculated the error achieved with the ensemble already estimated, and we compared its error with that produced by the same ensemble to which we added the last map that was designed: if this last map made it possible to reduce the error, it was selected and added to the previous ensemble, otherwise, it was removed. The procedure was repeated until the number of iterations I that governed the addition of maps was reached. Over the second step, the ensemble created previously was pruned using a backward search technique and an error calculation. Each time, a map was removed from the ensemble under consideration, and the error produced by this ensemble was estimated. When all maps were removed once, the ensemble that led to the lowest error was selected, and the process was repeated until all maps were discarded. Then we looked for the ensemble that led to an error that was statistically close to the lowest error and that was made up of the smallest number of maps. These final ensembles were used to make forecasts.

Here, as well, we had to define several parameters that governed the maps themselves (learning step, neighborhood function) as well as the ensemble of maps (largest size of a map, number of iterations, number of variables). Parameters that corresponded to the learning step and the neighborhood function were selected as follows, with each learning sample. We first drew at random 100 subsets of variables among those selected to design models, and we built, with each subset, 500 maps ranging from 50 to 1000 in size, with a step size of 50. Each time, we tested different values for the learning step (between 0.01 and 1 with a step size of 0.1) and for the number of iterations (between 10,000 and 100,000 with a step size of 10,000), and we set the neighborhood radius to the number of neurons in the maps under consideration. Maps were estimated with 50% of a learning sample and their predictive ability was assessed with the remaining 50%. We chose the learning rate and the number of iterations that led, on average, to the lowest error. To determine the maximum number N of a map, we drew at random 100 subsets of variables, and with each subset, we tested different values for N ranging from 50 to 500 with a step size of 10. The map weights and accuracy were estimated as done previously, and we finally selected the number N that led to the lowest error. With this number $N ,$ we repeated the same calculations to assess parameter k that corresponded to the number of variables to be used with each map, using values ranging from 2 to 20, with a step size of 1, and here as well we finally selected the value that led to the lowest error. The algorithm used to estimate dy namic models is presented in Appendix 2.

## 5.4. Results estimation

Forecasts were estimated using three measures of performance. We first calculated model accuracy as it is traditionally done: the number of true negatives and the number of true positives were summed and the sum was divided by the total number of firms. We then calculated an F2- score that makes it possible to give more weight to true positives, that is, to bankrupt firms that are correctly classified, than to true negatives. Because the costs of misclassification are very asymmetric in the field of bankruptcy prediction, it is important that models offer a good ability to correctly forecast the fate of firms that will go bankrupt. It is precisely for this reason we chose a cutoff value for each model that maximizes the F2-score. The cutoff value was estimated with each learning sample and was used to estimate model accuracy with test samples. The F2-score was calculated as follows: $( 1 + 2 ^ { 2 } ) \ ^ { \circ }$ precision \* recall $\prime ( 2 ^ { 2 ^ { \circ } { } ^ { * } }$ precision + recall), with precision = true positives / (true positives + false positives) and recall = true positives / (true positives + false negatives). Finally, we estimated a third measure, the area under the receiver operative curve (AUC), which is another way of reporting the performance of a model, especially when the costs of misclassification are asymmetric and the classes are unbalanced [31]. The AUC makes it possible to represent, on a graph, the proportion of true positives and the proportion of false positives, while changing the cutoff value.

One first observes in Table 8 that results change depending on the period over which data were collected. The weakest results, whatever the criterion, were obtained with data from 2012, which corresponds to a period during which firms experienced substantial variations in their environment. These variations certainly weakened some of those firms that might have survived if the environment had remained stable, and explain why model accuracy is reduced. By contrast, the best results were achieved with data from 2004 and 2016, because samples from 2003 and 2004, and those from 2015 and 2016, experienced less changes in economic conditions than the others. On the whole, all methods seem to be sensitive to fluctuations that may affect the econ omy. This result is consistent with what has already been shown in the literature; such changes lead to population drifts that move the bound ary between failed and non-failed firms, making it difficult to forecast the fate of those that will die [44].

One also observes in Table 8 that the average difference between correct classification rates estimated with ensemble-based models and those estimated with single models is 1.74 percentage points; the same difference assessed with F2-scores is 4.90 percentage points, and that assessed with AUCs is 0.045. All these differences, even if they are sta tistically significant at a threshold of 1%, are rather low, which is also consistent with the literature [35,45]. On the whole, dynamic models lead to the most significant differences, followed by XGboost and boosting.

Finally, when one analyzes the statistical significance of the differ ences between the average correct classification rate of each model type, those between dynamic models and the other models are all significant at a threshold of 1%. With F2-scores, the same differences are also sig nificant either at a threshold of 1% (single, bagging, and random forest models), at a threshold of 5% (boosting), or at a threshold of 10% (XGboost and random subspace). With AUCs, they are all significant at least at a threshold of 5% except with XGBoost.

These results show that dynamic models, when they are designed using single-period data, perform better than all other models, even if the magnitude of the differences is not that large. However, the way dynamic models embody the boundary between firms better accounts for its structure than what can be done with any single or ensemblebased model. The results also show that the techniques on which ensemble-based models rely, which give a large place to chance and resampling schemes, are not the only ones that can lead to more precise results than those obtained with traditional single models.

## 6.2. Results estimated with sectorial samples, multi-period data and data segmentation

One knows that bankruptcy prediction models, when estimated using financial data that characterize a set of firms belonging to different sectors, cannot fully capture all the differences that may exist between failed and non-failed firms [5]. Indeed, each sector largely determines the financial structure of firms, and especially the capital structure, the level of indebtedness, the level of payroll, the size of fixed assets, which means that two firms belonging to two different sectors but having an identical financial profile may present a very different probability of survival from one another. This is why we estimated the same models as those presented previously but using data that characterized firms belonging to five different sectors that encompass, in France, more than 80% of all bankruptcies. The results are presented in Table 11. We solely present those estimated with multi-period data and data segmentation.

Table 11 shows, for each sector, the results estimated with all traditional ensemble-based models by accuracy measure, but also the best result among all results estimated with these ensemble-based models and those calculated with dynamic models. This table in dicates that model accuracy measured with a correct classification rate is rather stable from one sector to another and that, on average, dynamic models outperform all other models. The average difference between the results achieved with dynamic models and those calculated with the best models is of 4.01 percentage points, and 4.40 percentage points with those computed with all models, and all the differences are statistically significant at a threshold of 1%.

This table also indicates that model accuracy, when measured with criteria that take into account both types of error, becomes rather unstable with traditional ensemble-based models. The average amplitude (difference between the maximum and the minimum) of F2-measures estimated with ensemble-based models is 6.09 percentage points, and 6.41 with the best ensemble-based models, compared to 1.79 with dy namic models. With AUCs, these figures are 5.38, 5.82 and 1.44 respectively. Moreover, with F2-measures, dynamic models are much more accurate than all other models, with an average difference of 8.38 percentage points with all ensemble-based models and 7.55 with the best models. Here as well, all differences are statistically significant at a threshold of 1%. And with AUCs, all differences are statistically signif icant at a threshold of 5%, except in one situation, where it is significant at a threshold of 10% (with data from the services sector). These figures show that dynamic models are much more prone to correctly forecast the fate of failed firms than can be done with all other models, and therefore they make it possible to drastically reduce type-II error, whatever the costs of misclassification. This characteristic is in fact essential for a bankruptcy prediction model since the costs of misclas sification are highly asymmetric. Indeed, if a bankruptcy model is used by a financial institution to decide whether to grant a loan to one of its clients, it can lead to a grant or to a refusal based on a wrong decision. In the first case, the wrong decision involves the loss of a capital that will not be reimbursed, in the second case, only the loss of a potential bar gain. All these results clearly show how the articulation between a segmentation of the data space, based on groups of firms sharing a similar evolution of their financial situation over time, and a fine-tuned modeling of the entire variety of such evolutions, make it possible to reveal failure patterns that other models cannot embody and, as a consequence, to significantly improve forecasts and especially those that correspond to failed firms.

## 6.3. Financial profiles that can be revealed using dynamic models

To get an insight into the way dynamic models embody certain forms of failure, we have analyzed the financial profiles of firms that were correctly classified by these models but incorrectly classified by tradi tional models. On the whole, the diversity of non-failed firms is too broad to discern any constants in their profile. However, with failed firms, the situation is different and two main characteristics seem to emerge. First of all, the firms that belong to this group present a rather average financial profile that is relatively stable over time and less erratic than that of all firms that will go bankrupt. In general, most bankrupt firms share a similar. changing financial structure from one year to another, as if they were looking for an equilibrium they would never reach. However, among these firms, those that exhibit the lowest variations on average are more frequently well-classified by dynamic models than by all others. Secondly, firms classified correctly by dy namic models and incorrectly by other models share a working capital requirement (WCR) that is less problematic than that of all other failed firms. The French economy mostly relies on small and medium-sized firms, and this type of company corresponds to the majority of bank rupt ones. It is therefore not surprising that WCR represents a major indicator of weakness. Indeed, the amount of inventory, trade receiv ables and trade payables strongly determines the level of cash of this type of firm, which, in turn, determines their survival ability. It appears that dynamic models have a better ability than others to discern firms for which the decrease in WCR components over the last three years of their life is rather low, and often as low as that of many non-failed firms, but sufficiently low to lead fragile firms to failure. In fact, dynamic models manage to detect some weak signals of the deterioration of a firm’s financial health that cannot be embodied by traditional models and that explain bankruptcy. This is in accordance with what we mentioned in the introduction. Certain symptoms that may suggest that a firm is not completely in bad shape and is able to survive sometimes reveal some thing quite different, and clearly here, dynamic models do well in discerning those which may be having a deceptive influence.

## 7. Conclusion

The method we propose in this article illustrates one way of incor porating firm history into a model and to what extent this can contribute to making forecasts more precise than those offered by traditional methods. The contribution of this study to the literature is three-fold. First, it is methodological, since it offers a modeling framework of the way firm financial health may change over time that is likely to better embody the relationship between a probability of failure and a partic ular temporal financial profile than what can be achieved with other methods. Second, it is empirical, since not only does it significantly improve forecasts, but it also improves them when it comes to predicting the fate of failed firms. Third, it is practical, because dynamic models can be used to help companies monitor their risk of failure. Indeed, it is very easy to graphically represent the financial situation of a given company and compare this situation to those estimated with the clusters and prototype sequences that were used to design dynamic models. Fig. 1 represents such a possibility and shows to what extent a company is more or less likely to fail.

Nevertheless, our study has some limitations. From a conceptual

point of view, dynamic models rely on empirical temporal profiles but one knows little about their theoretical aspect and nothing suggests how to represent their true diversity. Several research works have already shown that such profiles are not artifacts $[ 3 0 , 3 6 , 3 7 ]$ , but they did not demonstrate whether there exist conjunctural as well as permanent profiles. An in-depth analysis of these profiles could be useful to improve the way modeling methods can embody their variety as well as to analyze the relationship between their representation and model accu racy. From a methodological point of view, our study does not take into account very young firms, that is, firms younger than three years, and hence cannot be used with them. However, from a practical point of view, this weakness is partly mitigated by the fact that the main risk incurred by financial institutions essentially comes from firms that are not young, because they are not subject to the liability of newness and consequently their probability of failure is much harder to estimate than that of very young firms. Finally, again from a practical point of view, dynamic models do not offer the same ability to easily interpret their results as that presented by simple, linear models. It is indeed difficult to precisely explain how an ensemble of self-organizing neural networks led to a given forecast, while in many financial institutions, when an analyst uses a model as a decision support system, this model must be interpretable [39]. This suggests that it might be worthwhile to study a means that would make it possible to extract meaningful information from different self-organizing neural networks and that could lead to an understanding of how such networks reach their decisions. A study of this subject deserves attention.

2.3 Estimate, with respect to the size of the maps, the increase in the quantification error;

2.4 Select the map having a size immediately below the one leading to the highest increase;

3. With each map k, cluster neurons into meta-classes using a hierarchical ascendant classification and 3 aggregation criteria (Ward, average linkage and complete linkage) and find the most homogeneous partition as follows:

3.1.2 Assign to each neuron the label of the majority class computed with all criteria. In case of a tie, assign the label of the majority class of it immediate neighbors. In case of another tie, assign a class randomly;

3.1.3 Evaluate the resulting partition using homogeneity indexes;

3.2 Repeat Step 3.1.1 to 3.1.3 with $n c = n c + 1$ until $n c = 1 0 ;$

3.6 Recode the labels of the meta-classes using a scale of firm financial health.

4. Cluster firms belonging to sample L using the meta-classes estimated with each map as follows:

4.4.1.1 Cluster all sequences using the Ward, average linkage and complete linkage criteria;

4.4.1.2 Assign to each firm the label of the majority sequence calculated with all criteria. In case of another tie, assign a class randomly;
