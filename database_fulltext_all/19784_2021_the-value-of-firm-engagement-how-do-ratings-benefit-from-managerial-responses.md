# The value of firm engagement: How do ratings benefit from managerial responses?

Jie Sheng <sup>a,\*</sup>, Xiaojun Wang <sup>a</sup>, Joseph Amankwah-Amoah <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, University of Bristol, Howard House, Queen’s Avenue, Bristol BS8 1SD, UK <sup>b</sup> Kent Business School, University of Kent, Sail and Colour Loft, Medway, Kent ME4 4TE, UK

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Firm engagement Firm-generated content Managerial response Online review Text mining

## A B S T R A C T

This research explores firms’ digital presence in an online interactive network. Anchored in the literature on firm engagement and firm-generated content, this study discusses and empirically examines the impact of managerial response to online reviews on future ratings. A big data approach coupled with text analytics is employed on a large field dataset to analyse both structured review/response metrics and unstructured response text. Results show that an increase in future review valence is positively associated with the intensity, promptness, and sentiment of managerial responses, and the magnitude of effects is contingent upon firms' rating status and market position. Mixed evidence is obtained for comprehensiveness and standardisation of response content. These findings reveal that the influence of firm engagement in the online review network is likely to be shaped by the goodwill signalled by managerial responses.

## 1. Introduction

In the digital era, firms’ pivotal role in engaging with customers and other stakeholders on online platforms has been elevated to the forefront of firms’ marketing and service operation strategies in recent times [1,2]. One of the contemporary mechanisms for effective engagement is online communities which foster new social interactions and change the way firms connect with customers [3,4]. Often intending to retain cus tomers or attract new customers, firms engage in online social in teractions by proactively creating a platform or participating in an existing third-party organised group. Such digital presence has a public nature that transforms a dyadic firm-customer relationship into poten tial influence on a broader network.

While engagement has received considerable attention from re searchers [1,2,5], the preponderance of the existing research has far concentrated on individual-level customer engagement [6]. The dis cussion on firm engagement – a term conceptualised to describe actions taken by focal firms to interact with customers via online platforms – is still at an early stage [7]. Scholarly efforts have been devoted to exploring how marketer-generated content affects consumers’ engage ment intention measured by behavioural matrices such as comments, likes, and sharing [8,9], whereas little attention has been paid to sentimental engagement [10]. There are inconclusive discussions on the efficacy of firm engagement, particularly concerning how and to what extent firm engagement leads to attitudinal changes in an online network and what optimal approaches are to manage firms’ online presence.

Against this backdrop, this research investigates firm engagement behaviour and its power in affecting collective opinions. We focus on a typical case of firm engagement – managerial responses to online re views – and explore the relationship between responding approaches and future online ratings. This issue has received growing scholarly in terest given its importance to online reputation management (e.g., [11,12]), yet limited evidence has been documented to justify the effectiveness of various engagement approaches in influencing customer ratings over time. Responding is not merely a one-off action or policy change but a strategy that may have a continuous impact on public perceptions of the focal firm over the long term. A set of measures of firm engagement is developed to assess the conjoined effects on future rat ings. In particular, textual features of response content are incorporated into the framework. Drawing on a sample of 765,082 reviews and 339,123 responses of 1037 hotels over an approximately 15-year period, we examine both the behavioural and textual cues embedded in mana gerial responses and their associations with customer ratings. The main finding is that sustainable growth in ratings can form when firm engagement displays greater intensity, promptness and positivity, and these effects vary with firms’ rating status and market position.

This study adds to the theorisation of firm engagement and the line of research on firm-generated content by comprehensively investigating the operational value of both engagement behaviour and content on collective sentiment [13]. The mixed-method approach coupling textual and statistical analyses on a big data set enable us to respond to one of the research calls in [7] about assessing how much advantage firm engagement can generate. Our analysis of the dynamic in engagement behaviour and interactions in a time series extends the current research frontier by simultaneously testing the significance of firm engagement characteristics and identifying the most influential attributes. In so doing, we move beyond answering the question of whether or not to engage and attempt to delineate the best responding strategy for firms operation and management of online social media presence.

The remainder of the article is organised as follows. Section 2 re views relevant literature on firm engagement, and hypotheses about managerial responses are developed in Section 3. Section 4 explains the research methodology, and empirical results are reported in the following section. The last two sections conclude the paper with a dis cussion on findings, implications, and future research.

## 2. Online firm engagement

Extant research on engagement has mainly focused on interpreting engagement among customers and attempting to discern customer engagement behaviour in the online social interaction network (e.g., [14–16]). However, an online social interaction network also involves actors other than customers, such as firm managers, employees, regu lators, and platforms, and engagement essentially reflects the connect edness among multiple actors [1,17]. Engagement behaviour of actors is affected by their immediate network of actors and the surrounding network [18]. In light of the actor interdependence, recent de velopments have broadened the scope of engagement research to the conceptual domain of actor engagement, a general actor-to-actor perspective that moves the focus from the dyadic firm–customer re lationships to network relationships among a variety of versatile actor roles in service ecosystems [19].

The increased connectivity and sociality among actors in the virtual environment bring firms opportunities to harness the social interactions among customers in online communities. One primary research interest is concerned with firm strategies and tactics in interacting and engaging with customers. Godes et al. [20] identify four general action plan regarding firms engaging in social interactions: firms acting as an observer, a moderator, a mediator, or a participant to learn from, facilitate and generate online communications. Superior performance is achievable if firms orientate their business to interact with customers, gain insights from such interactions, and leverage it to sustain customer relationships [21]. Fisher [7] theorises that firms’ competitive advan tages to engage in online communities are derived from the information, influence, and solidarity benefits of the firm–customer interactions.

Firm engagement emphasises that businesses are not only aware of the importance of online social interactions but also play a role in the network by interacting with customers on the virtual platform [22]. Notwithstanding its growing strategic importance, online firm engage ment appears to be an under-explored topic [1,7]. Though not neces sarily using the term “firm engagement”, marketing research has discussed how types and characteristics of the firm- or marketergenerated content in social media affect consumer engagement behav iour such as commenting, liking and sharing [8,9], whereas relatively little discussion centres on the impact of firm activeness or messages on user sentiment [10,23]. Concerning an online review setting, there is a handful of studies demonstrating that firms’ adoption of online response practice can contribute to the improvement of ratings by existing cus tomers (e.g., [24]) and prospective customers (e.g., [11,12,25]). Many of these studies employ a difference-in-difference approach. While the method best suits specific research contexts, it leaves space to explore whether the discovered effect is sustainable long after the policy change. Besides, prior studies consider timing [26], tailoring [25], and depth [27] of managerial responses as essential features. What remains unclear is the relative importance of distinct features of responses in relation to future online ratings, which requires a systematic and comprehensive examination of the “quality” aspects of responses [23], including both behavioural cues such as frequency and timeliness of posting responses as well as the responses content such as the actual messages and atti tudes reflected in the responses (see Table 1). An understanding devel oped from a joint evaluation of potential effects over time helps determine optimal engagement strategies.

## 3. Managerial responses and customer ratings

As delineated earlier, firm engagement in this study refers to a firm’s online responding to customer reviews. In light of the network perspective of actor engagement [18], we argue that firm intervention influences not only current reviewing customers but also bystanders who observe previous reviews and responses, and the interactive engagement process will ultimately shape the valence of engagement over time [18].

Firm engagement can impact the network by building trustworthiness and swaying customers’ perceptions and expectations [27]. There are two occasions when firm responding can influence customers who observe the interactions between focal firms and current customers. First, at the pre-purchase stage, when customers read reviews to aid decision-making, there is a chance for firm responses attached to the reviews to be observed. Existing research has confirmed the informational value of online reviews (e.g., [30]). Likewise, response messages can reveal latent qualities of services by delivering informa tion about unobservable attributes, which is an additional source offered for individuals’ consideration and assessment [27]. The presence of multiple cues in managerial responses affects customers’ inferences about firms’ trustworthiness [31]. Through acknowledging the strengths or deficiencies revealed in the reviews and replies, prospective cus tomers may adjust their expectations accordingly [11], and the level of satisfaction increases with a positive disconfirmation of beliefs after consumption. The second occasion is at the post-consumption stage when customers decide to write reviews. Customers, particularly first time reviewers, may browse and use earlier reviews as a reference point. This leaves a chance, though perhaps not very high, of managerial responses influencing how customers rate. For instance, an unsatisfied customer reads prior reviews and responses before rating the service and discovers that comments complaining about similar issues have been responded to by managers with a detailed account of the problem and an endeavour to remedy it. Having a reasonable explanation from man agers may alleviate the negative opinions to be less critical, and hence the rating may be slightly higher than in the circumstance where no responses are observed.

Taken together, firm engagement influences customers’ ratings by shaping expectations and nudging attitudes towards firms. Existing research has answered the question of whether or not to engage, with evidence documenting that firm engagement (i.e., managerial responses in the present research context) improves future customer engagement valence $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ review ratings). A more critical query following is about effective ways to engage and how firms adjust engagement strategies given customer engagement’s collective nature, such as valence and variability of reviews [32–34]. In reviewing relevant literature, it is found that existing studies are primarily interested in two dimensions of firm engagement. One is the engagement behaviour, often examining response provision and speed, and the other focus is on the textual characteristics, such as message tailoring and depth. Empirical results of these engagement attributes are mixed, and no attempt has been made to assess each facet’s relative importance to customer sentiment. Accordingly, we identify five quality attributes (shown in Fig. 1) whose association with customer engagement has been alluded to in earlier discussions. Our research questions address which firm engagement attributes are more substantial for customer engagement valence and how future online rating is influenced concerning the interplay between firm engagement (managerial responses) and current customer engagement (online reviews). Below we develop our hypotheses.

## 3.1. Intensity

The first inquiry concerns whether high-intensity firm engagement is preferable. The number of responses displayed on a site is the most visible element of online responses. It is the first signal of responsiveness delivered to a viewing customer. A high level of firm engagement in tensity implies that responding is ongoing but not on an ad hoc basis, and the firm invests significant efforts in reading and replying to reviews [23]. Such signals are likely to be interpreted by viewers and affect their perception and expectations about the business, according to the sig nalling theory applied in consumer research (cf. [35]). Besides, when previous reviews are negative and ratings are sparse, providing more responses plays a more substantial role in nudging review readers opinions towards being positive, while the impact of responses might be trivial when existing reviews already present consistently positive feedback. Considering that review volume varies across firms, we use response ratio (the percentage of reviews being responded to) to mea sure firm engagement intensity and hypothesise the following:

## H1. Response ratio is positively associated with future ratings.

H1a. The positive relationship between response ratio and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are low.

H1b. The positive relationship between response ratio and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are sparse.

## 3.2. Promptness

The promptness of firm engagement pertains to the time interval between a customer review and the focal firm’s corresponding reply. The importance of timeliness has been widely documented in the service evaluation and recovery literature for offline settings (e.g., [36]). In an online environment, proactive and timely responses are shown to be effective in managing customer complaints [37,38]. Customers’ in ferences about a firm’s trustworthiness are likely to be influenced by the promptness of firm engagement [31]. A rapid response indicates organisational efficiency in dealing with customer feedback, leading to a positive evaluation of a firm’s reliability. In particular, the impact of prompt responses to negative and sparse reviews is potentially greater because a delayed response leaves unfavourable reviews either without a reply or unchallenged, which may damage the focal firm’s image. Besides, promptness affects responses’ visibility on the platform, which is critical for onlookers to see the firm–customer conversations [25]. A delay in responding reduces the probability of being observable to many customers and is unlikely to provoke reactions [12]. We hypothesise:

H2. Response promptness is positively associated with future ratings.

H2a. The positive relationship between response promptness and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are low.

H2b. The positive relationship between response promptness and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are sparse.

## 3.3. Comprehensiveness

Research on electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) has shown that the length of reviews has direct and indirect effects on readers’ perception of communication quality (e.g., [39,40]). Anchored to this line of thinking is the suggestion that the length of responses may likewise make a dif ference to potential customers. Long responses cost firms time and effort to write, signifying firms value the opportunity and being willing to engage. Besides, analogous to the fact that detailed reviews can reduce service quality uncertainty by revealing greater details [40], long re sponses may contain comprehensive information and thus increase in formation diagnosticity [41] that helps customers develop realistic expectations or self-address concerns. Though an extended response is conceivably more informative and influential than a short one, the benefit may diminish after a certain point when responses become un necessarily complex. Adverse effects may arise because lengthy re sponses can overload readers with information and require reading and processing efforts. That being the case, we expect an inverted-U-shaped relationship, in which marginal effects reduce after an optimal number of words. It is hypothesised:

H3. Response length has an inverted-U-shaped relationship with future ratings.

H3a. The relationship between response length and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are low.

H3b. The relationship between response length and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are sparse.

## 3.4. Standardisation

Response standardisation is concerned with whether responses are uniform or tailored to reviews. Presumably, a firm may follow a certain strategy or approach in responding throughout its social media engagement practice. Responses can be personalised and the textual content is pertinent to the review themes [42]. which indicate firm engagement efforts being devoted to creating high-quality online com munications, resulting in a positive evaluation of the firm [31,43]. In contrast, a response written in a standardised format is generic and irrelevant to the review message. Responses to previous reviews are repeated with the same or similar text, which can be interpreted by customers as boilerplate, leading to an impression of firms being un concerned but merely ‘ticking boxes’ to interact with customers. Particularly as regards negative and dispersed reviews, standardised responses do not offer explanations or solutions to the issue complained and are thus futile in terms of generating a positive impact on review readers. Therefore, we hypothesise about how the degree to which a firm uses a standardised approach to respond affects customers:

H4. Response standardisation is negatively associated with future ratings.

H4a. The negative relationship between response standardisation and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are low.

H4c. The negative relationship between response standardisation and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are sparse.

## 3.5. Valence

The valence of firm engagement refers to the verbal and more importantly the non-verbal (i.e., tone of voice) facets of online communication, which reflects sentiment in the firm-generated content and attitude towards vocal customers [44]. The interactional justice theory has explained that interpersonal treatment is critical in deter mining an individual’s reaction to an organisation [45]. Different stra tegies (e.g., empathetic or defensive) could have a divergent impact on customer evaluation of firms (e.g., [26,46]). A positive-valenced response puts customers’ concerns first, such as appreciating cus tomers’ praise, accepting responsibility for failure, taking corrective or preventive action, and making promises to change soon. Politeness and respect are shown in the messages with responses written in a courteous and business-like way, leading to greater satisfaction due to increased inference about the firm’s professionalism [47]. In contrast, a response of negative valence presents little sincerity, manifested in such as the denial of accountability for unfavourable incidents, taking no action or paying lip-service towards failure, pointing an accusing finger at others, or speaking in an aggressive tone. Adverse effects on future ratings may develop as a result of negativity in responding. It is natural to expect the emotional valence of responses to be predicated on the original reviews, and we anticipate a proactive and positive approach is more critical to dealing with negative and polarised reviews. Accordingly:

H5. Response valence is positively associated with future ratings.

H5a. The positive relationship between response valence and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are low.

H5b. The positive relationship between response valence and future ratings is stronger when prior ratings are sparse.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Data

Given the importance of firm–customer interactions in the service industry, we employ data from the hospitality sector with a specific focus on London hotels. In addition to London being a globally popular travel destination, the large number of customer reviews and managerial responses on a range of hotels in the city meets the expectations for sampling and the requirements for the research purpose. Our data are retrieved from TripAdvisor. Managerial responses are permanently displayed on the website and available for all to read. The entire review and response history of London hotels listed on the site at the time of data collection are downloaded.<sup>1</sup> We collect all available information about customer reviews and managerial responses, including numerical ratings, review titles and text, reviewer identities, review date, response date, response text, and responder identity, as well as hotel information including overall customer ratings, star ratings, and the number of rooms. The raw data set contains 813,287 reviews and 368,758 mana gerial responses of 1063 London hotels over a period of approximately 15 years (December 2001–March 2016).

<sup>1</sup> At the time of data collection (March 2016), there were 1063 hotels in London on the site. The sample is restricted to hotels with formal accommo dation and full services and excludes other types of accommodation, such as special lodgings, holiday rentals, B&Bs, and inns to maintain data consistency and eliminate possible impacts of firm types. The sample ranges from the very first review of each hotel to the latest one at the end of data collection period.

Data cleaning starts by removing duplicate records generated in the automated data collection process. Next, 26 hotels with null reviews are removed. The cut-off date is set to be the end of February 2016 due to data collected for March are incomplete. Furthermore, to prepare for the text analysis, language detection is conducted on response text and 22,492 records with non-English responses are excluded. The final dataset includes 765,082 online reviews and 339,123 managerial re sponses, with the oldest review dated in December 2001 and the most recent in February 2016.

## 4.2. Text analysis

Text analysis is performed on response content to analyse its textual features. In pre-processing the response text, stop words are removed and all characters are decapitalised. Data are then imported into Rap idMiner Studio, an industry‑leading open-source platform for data mining, machine learning, and predictive analytics [48]. Corresponding to the comprehensiveness, standardisation, and valence of firm engagement in the research framework, the text mining follows three steps to measure the length, degree of content similarity, and sentiment of response messages, respectively.

## 4.2.1. Document representation

Response text is tokenised into individual terms to reduce document complexity and transform unstructured text data into a quantitative format. To be more specific, each response’s full text is split into a sequence of tokens describing the document content [49]. Among several options for separating a document text, such as delimiters, nonletters, specified characters, named entities, regular expressions, and linguistic sentences or tokens, we use non-letter characters (e.g., whitespace, punctuation) as splitting points, resulting in tokens at the word level. As a result, the number of tokens represents each response’s word count, which is taken as response length.

## 4.2.2. Text similarity

We evaluate standardisation by comparing similarity in the content of all responses belonging to individual hotel. If a firm provides the same or very similar responses, it can be deduced that responses are highly standardised and vice versa. A vector space model using tf-idf (term frequency-inverse document frequency) schema is created to determine the similarity between documents. Within each hotel, we calculate the cosine similarity in each pair of responses – the resulting similarity matrix indicates how related two response documents are by measuring the cosine of the angle between them on a multidimensional vector space, with each token having an axis. The similarity score is bounded between 0 and 1, where a greater value indicates a smaller angle be tween the documents, implying that two documents share more words in common; in other words, they appear more similar in content. Each response has multiple similarity scores in relation to other response from the same hotel, and the values are averaged to obtain a mean similarity score for each response text. The entire sample of managerial responses presents a low level of content similarity, with an average of 0.078.

## 4.2.3. Sentiment analysis

To detect the emotional orientation embedded in managerial re sponses, we carry out a machine learning-based sentiment analysis to determine the positivity and negativity in responding. To start with, a training dataset is constructed by randomly selecting 350 response ex amples and manually assigning a ‘positive’ or ‘negative’ label to each response using principles explained in developing the fifth set of hy potheses. We build a classification model using a linear support vector machine (SVM) learner with ten-fold cross-validation. The performance of the SVM learner is satisfactory, with 96.0% accuracy, 96.6% preci sion, and an overall F-measure of 97.7%.<sup>2</sup> The trained model is applied to all vectored text, and each response is labelled as ‘positive’ or ‘negative’ with a confidence score ranging from 0 to 1. The confidence score indicates the strength of prediction. We record the confidence level for positivity as the measure of response sentiment, with a higher score indicating a positive valence. The average sentiment level in the sample reaches 0.686, with the lowest at 0.075 and the highest at 0.951.

## 4.3. Variables and model specification

We aggregate reviews and responses to a monthly level (t) based on review dates and organise all the data in a hotel-month panel. Table 2 presents a description of variables and summary statistics. The depen dent variable is online ratings (Rating), measured as the average customer rating score of hotel i in period t to capture customer engagement valence. Rating is modelled as a function of two groups of independent variables. The first group includes descriptors of manage rial response features, which are response ratio (Ratio), average days taken to respond (Days), the average length of response text (Length), the average similarity scores of response messages (Standardisation), and the average sentiment score (Sentiment) of hotel i in period t-1. Days and Length appear to have extreme values and are thus winsorized at the 99th percentile to reduce the outlier effect. These two variables are logarithm-transformed to achieve close-to-normal distributions. The other explanatory variables involve descriptors of the review status of hotel i accumulated in all periods before t, including the moving average (Valence) and moving standard deviation (Variability) of ratings.

Our sample covers 1037 hotels across 171 months. The mean of monthly rating and the moving average of ratings is 3.559 and 3.508, both with negative skewness (− 0.843 and − 0.683), indicating re viewers are generally generous in rating on this site. The mean of monthly response ratio across all panels is 22.1%, and the mean of monthly average responding speed is 17.13 days after reviews being posted. Regarding the textual features, responses contain 88 words on average and the overall content similarity is at a low level. The average sentiment score is 0.658, showing a relatively positive tone in responding. Correlations among variables are checked using Pearson correlation coefficients (Table 3). The majority of variables are corre lated at a 0.05 significance level, with the highest correlation coefficient of 0.740.

In view of the time lag between a managerial response and a review post, and eWOM effects are often cumulative and last for several weeks [50], we adopt one-month lagged explanatory variables, based on the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion statis tics, to examine their relationship to the following period’s customer ratings. The analysis uses the timestamps of review posts, and hence the response variables exhibit hotel i’s responses to time t-1’s reviews. Next, to rule out multicollinearity, the variance inflation factor (VIF) is examined, and results show that all VIF scores are less than 1.50 (see Table 3), well below the common threshold. Accordingly, multi collinearity is not an issue in the model. Furthermore, all variables stationarity is checked with two Fisher-type unit-root tests (i.e., the Dickey-Fuller test and Phillips–Perron test). Results provide sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis of unit roots, suggesting that all variables are stationary at all panels.

A few endogeneity issues need to be addressed. First, the inherent service quality and managerial expertise affect the level of customer satisfaction. A higher star-rated and larger-scale hotel is more likely to implement managerial response strategies and has better customer rat ings than a small-size and lower-ranked hotel. Two hotel characters $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ Star – hotel star class, LnSize , − number of rooms) are included to control for time-invariant hotel specific factors.<sup>3</sup> Second, there might also be unobservable contemporaneous quality investments $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , a major improvement in service quality and firm capabilities) or a service failure crisis that can cause changes in customer ratings and firm response behaviour. Variations in ratings can potentially reveal such shocks, and therefore we use the two review descriptors with moving values to account for the time-varying hotel specific factors. In addition, the panel data have an inherent hierarchical structure. The hotel-month observations of review and responses (level 1) are nested in firms (level 2). To isolate effects on ratings caused by the unobservable hotel-level heterogeneity and possible time effects varying across hotels, we adopt a multilevel model, which accommodates more than one level of clustering. The model includes both fixed and random effects and allows for variation between groups and estimation of group effects [51]. ## 5. Findings

## 5.1. Descriptive statistics

Across the sampled 1037 hotels, 734 hotels (70.78%) provide managerial responses. Response provision by hotels differs with respect to star class and rating levels (see Table 4). Hotels of a higher star class tend to be more active in responding than those that are lower-rated. Over 90% of 4- and 5-star hotels offer responses to more than half of the online reviews they receive. Approximately 77% of 3-star hotels respond, but the response rate is almost halved compared to that of luxury hotels. The number of responding hotels is smaller at the 3-star level or below, and a limited number of responses are provided. In terms of customer ratings, hotels with an average score of 4 and 4.5 are most active in responding to online reviews (87.43%). Compared to them, a fewer number of 5-score (63.64%) and 3/3.5-score rated hotels (67.47%) engage in responding, and the latter group’s response rate drops by 20%. Very few hotels rated below three respond to online reviews, and the response ratio is significantly lower than higher-rated hotels. A possible reason for above-average hotels being more engaged than below-average hotels is their willingness to make an effort to achieve high ratings by doing what the community values and the management expertise and resources that enable the responding to be implemented to a high standard.

We take a further look at whether hotels tend to respond to a specific type of review. Results show that high-quality hotels (4-star and above) treat positive and negative reviews equally. ## 5.2. Effects of managerial responses

The empirical examination starts with testing the effect of response variables individually to determine if each variable alone is associated with future ratings. Results show that response ratio, speed, and senti ment are strong predictors of subsequent ratings, while response length and standardisation are not. Marginal significance is detected for the quadratic term of response length, indicating a possible inverted Ushape relationship.<sup>4</sup> Next, response variables are tested in combination, and the significance of the five response attributes is consistent with when individual variables are used, except the disappearing effect of squared LnLength.

Reading the results in Column (6) of Table 5. response ratio has a significant positive influence on ratings in the subsequent period, sug gesting that in two extreme cases where hotels either provide no re sponses at all or respond to all reviews posted in the past month, responding is likely to increase the current monthly average rating by

0.026. Clear evidence is also found for a positive association between response sentiment and future ratings, showing that an increase of 0.1 in the average sentiment scores is associated with a 0.0137 increase in average ratings in the following period. LnDays is negatively related to future ratings, meaning that a shorter interval between reviews and responses is favourable. A 10% decrease in the monthly averaged response days is associated with an increase in average ratings of 0.0019. The observed effect has a strong statistical significance, though the effect size appears small.

It is hypothesised that the effect of various response attributes on future ratings can vary with the nature of reviews. As shown in Table 5, the inclusion of interaction terms between responses and reviews vari ables does not shift the main effects of the five response variables. Re sults in Column (7) demonstrate the effects of response ratio, days, and sentiment are analogous to those obtained in the tests without the interaction term. Concerning the potential moderating effects of review factors, review valence cumulatively averaged over time is found to be prominent but not review variability. More specifically, review valence negatively moderates the positive effect of Ratio and Sentiment, sug gesting that response ratio and sentiment tend to have a weaker positive influence when high overall ratings are formed, while their marginal effects on future ratings can be more significant if the current ratings are low. Besides, review valence yields a marginally significant positive moderation effect on response days, which implies the negative effect of response days is mitigated when prior ratings are high but worse in a reverse situation. Results of hypothesis testing are summarised in Table 6.

Furthermore, it is not surprising that the rating in time t is pre dominantly predicted by the ratings established in all periods before t, while the variability of previous ratings has a negative but not signifi cant relationship with future ratings. In addition, in a multilevel model, the random variables (i.e., hotel and time in this study) are used to capture the variation between groups. By interpreting the random effect parameters in the last two columns in Table 5, it is found that the hotel level variance is 0.014 and variance of time slope is approximately zero. Hence, only 7.74% (variance at time and hotel levels divided by the total variance) of the variance in ratings can be attributed to differences be tween hotels. The remaining observed effects are at the observation level (monthly review and response) within individual hotels.

For mid-range hotels. the main effects of managerial responses are consistent with base results and the moderating role of review valence is only detected for response ratio and days. In contrast, while the effect of response speed remains strong, response ratio and sentiment become trivial for luxury and budget hotels. An interesting observation is the divergent effect of response standardisation for hotels in the high- and low-end markets. Firms in the upscale range are typically rated high by customers, so it is likely that customers will not react sceptically to a standardised responding approach or even interpret it as a consistent and highly organised management practice. While standardised re sponses appear preferable for luxury hotels, budget hotels see the importance of tailoring responses to reviews. A possible reason is that low-end hotels may receive more critical reviews than hotels in higherend and whether the complaints and criticism are appropriately addressed and responded to via online platforms has a greater impact on ratings, according to our previous discussion on the possible moderation effect of review valence. Thus, low-end hotels need to customise re sponses to deal with raised issues and generate payback in ratings.

## 6. General discussions and managerial implications

Our work offers important implications for practising service man agers in engaging in online communications with customers. First, our results strongly support that response ratio, speed, and sentiment have significant associations with future ratings, and firms are advised to pay attention to these aspects that are most influential in the firm–customer communications. Responding in a frequent, prompt, and positive way advocates firms’ professionalism in engaging customers and hence builds trust and influence in the online network. The magnitude of impact varies with firms’ competitive positions in the market. While mid-range hotels need to keep an eye on the three vital elements, budget hotels are advised to act fast and tailor response messages, especially when prior ratings are sparse and polarised.

Promptness appears to be a priority and is constantly crucial in diverse contexts. In our sample, the average monthly response days have a mean of 17 and a median of 7. If responding speeds up from the mean to the median point, the average rating in a month is expected to in crease by approximate 0.012 scores. For response ratio and sentiment, responding approaches can be adjusted based on firms’ eWOM quality. In the situation where firms are experiencing more negative reviews in general, raising response ratio and showing a positive attitude is more effective in improving subsequent ratings compared to the situation in which the current ratings are already relatively positive. By responding in a proactive, polite and responsible way, firms can take advantage of online channels to perform service recovery, which lessens the destructive effects of unfavourable eWOM and offers the public an image of a responsible firm. There are, however, two noteworthy points. It is critical to determine how fast firms should respond to complaints. Im mediate actions may not always be ideal. Firms may fail to fully address issues raised in reviews, which can lead to an impression of firms only making a perfunctory effort. The right timing will depend on the nature of concerns expressed in reviews and possible solutions to solve the problems. The other matter needing attention is potential loss caused by overuse of upbeat language in responses. An overwhelmingly positive response can be perceived as insincere marketing behaviour. Customers may question whether the responses with a great deal of unction have substantial implications for a firm’s offline investment. Hence, responding should be positive but not rhetorical.

In addition to confirming firms’ impact in an online interaction network and customers appreciate firms engaging in a regular, prompt, and polite manner, we do not confidently conclude that response length and standardisation are in connection with future ratings, given that estimations about these two variables are not statistically significant across models. Although it is hypothesised that longer and tailored responses are potentially informative and demonstrate managerial will ingness to write proper replies, the presumed uncertainty reduction advantage associated with these two attributes is not discovered. One possible reason is that the review site shows only the first three lines of a response if customers do not click on the link ‘read more’ to unfold the rest of the messages. In this case, customers are less affected by the detailed textual content of managerial responses and thus hardly react to how long and similar responses are. There is only a shred of evidence offering indications of likely influence. The anticipated negative asso ciation between response standardisation and future ratings is observed for budget hotels. It is speculated that low-quality hotels may face a challenging eWOM environment, and customers looking for economical options care about whether and how managerial responses address specific comments. Accordingly, firms in the low end of the market should put extra effort into tailoring response messages. In contrast, this constraint can be eased for luxury hotels. With a standardised approach being preferable, high-end players should systematise responding to showcase their expertise in customer service and relationship management.

## 7. Theoretical contributions and future research

Customers frequently engage in online communities and platforms to voice their experience and views. While engagement continues to accumulate new research streams [1,2], an investigation into how firms effectively engage in online interactions is a notable shortcoming of the literature. Standing on a network perspective, the present study dis cusses firm engagement with a focus on its power in influencing customer attitudes and sentiment in their online engagement. Cus tomers’ sentimental engagement is a vital part of firm performance, yet no optimal approaches have been identified for firms to engage in online platforms [10]. We empirically examine the mechanism and magnitude of firms’ online engagement and obtain quantified evidence for advan tages brought by engagement endeavour and appropriate approaches [7].

This study extends the line of research on online firm-customer in teractions by examining both the behavioural and textual cues in firm engagement and their dynamics in a time series. Unlike earlier research relying on an experimental design (e.g., [26,31]), we gather longitudinal data on customers reviews and managerial responses in 15 years to deliver field evidence on the efficacy of firm engagement. A mixedmethod approach integrating empirical applications and text analytics proves valuable in deriving insights from both structured and unstruc tured data to feed into operations and decision making [52]. Mining textual content of managerial responses offers a new angle to look into the multifaceted issue, such as content similarity and response valence, which yields novel insights into the online firm engagement.

Further, going beyond a focus on the regime change from nonresponding to responding or a sole dimension of managerial responses in prior research (e.g., [11,12,25]), we incorporate multiple dimensions of firm engagement and examine their effects simultaneously to deter mine the relative importance. Considering the interplay between online reviews and managerial responses, their combined effects on future ratings are estimated with an attempt to elucidate how to respond given different eWOM scenarios in terms of review valence and variability. Doing so allows us to depict a clearer picture of which specific element of responses engenders an impact on future ratings and advise on how to respond to and manage online reviews in certain circumstances (not limited to customer complaints).

Several limitations should be acknowledged, addressing which pro vides avenues for future research. First, we focus on firms’ online ac tivities but cannot access unobservable offline activities, such as renovation and time-varying managerial expertise. The potential endo geneity concerns are mitigated by including firm-specific quality factor and using multilevel models to capture individual firm and time influ ence. This approach, however, does not fully account for variations in ratings that are led by individual consumption experience with the respective hotel; thus, the results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies may conduct a customer level analysis or include addi tional indicators of individuals to allay this concern.

Second, the research is designed to use London hotels’ reviews and responses on a single digital platform to ensure the compatibility of institutional environment and platform practice. Future studies should seek to disentangle the geographical, platform, and timeframe con straints herein to gain additional insights and extend the generalizability of findings. In particular, results should be carefully evaluated when generalising to other platforms. TripAdvisor organises reviews in chro nological order, with the most recent reviews appearing first. Customer can add filters to read a proportion of the entire review repository but cannot sort reviews by relevance and other attributes. In this setting, it is sensible to assume customers are exposed to reviews in a predominantly time-sensitive way. However, there are platforms displaying reviews in alternative sequences, such as by relevance or ratings. That being the case, users may be exposed to and thus influenced by reviews in a completely different manner, and the research setting should modify accordingly to take into account how the reviews are shown on the platforms.

Third, this research does not discover a strong effect of response length and standardisation on future ratings. It is speculated that the specific textual content and details of messages may be more accentu ated in influencing existing customers’ future satisfaction but not that of bystanders. We identify reviewers who have provided multiple reviews on the same hotel as returning customers, but our sample is insufficient to produce meaningful estimations. Future research is encouraged to obtain extra data on returnees to examine the potential impact of the textual features of responses on experienced customers or reviewers. Additional dimensions such as emotions and entities can be incorpo rated to dive deeper into responses’ textual and linguistic features. Fourth, we discussed in the theoretical development that there might exist two occasions when firms’ response actions can influence observing customers. While our research design does not allow for locating the precise time point where the influence occurs, future studies can propose an experiment to further decode the impact at pre- and postpurchase stages and compare influential magnitudes or mechanisms to determine the importance of factors at different phases.

Lastly, this study’s theoretical underpinning advocates the social and collective nature of engagement in a network environment. Our findings confirm that online interactions and presence spill over into observers other than focal participants. This suggests additional questions, such as whether any influence generated by the enhanced observability exists among firms and between firms and platforms and what implications will be for firms’ digital practices. Future effort can be devoted to empirically testing the networked effect, focusing on a broader range of actors and their social interactions.
