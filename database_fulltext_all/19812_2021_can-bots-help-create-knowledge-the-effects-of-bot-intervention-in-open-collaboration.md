# Can bots help create knowledge? The effects of bot intervention in open collaboration

Seonjun Kang <sup>a</sup>, Xiaojin (Jim) Liu <sup>b</sup>, Yeongin Kim <sup>a</sup>, Victoria Yoon a,\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, VA 23284, United States of America

<sup>b</sup> Department of Supply Chain Management and Analytics, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, VA 23284, United States of America

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Bots Algorithmic intervention Open collaboration Difference in differences

## A B S T R A C T

Many open collaboration platforms (e.g., Wikipedia) have recently utilized automated algorithmic agents, called Bots. to solve the issue of stagnating user participation. However, understanding of how such a bot agent affects user activities in open collaboration is limited. In this study, we examine (i) whether bot intervention affects user participation and (ii) whether the impact of bot intervention could vary by characteristics of open collaboration works. We pursue our research goals by utilizing a rich dataset from between 2005 and 2017 from an online open collaboration platform. The platform has recently utilized two types of unique bots that interact with human users in open collaboration. By employing difference-in-differences approach, we show that bot intervention in open collaboration leads to unintended consequences as it significantly demotivates user participation. We further show that the negative effect of bot intervention is alleviated if more active users are engaged or if covered topics have a higher concentration in a collaboration work. This provides directions for businesses to resolve the unintended demotivation problem in utilizing bot agents.

## 1. Introduction

Open collaboration platforms, such as Wikipedia, allow any indi vidual users to provide small fragments of content for knowledge collaboration works that gradually add up to a final outcome. The openness and decentralized ownership of contents in the platforms have greatly attracted more users to participate, which illustrates the rapid formation of their user base. The accessibility to vast amounts of knowledge built on a huge user base has led to ever increasing depen dence on the knowledge collaboration platforms in our daily life and businesses. In 2021, Wikipedia itself marked more than 5,000,000 article edits and 440,000 editors in its English version alone monthly [1].

However, despite the continuing success, many of those platforms have faced growth stagnation for the past decade. The decreasing user participation has challenged many open collaboration platforms. In fact, a large portion of user-contributed contents remains incomplete or falls short of community standards by the presence of various types of un wanted contents, such as inaccurate and off-topic postings. The man agement of collaboration works in the platforms depends critically on the voluntary participation of users, but the decline in their user base makes this very challenging and prompts them to seek solutions.

Bot agents (bots hereafter), one type of automated algorithmic agents in facilitating online knowledge creation, have been increasingly adopted by many platforms and may have the potential to address the user participation issue. Bots in open collaboration are programmed software agents designed to support various activities of human users in the production of collaborative knowledge [2]. For example, Wikipedia. com has introduced bots to counter vandalism and recommend tasks to potential contributors [3]. Reddit.com has implemented a bot to support moderators who carry out regulation tasks such as addressing illegal content, copyright violations, and terrorism [4]. Utilizing such an algorithmic agent in open collaboration holds significant promise as they can extend the human capability of tracking the collaboration process and identifying potential participation opportunities. Thus, it is expected that the implementation of such algorithmic agents can sup port user activities in the production of knowledge contents.

Despite the aforementioned issues that call for immediate attention to the open collaboration platforms, the question of how the bots affect user participation still remains unanswered. Findings in the literature are limited to case studies with a small sample size or relatively short observation period. Furthermore, the existing studies lack a specific type of bot intervention that might have a sufficient impact on the collabo ration process and therefore, it is insufficient to draw a conclusion [5–7]. We extend the research on the impact of bot intervention in open collaboration by utilizing a rich panel dataset in a unique and relevant research context. The objective of this study is to examine whether the bot interventions increase or decrease the user participation in indi vidual collaboration works. In order to obtain more nuanced insights into the bot intervention effect, we also aim to examine what factors of collaboration work moderate the effect of bot intervention.

We pursue our research goals by utilizing data from between 2005 and 2017 from an open knowledge collaboration platform - wikiHow. The platform has utilized several bots to maintain and support user activities in knowledge collaboration. Votebot allows users to vote on the order and importance of content items on a collaboration work (i.e. article) and reorders the items based on the votes. Stubbot directs users attention by flagging articles that need more edits and contributions to pass the quality check. Votebot and Stubbot were introduced on a large scale during December 2012 and July 2014, respectively. The platform has also implemented other bots, but the two focal bots in our study distinguish themselves from the others: (i) the two bots were developed by the platform’s administrator and were designed to implement pro grammed rules into infrastructure, constraining the work of users (e.g., screening articles based on quality, selecting contents to show based on votes, etc.); (ii) the location of their activities are shown on the frontstage of articles where human users are involved in general; (iii) rather than performing repetitive tasks that usually do not require feedback, these bots induce users to participate more actively by requiring users’ responses. These unique features of the two focal bots provide more relevant research settings for the purpose of our research on how the bot agent as a facilitator would change the way human users contribute to knowledge collaboration works.

We aggregate the revision edits on a weekly basis for our analysis. We identify the starting week of interventions by the two bots on arti cles, and then employ a difference-in-differences (DID) model to examine the effect of the bot interventions on user participation measured by the number of unique users participating in the knowledge collaboration work. We also examine the impact of bot intervention that could be moderated by characteristics of open collaboration articles. We consider two moderators – active users and topical concentration – to examine the varying impact of bot interventions, where active users is measured by the number of actively participating users (developed from users’ revision intensity), and topical concentration is measured by the distribution of topics being frequently covered at an article page at a given time (developed from the text context using a machine learning technique).

Our results show that bot intervention overall has a negative impact on user participation in the open collaboration works in the platform. In specific, user participation significantly reduces after bot intervention is present on pages. Further analysis shows that active users and topical concentration moderate the relationship between bot intervention and user participation. When there is an increased presence of either more active users or greater topical concentration of fewer topics on each page, the negative impact of bot intervention on user participation is reduced. These results suggest that the implementation of algorithmic agents like bots in open collaboration platforms may lead to unintended consequences as it demotivates user participation. Being aware of these negative effects, platform managers need to take a proactive approach and develop necessary supporting channels or mechanisms to enhance user retention in articles. We further reveal that the degree of the un: intended consequence is less in articles with more active users involved and fewer topics being covered. This implies that managers may need to encourage active users to interact more with those newly joined or infrequent users. In addition, it would be valuable for managers to better guide content contribution by recommending that one article should concentrate on a few focused topics rather than many diverse contents.

Our study broadly contributes to the literature on bot intervention in open collaboration. First, our study complements the current work on the understanding of the role of algorithmic agents in various settings and on different platforms. Although algorithmic agents may increase productivity in repetitive and laborious work, our study provides strong evidence that bot interventions can also have a negative effect on pro ductivity by reducing user participation. Second, we contribute to a better understanding of the boundary conditions for the algorithmic agents by identifying the varying effects on bot interventions by the number of active users and topical concentration. Both can help reduce the negative impact of bot interventions. Thus, we contribute to recent work that has examined interventions and approaches that businesses might employ to motivate greater production of online knowledge collaboration, and to resolve the unintended demotivation problem in the utilization of bots.

This paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we briefly review the literature on open collaboration and bots, following which we postulate the effects of bot interventions on user participation, and the moderating effects of active users and content concentration. In the subsequent section, we describe our empirical context, introduce our empirical analysis strategy, and present the results. Finally, we discuss our contribution to the literature and managerial implications.

## 2. Background and related literature

## 2.1. Open collaboration and bots

Open collaboration is a process that allows users to leverage their differences in interests, concerns, and knowledge to share, transfer, recombine, and reuse knowledge among parties [8]. With the wide spread popularity of internet technologies, such as social media, user generated contents, and the wiki, there has been an increase in knowl edge collaboration in online communities [9]. The unique characteris tics of open knowledge collaboration in online communities are the self organization in the absence of role definitions and the lack of traditional structural mechanisms and stable membership [10,11]. Since content production in open collaboration depends largely on the voluntary engagement of general users, it is critical for the platforms to maintain a sufficient user base to achieve sustainable success [11,12]. Previous studies have identified various internal and external factors affecting user engagement in open collaboration, such as personal motivation [13,14], participants pool [11,15–18], and content characteristics [19–21]. Complementing this stream of work, we investigate another source for user engagement– the role of bots.

Bot agents are computer programs that carry out tasks on behalf of human users [22]. Possessing the property of autonomy, an agent operates without the direct intervention of humans and has some control over its own actions and internal state [23]. An agent hence does not simply act in response to its environment; it is able to exhibit goaldirected behavior by taking the initiative. Depending upon their func tionality, agents can be classified as mobile agents which migrate from a computer to others autonomously [24], multiagents [23] which collaborate with each other to achieve their goals, algorithmic agents which performs a sequence of well-defined, computer-executable in structions to solve logical or computational problems [25], and others.

With the recent advance of web technology, there has been a huge increase in the adoption of bots for many online services such as chatbot for online conversation via text [26], spider bot as a web crawler [27], shop bot as a shopping assistant [28], social bot for social media [29], votebot [30], and stubbot for expanding incomplete articles in Wikipe dia [31]. Bots have proliferated and diversified well beyond human ability to support various human activities for online services [32]. Many open collaboration platforms have implemented bots in an effort to support user participation, and bots are now responsible for a sig nificant portion of contribution to knowledge collaboration [32]. This stream of research, while insightful, lacks a clear view on how bots affect user participation.

We synthesize the insights from the literature on online open knowledge collaboration [15,33,34] and intelligent agents [2,35]. Thus, our work lies in the intersection of these two streams of literature, which provides relevant theoretical background for our hypothesis develop ment. As part of our research model, we further study the boundary conditions of the effect of bots on user participation by looking at characteristics related to users and page content. In this regard, we contribute to the literature by generating deeper understanding the ef fect of bots and the relevant conditions.

## 2.2. Hypothesis development

There are several studies that examine the impact of intervention by bots in the process of open collaboration, but their findings are mixed. On one hand, the literature reports benefits of bot intervention in knowledge collaboration, such as improving communication between users, better governing agents, and evaluating a mass amount of collaboration works in a short time [6,35]. The production process in knowledge collaboration involves users complying with norms and standards established by consensus within the community [33]. Con tributors are required to consider how much and what information to include, and how much emphasis to put on information previously generated [34]. The information generated through the algorithmic tools can facilitate the way users process information to identify their roles in peer production, which could motivate user participation [33,34].

On the other hand. however. the literature also provides evidence that bot intervention may demotivate user participation and lead to unintended consequences. Bot intervention embeds programmed rules into infrastructure, which could conflict with the norms among the human users. For examples, several wiki platforms, such as Wikipedia and wikiHow, have adopted bots that conduct algorithmic quality checks of the works made by human users. As such a quality check done by bot agents, not by human users. changes the nature of the principle in knowledge collaboration “Wisdom of Crowds”, users may resist this change [5,35,36]. In addition, automated works implemented by bot agents may reduce individual users’ cognitive rewards in the process of knowledge collaboration. Users provide fragments of contents for collaboration works by exertion. Such an effort is considered costly, but users can also enjoy efforts for its own sake as efforts can offer intrinsic value during the process of knowledge collaboration. This effort paradox implies that users may place a lower value on effort and seeking it out under automation by bot implementation [37]. This consequence can be exacerbated with false assessments made by bots as false assessments may require contributors to expend efforts assessing the accuracy of the assessment [35]. Furthermore, bot intervention reduces users’ chances of going through social interactions among users, a key process in open collaboration. Users become more deeply involved with the community through social interactions. such as leaving comments in a shared workspace with other users, which further motivates them to contribute to the community [35,38,39]. Bot agents are generally not recognized as social agents by human users [40,41]. Lastly, the algorithmic interven tion may create a new stimulus that participants need to pay attention to and enforce them to seek for new routines to contribute, leading to additional burden to the participants.

This mixed evidence could be attributed to the fact that they are limited to case studies with a small sample size or relatively short observation period. More importantly, the existing studies have a lack of specific type of bot intervention that might have sufficient impact on the collaboration process. The types of bots studied in the literature are mostly designed to substitute repetitive and laborious activities of human users and the outcomes of using these bots can be highly predictable due to their lack of interaction with human users. Human users tend to have a stronger reaction to bot intervention (both nega tively and positively), when the bot is managed by the platform’s administrator, acting front-stage, and with a high degree of user au tonomy in responding bot works, could be referred to as “policy bot” [2]. In this study, we consider two policy bots – Votebot and Stubbot – implemented by the platform and examine their impact on the user participation by utilizing a rich panel dataset. Based on the mixed evi dence discussed above, we build competing hypotheses on the impact of bot intervention as follows.

Hypothesis 1A. Bot intervention is positively associated with user participation.

Hypothesis 1B. Bot intervention is negatively associated with user participation.

We also examine what factors of collaboration articles moderate the effect of bot intervention. Previous literature has shown that the out comes bot agents generate may not be homogeneous across the subjects to which they are applied. The unequal outcome could be attributed to the way in which the subject is perceived, such as the understanding and recognition of the outcome of the users who utilize the subject [42]. We consider two characteristics of collaboration works – distribution of active users and topical concentration in a collaboration article– that might moderate the impact of the bot intervention on user participation.

Social interactions play a critical role for users, especially inexperi enced users, in an open collaboration as users receive information about norms and standards of the community through social interaction in the online environment that depends critically on the voluntary participa tion of users [35,38,39]. Bot intervention can be disruptive to the pro cess of socialization in open collaboration as the algorithmic laws embedded in the bot agents can come into conflict with the contextual and social rules of human [39,41] and bot agents can be seen simply as content agents or managerial protocols rather than social agents [40,41]. The interface of individual collaboration articles not only serves as a venue for users to contribute to the community, but also as a venue for social activities among users. In this regard, we expect that the positive (negative) effect of bot intervention increases (decreases) when it is applied to the articles where there is more need for socialization. Active users in our context refer to the proportion of users who not only contributed to an article but also actively participated to the community in a recent period. We use active users as a proxy for the need of so cialization among the users in a collaboration work.

Algorithmic agents, such as bots, perform a sequence of rules defined by humans for a machine to follow. The rules which an algorithmic agent follows include human expectation of the outcome and the way the outcome is produced by the bot. When algorithmic intervention applied to an uncertain context, the utilization of the outcome produced by the agent could leave ineffective [42]. Topical concentration refers to the degree to which an article is represented by a small group of topics among the others being covered in the article. Because of the openness and the decentralized ownership nature of open collaboration platforms, the lack of convergence in article contents can create vague or even incorrect lines between different those individuals who converge on an idea and those who do not [11,43]. Having more distinct topics can increase the range and depth of information that contributors need to access and act upon [44]. Therefore, we expect that the positive (negative) effect of bot intervention increases (decreases) when it is applied to articles with more-concentrated topical distribution as the lack of convergence could lead to a reduction of reinforcement process for continued collaboration that the bot agents try to facilitate. Based on our discussion above, we build additional set of hypotheses as follows.

Hypothesis 2A. The positive (negative) effect of bot intervention in creases (decreases) when it is applied to articles with more active users engaged.

Hypothesis 2B. The positive (negative) effect of bot intervention in creases (decreases) when it is applied to articles with more-concentrated topical distribution.

## 3. Data

The dataset collected from wikiHow includes both bot interventions and user participation activities on knowledge collaboration articles. WikiHow is an online wiki-style community providing information on how to do, in terms of the process knowledge. Articles in wikiHow are categorized under many topics. However, we restrict the articles to those related to healthcare. Our dataset makes the study setting appropriate to investigate the impact of bot intervention on knowledge collaboration. In articles under such a category, users might be more deeply involved in collaboration as a higher level of expertise and qualification is required to make meaningful contribution. Moreover, different from other knowledge collaboration platforms on explicit facts, such as Wikipedia, the process sharing knowledge relates more to “Know-How”. The tacit knowledge requires a user has a deeper understanding of the process on how to solve problem. Furthermore, the platform is operated for social goods while supported by a for-profit company, which intends to benefit a larger population of online audience while maintains a positive rela tionship with contributors (i.e., users and financial supporters). The platform thus promotes user contributions to both popular common contents and important domain contents. Overall, these characteristics of our data will help us observe more evident effects of bot intervention in open knowledge collaboration.

The platform has introduced several types of bots over time to fulfill different types of tasks. However, we are particularly interested in two bots among them – Votebot and Stubbot (see examples in Fig. 1). Votebot updates the order of contents in a knowledge collaboration article based on voting (e.g., thumbs up or thumbs down) by the users. This inter vention is done automatically based on the votes of actual readers to reflect the public opinion about the contents. On the other hand, Stubbot automatically identifies articles that fail to meet the platform’s stan dards and flags them as potentially incomplete. Although these bots are different in their purposes and functionalities, they are both categorized as “policy bots” as they have the following characteristics in common [2]; (i) the two bots are controlled by administrators, constraining the user contribution (e.g., screening articles based on quality, selecting contents to show based on votes, etc.); (ii) acting front-stage (i.e., work in article space); (iii) with a high degree of autonomy (i.e., evaluating the work of users before acting). Votebot, one of the focal bots in our study, became active on a large scale during December 2012. Later, Stubbot, another bot, was introduced in July 2014. These bots are introduced at the platform level, which can be plausible exogenous events to the public users on the platform. At earlier stages, the imple mentation of the bots was limited to a small scale in a few specific knowledge collaboration projects. Later, the use of bots became more active.

To study the effect of bot interventions on user participation in in dividual pages, we collect detailed revision data for each health-related article pages (pages hereafter), through the API provided by the website (accessed in April 2017). The data covers over 5399 unique pages with 203,687 edit revisions created from January 2005 to March 2017. For each revision, we retrieve the text content of the revision, the contrib utors’ user ID (or IP address if the user is not logged in), and the time stamp of contribution by processing the raw data with original XML format. At the same time, we download the list of user IDs that belong to the “bot user” account group, following the literature [21]. The list enables us to identify the bot accounts and their edit activities in the revision data. Bots are widely used on the platform, starting in 2011. We find that 29.83% of edits on health care related pages were edited by bots. The initial transformed data is organized in a table for each revision. We then clean the text content (by removing stop words, removing none letter characters, and stemming the words), and compute for several metrics used in our study (details are provided in the subsequent sections). After that, we aggregate the revision data into a panel dataset by pages over weeks. Our final sample includes 5399 pages over 628 weeks with 106,246 unbalanced page-week observations.

## 3.1. Measures

## 3.1.1. Dependent variable

To assess the impact of bot interventions on user participation, we follow the recent studies and obtain the users’ edits and contributions by parsing the revision history of each page [19]. Particularly, we develop the measure of number of users by counting the number of unique users who contributed to the revision of the page in a week. This measure is critical to capture the dynamics of users’ activities through the page’s growth and change. To reduce the effect of the skewness of distribution, we transform the variable by log(1 + n).

## 3.1.2. Independent variables

One of the main independent variable in our analysis on user participation is postVotebot, which refers to an indicator that captures the earliest event by the Votebot intervention on a page. This indicator takes the value of 1 when the first Votebot intervention event is present in a page’s revision history in a week, and for every subsequent weeks following event, and 0 otherwise. In our study, the treatment is the Votebot intervention, and the treated subjects are pages with Votebot interventions. Similar measures are used in the literature to study the treatment effect when subjects are treated at different time points.

Similar to the Votebot intervention, we develop the measure for Stubbot intervention, postStubbot by creating an indicator that captures the earliest event by the Stubbot intervention on a page. This indicator takes the value of 1 when the first Stubbot intervention event is present in a page’s revision history in a week, and for every subsequent weeks following event, and 0 otherwise. In this case, the treatment is the Stubbot intervention, and the treated subjects are pages with Stubbot interventions.

To study the conditions that can change how bot intervention affects user participation, we include two moderators in our analysis. The first moderator is active users, which is measured as the number of active users present in a week who had contributed to most revisions in the project. That is, we define active users as those who have contribution experience within all the revision history on one page, following the literature [9]. Specially, based on the count of each participating users revisions on the page, we sort these users in descending order, and then calculate the ratio of revisions count to the total count. With the cu mulative ratios from top to bottom, we use a cut point of 0.8 of the cumulative ratio to identify the active and non-active users. We logtransform and demean active users in the analysis to avoid the effect of skewed distribution and multicollinearity.

The second moderator in our study is topical concentration. With all the overall page and revision content text, we first run a machine learning technique, Topic Modeling, to identify the topical coverage on all pages. Specifically, with the extracted text from the last revision per page each week, we run the topic modeling on the revision and word composition. Given the collection of unstructured text pages, topic modeling assumes that there are a certain number of latent topics distributed in all pages, and that each page contains one or multiple topics with different probabilistic proportions [45]. Assuming that each topic can be represented by different combination of words, topic modeling effectively segregate page collections into different themes or topics. It is important to know that one single page can hardly cover a large number of topics, but one or multiple topics. We check the ma chine learning results of topics range from 10 to 500, and find out that the results of the topic modeling become optimal when we set the number of topics to 30.<sup>2</sup> This number enables us to generate concise, coherent and semantically meaningful topics, indicating better con ceptual coherence with information retrieval by Deveaud et al. [46] and for adaptive LDA model selection based on a density method by Cao et al. [47]. With generated the topical distribution probability for 30 topics on a page, we then sum the proportions of top 3 topics and develop an aggregate measure to represent the concentration. The idea of concentration is similar to those used in the literature, which are often used to capture the industry sale shares by a few top players (e.g., top four) [48], top three or four dominant firms in competition [49]. A higher (lower) score would indicate that a revision has focused on fewer (more) topics.

## 4. Impact of algorithmic intervention on user participation

## 4.1. Difference-in-differences (DID) model

We use a difference-in-differences (DID) regression model to esti mate the impact of bot interventions on user participation. We formulate the regression model in the following form:

$$
y _ {p t} = \alpha_ {t} + \alpha_ {p} + \beta \cdot p o s t B o t _ {p t} + \gamma \cdot X _ {p t} + \epsilon_ {p t}\tag{1}
$$

where p indexes page and t indexes time in weeks; $y _ { p t }$ is the number of unique contributing users on page p at time $t , \alpha _ { t }$ is a vector of time fixed effects; $\alpha _ { p }$ is a vector of page fixed effects; postBot is the binary indicator for bot intervention which takes the value 1 if page p was revised by the bot (either Votebot or Stubbot) in time t, and 0 otherwise; $X _ { p t }$ is a vector of control variables. Studying the bot intervention in the treatment group compared to in the control group, we rely on the coefficient β in the DID estimation to examine the effect of bot on user participation.

## 4.2. Samples from propensity score matching

To further alley concerns that treatment and control pages may not be comparable, we apply the DID model in matched samples from the propensity score matching (PSM) technique, in which the set of treated and control pages that are statistically similar in terms of the user ac tivities and page characteristics.<sup>3</sup> We include control variables in the matching procedure for Votebot and Stubbot, separately. Our goal is to match a treatment page with a control page with similar characteristics. Thus, we aggregate page-week observations to the page level for the pretreatment weeks for the treated pages and all weeks for the potential control pages, with one year data prior the intervention. For each page, we refine the control variables by taking the average of the number of staff, revisions by the page creators, presence of other bots, and average page length. In our matching processes for the two sets of treatment by Votebot and Stubbot, we adopt the matching algorithm of one-nearest neighbor without replacement at the page level by considering all the newly constructed control variables, in order to construct a sample of matched control pages that are as similar as possible to the treated pages.

We check the matching performance for each bot, and find that the distance measure between treatment group and control group has a good overlap (Fig. 2). After the matching, the distance has a reduction in bias, and the distance of matched pages show a reduction in bias by 83.7% for Votebot, and 91.8% for Stubbot. All covariates have a reduction in bias too, and the matched covariates show a reduction in bias by over 63.7% for Votebot and 34.5% for Stubbot. Besides, we conduct Rosenbaum’s sensitivity analysis to see if our matched samples are robust to the possible presence of an unobserved factor. The result shows that Γ be comes significant at 2.3 for Votebot and 1.9 for Stubbot, suggesting no significant hidden bias in our matched samples. Additionally, we tested and visualized the difference in trend between the treatment group and the control group. We report the estimates of the difference along with 90% interval in Fig. 3. Overall, most of coefficients before the bot intervention appear to be statistically indistinguishable from zero and there is no systematic trend. With the matched samples for Votebot and Stubbot and the control variables, we run the DID analysis on the treatment effects of the Votebot and Stubbot on user participation.

## 4.3. Main results

We examine the impact of Votebot and Stubbot separately on the user participation, after we include control variables in our model. For reporting purpose, we focus on the DID analysis with the combined matched sample, developed from Propensity Score Matching. The result regarding the impact of Votebot is reported in column (2) of Table 2. It shows that Votebot has negative effects on user participation as its regression coefficient is negative $( \beta = \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 0 . 1 0 9 )$ and significant $( p <$ 0.001), which indicate that after the intervention of Votebot, there is

10.3% decrease in the number of unique user participating in a knowl edge collaboration page. We observed a similar result for Stubbot. The result reported in column (3) of Table 2 shows that intervention by Stubbot decreases user participation $( \beta = \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 0 . 0 8 4 )$ significantly $( p <$ 0.001), which mean that the implementation of Stubbot reduces user participation by 8.1%. In addition, we run a model in the unmatched sample and separately matched sample, and find similar observations that the bot intervention reduces user participation in a page. In sum, although there is some variation in the point estimate for bot inter vention across the models, we do not observe a systematic pattern to the direction of the variation, and we find the degree of the variation is small. The homogeneous trend across the models gives evidence that a smaller number of users contribute to online knowledge collaboration after the intervention by bots.

## 4.4. Robustness checks

## 4.4.1. Alternative dependent variables

In the main analysis, we use the number of unique users participated in the collaboration as the dependent variable. However, this measure may not fully capture user participation and contribution, and we run our model with three alternative measures of the dependent variable on a page: (i) number of revisions; (ii) absolute edit change; and (iii) content distance. Table 3 shows the results. We find that all coefficients of our interests are significantly negative, suggesting that the effect of bot in terventions is robust to different measures of user participation.

## 4.4.2. Results with constrained control group

In the above analysis, the treatment group includes those pages that have been revised by the bot (either Votebot or Stubbot) at some point in time, while the control group includes pages that have never been revised by these bots through the whole sample period. In this specifi cation, the possible confounding effect of other bots may threaten our identification as it prevents us from teasing out the effect of our interest. To reduce this concern, we construct a constrained control group of page-week observations, that have not been revised by any bot through the whole sample period. And we have 583 pages that follow in each of this specification of control groups. We then run a difference-in differences analysis of the data from the treatment group and more constrained control groups. The result is highly consistent as the esti mated effects of bot intervention decrease the user participation (see Table 4).

## 4.4.3. Placebo tests - randomization of bot intervention

In our analysis, we as sume that the bot intervention is plausibly exogenous, and the decision to introduce bot intervention on the platform is likely to be influenced by the overall platform-wide page content, quality control, and other platform-level factors, rather than by the individual user or the single page. We randomize the assign bot intervention to pages to check our assumption.

First, we randomly selected a week as a false event time for treated pages. ## 4.4.4. Timing effects around bot intervention

Our empirical analysis is valid under the key assumption that the timing of bot interventions is exogenous. However, under endogenous circumstances, it would be possible to see decreased user participation even before the implementations of bot interventions. To check endo geneity issue, we run a model and estimate the effect of intervention timing on user participation using the method suggested by Bertrand and Mullainathan [50]. As shown in Table 6, we find that coefficient estimates for two and three (and more) weeks prior to the bot inter vention are not significantly different from zero (relative to the week just before the bot intervention). The results suggest that user partici pation only starts to decrease significantly after Votebot and Stubbot interventions, which confirms the validity of our analysis. These model based results are in lines with our visual inspection analysis in the Propensity Score Matching process (see Fig. 3). Thus, we increase our confidence that the results are not driven by other confounding factors.

## 4.4.5. Additional robust checks

To further strengthen our conclusions, we conduct another set of robustness checks. First, it is possible that as pages become more mature fewer edits are needed. If the bot intervention was applied to articles with older ages or more edits already made by users, an increase in age or edits of article would decrease the participation, which could also explain our main empirical finding. To check this concern, we conduct two additional robustness checks as follows: (i) We run the analysis with additional control variable “page maturity” (i.e., the page age in terms of weeks, which accumulates before it first received bot revision, and holds the same after the bot revision), (ii) we add “cumulative edit change” to the model as additional control which captures the number of total edit change of contents over time. Consequently, we obtain qualitatively consistent results for the predicted relationships.

Second, our analysis focuses mainly on quantitative aspects rather than qualitative aspects of user participation. The bot intervention could possibly demotivate low-quality works but rather promote high-quality works, which would lead to a decrease in user participation overall. If this is the case, the decrease in user participation does not necessarily become an “unintended consequence”. To control for article-level quality, we included the number of images, the number of unique URLs provided in collaboration work, and page popularity (i.e., the ratio of weekly number of users on a page relative to the median number of all pages) as additional controls. ## 5. Varying effects of bot intervention

In this section, we examine the varying effect of bot intervention by considering two moderators, active users and topical concentration, in the regression analysis. The inclusion of the moderators enables us to cap ture the conditions at which the bot intervention effect can be stronger or weaker. Our model for this moderation analysis is as follows:

$$
y _ {p t} = \alpha_ {t} + \alpha_ {p} + \beta_ {1} \cdot p o s t B o t _ {p t} + \beta_ {2} \cdot M _ {p t} + \beta_ {3} \cdot p o s t B o t _ {p t} \times M _ {p t} + \gamma \cdot X _ {p t} + \epsilon_ {p t}\tag{2}
$$

where $M _ { p t }$ is either active users or topical concentration.

The results about the moderating role of active users are shown in Table 8. We find that the negative effect of Votebot intervention is significantly reduced when active users participated. We find similar results for Stubbot. We find positive and significant coefficient estimate of 0.107 (in column 2) for postStubbot × activeusers , suggesting that there is a large increase in user participation (by 11.3%) on pages with a higher level of active users participated compared to those with a lower level of active users participated, after the Stubbot intervention present in a page. Analyses using separately matched sample and unmatched sample show consis tent results.

## 6. Discussion

Our study investigates the impact of interventions by two policy bots – Stubbot and Votebot – on user participation in knowledge collabora tion and has the following notable findings. First, intervention by either Stubbot or Votebot leads to the unintended consequence of showing a negative association with the number of participating users in a knowledge collaboration article page. Second, the unintended conse quence is alleviated by active users and concentrated topics. Our robustness checks indicate no directional changes in the results with different model specifications and moderate potential endogeneity concerns.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

These findings have several important implications for knowledge collaboration in online platforms. Previous research has reported that internal and external human factors affect user participation in knowl edge collaboration. With the increasing reliance on bots to manage user activities in knowledge collaboration platforms. there is a need for attention to evaluate how bot interventions alter the way users partici pate [2,5,35]. This study contributes to the literature as it provides a large-scale and systematic analysis of bot interventions in online knowledge collaboration. We document the rigorous and negative ef fects of bot interventions on user participation, and argue that bot in terventions can disrupt knowledge creation [35,36]. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to verify the effect of bot intervention in online knowledge collaboration by rigorous economics approaches. Additionally, we find that the negative consequence of bot intervention reduces when it applies to articles with more active users and increased topical concentration. This could imply that the effect of bot interven tion is not equally distributed across different users and projects. This result complements new evidence in the literature on the role of user experience and topics [15,44,51].

Our results from Stubbot may also be explained by the findings in the literature on adaptive cost theory literature. This theory explains that the cost of adapting to stressors left the individual with fewer available resources to attend to their other needs or offer additional effort to assist [52]. In our context. individual users do not seek to protect and withhold their knowledge but instead are simply unable to share their knowledge. Online knowledge collaboration is often seen as interpersonal helping. Thus, when there are taxes on the individual's attentional capacities from environmental or physical demands/stressors (i.e., bot intervention), there is a decrease in interpersonal helping, seen here as decreased participation in online knowledge collaboration [53]. Inter estingly, our findings from Stubbot stand in contrast to the findings in the literature on feedback intervention. Feedback Intervention Theory (FIT) predicts increased efforts after negative intervention noting a discrepancy between performance and standard of the user’s work [54]. A recent study by Zhu et al. [55] shows that the impact of feedback on user contribution in knowledge collaboration is significant for being negative only for inexperienced users. Collaboration works flagged by Stubbot decrease user participation, and this negative effect is exacer bated for less experienced users. A possible explanation is that the action of the bot (i.e., flagging articles) could be perceived as too aggressive or constraining, which could demotivate users. Additionally, the inter vention is sometimes done without sufficient explanation to the users, and this could raise difficulties in reaching consensus on defining com munity standards [40].

On the other hand, our results from Votebot can be explained by the findings in the literature on social influence. The existing literature ex plains that individuals may tend to conform to the opinions of the ma jority, or might be reluctant to express their opinions when they differ from the public due to the fear of loss of reputation by majority disap provals [56–58]. In our context, interventions by Votebot lead the ma jority opinion on the contents of articles as it rearranges the order of contents based on user votes. Therefore, the effect of majority opinion shown in the literature may also be applied to our research context.

## 6.2. Practical implications

From a practical perspective, our results provide guidance for online knowledge collaboration platforms to better manage user activities and collaboration projects. While decentralized governance is the key premise in open collaboration, since the early 2010s there has been extensive discussion on adopting algorithmic agents that are managed by a central group of staffs in open collaboration platforms [2,35]. Although bot agents are designed to facilitate collaboration works by supporting activities of human users, the question regarding whether bot intervention facilitates user participation and whether it helps sus tain the user base are still unanswered. Our research shows that bot intervention can lead to a decrease in user participation in knowledge collaboration works, which goes against managers’ expectations. As our findings suggest, a bot intervention may potentially do more harm than benefit to the open collaboration community, and open collaboration platforms are advised to have cautious considerations before imple menting bots especially when it comes to user retention. Particularly, as reported in Aaltonen and Seiler [21], an initial user’s participation triggers further growth in production in knowledge collaboration plat forms. Since expanding and maintaining user base are the key de terminants of sustainable growth in knowledge collaboration business models, the hasty introduction of bot agents may become a major impediment to success of knowledge collaboration platforms, especially those in their early stages. When implementing bot agents, platform managers need to take a proactive approach and develop necessary supporting channels or mechanisms to mitigate the negative conse quences of bot intervention while maintaining the collaboration works effectively using bot agents. For example, like Wikipedia’s Bot Approval Groups (BAG), open collaboration platforms can invite users in the process of bot development and actively share their opinions before implementation of the bot.

Our results also find that the effect of bot intervention does not universally apply to all knowledge collaboration works; the results imply that the degree of consequence depends on the number of active participants and degree of topical concentration in collaboration works. After Wikipedia’s enormous success, there have been many wiki-like platforms that are specialized in niche domains, such as wikiHow in process knowledge, Scholarpedia in academic research, and Clinfowiki in healthcare. Our results indicate that it is imperative for platform managers to conduct a thorough analysis of their platform’s collabora tion contents and user population since the characteristics of collabo ration works and user base vary depending on the focus area. The results also imply that managers may need to encourage active users to interact more with newly joined or infrequent users during the implementation of bots. In addition, it would be valuable if managers can better guide content contribution by recommending that one article should concen trate on a few focused topics, rather than many contents.

## 6.3. Limitations and future work

This paper has some limitations, which can provide directions for future research on bots and other algorithmic agents in online knowl edge collaboration. First, it would be interesting to examine how human interventions differ from algorithmic interventions on online knowledge collaboration. Specifically, it would be important to know whether the two approaches can complement each other to promote user contribu tion and retention. Second, using archival data collected from one online knowledge collaboration website, we conduct a longitudinal analysis on pages and related user participation and contribution before and after bot interventions. Although the study on a single website can minimize the spurious effects of other factors, such as platform culture, locations, and leaderships, and content heterogeneity, yet, future studies need to examine bot intervention outcomes across many other online platforms and communities. Third, from a methodological perspective, we take a quasi-experimental approach and apply a difference-in-differences analysis to ensure that our research results are rigorous and robust to many empirical threats. Yet it is impossible to account for all the sources of bias in a knowledge collaboration setting. For example, we do not have information regarding the user background and their true moti vations to participate. A potential extension of this study is to conduct a randomized control experiment to further examine the effect of algo rithmic interventions. Finally, future research could also verify the im plications of bot interventions and artificial intelligence (AI) applications in offline business settings.

Journal of Operation Research Society, and other journals. She is the recipient of the VCU School of Business 2016 Faculty Award of Excellence. In 2018 she is named as a Dean’s Scholar Professor at the VCU School of Business. She is a senior editor of Decision Support Systems.
