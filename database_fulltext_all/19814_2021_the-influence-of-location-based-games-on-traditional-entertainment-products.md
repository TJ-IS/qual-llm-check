# The influence of location-based games on traditional entertainment products

Cristina Mihale-Wilson <sup>a,\*</sup>, Patrick Felka <sup>a</sup>, Oliver Hinz <sup>a</sup>, Martin Spann

<sup>a</sup> Information Systems and Information Management, Goethe University Frankfurt, Frankfurt am Main, Germany <sup>b</sup> Institute for Electronic Commerce and Digital Markets, Ludwig-Maximilians-Universitat ¨ (LMU), Munich, Germany

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Location-based games Mobile games Cinemas TV PC gaming Instrumental variable approach

## A B S T R A C T

Digital technologies and in particular the prevalence of mobile devices and high-speed mobile data networks enable new entertainment products such as location-based games (LBGs). Due to their requirement for constant user mobility, LBGs have the potential to affect individuals’ consumption patterns of entertainment products and can thus change the nature of business in the context of this emerging technology. The obiective of this study is to identify and measure whether LBGs’ advent has shifted the consumption of other entertainment products and generate insights which can support practitioners with their strategic business decisions. To this end. we analyze the consumption of the most popular entertainment products (i.e., TV, PC-based online games, and cinema) before and after the release of Pok´emon Go. Our results extend prior literature and show that the effect of LBGs—as a new type of entertainment product—is not confined to one product category but can affect multiple product categories of the entertainment industry. In particular, our estimation results indicate that LBGs can attenuate TV and PC online games consumption, but enhance cinema attendance.

## 1. Introduction

The prevalence of powerful smartphones—equipped with cameras, mobile Internet access, and location-detecting technologies—has enabled the creation of new products that could potentially impact the media and entertainment industry [1]. Location-based games (LBGs) are a prominent example which utilizes the core features of smartphones (e.g., wireless connectivity, GPS), and Augmented Reality (AR) technology, to provide new entertainment experiences.

LBGs are a type of mobile game in which the gameplay depends on the users’ location. In fact, to advance in the game, LBGs require their users to be almost constantly mobile. This characteristic is unique to LBGs since none of the other entertainment activities, no matter whether they can be enjoyed at a particular location (e.g., at home, in special venues) or on mobile devices (i.e., while in transit), require constant user mobility. Currently, the most popular LBGs on the market are Ingress and Pok´emon Go (PoGo)—two games which have helped significantly to establish LBGs as a new and important category of entertainment products. Within the first year of its release, PoGo generated \$832 million USD in player spending [2]. Although player spending declined somewhat in 2017, it followed an upward path ever since. In 2020, four years after its launch,

PoGo has been one of the games enjoying steady organic growth in terms of digital game sales. Within the first 10 months of 2020, the game has accumulated \$1 billion USD in player spending globally and a total of \$4.2 billion USD since its release in 2016 [2].

The success of these two LBGs has prompted the release and announcement of several new LBGs (e.g., Jurassic World Alive; The Walking Dead: Our World, Harry Potter Wizards United, Ghostbusters World, or Ingress Prime [3], a comprehensive overhaul of Ingress). Thus, rather than being exceptions, PoGo and Ingress are more likely the frontrunners of a growing entertainment product category which has the potential to change the current consumption pattern in the entertainment industry by attracting consumers’ time and attention.

Prior studies have demonstrated that due to their requirement for constant user mobility, LBGs such as Ingress and PoGo can change consumers’ mobility patterns and can motivate people to spend more time outside and even go to places they have never been before ([e.g., 4,5]). Under the premise that the leisure time spent on one activity cannot be spent on another one [6], PoGo’s potential to motivate con sumers to spend more time playing LBGs might be at the expense of other entertainment activities and products. However, this conclusion remains to be verified empirically, since albeit LBGs, and in particular

PoGo, garnered much attention from academia, the existing studies do not offer systematic empirical evidence on which entertainment prod ucts are affected by LBGs and to what extent.

This gap in research is intriguing and deserves scholarly attention since entertainment has a high economic impact not only on the national level [7] but also globally [8]. As consumers spend more time on entertainment than any other product category [8], the entertainment industry is ex pected to grow from 1.9 trillion USD global revenue in 2017, to 2.4 trillion USD in 2022 [9]—which is about the size of the global e-commerce sector. The fact that this growth is not expected to be equal across the industry subsectors, and the digitally enabled entertainment sub-sectors are expected to be outperforming the growth of traditional entertainment products (e.g., TV, books or radio); [9] supports the notion that technology-enabled products such as LBGs have the potential to substantially change the entertainment industry.

Traditionally, sustainable business success requires managers to iden tify and exploit consumer trends [10]—i.e., to foresee and react to the case that consumers replace certain products and services with others. Although this applies to many, if not all industries, predicting how new technology-enabled products might affect existing ones is especially crit ical for the entertainment industry, as this industry has been particularly sensitive to such external shocks. In fact, as prior research suggests, new technologies have challenged or disrupted existing product lines and rev enue models [11] within several entertainment sub-sectors (e.g., books, newspapers).

Considering that the entertainment industry is not only characterized by high competition due to the high and heterogeneous supply of entertainment products, but also by high production costs and with high failure rates, managers of entertainment-related businesses need to be able to foresee and react to how new entertainment products are likely to affect their businesses. For other marketers, since advertising is the backbone of many brand relationships, understanding how new enter tainment products affect traditional ones (such as TV) is essential for building and maintaining optimal customer relationships [11].

Besides the practical relevance of studying LBGs impact direction and magnitude on other entertainment products, investigating this research question also contributes to the advancement of the current state of theory and literature on cross-media dynamics in the entertainment industry. Although preceding literature investigated the impact of new technologyenabled entertainment products, it has done so by focusing predominantly on the corresponding product category (e.g., the impact of e-books on print books, the effect of news apps on newspapers), with less focus on potential spill-over effects for the consumption of other entertainment product categories. Therefore, this study investigates LBG’s effect on other entertainment products in terms of impact direction and magnitude. To this end, we analyze the impact of PoGo’s release (as an instantiation of LBGs) on the consumption of the most popular entertainment activities (e. g., watching TV, playing PC online games and going to the cinema).

The remainder of this study is organized as follows: After providing an overview on relevant theories and related work, we explain our empirical approach and our analysis results and conclude with a dis cussion of the implications and limitations of our work.

## 2. Theoretical background and related work

By definition, entertainment refers to an “activity that is diverting, amusing, or pleasing and that agreeably occupies the viewer’s time and attention” [12]. Hence, in contrast to responsibilities and chores, entertainment relates to activities that individuals are looking forward to seeing, hearing, or doing [12]. To date, there is a plethora of entertainment products and activities, but among the available forms of entertainment, film-related contents (e.g., movies watched on TV or at movie theaters, or TV productions) and programmed content (e.g., console games, PC online games, and mobile games) are currently by far the most popular and economically relevant entertainment prod ucts [13].

Our study relates to several streams of literature. First, it relates to the literature on substitution and complementarity effects between products and various product categories. Second, it relates to the stream of literature analyzing cross-media dynamics in the entertainment in dustry and the impact of new technology-enabled media on their more traditional counterparts. Third, our study also adheres to the literature on LBG games, and more particularly on their characteristics and their impact on human behavior. In the following, we present and discuss relevant theoretical considerations and central insights from these three streams of literature.

In general, the market impact of new products can usually be pre dicted depending on the extent to which new products either crowd out or complement existing ones [14]. This, in turn, depends on whether consumers regard the new product as a substitute or rather a comple ment for the existing products.

A product substitutes (complements) another if a price increase (decrease) for one product leads to higher sales of the other product [15]. However, consumers’ consumption decision making is a complex and dynamic process which does not depend only on other products price but also on the availability and characteristics of “other products” [15,16]. Further, considering the vast diversity of products available to consumers, it is important to categorize products for the analysis of inter-product effects (e.g., [15,17]). Albeit the scarcity of research ef forts aiming to understand how product categories evolve, or how to identify them (e.g., [15,18]), previous research shows that product categorization can be based not only on a products’ purpose but also on individuals’ perception of the products’ attributes in a particular context [16]. Moreover, products appertaining to various categories can be substitutes (complements) in use, or occasional substitutes (comple ments) [15]. The relationship of substitutes or complements in use im plies that products serve a very similar purpose, appertain to different categories but have important salient attributes if compared with each other. In this case, the consumption behavior is driven by these attri butes and their fit to consumers preferences [15,16].

In contrast, in the case of occasional substitutes or complements, products from various categories substitute or complement other products only in certain circumstances [15]. This relationship follows the rationale that consumers’ decision to engage with one particular entertainment product will usually diminish or replace the consumption of another one [19]. Further, it follows the notion that consumers’ demand for enter tainment products is determined by their limited resources (e.g., leisure time, money, attention). Presumably, having products which serve a more generic purpose (e.g., entertainment, communication) in the wake of budget or time constraints, consumers can engage with only one particular product that will in that case substitute all others.

Besides the existence of these two types of relationships between products and product categories, there exist more dynamic relationships shaped by technological progress and changes in market structures [15]. More specifically, in certain industries, such as the telecommunication or entertainment business, technological progress and market restruc turing can induce a relatively fast shift in consumer behavior and change not only the dynamics “within substitute categories or complements but also between the two” [15], turning product categories which used to be substitutional into complementary ones and vice versa. In the telecom munication industry, for instance, due to instable network service dur ing the introduction phase of mobile phones, individuals perceived pagers as a complementary product which help to bridge the mobile service time-outs. However, as mobile networks improved their stabil ity, mobile phones substituted pagers [15].

Previous research on the impact of new technology-based entertain ment products on existing ones concentrates on the assessment of such new products on their traditional counterparts (i.e., the effect of the introduction of e-books on printed books; the effect of online newspapers and apps on printed newspapers), while neglecting inter-category dy namics and thus the effects of new products (e.g., LBGs) on seemingly unrelated entertainment products (e.g., TV, cinema, PC online games).

Besides, the current state of the literature on cross-cannibalization effects in the entertainment industry presents somewhat mixed results of the impact of new-technology enabled products on existing ones. While some studies found (at least weak) cannibalization effects among the new and the existing products (e.g., [14,20–22]), others showed that new and existing products can act as complements to their traditional counterparts (e.g., [16,23,24]). Ghose et al. [21], Forman et al. [25] and Chen et al. [20], studied the impact of the introduction of eBooks or marketplaces for used books on their printed counterparts and found only weak cannibal ization effects. Further, Liebowitz [26], and Wlo¨mert and Papies [22] found a negative effect of piracy and streaming in the music industry.

In contrast, other studies that analyzed the impact of piracy and streaming on TV and cinema consumption showed that technological development does not necessarily have to be negative and can some times boost the consumption of entertainment content [27]. On a similar note, Hashim and colleagues [28] assessed the effects of digital movies on physical movie sales. They found that digital rentals significantly reduced sales of physical movies but digital purchases did not. Addi tionally, Smith and Telang [29] revealed that the legitimate free dis tribution of videos (e.g., in free TV) can boost the sales in paid channels, while piracy does not cannibalize sales for movies in paid channels. In fact, movie producers can compete with free channels through product differentiation [29].

The fact that technological developments and related new products can drive fast change and market restructuring can be illustrated by the gaming industry. Gaming is an umbrella term for several types of games that can be played on different devices and occasions (i.e., console games, online and offline games, PC games, mobile games, LBGs). With the advent of personal computers and fast Internet connections, console games got surpassed in terms of consumption by PC online games [30]. Similarly, the proliferation of powerful mobile phones enabled the rise of mobile games, which can be played at any time and location. Further, the equipment of the mobile phones with cameras, fast and stable mo bile Internet, and location-based technologies facilitated the develop ment of LBGs—a type of mobile game in which the gameplay is heavily dependent on users’ location.

In LBGs, users can advance in the game only by changing their loca tion, as they are rewarded based on the distance walked and the special ingame locations they visited. Such special in-game locations are locations of public interest, in the real world. By visiting such special in-game lo cations, users can either perform specific tasks or gather other useful artifacts which will allow them to advance in the game. In Pok´emon Go (PoGo), for instance, users are required to roam through the streets and gather, train, and battle Pok´emon [31]. In order to catch Pok´emon or to be able to battle the Pok´emon, users must visit special in-game locations called Pok´estops or Arenas and gather game artifacts such as Pok´eballs, revival potions, Pok´emon luring modules, and many more. In PoGo, users are rewarded, the further they walk, the more Pok´emon they catch, and the more Pok´emon they train or battle. However, users can also pay their way through the game and purchase various items they would usually find for free at Pok´estops or Arenas.

In economic terms, PoGo counts to the most successful mobile games, after Candy Crush Saga, with a total of 2.2 billion USD lifetime revenue until the end of 2018 [32]. Hence, it is not surprising that PoGo has garnered a lot of attention, from both practitioners and academics of various disciplines. Besides medical studies reflecting upon PoGo’s po tential to combat obesity and legal studies contemplating the legal im plications that arise from users being reckless when playing the game, PoGo also enjoyed some scholarly attention from business research. Accordingly, the current body of literature presents studies investigating PoGo’s effect on individual activity levels, PoGo’s potential to change individuals’ mobility patterns, and thereof resulting economic spill-over effects. Colley and colleagues [5], for instance, investigated the impact of PoGo’s mobility requirement from a geographical perspective; Faccio and McConnell [33] explored the relationship between PoGo’s introduc tion and vehicular crashes; Gabbiadini et al. [34], Howe et al. [35],

LeBlanc and Chaput [36] explored if consumers increase their physical activity levels due to the game; while Pamuru et al. [31] focused on the economic potential of LBGs for local restaurants. Based on surveys con ducted with participant groups of varying sizes (starting from 100 to almost 1000 participants), the results of prior studies suggest that PoGo players increased their physical activity levels after installing the game; participants were reportedly willing to take on extra outings or adapt their usual mobility patterns just to advance in the game. However, as research further suggests, these effects faded away quickly, and consumers’ activity levels returned close to the initial state within three to four weeks.

In addition to numerous research efforts relying on self-reported data, there are three studies analyzing PoGo based on observational data (see Table 1). Althoff et al. [37] evaluated information from wearable sensor data and search engine logs for 32 thousand users over three months to explore changes in user activity due to PoGo. Similarly to studies con ducted on self-reported data, Althoff et al. [37] also found a statistically significant increase in user activity due to PoGo, for up to four weeks after starting to play the game. In contrast to Althoff et al. [37], Faccio & McConnell [33] used the introduction of PoGo as a quasi-experiment to study the economic and human cost of using apps and smartphones while driving. In their work, Faccio & McConnell [33] link the location of Pok´estops to the number of accidents that happened in the vicinity of such games and find that playing PoGo while driving relates to an increase in insurance premiums of 2.47% or more. Another notable research on PoGo is the work of Pamuru et al. [31]. In their study, Pamuru et al. [31] investigated PoGo’s effects on restaurants reviews. In particular, the scholars explored empirically whether restaurants involved in PoGo – i.e., restaurants which happen to be Pok´estops or Arenas can benefit from PoGo by attracting PoGo players to visit, eat and harvest the in-game artifacts users can get at these special in-game locations. Based on observational data from four months before and after PoGo’s release (July 2016) and 10,304 reviews of 3424 restaurants in the city of Houston, Pamuru et al. [31] found a weak statistically significant relationship between restaurants that are PoGo artifacts and their reviews, but only within seven days after PoGo’s launch. Finally, striving to consolidate the multitude of studies and findings related to PoGo’s effect on consumers’ physical activity, scholars have also compiled studies on the meta-effects of the game on consumer mobility (e.g., [38,39]).

Altogether, prior studies corroborate that PoGo and by extension LBGs have the potential to change consumers’ mobility and activity patterns. More specifically, prior research presents empirical evidence that due to their requirement for constant mobility, PoGo and other LBGs can make consumers spend more time outside, change their mobility patterns and deviate from their usual route, or even go new routes and visit new cities.

Despite the variety of research questions addressed in related research, there are currently no studies investigating how LBG’s motivational power to draw people to spend more time outside, is affecting other entertain ment products’ consumption levels. This gap is surprising given the current interest on the relationships between new and traditional entertainment products. We study how LBGs affect the consumption of other entertain ment products based on observational field data. To be more specific, we use observational field data on three of the most popular entertainment products (i.e., TV, online games and cinema) to monitor their consumption levels before and after the introduction of PoGo (as an instantiation of LBGs).

## 3. Data and methodology

In this study, we focus on a geographical region in Germany that is comparable to the size of Vermont (i.e., approximately 25,000 square km). This geographical region has a population of 6.2 million and comprises densely populated urban areas as well as less-populated rural areas.

## 3.1. Data

To explore our research question we collected various observational field data records reflecting the activity levels in the entertainment products of interest: PoGo, PC online games, TV and cinema, from the 1st of January 2016 to the 09th of October 2016 (283 days). Observa tional field data are suitable to identify (1) which and (2) to what extent PoGo replaces any other of the three entertainment activities in focus of this study. Observational field data does not have the limitations of selfreported data such as a possible social desirability bias,<sup>1</sup> acquiescence biases<sup>2</sup> or misjudgment or subjectively false recalling of the past [43]. Table 2 provides an overview of the operationalization of the main variables, while the summary statistics for these variables are presented in Table 3.

The data reflecting the popularity of our focal LBG (i.e., PoGo) were collected via a mobile phone security software. Although the number of active PoGo players is strictly classified information, we have access to a large dataset provided by a firm that runs security software on over one million mobile devices. This software allows us to see anonymous usage data for different apps. We can assume that the security firm’s user base is representative for smartphone users in our area of interest because we correlated this information with Niantic’s infrequent press releases on active players per day and found that our user estimate was reasonably consistent.

The PC online games related data were collected from a popular platform with more than 60 different online games. Since this platform is not optimized for mobile operating systems, playing the games on this platform makes only sense on desktop PCs or laptops. We have access to the number of daily active users playing games on the platform. Hence, analogous to our focal LBG game, we use the number of daily active users on the platform to monitor the consumption of PC online games before and after PoGo’s introduction in July 2016.

The data on the TV viewership were provided by a market research company measuring TV audiences on stationary appliances (i.e., mobile TV viewers are not included in these figures). The TV audiences in the area and period of interest are measured by the hourly number of TV viewers aged three and above. Based on this information, we can iden tify the TV viewership during the early morning, daytime, and primetime (7–10 pm) slots. Since prime-time is the most popular time to watch TV, we use the prime-time viewership as our primary indicator of TV consumption.

The consumption levels of movies at theaters are based on the daily attendance data of seven multiplex cinemas in the area of interest. For reasons of confidentiality, we cannot disclose the names and exact loca tions of the movie theaters included in this study. Additionally, we had to transform the absolute numbers of cinema visitors per day such that for each cinema, the day of the year with the lowest number of visitors was set to 1. The remaining days were put in relation to this reference day by dividing the daily values of visitors by the number of visitors on the reference day. Besides the daily cinema attendance, we also gathered geospatial information on the surroundings of each cinema. This geo spatial information reveals whether the analyzed cinema is a Pok´estop or Arena in PoGo, or not. Because a cinema being a Pok´estop or Arena is at random, we can test for an activity difference between movie theaters which are Pok´estops or Arenas and those which are not.

To ensure comparability, we index all data sets, i.e., transform the number of TV viewers, Pok´emon Go players, and Active Users on the online gaming platform in the same way that the cinema data were indexed. Table 2 explains the operationalization of these variables in detail.

## 3.2. Estimation method

To identify whether and how the popularity of LBGs affected the consumption of other entertainment products, we monitored the activity levels on the PC online games platform, the movie theater attendance, as well as the number of TV viewers, before and after PoGo’s release. To ensure that our results capture only PoGo’s popularity effect on the entertainment products and control for endogeneity, we employ an Instrumental Variable (IV) regression model using our unique dataset on the indexed daily number of users in PoGo and exogenous mobile network outages as an IV.

Keeping in mind that mobile network outages are outages that are not caused by a high number of PoGo players, but rather by unrelated events such as hardware problems, such mobile network outages influ ence the number of PoGo players (as PoGo requires a mobile Internet connection in order to be able to play the game) but not the other entertainment products. ## 4.2. LBG’s effect on TV consumption

To measure LBG’s effect on TV consumption, we focused on the primetime hours (i.e., the time between 7 pm and 10 pm), as these are the most popular time to watch TV (see Table 3, section c). The estimation results (see Table 5, column 2) indicate a small but statistically significant negative trend in TV consumption during prime-time. Furthermore, Table 5, column 2 shows a negative effect of LBG’s on the TV viewership. In this context, an increase in the PoGo user base is related to a statistically significant decrease (p < .01) in the indexed prime-time viewership of 0.011 units. In absolute terms, an increase in PoGo’s user base relates to a decrease of the prime-time TV viewership in our observation area by approximately 11.28<sup>4</sup> thousand individuals, or 0.56%.<sup>4</sup>

According to Statista [47], the PoGo player base reached during the observation period a total of 7.1 million active users on a national level. If we assume that these players are more or less equally distributed across the different states in Germany, our focal area accounted for approxi mately 532 thousand players.<sup>5</sup> Combining our finding that PoGo’s release is related to a decline in prime-time TV consumption of 0.56% with the assumed PoGo player base in our observation period, our results imply that about 2.12%<sup>6</sup> of the PoGo player base decided to catch Pok´emon instead of usually watching TV during prime-time.

The trend variable in our model captures the attrition of the primetime TV viewership for instance due to migration of TV viewers to streaming platforms. Hence, our estimation results are very likely indi cating changes in the consumption of prime-time TV after PoGo’s release. However, to corroborate the reliance of our results we con ducted further investigations to rule out other potential disturbances in the effects identified. Based on our investigations on the area and period of interest, we could find no additional motivating factors or changes in the general conditions that would accelerate the naturally occurring attrition of TV viewers or their migration to streaming services. For instance, during the period of interest and in the area of interest, the Internet infrastructure and thus one of the leading facilitators of streaming services remained the same. Specially, we could find no Internet upgrades or infrastructure expansions for the region and period of interest. Likewise, we could find no relevant infrastructural changes in the time shortly before 2016. Also, as outlined above (in the Esti mation Method section of the paper) we estimated all our models with a time trend variable but also models with monthly dummies. The models with the monthly dummies capture special offers or events which would have enticed more individuals than usual to stop watching TV and transition to streaming services. The results of the estimations with the monthly dummies show that PoGo’s effect on the prime-time TV view ership is stable.

Based on these estimation results and the considerations discussed in the theoretical section, we conclude that TV and LBGs are occasional substitutes. According to the literature, occasional substitutes refer to the case in which consumers choose—based on situational constraints (e.g., budget or time restrictions)—from a pool of available products from various categories. Thereby, the available products serve a more generic purpose (e.g., here: entertainment) but not necessarily a similar specific purpose (e.g., playing a game, watching a movie). In this case, time and LBGs constant requirement for mobility are the main factors for consumers to either watch prime-time TV or go out and play PoGo.

## 4.3. LBG’s effect on cinema attendance

Table 5 column 3a, presents our estimations regarding LBG’s effect on cinema attendance. The results show a small but statistically signif icant negative trend in the number of cinema visitors (p < .01). Addi tionally, the results indicate that PoGo had a statistically significant (p < .01) positive effect on cinema attendance across all seven cinemas. An increase in the PoGo user base translates into an increase in the indexed cinema attendance by 1.4 units, or 13.84%.<sup>7</sup>

Striving to ensure that the effect observed is due to PoGo and not driven by blockbuster movies which would attract more cinema atten dants than usually, we additionally scrutinized the movie program of the cinemas assessed. In the course of this analysis, we found that none of the top 10 blockbuster movies in 2016 had been released around PoGo’s release in the focal country.

Additionally, to delve into LBG’s effect on cinemas, we exploit the geospatial information which reveals whether the analyzed cinema is a Pok´estop or Arena in PoGo, or not, to perform a Differences-in-Differences (DID) analysis [48]. In this analysis, the control group entails the cinemas which are not Pok´estops or Arenas in PoGo, while the treatment group consists of the cinemas which are Pok´estops or Arenas in the game. The treatment consists of a cinema being a Pok´estop or Arena while the treatment date is PoGo’s release (13th of July 2016).

At this juncture, it is essential to note that we assume that a cinema being or not being a Pok´estop or Arena in PoGo is random so that cin emas cannot self-select to receive the treatment. Further, to increase the robustness and validity of our estimations, we use the propensity score matching technique to identify cinemas that are as similar as possible before PoGo’s release [49]. This way, we identified four similar cinemas (two treated and two untreated) which will serve as our data basis for our further DID analysis. Moreover, given that the validity of the DID analysis hinges on the common trend assumption [50], for the control and treatment group during the pre-treatment period, we analyzed the trends of the control and treated cinemas during the pre-treatment period and conclude that these follow very similar trends.<sup>8</sup> The DID estimation results corroborate PoGo’s positive effect on cinema atten dance and reveal a statistically significant marginal activity difference between movie theaters which are Pok´estops and those which are not. More specifically, as our estimation results suggest (see Table 5, column 3b), if a cinema is a Pok´estop or Arena in PoGo it will experience an increase of visitors by 0.51 units or 7%.<sup>9</sup>

Based on these estimation results, we conclude that LBGs and cinema attendance are positively related. This positive effect can be enhanced if cinemas and LBGs are interlinked via Pok´estops and other in-game arti facts. Although PoGo’s positive effect on cinema attendance might be surprising at first, our results complement the findings of more surveybased studies which report that PoGo has the potential to change con sumers’ mobility and activity patterns (e.g., [5,36,37,51]). Colley and colleagues [5] and LeBlanc and Chaput [36], for instance, reported that some of their study participants admitted to having changed their itin erary in order to play PoGo. Some of the participants in these studies also reported having visited other cities and places they have never seen before, just to advance in the game. Again others admitted to having used the game for a family outing, and that their family outing sometimes ended with a visit to the movie theater.

In terms of the considerations discussed in the theoretical section of the paper, the estimation results point to an occasional complementarity relationship between movie theater attendance and LBGs. According to the literature, occasional complements refers to the case in which products appertaining to one product category can—in certain occasions—boost the consumption of products in another category. In practice, cinema and LBGs serve the generic purpose of entertainment, but they do not have a similar specific scope. Accordingly, the estimation results point to an oc casional complementarity relationship between cinemas and LBGs, with LBGs’ requirement for constant mobility playing once more a decisive role in this relationship. Since LBGs require users to leave their house and play PoGo, users are maybe also more likely to use this outing for a cinema visit [5,36]. Even more, in the case that cinemas are linked to the LBGs via Pok´estops or other in-game artifacts, the occasional complementarity between cinemas and LBGs grows even stronger. At Pok´estops and Arenas PoGo players can gather special items which help them advance in the game, but such items are released for each player only once every 5 min. Therefore, it is plausible that PoGo players wanting to maximize their items may extend their stay at the cinema & Pok´estop by watching a movie.

## 5. Discussion

The goal of this study was to identify whether and how LBGs impact consumers’ consumption of other, existing entertainment products. To this end, we measured the change in the consumption of PC online games, TV, and cinema before and after the introduction of a popular location-based game (i.e., Pok´emon Go). Our empirical analyses show that LBGs can shift consumption patterns of entertainment products and significantly reduce the consumption of TV and PC online games, but they can also act as complements for movie theaters. These findings can be explained based on consumers’ mobility patterns and the unique mobility characteristics of LBGs, as well as LBGs potential to change consumers’ behavior (e.g., [31,35,37]). According to Cho et al. [4] and Colley et al. [5], who studied the mobility patterns of people by exploiting location-based social networks and geodata from mobile phones, consumers usually move within a limited region and visit remote places only if motivated to do so (e.g., visiting friends, tourism, etc.). Given that LBGs require their users to be mobile and go to remote areas, such games are changing consumers’ mobility patterns by giving them a reason to go to certain (more remote) venues—which might be places like movie theaters. Altogether, our findings have theoretical and managerial implications.

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the existing literature on cannibalization effects within the entertainment industry by investigating how the intro duction of new products in one sub-industry (i.e., gaming) affects products from related sub-industries (i.e., other entertainment sub-sectors). While prior literature suggests that “cannibalization is more likely to emerge in markets with a high degree of product differentiation” [52], our results reveal that the relationships between new and existing products can be far more complex and multi-faceted. In fact, our results validate the notion that effect of LBGs—as a new type of entertainment product is not confined to one product category but can affect multiple product cate gories of the entertainment industry. Additionally, our results indicate that certain characteristics—such as an entertainment product’s mobility requirements—can influence the substitution and complementarity ef fects that emerge between such products.

Notably, the current literature usually investigated the effect of new technology endorsed entertainment products on the existing ones in the corresponding sub-sectors (e.g., the impact of e-books on print books, the effect of news apps on newspapers). Our results suggest that the introduction of one type of entertainment product (e.g., LBGs) can go beyond the consumption of entertainment products appertaining to the same sub-group (e.g., games). Besides, our estimations validate the importance of other seemingly unrelated products and supports the extended framework on complementarity and substitution proposed by

Shocker et al. [15]. Accordingly, we make use of this extended frame work to contemplate and explain the dynamics between products appertaining to various product categories. Against this backdrop, our study underlines the urgent need to develop appropriate categorizations of entertainment products which enable a preliminary assessment of the impact of new products on existing ones before the actual introduction of a new product. Moreover, given the diversity of entertainment products and their characteristics, more research is necessary that aims at understanding how and why new entertainment products affect such products in other categories than the one they appertain to. Such research is not only crucial for the extension of the current body of theory but also relevant from a managerial perspective.

## 5.2. Managerial implications

From a managerial perspective, knowing the current and future consumer trends—i.e., predicting which products consumers are likely to start or stop consuming in the future—is essential for a firm’s sus tainable success. Accordingly, our study presents insights that have a direct impact on various enterprises. For instance, our estimation results reveal that LBGs could threaten other popular entertainment products by attracting individuals’ attention. This, in turn, may ultimately attenuate other products’ revenues, at the same time, might boost local (brick and mortar) businesses.

Prior literature explored mainly the effect of new technology endorsed entertainment products on the corresponding product sub-sector. Thus, it implicitly highlighted the importance that managers should be aware of new technology-endorsed products joining their product sub-sectors. Our results extend prior literature and show that besides the introduction of products in their corresponding sub-sector, managers should also pay careful attention to other seemingly unrelated new products in other subsectors. In this vein, book publishers, for instance, need not only to watch out for technology-enabled products that can significantly reshape the book markets, but also for new products from the gaming sub-sector, music or movies sub-sector.

Based on the insights presented in our study, enterprises involved in the PC online gaming, TV, or movie theater business can develop suit able strategies to either enhance or avoid LBGs collateral effects. For example, companies related to the TV or gaming sub-sectors may offset LBG’s negative impact on them by re-focusing their offerings by aiming to capture additional age groups that are not particularly active in LBGs. Further, such firms might invest in a diversification strategy, as the loss in revenue due to LBGs could be offset by investing in products that remain either unaffected or even profit from positive developments in LBGs.

On the contrary, firms for whom LBGs are beneficial can seize on LBGs’ popularity by actively collaborating with game developers, or at least finding ways to be involved in LBGs. Restaurants, for instance, could engage (if possible) in partnership programs [31] to try and garner a higher number of visitors (if the cost of the partnership does not outweigh the monetary benefits) The same applies to cinemas, but maybe also be the cased for gas stations, shopping centers, or any other location-bound businesses. This way such businesses can benefit from the vast possibilities Location Based Technologies can offer. After all, in addition to attracting customer to their venues, such businesses can also leverage Location Based Technologies to as a strategic tool able to pre dict and enhance future consumer visits [53].

Additionally, our findings can also support marketing budget allo cation. Since the results of our analyses suggest that the release of a popular LBG attenuated not only prime-time television attendance but also the user base of online games, advertisers might want to consider including LBGs as an additional channel to reach target audiences effi ciently [23,54].

Besides these managerial implications, LBGs could also extend their influence to other industries, such as insurance and health service. The decline in TV viewers during prime-time hours, for instance, is somewhat in line with previous research sustaining that LBGs have the motivational power to change people’s exercising habits. Although previous literature found that the positive effect of LBGs on people’s activity levels is only short-lived, our estimation results suggest other wise. More specifically, based on our data, which entails three months after PoGo’s introduction, our results are supporting that consumers activity change can last longer than one to four weeks. Subsequently, we conclude that due to the LBGs motivational power to go outdoors and be active (over at least three months), this gaming genre could help combat obesity and other sedentary lifestyle-related diseases.

Considering that physical inactivity is, after smoking, the second leading cause for preventable death [36], and gaming is usually related to a sedentary entertainment activity, it is interesting to see how new technologies can transform a rather unhealthy type of entertainment activity in such a way that it can actually contribute towards a healthier lifestyle. Thus, in LBGs’ case, the gaming genre’s requirement for con stant consumer mobility can present previously inconceivable oppor tunities for health services or insurance firms.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Albeit our efforts to ensure highly robust results, we have to note two main limitations which provide avenues for further research: First, our study is a snapshot of three months after PoGo’s introduction, so that it could be argued that the effects observed will not be sustained in the long-run. However, supposing that PoGo’s effect on other entertainment products would indeed fade away after the three months, it is pertinent to expect that soon, a new blockbuster LBG will manage to have a similar effect on consumers as PoGo. Ultimately, the success of LBGs such as Ingress and PoGo has prompted the development and release of several new LBGs: “Jurassic World Alive”; “The Walking Dead: Our World”; “Ghostbusters World”; “Ingress Prime” or “Harry Potter Wizards Unite” [55]. Niantic alone presents a full pipeline of LBGs that build on the same technology as Pok´emon Go.

Another notable limitation could be the fact that PoGo plays a central role in our identification strategy. After all, scholars repeatedly empha sized that this particular LBG managed to attract substantial media attention, which in turn might have affected consumers’ adoption be haviors. Yet, considering that Ingress and especially PoGo have helped that LBGs developed from a formerly niche mobile gaming product to an established mass adopted type of entertainment genre with many more LBGs to come, we expect that the impact of LBGs issued in the future will be again substantial so that the effects observed in relation to PoGo are also very likely to be observed after the introduction of other LBGs.

Finally, we note that despite these limitations, we still expect that the direction of LBGs’ impact on existing entertainment products will be stable also for other LBGs. To empirically substantiate this rationale, future research may analyze the impact of other LBGs on the con sumption of alternative entertainment products, such as TV, PC online games and cinema.

## 6. Conclusion

Sustainable business success requires managers to identify and exploit consumer trends (i.e., changes in consumption patterns). This holds for many industries, but especially for the entertainment in dustry, which suffered when new technologies challenged or disrupted their revenue models [13].

In general, new technologies are often involved in the development of new entertainment products which then galvanize individuals' resources away from other entertainment products towards themselves. The prevalence of powerful smartphones equipped with cameras, mo bile Internet access, and location-detecting technologies, for instance, has enabled the development of location-based games (LBGs)—a mobile game genre which requires constant user mobility to advance in the game. However, when users suddenly spend their leisure time with new technology-enabled products, the adoption of these new technologyenabled products come to the expense of other, traditional ones.

Under the premise that the leisure time spent on one activity cannot be spent on another one [6], understanding how LBGs might impact other entertainment products and other businesses [31], is an essential step towards more informed managerial decisions not only for entertainmentrelated businesses but also for other businesses, such as insurance com panies, restaurants or gas stations.

Our study presents empirical evidence that LBGs indeed managed to attract the attention of a substantial amount of consumers, but also, that LBGs can present new avenues for location-based advertisement, inno vative business models and promotion campaigns. In any case, consid ering the high number of LBGs in development or about to be released, the advent of more LBGs will be interesting to observe.
