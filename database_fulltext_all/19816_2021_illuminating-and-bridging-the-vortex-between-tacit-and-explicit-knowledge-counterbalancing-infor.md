# Illuminating and bridging the vortex between tacit and explicit knowledge: Counterbalancing information asymmetry in high-value low-frequency transactions

Mark Hoksbergen , Johnny Chan , Gabrielle Peko , David Sundaram

University of Auckland, Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Private Bag 92019, Auckland 1142, New Zealand

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Decisions Decision making Decision support Knowledge management Knowledge transformation Tacit knowledge Explicit knowledge High-value low-frequency transaction Dark side of knowledge Real estate

## A B S T R A C T

Asymmetric information could harm a stakeholder in a transaction when they are not aware of the disparity of the information among the various stakeholders. Understanding the relationship and interaction among stake holders is imperative for making informed decisions. It becomes even more critical in the context of high-value low-frequency transaction (HVLFT). This paper delves into the different forms of knowledge (data, information, knowledge, wisdom, understanding, experience and learning) that are essential to support HVLFT. If a form of knowledge can be categorised as neither tacit nor explicit, it belongs to the proposed knowledge dimension X. This paper further explores the transformation of different dimensions of knowledge at the disruptive vortex between tacit, explicit, and X. The interactions between these three dimensions of knowledge are validated through a study of knowledge asymmetry among HVLFT stakeholders in the real estate industry of NZ. We conclude with the proposal of a blockchain-based architecture to counterbalance information asymmetry caused by X.

## 1. Introduction

Defining knowledge has been a philosophical debate over centuries. This paper aims to contribute to the field of knowledge management by extending our current understanding of the dichotomic divide between tacit knowledge and explicit knowledge. Through our study of high value low-frequency transaction (HVLFT) in relation to the real estate industry of New Zealand (NZ), a new dimension of knowledge has been identified and conceptualised to co-exist and interact with tacit knowl edge and explicit knowledge. This new dimension X incorporates knowledge, understanding, experience and learning that could not be clearly defined as tacit or explicit. It includes knowledge that is lost, not understood, or misappropriated. Such knowledge belonging to dimen sion X is at the root of information asymmetry between stakeholders in an HVLFT.

The motivation of this research is to explore the knowledge dimen sion X and to illuminate the interrelationships among the knowledge dimensions of tacit, explicit, and X. This paper primarily consists of seven sections. Section 2 explores the overarching concept of knowledge management in the context of HVLFT, which includes a discussion on tacit knowledge and explicit knowledge. A clarification of dimension X follows this section through a case study in Section 3. Section 4 proposes a conceptual model that incorporates the three dimensions of knowledge to elucidate the relationship and interaction among them. The applica tion of the proposed model to a typical HVLFT in the real estate industry is illustrated in Section 5, together with its generalisation to the ship building and the art industry. Section 6 proposes a blockchain-based decision support architecture to ameliorate information asymmetry in HVLFT by transforming tacit knowledge and knowledge in dimension X into explicit, verifiable and trusted knowledge. Section 7 concludes the paper.

## 2. Literature review

In the following Section 2.1, we discuss the traditional dichotomy between tacit and explicit knowledge through historical and academic lenses. This is followed up in Section 2.2 by discussing the systemic problem of information and knowledge asymmetry in general and in particular among HVLFT. Section 2.3 delves into the causes of such asymmetry and explores the dark side of knowledge.

## 2.1. Knowledge management, tacit knowledge and explicit knowledge

Knowledge management is often viewed as the interaction among people, process, and technology. This interaction is frequently portrayed in a firm setting. Brooking [7] advances a broader view and defines knowledge management as the approach to manage human-centred assets. Its function is to guard and grow knowledge owned by an indi vidual, and when possible, to transfer the assets into a form more readily shared with others. The theory's crux is to devise different strategies to capture tacit knowledge from individual and convert it into explicit knowledge, making it readily available and transferable [38].

The distinction between tacit knowledge and explicit knowledge is a fundamental concept in knowledge management. It was first purported by Polanyi [49], “we know more than we can tell.” The difference be tween tacit and explicit is also acknowledged by Alavi and Leidner [2]. Nonaka and Takeuchi [47] define tacit knowledge as “personal knowl edge embedded in individual experience and involves intangible factors such as personal beliefs, perspective and value systems.” Explicit knowledge is the converse of tacit knowledge. Explicit knowledge can be expressed in code and is not subject to multiple interpretations. Tacit knowledge can be subdivided into knowledge that has not been for malised, and knowledge that cannot be formalised. Hedesstrom and Whitley [30] emphasise that once knowledge has been formalised, it becomes explicit. This is further extrapolated by Nonaka and Takeuchi [47] in their SECI model showing the complexity of coding tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge. While Dalkir [12] posits that the primary division of knowledge is between tacit and explicit, Collins [11] notes that tacit knowledge is a relative concept: what could be easily articulated by one person may be very difficult to externalise by another. Thus, the same piece of knowledge may be considered explicit for some but tacit for others. There is also a plethora of literature which defines implicit knowledge as all non-explicit knowledge [14]. In this paper, we treat implicit knowledge and tacit knowledge as equivalent.

Davenport and Prusak [13] describe knowledge as a mix of experi ence, contextual information, value and expert insight. Within an organisation, knowledge is embedded not only inside document re positories but also among routines, processes, and practices. Fahey and Prusak [21] argue the importance of creating a shared context. The starting point of managing knowledge is to convey the shared context between individuals participating in knowledge sharing [45]. The presence and absence of trust may impact stakeholders' willingness to share knowledge [8,63].

## 2.2. Information and knowledge asymmetry in HVLFT

Knowledge sharing in a competitive environment has its own set of challenges. Stakeholders participating in an HVLFT often face the issue of information and knowledge asymmetry [15,48]. Asymmetric infor mation and knowledge have been a concern since the very origin of humanity. There have been several approaches to obtain a fairer outcome of a transaction. One of the earliest records is the envy-free cake cutting technique based on “divide and choose” described in the Bible. Economists have discussed the asymmetric information problem since the eighteenth century [26,39]. An asymmetric information tax onomy was formalised in the 1970s through several notable contribu tions. Akerlof [1] describes the negative financial impact that information discloser has on a transaction from a seller's perspective. The taxonomy is further extrapolated by three models underpinning the behavioural experience between the sender and receiver of asymmetric information. First, we have an adverse selection model proposed by Rothschild and Stiglitz [54]. Today, it is still used in economics, insur ance, and risk management portraying the seller having more informa tion than the buyer. Second, the moral hazard model explains the change in a buyer or a seller's behaviour due to the lack of perceived risk [33]. Finally, Spence [57] describes two parties having access to different information signified by the signalling model as he observes the knowledge gap between organisation and job applicant.

In the quest of finding the best negotiation tactic/strategy in an environment dominated by asymmetric information, a fundamental principle of game theory was developed, and aptly named the revelation principle [20]. This principle is founded on the agent being incentivised to tell the truth [36]. It provides the discovery of the best negotiation mechanism in the least amount of time. Opposing the revelation prin ciple is the Bayesian game theory. It assumes negotiating agents having incomplete information [40,42]. Under the Bayesian game theory and the associated auction strategy, selling an object and trying to maximise the return from multiple buyers is valid from a seller's perspective [41]. Various types of auctions could be used, e.g. English, Dutch, and Sealed bid [52]. The Bayesian game theory is currently the modus operandi for most HVLFT [1]. Such an orientation towards hiding, losing, modifying, intentionally distorting, misappropriating, and not revealing informa tion is at the heart of information asymmetry. We discuss this dark side of knowledge in the following section.

## 2.3. Dark side of knowledge, sticky knowledge and leaky knowledge

The dark side of knowledge is conceptualised as an unwillingness in sharing knowledge to avoid losing a competitive advantage [55]. As described by Alter [3], the dark side of knowledge management includes distortion (e.g. manipulating specific knowledge in favour of particular interests, viewpoints, or beliefs), suppression (e.g. creating obstacles and using knowledge that is contrary to specific interests), and misappro priation (e.g. modification, stealing, or inappropriate revelation of knowledge). In a transaction with multiple unaligned stakeholders, the dark side needs to be revealed and understood how it could influence the knowledge shared among them. In the context of an HVLFT, the rela tionship and interaction between the different stakeholders within the transaction need to be understood [27]. In addition, sticky knowledge and leaky knowledge are also important concepts that characterise knowledge sharing. There are two schools of thought on knowledge stickiness. Education scholars measure knowledge stickiness by how much the transferred knowledge is retained with the learner. Manage ment scholars describe sticky knowledge as tacit knowledge being difficult to codify and transfer [22]. For knowledge supporting an HVLFT, we adhere to the management view of sticky knowledge. Motivational barriers to knowledge transfer are well known in the management literature [34]. Resistance to change, competitive advan tage, job protection, and lack of incentives are some potential barriers; and transfer of knowledge is often laborious, time-consuming, and challenging [61]. Therefore, knowledge stickiness is further exacerbated by the difficulties in the knowledge transfer process within an organisation.

Knowledge leakage occurs when information seeps out of a system, causing knowledge to be lost. For an HVLFT, it is essential to distinguish the difference between the dark side of knowledge and knowledge leakage. The leakage of knowledge is an unintentional loss of knowl edge, but the dark side represents the intentional misuse of knowledge. To use a metaphor, we see knowledge flowing through plumbing pipes between stakeholders. The stickiness causes blockage in the flow, often through time and changing stakeholders; the leakage is the loss of knowledge [17].

Evidently, the literature distinguishes additional concepts of knowledge apart from the classic tacit and explicit division as described. Also, the understanding, experience, and learning are important con siderations in an HVLFT. The different forms of such knowledge (data, information, knowledge, wisdom, understanding, experience, and learning), which is neither tacit nor explicit, belongs to our definition of knowledge dimension X (Fig. 2). In our TEX model, knowledge in dimension X can be formally defined as knowledge that has been sup pressed, distorted, or misappropriated; intentionally or unintentionally; resulting in ambiguity and increased risk in decision making. We suggest that knowledge in dimension X is distinct from tacit and explicit. To further observe and investigate the phenomenon of asymmetric infor mation in an HVLFT, the TEX model and the different forms of knowl edge in dimension X, an exploratory case study using an interpretive lens was employed. The following section describes this case study and the research questions that guided its design.

3. A case study of decision makers, information asymmetry, and dimension X in high-value low-frequency transactions

## 3.1. Introduction

Given the focus of the research inquiry is on the information and knowledge asymmetry in an HVLFT, three overarching research ques tions were formulated. First, what is asymmetric information in an HVLFT? Second, what causes asymmetric information in an HVLFT? Third, how can asymmetric information be counterbalanced in an HVLFT? To answer these questions, and to validate and refine the TEX model including dimension X, an exploratory case study using semistructured interviews, guided by an interpretive lens as the philosoph ical assumption was employed. The case study method has been advanced for information systems research ([67]; [68]). It allows the exploration of a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context to gain insight from the nature and complexity of existing structures and processes ([69]; [70]). The case study method utilises multiple sources of evidence together with deductive and inductive processes to formalise the theory ([69]; [18]; [70]). According to Yin [64], identifying the unit of analysis and the “key” participants are vital to a case study design. We scope our investigation through a case study in the real estate industry of NZ. The NZ residential real estate has all the characteristics of an HVLFT as well as several unique features. For instance, nearly a third of New Zealanders use real estate as a primary long-term investment strategy. To compound matters, a buyer of real estate in NZ does not have a dedicated agent to work on their behalf; instead they depend on the seller's agent to assist with the transaction.

During an initial pilot study, the notion of dimension X in an HVLFT emerge. Also, seven main categories of stakeholders were identified in the NZ real estate industry: buyer, seller, agent, government, lawyer, banker, and specialist [32]. These seven stakeholder categories became the basis for identifying the research participants of the case study. Thirty-two semi-structured interviews were conducted with participants from each of the stakeholders' categories. The interviews were con ducted and recorded via Zoom due to the COVID-19 situation. In total, 983 min of interview data were recorded, transcribed into 305 pages of text and 161,979 words (Table 1).

Due to the nature of high-value and low-frequency, the possibility of single, double, or triple loop learning is less applicable among HVLFT for some stakeholders [4]. ## 4.3. Counterbalancing asymmetric information through the transformation of tacit and X knowledge into explicit knowledge

The red arrow and the blue arrow depicted in the dynamic TEX model as shown in Fig. 4, represent the flow of knowledge between T, E and X. The larger E is, the less uncontrollable risk there is in a trans action. Studying the TEX model aims to minimise X by focusing on the various flows and how they impact the overall transfer of knowledge. The TEX model is continuously changing depending on three main variables. First, the level of knowledge of the participant. Second, the amount of T converted to E. Third, the amount of X knowledge trans ferred to either T or E.

The dimension X represents a category of knowledge that has been withheld, distorted, suppressed and misappropriated in the context of a transaction. It is closely aligned with fraud. The desire to defraud someone (or increase one's financial position) could result in the sup pression or distortion of tacit or explicit knowledge which leads to knowledge in dimension X. This is of particular relevance in the context of high-value transactions, where the value could change significantly if complete knowledge of the item or asset is transparent, available and explicit. The dimension X also incorporates knowledge that is not un derstood or is hard to convey. It contributes to the information and knowledge asymmetry in a transaction. Reducing the dimension X will, in turn, lower the risk in a transaction. A practical example of this happened to a purchaser of a NZ\$250,000 flat in Wellington, NZ. Shortly after purchasing the flat, he found out that he needed an additional NZ \$200,000 to comply with the new earthquake regulation.

Considering the responses to the semi-structured questions posed to the key decision makers and stakeholders involved in the study, and reflecting on the various aforementioned knowledge flows of the TEX model, six prescriptions were developed. These prescriptions not only offer insight to mitigating the problems of information and knowledge asymmetry in general, and particularly in an HVLFT, but also can sup port the design and implementation of knowledge management and decision support systems platforms specifically for HVLFT.

Prescription 1 Reducing X might decrease information and knowledge asymmetry and minimise the risk of a transaction. As mentioned before, the chance for having single, double or triple loop learning for the buyer in HVLFT is low because the opportunity to reflect on mistakes is rare. Since the seller often has the advantage of asymmetric information [44], that could lead to sub-optimal decision-making by the buyer. Besides from real estate [50,53], asymmetric information and knowledge in a transaction have been studied in other contexts like automotive [1,31] and business process design [56]. This inequality should be addressed by educating stakeholders about all three dimensions of the TEX model. The buyer needs to understand the T and E made available to them and be aware of others' motivation to misuse X. For example, a novice real estate buyer presented with E such as technical drawing or report, may not understand its implication to the purchase of the property.

Prescription 2 Educating the stakeholders could reduce the knowledge flow from E or T to X. If the aim is to reduce X by regulating some current practices embedded in certain HVLFT, that would require government or industry regulations to be in place. For example, there is a lack of dedicated professional to support the buyer of real estate in NZ. The seller has an agent that is contractually and legally committed to, and that same agent has hardly any legal obligation towards the buyer (except blatant misuse of information). On the other hand, there is also a lack of motivation from the agent to maximise the sales price for the seller because the commission is structured as a percentage of the sales price.

## 5.2. Generalisability of the TEX model

The TEX model could be applied to other bodies of knowledge outside the real estate industry of NZ or the context of HVLFT. The lack of a dedicated buyers agent within the NZ real estate could partially limit the generalisability in other real estate markets where the sellerbuyer dynamics maybe different.

Every defined body of knowledge would have T, E and X. There are a number of practical challenges in implementing the TEX model. The first challenge is to determine the scope of a specific body of knowledge. Sizing the primary circle of knowledge for a particular industry is complicated and subject to multiple interpretations. The second chal lenge is to attribute the size of the three dimensions for a specific body of knowledge. Particularly, X could be hard to define. The third challenge is the speed of the evolving nature of a specific body of knowledge.

To illustrate these challenges, we could use the boating industry as an example by dividing its evolution into three stages (Fig. 7). In the first stage, when boats are handcrafted and skills are handed down from father to son, the body of knowledge size is small, and the majority of the knowledge is in dimension T. In the second stage, the boats and the body of knowledge grow larger in size. More knowledge is made explicit and shared through different means. Plans are drawn up so that more people could work on and understand what needed to be done to build these bigger boats. In the third stage, we have large ships built with labour and materials from all over the world. This increased complexity expands the body of knowledge size and requires more explicit knowledge such as computer modelling, detailed plans, etc., all of which could be sup ported by a blockchain-based decision support platform (e.g. Fig. 11). As the variations of boats increase from cruise liners to competitive racing boats, these variations could diffuse and split the body of knowledge into multiple ones.

The example of the boating industry can be extrapolated to numerous industries involved in HVLFT, such as high-end jewellery, automotive, and art industry. A practical example can be found in the fake art debacle perpetrated by Knoedler & Company in New York, 2011. Over a period of 15 years Knoedler, a distinguished art gallery sold 30 fake paintings with an estimated value of 63 million dollars [6]. An exhibit is often sold through tacit information and relies on industry to verify the provenance of the exhibit to underpin its value. The transfer of information is often produced for one potential buyer and repeated by the next. Resulting in a lack of saved historical data due to information collected by individual buyers and not by the exhibit. This process of generating, distributing and verifying information is both costly and fallible. It amplifies the interplay between T, E, and X, which signifi cantly increase the risk in such a transaction. Potentially, a blockchainbase decision support system, which allows access to well-defined, his torically complete, trustworthy information for each exhibit could resolve the aforementioned issues. ## 6. Bridging dimension X: Blockchain-based decision support architecture

Blockchain has grown its popularity with Bitcoin since 2008 [43], and it is enjoying a blossoming beyond cryptocurrency and money transfer. It can be used as an architecture to support many types of transactions, from logging an event to signing a document, voting, allocating energy between parties, and far beyond. The annual block chain enterprise application market is set to reach 19.9 billion by 2025 [28].

## 6.1. Blockchain architecture

The blockchain technology provides a decentralised, open, Byzan tine fault-tolerant transaction mechanism, and it promises to become the infrastructure for a new generation of Internet interaction [60,62]. A blockchain is a chain of blocks serving as a continuous ledger of records. A typical block consists of a header with a reference of the previous block and who created it, a time stamp, and a list of records. The most crucial feature is traceability. For each record, it is possible to trace its origin, and by whom it has been created and exchanged. The verification of each transaction is done by every participant (node) of the chain [62]. The blockchain technology solves the double-spend problem with the help of public-key cryptography, whereby each user is assigned a private key, and a public key is shared with all other users. The characteristics and architecture of blockchain are presented in Fig. 8. It is a distributed database comprising records of transactions that are shared among participating parties. Each of these transactions is verified by the consensus of a majority of the participants in the system, making fraudulent transactions unable to pass collective verification. Once a record is created and accepted by the blockchain, it can never be altered [66].

## 6.3. Decision support system architecture with a Blockchain domain layer

To develop a system architecture across an entire industry is chal lenging. Sharing information is currently monetised by the various stakeholders, and they would not benefit directly from sharing their information on a decentralised database. To convince each stakeholder, they need to be motivated to participate in the coalition of the willing. The sheer scale of data and the number of different databases is con founding. Each stakeholder group holds a large amount of data, spread out over multiple databases. In order to organise and streamline the information, a database protocol needs to be agreed upon between the various stakeholders involved. The IPFS (InterPlanetary File System) can be used as a network for storing and sharing data in a distributed file system. The original content can be stored with the actual stakeholders. Whilst the information on the IPFS database would hold the tokenised information that has been attested. Information used in the newly created system architecture can be hashed and time stamped to provide immutable and historical documents that use content addressing to refer back to the original document. Using blockchain as a distributed ledger seems to fulfil most of the characteristics needed to reduce the cost and increase the reliability of information required for an HVLFT. In an environment where there is no direct trust between the various stakeholders, using different layers of attestations to verify documents is fundamental to this real estate blockchain system architecture's success. For example, the government can attest core real estate documents, a lawyer can attest a person's identity and a buyer could add chattels, which can be attested by the real estate agent. ## 7. Discussion and conclusion

One of the implications of applying the TEX model in the context of real estate of NZ is to increase E by reducing T and X for each stakeholder in a transaction to lower the risk for everyone. But since it is not bene ficial to all stakeholders to share what they know, that affects the motivation for them to minimise T and X. Potentially, we could ameliorate that by utilising knowledge-supporting information systems, which are often linked to knowledge management in the context of or ganisations but not industry [25,29,37,47,58].

One emerging technology that could be meaningfully used together with the TEX model is blockchain. It is based on the concept of having a single source of truth that is decentralised, immutable, historical and verifiable [60,65]. Using blockchain as a distributed ledger seems to fulfil many of the characteristics needed to reduce the risk and increase the reliability of shared information and knowledge in a transaction. The different stakeholders have different motivation to accept or reject technological responses. The acceptance of new technology is often costdriven (resist) or efficiency (accept) as illustrated in Table 8.

Using our study as an example, initially among the seven stake holders, only two would benefit directly from introducing a blockchain solution. The council may increase their total knowledge per property. The buyer may lower the due diligence process cost and benefit from the increased amount of verifiable information and knowledge. In the case of real estate, the government has to be the catalyst in driving change that could lower the risk and cost in all the transactions. They should provide policies to counterbalance asymmetric information and knowledge through minimizing T and X while maximising E on the asset to be transacted. As for the seller, risk reduction does positively impact the sales price. since higher perceived risk correlates with lower price a buyer is willing to offer [35]. The agent, lawyer, banker or specialist could be motivated to share knowledge more explicitly if the seller ac quires and registers various reports and assessments of their property to a blockchain solution, which provides an in-depth and complete picture of the asset to reduce the risk to the buyer. If such behaviour is rewarded by the market or the policymaker, eliminating asymmetric information and knowledge for HVLFT could be transformed into a competitive advantage [46].

The TEX model is an incipient attempt at (a) exploring dimension X (b) illustrating knowl edge asymmetry (c) visualising the risk for key decision makers and stakeholders in a transaction and (d) foundational for building decision support and knowledge sharing platforms. The TEX model could be used as a lens to view the risks for key decision makers in many types of transactions. We believe it is generalisable to other categories of HVLFT in different industries.
