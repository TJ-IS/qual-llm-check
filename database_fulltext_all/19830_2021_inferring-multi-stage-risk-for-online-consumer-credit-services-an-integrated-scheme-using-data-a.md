# Inferring multi-stage risk for online consumer credit services: An integrated scheme using data augmentation and model enhancement

Jilei Zhou <sup>a</sup>, Cong Wang <sup>b,\*</sup>, Fei Ren <sup>b</sup>, Guoqing Chen <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Information, Renmin University of China, Beijing 100872, China

<sup>b</sup> Guanghua School of Management, Peking University, Beijing 100871, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online consumer credit risk Default Delinquency Phone usage data Machine learning Model interpretation Welfare analysis

## A B S T R A C T

In recent years, online consumer credit services have emerged in e-commerce. Although such services boost sales, the best way to allocate credit to consumers is a critical issue to be explored. In this paper, a comprehensive scheme is proposed using data augmentation and model enhancement to infer online consumer credit risk. The proposed scheme augments consumer profiles by incorporating phone usage information to alleviate the “thin file” challenge and enhance the predictive model by taking a multi-staged view of consumers’ repayment timing to achieve a more finely grained credit risk determination. A three-step analysis, including prediction evaluation, model interpretation using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), and welfare analysis, was performed to evaluate our proposed scheme’s efficacy. We found that phone usage information enhanced predictive perfor mance and that underlying psychological mechanisms can be analyzed by corresponding feature interpretations to theories. The follow-up welfare analysis illustrates the business value of the proposed scheme.

## 1. Introduction

Online consumer credit services have become an important means, in addition to credit cards, for making purchases on e-commerce platforms. Such services enable online consumers to divide payments into in stallments over several months. Prominent e-commerce platforms such as Amazon.com, Taobao.com, and JD.com increasingly encourage their consumers to use services such as the “buy-now-pay-later” scheme (see Fig. 1). This is a convenient tool to facilitate purchase conversion and minimize losses. For example, Ant Check Later, an online consumer credit service provided by Taobao.com (see Fig. 1(b)), reported that consumers who spent less than 1000 yuan (about 153 dollar) per month now spend 50% more online using the Ant Check Later service [1].

Although they increase sales, online consumer credit services must deal with one critical issue—consumer credit risk assessment. Given the high balances of online consumer credit services, a high level of credit risk could severely hamper the profit of e-commerce platforms. Consider JD Baitiao (the credit service provided by JD.com (see Fig. 1(c)) as an example, its balance was 10.7 billion dollar until 2019; however, expanding its consumer base led to a 23.4% increase in the consumer default rate [2]. In this regard, inferring the potential credit risk of consumers when allocating credit to the “buy-now-pay-later” scheme becomes quite significant and meaningful for platforms.

Practitioners face two challenges in addressing the issue of consumer risk profiling. One is the “thin file” challenge caused by a lack of con sumer information in the online consumer credit service context. Unlike traditional credit scoring for credit cards wherein borrowers’ official credit records (e.g., Fair Isaac Corporation (FICO) scores and credit histories) are available [3] and for microloan/peer-to-peer (P2P) lending wherein borrowers are required to submit abundant personal information (e.g., income, contacts, etc.) [4], online consumer credit services require very limited information from consumers because they aim at facilitating the online shopping process. Using JD Baitiao as an example, the information that consumers must submit includes only a valid identification (ID) number, a cell phone number. and a debit card number. For online consumer credit services, such information is quite limited and could hardly be regarded as sufficient for effectively judging consumer credit risk. Therefore, profiling consumer risk in a more comprehensive manner is desirable and necessary. Another challenge is assigning the proper consumer repayment timing partition when determining consumer credit risk. In practice, it is often presented as a binary setting; whether or not consumers repay on time determines their dichotomous credit risk status [5–7]. Not repaying on time is generally considered unfavorable and affects consumers’ future loan prospects, which usually then excludes potential borrowers that do not repay on time. However, with online shopping, the consequence of not repaying on time might be mixed. Some observations of online consumer credit services have revealed that not repaying on time, from the perspective of platform profit, is not necessarily unfavorable and could even be desirable since late payments might induce additional gains in the form of interest and fines [8]. Therefore, the binary setting for repayment timing requires in-depth investigation to maximize profits.

To address the two aforementioned challenges, we devised a comprehensive scheme using data augmentation and model enhance ment (see Fig. 2). To tackle the “thin file” challenge, we augmented the data by incorporating additional pieces of information about consumers from external sources. In this study, we collected and used extractable consumer mobile phone usage data to predict consumer credit risk. Notably, mobile phone usage behavior reflects consumers’ personality traits [9,10]. Several studies [9,11] and industry practice (e.g., the company LenddoEFL) have shown that psychometrics, especially per sonality traits, help to credit risk prediction. Based on the relationships between psychometrics and credit risk, a model using this augmented data to objectively capture psychometrics can better explain and predict consumer credit risk. For the challenge of properly setting the repay ment timing partition, the conventional dichotomous prediction module was enhanced by taking a more finely grained perspective. The key enhancement of the more finely grained view is in identifying the stages of risk in terms of repayment timing whereby a three-staged partition is used to describe consumers’ repayment behaviors—the “grace” stage, the “delinquency” stage, and the “default” stage. Grace means repaying within a tolerable period, delinquency means repaying outside the tolerable period but not too late, and default means repaying too late or not at all. The inclusion of both the grace and delinquency stages is deemed both economically and operationally beneficial to platforms. Economically, allocating credit to consumers who make repayments during the delinquency stage can provide both a sales boost and higher marginal income because platforms can impose additional fines on de linquent payments. Operationally, differentiating and analyzing con sumers into different stages can enable e-commerce platforms to better manage credit risk. Consumers in delinquency and default might have different psychological antecedents [12], which can help platforms execute corresponding interventions to prevent delinquency from pro gressing into default.

To systematically evaluate the efficacy of our proposed scheme, a three-step empirical analysis was performed on a real-world dataset collected from an online consumer credit service provider with phone usage information obtained from a Chinese communications operator (see Fig. 3). Step 1 aims at understanding the predictive power of our proposed scheme. Given the ordered nature of the labels of consumer credit risks, an ordinal classification problem was considered. To handle the sample imbalances caused by differentiating between grace, de linquency, and default consumers, both individual and ensemble clas sifiers (i.e., random forest, AdaBoost, XGBoost, and stacking) were implemented and evaluated. Based on the best-performing predictive model, Step 2 conducts a model interpretation to provide decisionmakers with managerial insights regarding online consumer credit ser vices. A theoretically grounded explanatory method, i.e., Lundberg and Lee’s [13] Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) framework, is used to analyze the roles that various features play in identifying each type of consumer credit risk. Step 3 is a follow-up welfare analysis that provides additional managerial insights from both platforms’ and consumers perspectives.

The proposed scheme yields several interesting findings. First, from the prediction results, the enriched consumer profiles created by incorporating phone usage information significantly increase the model’s predictive performance and the stacking meta learning (StaML) is more advantageous than the others. Second, the SHAP model inter pretation framework provides several insights to practitioners regarding multi-stage credit risk management. Furthermore, by combining the significant features selected by the Shapley values with the theoretical underpinnings of phone usage data, we create a useful example for future research that unpacks the role of personality in considering a more finely grained partitioning of credit risk. Third, the welfare ana lyses illustrate that the proposed scheme benefits both platforms and consumers in need. With our scheme, more consumers that would be excluded by means of conventional inspection are approved. In other words, our scheme can help consumers to access appropriate and affordable financial services (i.e., online consumer credit service), which is consistent with the notion of financial inclusion [14].

According to the design science research knowledge contribution framework proposed by Gregor and Hevner [43], our paper is an ex aptation research which constructs a socio-technical artifact to extend known solutions (i.e., phone usage data and ordinal classification methods) to new problems (i.e., online consumer credit service). In summary, the proposed method aims to extract main design principles which not only contribute to design theory but also help practitioners to solve the “thin file” problem and automate the multi-stage credit scoring process. The proposed method is deemed to be of decision support na ture in twofold: one is to support the decision-making for the users of the online platforms in acquiring or providing credit services with the designed functionalities; and the other is to support the decision-making for the platforms in credit allocation as well as to serve as a core system component with designed functionalities.

Next, individual classifiers dominate the credit-scoring literature. However, prior studies have demonstrated that ensemble methods perform better than individual methods for the purpose of credit scoring [15]. ## 2.2. Categorization of consumer information: hard vs. soft

The informational input into credit risk predictive models can be categorized into “hard information” and “soft information”. Hard in formation directly reflects borrowers’ financial status. Soft information, such as social network characteristics and facial photographs, in contrast to hard information, has no direct relationship with a borrower's financial status. Early research, in which borrower demographics and transactional features were most commonly used as model inputs, emphasized the role of hard information in predicting borrower default. In recent years, soft information, especially social media information, has exhibited good predictive power $[ 6 , 2 0 , 2 6 ]$

An emerging form of soft information considered by recent studies is mobile phone usage data, which include a series of records including phone calls, messages, data volume, and app usage. In practice, phone usage data present some advantages. First, phone usage data are abundant and universal because of the widespread adoption of personal mobile phones. In 2020, the number of global mobile phone users is projected to total 3.6 billion [27], with a penetration rate of 46.2% given the current global population of 7.8 billion. Second, phone usage data are authentic since they are automatically recorded.

As shown in Table 1, phone usage data has demonstrated the emerging predictability in credit risk detection. Both San Pedro et al. [18] and Oskarsd<sup>´</sup> ottir ´ et al. [24] focus on credit card service and demonstrate the added value of including call data for credit scoring. Bjorkegren ¨ and Grissen [25] predict repayment behaviors of thin-file subscribers who were offered telecom’s postpaid plans and opted in. Our study is similar to Bjorkegren ¨ and Grissen [25] in motivation, which is introducing phone usage data to augment thin-file individuals’ pro files. Instead of late payment behaviors of postpaid bills, our study wants to go further and link phone usage data to the credit risk of financial services (i.e., online consumer credit service). Moreover, our study in vestigates the predictive power of phone usage data in distinguishing between the various stages of consumer credit risk and explores the interpretation of the multiple features.

## 2.3. Theoretical underpinnings of applying phone usage data

Previous research about phone usage data in borrower default pre diction adopted a solely data-driven approach without any theoretical underpinnings [7,23]; that is, it is predictive rather than explanatory. In this section, based on the theoretical perspective of personality, we expect the consumer personality traits underlying phone usage behavior to influence multi-stage repayment behaviors.

Studies on the antecedents of credit scores suggest that different personalities might influence default-related economic behaviors, including planning behaviors, control spending, and debt behaviors [9,11]. The “Big Five” personality model is used for assessing person ality that hierarchically organizes personality traits into five key dimensions—conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroti cism, and openness [28]. In previous studies, the direct measurement of personality traits usually relies on data drawn from lengthy question naires, which is at odds with the efficient and convenient nature of consumer credit services. As consumers’ personality traits are reflected in their daily behaviors, data analytics can provide clues about con sumers’ personality traits and therefore be linked to their credit risk. Extant research has established a reliable relationship between mobile phone usage data and personality traits (see Table 2). Since phone usage data are able to capture many different behaviors, we organized a behavioral framework derived from previous studies comprised of social interactions, daily activities, and mobility patterns [29–31]. Social in teractions refer to behaviors describing telecommunications contacts and social connections. Daily activities summarize activities that satisfy an individual’s daily needs. Mobility patterns characterize the geographic areas in which a person typically spends most of her or his work and leisure time.

## 3. Data and feature set construction

The credit data used in this study were randomly selected from an online consumer credit service provider in China established in 2006. When making purchases from online merchants verified by the platform, consumers can divide their purchase payments into installments made over no more than six months by using the online consumer credit ser vice. Consumers’ repayment data were matched with their phone usage data as provided by a major Chinese communications operator, forming an augmented consumer dataset used in our study, which contains detailed personal behavioral data including demographic, transactional, and repayment data for 16,481 consumers in 2017.

In our study, consumers’ repayments were distinguished into three different stages, i.e., grace, delinquency, and default. Given the fact that persistent delinquency is a prevalent form of consumer default [37], consumers’ repayments were analyzed at the loan rather than the in stallment level. Following Chehrazi and Weber’s [12] definition, transactions that were fewer than 30 days past due were considered in the “grace” stage. Notably, customers who paid on time are included as the members of the grace group. By contrast, transactions that were more than 30 days past due were considered delinquent. Transactions finally entered the default stage when consumers failed to repay for more than 90 days. Using these partitions, the number of consumers corresponding to the grace, delinquency, and default groups was 11,710, 2211, and 2560, respectively. To handle the imbalance between the various classes, an oversampling technique was used for the two smaller classes.

Feature set construction is an essential step in developing a credit risk profiling scheme. The intrinsic features and phone-related features were combined, formulating a 443 dimension feature set. Intrinsic fea tures analyze consumers’ demographic and transactional characteristics whereas phone-related features distinguish each consumer by analyzing her or his phone usage behavior.

Traditionally, most supervised learning models of consumer default prediction are constructed using intrinsic features including borrower demographics and transactional characteristics [5,8,19]. Incorporating previously used variables from this dataset, a comprehensive intrinsic feature set was formed. Concretely, the extracted variables were separated into two sets. The first set contains statistics related to consumers characteristics including age, gender, and registration information from the e-commerce platform. The second set includes descriptive transactional characteristics such as store ID, bill installment period, bill down payment, total loan amount, etc. From the data, online consumer credit services cover a vast untapped pool of unbanked users whose official credit records (i.e., FICO scores) are unavailable, resulting in a major challenge for service providers.

The other part of the feature set is comprised of features extracted from phone usage information. Based on the discussion in Section 2.3 as well as data availability, we likewise emphasized three specific types of phone usage behaviors—social interactions, daily activities, and mobility patterns. The phone usage features used in our study are not identical to the features explored in previous studies (see Table 2) because of differences between the data sources. Moreover, several new features that were not covered in previous studies are included herein such as features related to social interactions, namely, statistics on their number of contacts, the regularity of phone calls, and text messages. Features related to daily activities were separated into usage time fea tures and service features. Usage time features include the time spent by consumers on phone calls, text messages, and the sum of phone calls and messages, which imply individuals’ sleeping patterns. Services features encompass the frequency of various types of services, such as police, bank, lawyer, casino services, etc. In light of previous studies, mobility patterns based on phone usage data were summarized in terms of the number of trips, total days on a trip, frequency of departures and des tinations, and the number of distinct departure and destination pairs. To differentiate between the short- and long-term effects, the features were calculated into granularities of one, two, and three months. The daily statistics were further decomposed into different time slots, i.e., morn ing, afternoon, and night.

## 4. Model results, interpretation, and prescriptive insights

This section focuses on eliciting the best predictive method for the proposed credit risk profiling scheme. To differentiate between multistage repayment behaviors (as shown in Fig. 2), a multi-class classifi cation problem was formulated in light of staged partitioning. Further more, given the ordered nature of the labels of three classes, an ordinal classification problem was considered. Our goal was to elicit the bestperforming classifier using various data types to form a credit risk profiling scheme and accordingly provide an interpretation of the model.

Both individual and ensemble classifiers were considered for evalu ation including four individual classifiers—artificial neural networks (ANN), logistic regressions (LR), and support vector machines (SVM)— as well as parallel and sequential ensembles. Two popular types of ensemble methods, bagging and boosting, which correspond to parallel and sequential frameworks, respectively, were compared in our study. The random forest (RF) method was used to represent the bagging al gorithm, adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) represent the boosting algorithm, while stacking meta learning (StaML) represent the heterogeneous ensemble learning algo rithm. ## 4.2. Model interpretation using SHAP framework

Although the StaML algorithm exhibited the best predictive accuracy in classifying multi-stage consumer credit risk, the model remains a black box model not subject to interpretation. In addition to predictive performance, the ability to properly interpret a predictive model’s output is extremely important. To achieve accurate and consistent model interpretation, SHAP, a unified framework proposed by Lundberg and Lee [13], was applied to our study. The goal of SHAP is to explain the prediction of an instance by computing the contribution of each feature to the overall prediction. The contribution was measured by Shapley values derived from coalitional game theory and instructions about how to fairly distribute the “gain” (i.e., prediction) among the “players” (i.e., features) in the “game” (i.e., prediction task) [39].

Notably, the Shapley values can be done on both global samples and individual cases [13], which makes the proposed scheme interpretable enough for both practitioners and consumers. For the consumers, an interpretable credit scoring model can provide clear explanation of the rejection of applications. An example of individual SHAP value is illustrated in Fig. 4. For a consumer who is predicted to be a defaulter, the value of “max\_contact\_early\_morning\_cnt” increases the default risk by 13%, while the value of “max\_contact\_holiday\_cnt” decreases the default risk by 6%.

For the e-commerce platforms, interpreting the consumer credit risk profiling model not only reveals how a model might be improved but also explains the relative importance of the various features of multistage credit risk to generate valuable managerial insights. Figs. 5 (a)- (c) illustrate the Shapley value summary plots that combine feature importance with feature effects. Each point on the summary plot rep resents a Shapley value for a feature and an instance. The position on the y-axis is determined by the feature and on the x-axis by the Shapley value. The color represents the value of the feature, ranging from low (blue) to high (red). Overlapping points are jittered in the y-axis direc tion, giving a sense of the distribution of Shapley values by feature. The features are ordered according to their importance. According to Lundberg et al. [13], the idea behind SHAP feature importance is simple: features with large absolute Shapley values are important. For example, in Fig. 5(a), the reg\_period (i.e., lifetime of the cell phone number) feature is a strong predictor for detecting grace period transactions. The color of the reg\_period feature indicates that a higher value for the reg\_period feature increases the grace period probability. Other features can be similarly explained.

## 5. Business value analysis

Based on the best-performing predictive model combining phone usage data and the StaML algorithm, a follow-up welfare analysis was conducted to provide additional managerial insights.

## 5.1. Welfare for platforms: higher accuracy, more returns

The above results have shown that the statistical performance of the comprehensive scheme using data augmentation and model enhance ment is significantly better than models without phone usage data. Another question that remains to be answered is, whether deploying advanced classification models is economically sensible for the e-com merce platforms.

A related measure used in earlier credit scoring research is Expected Maximum Profit (EMP), which aims to select the classifiers with the highest profit [16,24]. The EMP measure takes into account the ex pected profits and losses of credit granting, and is tailored towards the business goal of credit scoring. For the EMP, various parameters need to be specified, including a constant return on investment (ROI) and the distribution of loss given default (LGD). Based on the methodology by Bravo et al. [41] and Verbraken et al. [16], Brovo et al. [42] have pro grammed an R package, that is publicly available at CRAN, which pro vides a computational tool to calculate EMP.

The results can be seen in Fig. 6. The value of EMP measures the incremental profit as compared to not building a credit scoring model. The EMP fraction represents the fraction of applications that should be rejected in order to obtain the maximum profit. According to the results of EMP, we find that model StaML and model AdaBoost has the highest profit, followed by models LR and XGBoost. Moreover, the ranking of the values for the EMP is largely consistent with the ranking of the CEM values in Table 6, and again model StaML is considered best. Based on the EMP fraction values, the fraction for model StaML is higher than the rest with the profit remaining the same. indicating that the model fo: cuses on the most profitable customers.

## 6.1. Practical implications

The proposed method helps to solve the “thin file” problem by sug gesting an alternative data source for determining consumer credit risk. Due to the fierce competition among e-commerce platforms, online consumer credit services turn to untapped, unbanked consumers with thin financial data, such as FICO score, official credit records, default history etc. Our proposed method addresses this problem by using a non financial data $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ phone usage features and transactional character istics) to predict credit risk. The phone usage data used in our method show great predictive power and, more importantly, are abundant, universal, and authentic; their credibility and generality can be guar anteed. The integrated tool proposed in this study also helps to automate the multi-stage credit scoring process with higher accuracy and more profits. In the context of online consumer credit services, traditional manual evaluation is labor- and time-intensive. It might also introduce human bias. We establish an effective, low-cost tool with which ecommerce platforms can handle sparse and imbalanced observations. Furthermore, using the SHAP framework not only provides insights into how the model can be improved but also supports understanding of the modeling process and helps practitioners to better conduct risk management.

In summary, the proposed method aims to extract main design principles which not only contribute to design theory but also help practitioners to solve the “thin file” problem and automate the multistage credit scoring process. The implementer of the proposed method is e-commerce platforms with online consumer credit services. The user of the proposed method includes both providers and applicants of online consumer credit services. The context of the proposed method is the credit allocation process where e-commerce platforms should evaluate multi-stage credit risks of thin-file consumers.

## 6.2. Theoratical contributions

Our study contributes to the literature on credit scoring in the following ways. First, we develop a credit scoring scheme that takes a multi-stage credit risk model into account. Most previous studies have adopted a binary view of repayment timing when determining default, i. e., whether consumers repay on time or not. Such arbitrary filtration can result in profit losses because they ignore the potential profit to be gained from delinquent consumers. Second, we enrich the literature by including phone usage data, a new type of soft information, in predicting consumer credit risk. The identified features play a significant role in predicting credit risk at different stages. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to introduce phone usage features for credit risk prediction in online consumer credit services. Third, we extend the literature by making a methodological contribution. Our study is one of the first to propose a consolidated credit risk profiling scheme using both data augmentation and model enhancement for consumer credit ser vices. The integrated scheme, combining a state-of-the-art machinelearning algorithm with phone usage data, offers a tool with a high level of predictive power for handling finely grained classifications of credit risk. Lastly, this study adds theoretical implications by interpreting the complex black box model by calculating Shapley values for each feature. It is the first in the credit scoring literature to apply a SHAP framework to interpreting complex machine-learning models. A small overlap was found for selected important features between the different stages, which can enable platforms to execute corresponding interventions for the various types of deviant credit behaviors. Moreover, through the lens of personality. we provide more prescriptive insights into the various relationships between personality traits (i.e., the Big Five) and the three repayment stages (i.e., grace, delinquency, and default). To our knowledge, our study is the first to unpack the role of personality in multi-stage credit risk evaluation.

## 6.3. Legal and ethical consideration

We first discuss the legal issue for using phone usage data. In China, the “telecommunications and internet personal user data protection regulations” were established in 2013, which deals with if, and how, personal user data collected in the process of providing telecommuni cations services and Internet information services can be used. The regulations in particular mandates that telecommunications business operators and Internet information service providers should clearly notify users about the objective, method and scope of information collection and use, the period of information preservation, the channels to consult and correct information, as well as the consequences for refusing to provide information and other matters. According to the regulations in China, the legitimacy of our study should be discussed in which data are collected from a China telecom company. The consumers used in this study are randomly selected from one of the online con sumer credit service platforms in China. Before consumers request a service on the platform, they are asked to sign an informed consent, where the platform explains in detail that the information of individuals may be given to a third-party organization under the protection of pri vacy. Besides, all data used in this study are anonymous. Since the proposed method must rely on consumers’ phone usage data, an implementation can be designed to mitigate privacy risks. It can be opt in, so that only consumers who consent are screened with the system.

In addition, the ethical use of privacy data in credit scoring has been an area of increased focus for researchers over the last couple of years [7,24,25]. Accordingly, the ethical problem of collecting and analyzing consumers’ behavior data in the context of online consumer credit ser vice requires careful consideration. There is an ethical concern in using data that depends on the social interaction (e.g., the time to sleep) and the demographics (e.g., gender) of the borrowers to restrict funding to them. This is of course not a practice that should be recommended from the results of our method, as it would constitute unfair discrimination. However, when the unbanked do not have any official credit informa tion that allows e-commerce platforms to make a decision. then social interaction variables and demographics can clearly contribute to in crease financial inclusion. Thus, we propose that the use of this data be done in strict positive terms. This can be easily done when constructing a credit scoring DSS: we can simply assign the neutral score to the infor mation which would unfairly punish the borrowers, leaving the positive score that would provide easier access to online consumer credit ser vices. For the transparency of the credit scoring DSS, we also propose that an implementation can be designed to provide transparency as well as mitigate privacy risks. For example, when rejecting applicants, a summary of the credit risk can be revealed to them rather than the un derlying features describing the behavior.

## 7. Conclusion

This study focuses on the credit allocation problem in emerging online consumer credit services and presents a consolidated consumer credit risk profiling scheme that integrates additional consumer behavioral data to predict repayment behaviors and analyze their un derlying theoretical rationales. To better quantify consumer credit risk, we exerted a granular view of consumer repayment behaviors to distinguish between multiple stages of consumer credit risk. The stateof-the-art results of our model in predicting three types of late repay ment behaviors (i.e., grace, delinquency, and default) support the idea that phone usage data are effective in inferring consumers’ repayment quality when allocating credit. To interpret the complex black box model, a unified framework, SHAP, was applied in calculating the contribution of each feature. Significant cues identified from the process were used to detect default behavior in practice. In addition, through the lens of personality, we constructed a link between consumer phone usage behavior and their personality traits and provided an underlying psychological mechanism for predicting deviant consumer credit be haviors. Finally, a series of welfare analyses were performed, demon strating the effectiveness and advantages of our proposed scheme in effectuating higher welfare to both platforms and consumers in need.

Our work merits future research in the following aspects. First, future studies might test the psychological mechanisms of phone usage be haviors in predicting multi-stage credit risk. Second, the value of other forms of soft information (e.g., browsing and searching behaviors on ecommerce platforms) could be further explored. Finally, future research might extend our cross-sectional dataset to panel data, which could generate insight into how changes in borrowers’ phone usage patterns predict the varying hazard rates of borrower default.
