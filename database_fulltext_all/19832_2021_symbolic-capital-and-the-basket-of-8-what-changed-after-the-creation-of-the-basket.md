# Symbolic capital and the basket of 8: What changed after the creation of the basket?

Wallace Chipidza <sup>a,\*,1</sup>, John Trip p <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Center for Information Systems and Technology, Claremont Graduate University, Claremont, CA, USA

<sup>b</sup> Department of Management, Clemson University, Clemson, SC, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Senior scholars basket Symbolic capital Collaboration Publication inequality Journal quality

## A B S T R A C T

The creation of the AIS College of Senior Scholars' basket of journals (SSB) was an attempt to address the perception that. compared with other fields, only a few Information Systems (IS) journals were widely considered elite-level journals in tenure and promotion cases. We propose that the creation of the SSB provided a signal to the IS field that conveved an additional stock of symbolic capital to the journals in the SSB. We theorize that the symbolic capital signal provided by the SSB changed the individual publication behaviors of IS researchers, specifically regarding collaboration and submission concentration. We observe that this behavioral change reduced journal participation inequality and influenced journal quality metrics for journals included and excluded from the SSB. We report a study using 5830 articles from ten journals over sixteen years (2000–2015). Our results suggest that IS scholars responded to the symbolic capital signal of the SSB in a bifurcated manner. Regarding IS research production, the level of collaboration increased at most journals, and a broader group of IS researchers published in those journals. This shift in behavior led to a reduction of inequality regarding insti tutional and geographic representation in publishing. However, we also report unexpected variation in the journal quality metrics of SSB journals vis-a-vis \` non-SSB journals that suggest that IS researchers largely ignored the SSB signal from a research consumption standpoint. Finally, we provide several recommendations for the continued evaluation of the SSB by the Senior Scholars and tenure and promotion committees.

## 1. Introduction

In early 2007, the Association for Information Systems (AIS) College of Senior Scholars identified a “basket” of journals (SSB) as the top journals in the IS field. The basket initially included six journals: Man agement Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ), Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS), Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS), European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), and Information Systems Journal (ISJ). In 2011 the SSB was enlarged to include two more journals, Journal of

Strategic Information Systems (JSIS) and Journal of Information Technology (JIT), to become the “Basket of 8” [1].<sup>2</sup> The SSB was created with at least one explicit goal: “to provide more consistency and meaningfulness to tenure and promotion cases.”<sup>3</sup> Many within the IS community believed that the low number of highly esteemed IS journals hindered the ability of young scholars to achieve tenure and earn further promotion [2]. Quoting findings from earlier bibliometric studies, Dennis et al. [2] characterized MISQ and ISR as the only IS journals “beyond reproach” regarding their quality (p. 4). At the same time, the authors found that only a minuscule fraction of junior IS faculty successfully published in these two journals. Due to the associated difficulty of achieving tenure, an academic field risks extinction if it possesses few quality publication venues [2].<sup>4</sup>

Tenure committees typically emphasize journals included in well established lists such as those compiled by the Financial Times,<sup>5</sup> the University of Texas at Dallas, the Association of Business Schools (ABS), the Australian Business Deans Council, or the German Academic Asso ciation of Business Research (VHB). We argue that the impact of credible entities' evaluation of journal quality is recognized through the alloca tion of symbolic capital. Symbolic capital means the amount of prestige – good reputation, impeccability, respect – an entity holds [4,5]. In any context, when actors that possess high credibility make positive judg ments about other elements within that context, the credible actors (e.g., the Senior Scholars) allocate symbolic capital to the other elements (i.e., the journals). As symbolic capital is allocated, the resulting configura tion of symbolic capital leads to significant uniformity of perception among participating entities. Symbolic capital thus serves as “(an) in strument … of social integration” that makes possible a consensus on the state of the world [5] (p. 79). In the context of the IS field, journal rankings from credible bodies allocate symbolic capital, which leads to more uniform perceptions regarding the quality of the ranked journals. The consensus on the state of journal quality often proves consequential in tenure cases [6].

Although MISQ and ISR have been universally accepted as being excellent journals when evaluating tenure cases, many IS scholars, especially those outside the United States, have held other journals such as JMIS, ISJ, EJIS, Decision Support Systems, IEEE-Transactions on Engi neering Management, and Information & Management in high esteem. As such, these journals (and others) have historically been positively evaluated in tenure and promotion cases at many institutions worldwide [see 7,8]. The Senior Scholars' creation of the SSB was intended to signal to the field that other journals, especially from outside Region 1, deserved to be perceived as excellent journals.

We argue that the creation of the SSB allocated a new stock of symbolic capital among IS journals – over and above that conferred by established rankings. This likely would not result in significant changes to perceptions of MISQ and ISR which were already judged to be “un questionably top quality research journals” [2] (p. 2). However, the additional stock of symbolic capital conferred by the SSB served as a new signal to IS scholars that the other SSB journals were of higher quality and more “excellent” than non-SSB journals [1]. The SSB provided a mechanism for IS researchers to “gauge the relevant hidden qualities” of competing journals [4] (p. 223).

This allocation of social capital by the SSB was made even stronger by the manner of presentation of results in the AIS research ranking service (RSS).<sup>6</sup> The AIS established both the SSB and RSS, and the latter only considers publication in SSB journals for ranking. Using the RSS, rankings can be calculated using the full SSB or configurable subgroups of the SSB journals. No journals outside the SSB can be searched in the RSS, and thus, placing high in the RSS requires a focus on publication within the SSB. As institutions and researchers tout their ranking in the RSS, the RSS reinforces the level of symbolic capital allocated by the SSB.

At its inception in 2007, there were debates on whether the SSB should include six or eight journals. This dispute was settled in 2011 with the official inclusion of JSIS and JIT in the SSB [8,9]. That JIT and JSIS fought to be included in the SSB underscores the perceived importance of inclusion in the SSB. This perception of importance is well-founded. For example, recent news articles from IS departments tout their faculty's achievements as reflected in rankings as calculated in the RSS (e.g., the University of Cologne,<sup>7</sup> University of Florida,<sup>8</sup> the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology,<sup>9</sup> and Temple Uni versity<sup>10</sup>). This suggests that institutions across the world highly value authorship in the SSB journals. Further, recent IS literature reviews frequently limit their samples to the SSB (e.g., [10]), and scientometric studies have considered the collaboration network of SSB authors as representative of the IS field (e.g., [1,11,12]).

Assuming that the SSB sent a signal regarding the quality and desirability of publishing at its constituent journals, it is likely that re searchers would identify the signal sent by the allocation of new sym bolic capital and determine that it is more attractive to publish in the newly designated SSB journals vis-a-vis non-SSB journals. If true, this change in perception likely will drive changes in researchers' publication behaviors.

Further, suppose the community of IS researchers received the symbolic capital signal. In that case, high-quality research would likely be more concentrated in the SSB journals vs. non-SSB journals. If this is true, the relative perceived quality of the research published in the SSB would diverge from the perceived quality of that published in non-SSB journals after the establishment of the SSB. The previous discussion motivates the following research questions:

## RQ 1. Did the creation of the SSB change researchers' behaviors?

## RQ 2. Did the creation of the SSB change the relative journal quality of SSB journals versus non-SSB journals?

Anecdotal evidence exists that the symbolic capital signal created by the SSB was received by the field, as the term “basket of 8” has infiltrated the IS lexicon. Schools advertise SSB publications in job postings and recommend to junior faculty and Ph.D. students to target their sub missions to its constituent journals. However, to date, the impact of the SSB on researcher behavior and associated journal-level outcomes has not been empirically examined. Further, little is understood regarding the mechanisms through which the SSB designation led to these out comes. To explore this phenomenon, we investigate both individual publication behavior and journal-level quality outcomes of the SSB.

We propose that the SSB signal was likely to change researchers behavior regarding both the production of research and the consump tion of research. By production of research, we mean the processes through which researchers generate publications. For instance, as coauthored articles have higher perceived quality [13] and increase pro ductivity as measured by the number of authored publications [14], the SSB might drive authors to collaborate more and with a wider variety of authors who may have previously published in the SSB.

By consumption of research, we mean the patterns by which re searchers search for and utilize (i.e., cite) previously published work. If the field received the SSB signal, it would mean that researchers would have higher esteem for research published in the SSB and that they would be more likely to include citations of that research in their studies. This would potentially lead to a divergence in the perceived quality of SSB journals vis-a-vis \` non-SSB journals, which may be observable through proxies such as citation counts and other traditional metrics.

To compare SSB vs. non-SSB journals, we include in our study two other journals, Decision Support Systems (DSS) and Information and Management (IM).<sup>11</sup> These journals are not part of the SSB but are considered to possess commensurate quality with at least some of the SSB journals by many IS scholars and other ranking bodies such as the Association of Business Schools (ABS). 12

The remainder of this article proceeds as follows. First, we provide a theoretical background for our investigation into variation in researcher behavior post-SSB by using the proxy of changes in the social structure of the discipline. Second, we theorize regarding the measures of journal quality that reflect potential changes in perceived quality of journal output post-SSB. Next, we report the results of a study that explores differences in both individual-level and journal-level outcomes of the SSB. Finally, we provide a discussion regarding our results and their potential implications.

## 2. Theoretical motivation

This section elaborates on our theoretical bases for the anticipated impacts of the SSB on individual-level publishing behavior and overall journal quality.

## 2.1. Symbolic capital

Symbolic capital accrues from possession of prestige, status, and positive reputation [15] and can belong to individuals, organizations, and other entities [16]. According to Bourdieu [17], competition for symbolic power in an academic field drives researchers' behavior. Journal rankings are a prominent source of symbolic capital to the extent that they influence which journals researchers target for sub mission [18]. Decisions by institutions such as Web of Science, Financial Times, and in the case of IS, the College of Senior Scholars to rank journals or create exclusive lists may end up determining the prestige and reputation of academic scholars and institutions, as well as estab lishing norms for evaluation of faculty in tenure and promotion cases [18]. By their exclusivity, the implicated journals accumulate symbolic capital, making them attractive for researchers.

The decision by the College of Senior Scholars to create the SSB bestowed the journals with symbolic capital in the IS field. Whereas previously some perceived that only MISQ and ISR were of sufficient quality to contribute to successful tenure cases [2,19], the SSB signaled to both IS scholars and other disciplines that the other journals were of high quality as well. Viewed through the lens of symbolic capital, we expect the additional journals to be more attractive to IS researchers after the SSB designation, given that exclusive journal lists may alter what is valued (SSB publications) or not valued (non-SSB publications) in an academic field [18]. The added attraction should therefore influ ence how IS researchers produce and consume research published in these journals.

Research production means the relationships, processes, and prac tices enacted to create and publish a research study. We see this reflected in the patterns of collaboration among researchers. Research con sumption means how researchers leverage the previous work of others. We observe this using the proxy of citations of prior work. Articles of higher perceived relevance and quality are likely to be more widely read and cited in future research.

Section 2.2 describes the identification of changes in the research production patterns of the IS field by examining variation in collaboration patterns and institutional representation before and after the establishment of the SSB. Section 2.3 describes the identification of changes in research consumption by examining changes in perceived quality of SSB vs. non-SSB journals pre- and post-SSB.

2.2. Research production in the IS field. Examining the social structure of publication

Researchers receiving the SSB signal would likely change their ac tivities to increase the probability of publishing in the SSB. This would likely impact the level and extent of collaboration, as collaboration has been shown to lead to higher-quality research output and better publi cation success [14,20]. Further, higher incidence and extent of collab oration would likely lead to new social ties among researchers seeking to publish in the SSB. Institutional and regional representation is another crucial feature of the social structure of a discipline. By institutional representation, we mean the number of unique institutions (academic or otherwise) affiliated with authors publishing in a given journal. Due to more consistent perceptions of higher quality, SSB journals will likely attract submissions from a broader range of institutions postdesignation. We categorize variation in these features as indicators of changes in research production within the IS field.

## 2.2.1. Increase in the incidence and extent of collaboration

Collaboration means “working together on a research project, designing it and/or undertaking the project together, and publishing on its results together” [21] (p. 696).<sup>13</sup> There are two dimensions of collaboration: these are 1) the incidence of collaboration, defined as the ratio of articles with multiple authors vs. articles with a single author [22], and 2) the extent of collaboration, defined as the number of au thors per article [23].

There has been an increase in the incidence and extent of collabo ration across academia as a whole as well as in the IS field [11,24]. There are several reasons for this trend. First, the increasing pace of the expansion of scientific knowledge, exacerbated by the swift pace of change in the IS field, means that most individuals find it difficult to specialize in multiple areas. Researchers can leverage co-authors' specialized knowledge by extending their collaboration networks and incidence of collaboration [25]. Second, a higher preference for collaboration may also reflect the priorities of funding institutions such as the National Science Foundation in the US [26]. A third reason for the trend toward collaboration is associated with the increasingly quanti tative nature of IS research [24]. The movement toward using progres sively more sophisticated quantitative research methods may require engaging specialist co-authors who know novel and emerging methods [27]. Finally, there is now the ubiquitous availability of tools that enable collaboration across geographic space, allowing for the formation of more extensive collaboration networks [24.25]. While multiple studies on IS collaboration have focused only on the SSB journals, this study examines whether collaboration has also increased similarly in other respected journals that are not part of the SSB.

Collaboration increases an author's productivity as measured by the number of authored publications [14]. When a researcher publishes in a highly esteemed journal, the journal allocates symbolic capital to the author. The higher the number and perceived quality of a researcher's publications, the higher the individual level of symbolic capital allo cated to the author [13]. In academic publishing, accumulated indi vidual symbolic capital translates into higher earnings and promotion opportunities [28,29]. Researchers use collaboration and co-authorship to reduce the effort per publication, increasing the number of papers they are involved in and ultimately providing a higher potential to in crease their earnings and promotion chances.

Further, collaboration links researchers with complementary skills (i. e., writing, methodological, theory-building, etc.) and allows them to produce more creative and higher impact research [21,24]. Finally, citation rates are higher for co-authored papers than for sole-authored articles [30,31]. Hence, collaboration potentially increases the individual-level symbolic capital of an author and, correspondingly, the ability of an author to successfully publish and achieve promotion.

## 2.2.2. Targeted submission to the SSB and reduced publication inequality

In addition to increased collaboration, if the symbolic capital endowed in a particular journal increases, so would the set of individuals and institutions aiming to publish there. Before the SSB, there were widely varying perceptions of the journal quality outside of the FT50, in part due to the importance of different journal lists within each region. This led to significant differences in publishing behaviors across regions [32,33]. The SSB signal may have alerted IS researchers from all regions to esteem the SSB journals, which may have led to greater unity in the evaluation of journal quality across regions, leading authors to target their submissions there.

The redirecting of research output to other regions' journals likely led to a reduction in publication inequality. Inequality in research publi cation is the “degree to which publications are concentrated in the hands of a few (entities)” [34] (p. 114); these entities may be individual re searchers, institutions, and geographical regions, among others. A healthy breadth of research questions, theoretical perspectives, research study design, and research methods is vital for advancing any academic discipline [see [35,36]]. However, past studies in other fields such as economics and political science show that researchers at elite in stitutions exert a disproportionate influence on the direction of scientific research [37].

At the journal level, regional and institutional inequality of publi cation in elite journals is a significant concern. The perceived over representation of North American researchers in the elite journals of the field, and the underrepresentation of others, have led to a perception of North American dominance of the IS discipline [33,38]. Critics charge that creativity and quality of published research suffer due to this domination. These critics argue that the field should facilitate broader representation across regions due to the regional differences in topic focus, theoretical perspectives, and research methods. For example, Asia (Region 3) research values behavioral and design science approaches [7], European research places a heavy emphasis on socio-technical and interpretive approaches to understanding IT phenomena, and North American research is much more likely to be characterized by positivist perspectives and quantitative methods [3,24,33].

However, the SSB may also have increased inequality if more North American researchers (by far the largest block of AIS membership) concentrated submissions to SSB journals, pushing out other regions. If this were the case, the SSB would be responsible for the exact opposite effect of that intended by the Senior Scholars – namely, recognizing the important and unique contributions of competing theoretical and ontological perspectives outside North America.

We examine whether the SSB led to more target submission patterns within the SSB using the proxy of publication inequality. We investigate whether institutional and regional inequality changed following the SSB designations and whether those changes were consistent across SSB and non-SSB journals. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to examine the extent of institutional inequality in publications in elite journals. By doing so, we explore changes in research production pat terns and associated impacts of the SSB on equality within the IS field.

## 2.3. Research consumption in the IS field. Perceptions of journal quality

If the SSB journal designation led to an increase in the desirability of publishing in the basket journals (due to higher perceived quality and the argument for the paper being an essential part of a tenure case), higher-quality submissions would likely be concentrated in the SSB journals. We conceptualize the perception of journal quality by exam ining the number of citations of each article in a journal.

A total citation rate of a journal is one indicator of its perceived status. The creation of the SSB increased the profile and visibility of its constituent journals – signaling that they have higher amounts of sym bolic capital. Increased symbolic capital will attract more submissions to these journals, which leads to greater competition for publication. This increased competition should improve the average quality of the articles published in these journals. Higher-quality research is more likely to be cited than lower-quality research [8,39]. Thus, SSB journals are ex pected to publish more compelling and citable research [40]. Hence, after the designation, the relative perception of the research impact of articles published in the SSB should rise compared with non-SSB journals.

The higher perception of the research impact of the SSB journals should lead to researchers referencing more articles published in SSB journals than non-SSB journals, as it is more likely to be deemed highquality, rigorous research. Further, authors might strategically cite previous work in their target journals to show that their research is relevant to that journal or signal that they value the journal's contri butions to the field [1]. Hence, the creation of the SSB may impact the citations of the articles published in its constituent journals. Conversely, there might also be an adverse impact on citations in non-SSB journals. Our study examines changes in citations per paper pre- vs. post-SSB creation across SSB vs. non-SSB journals.

## 2.4. Summary

We propose that there are two potential outcomes of the SSB signal within the IS field. The first is that the SSB designation provided a signal of new symbolic capital that impacted researchers' behavior regarding research production and consumption. For example, suppose re searchers attribute higher status to membership in the SSB. In that case, at least some of the increase in the extent and incidence of collaboration in IS research is a consequence of the designation. Therefore, from a research production standpoint, a higher level of collaboration is ex pected in SSB journals vs. non-SSB journals, leading to broader institu tional representation in the SSB. Second, when considering research consumption, if the SSB signal was salient to the IS field, then SSB journals should reflect an increase in citation levels that are not present in other journals.

In contrast, it is possible that the symbolic capital signal associated with the SSB designation was ignored and did not affect the behavior of IS researchers. Change is natural and will happen regardless of the Se nior Scholars' actions. The decision to designate some journals as SSB journals would be inconsequential, leading to no observed research production and consumption changes. Therefore, the effect of the SSB is an empirical question, which we explore in the study described below.

## 3. Method

To explore the impact of the SSB, we designed a study to identify changes in research production and consumption behaviors and whether these changes were significant and consistent across both SSB and non-SSB journals. Our methodology resembles a natural or quasi-experiment rather than a randomized controlled trial experiment. We describe this study below.

## 3.1. Data collection

We utilized data from the SSB journals, as well as DSS and IM. The dataset was built using the Web of Science database and included arti cles published in the included journals from the beginning of 2000 until the end of 2015. For each article, we collected the article title, authors, year of publication, and the number of citations.<sup>14</sup> We categorized ar ticles into pre-SSB (2000–2007) and post-SSB (2011–2015) periods with a three-year buffer period in-between (2008–2010) to account for news of the SSB designation to diffuse through the IS researcher population and for the length of journal review cycles. Following other coauthorship network studies, we omitted book reviews and editorials from the analysis [11,12,24,41]. For each author, we used biographical information included in published papers to record the author's insti tution, country, and AIS region at the time of publication. Table 1 de scribes the variables that were collected.

## 3.2. Data analyses

The extent of collaboration is measured as the average number of authors on a publication. The incidence of collaboration is the propor tion of publications with at least two authors. We performed an analysis of variance (ANOVA) to determine whether significant differences exist in the means of the extent and incidence of collaboration corresponding to the pre- and post-SSB creation periods.

We calculated and compared the Gini coefficients for the journal networks pre- and post-designation to examine institutional and regional inequality trends. The Gini coefficient measures the extent to which there is a uniform distribution of event incidence across a particular category [42]. In this study, our categories are 1) authorship by the institution and 2) authorship by AIS region. Gini coefficients range from zero to one; generally, higher Gini coefficients signal higher inequality, i.e., there is a non-uniform concentration of publications in a journal by institution or region; lower coefficients closer to zero signal more uniform distribution and lower inequality [34]. To calculate the Gini coefficient, we assigned a score for each co-author equal to the fraction 1/n for an article with n co-authors. For the AIS region attribute, the sum of articles authored by each region within a given period equals the sum of all the authors' fractional scores with institutions in that region.

To investigate the impact of IS research, we collected citation in. formation for the articles published in the ten journals from 2000 to 2015. We use the number of citations of a paper as a proxy for its impact. We created a data set with a row for each article, with the year and citation count (the raw impact variable) as constituent columns. Older studies have had a more extended period to be read and cited than newer articles. To control for the effect of time on the number of citations of a paper, we utilized the age-adjusted citation count using the following formula [43]:

$$
S ^ {C} (i) = \gamma^ {*} \frac {C (i)}{(A g e (i) + 1) ^ {\delta}}
$$

where $s ^ { C } ( i )$ is the age-adjusted citation count of the article, Age(i) is the current age of the article, C(i) is the current citation count of the article, and γ and δ are constants chosen to adjust for age. We choose γ = 4, and δ = 1 because they have been shown through experiments to improve upon the h-index and capture the research influence of recent articles [41,43]. The formula captures the potential of new papers to acquire new citations in the future. In the literature, the age-adjusted citation is the basis for the contemporary h-index or hc-index metrics used to rank journals across IS (e.g., [1,41,44]) and other disciplines (e.g., [45,46]).

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Sample description

Table 2 shows the regional composition of authors published in the ten journals in our sample. According to geography, the author distri bution is 54% AIS Region 1, 28% Region 2, and 19% Region 3. The North American journals MISQ, ISR, and JMIS, are dominated by Region 1 researchers with representation exceeding 70%. Researchers from Re gion 2 form pluralities in the European journals EJIS, ISJ, and JSIS, and they form a majority of 54% in JIT. Although Region 3 researchers represent between 12 and 19% of authors in the SSB, they constitute 32 and 36% of authors published in DSS and IM, respectively. The regional distribution of authors in a given journal suggests a proxy for the value placed on a journal by researchers and institutions from a given region.

The following section explores how research production and con sumption in IS research changed post-SSB.

## 4.2. Changes in research production post SSB

We describe our results on the aggregate level for each research question, i.e., for all journals, then describe individual journal results, with a particular focus on any differences between the SSB and non-SSB journals. Doing so allows us to explore changes that may be associated with general trends in the field and then drill down to changes that explain the impact of the SSB. We explore Research Question 1 (“Did the creation of the SSB change researchers' behaviors?”) by evaluating collaboration and regional and institutional representation patterns preand post-SSB.

## 4.2.1. Collaboration

There was a substantial increase in the incidence of collaboration– meaning the proportion of publications with at least two authors – across the combined journal set, from 83% to 92%. This is a small effect size (0.13) as measured by Cohen's f. All journals except MISQ and JAIS experienced increases in the extent of collaboration following the cre ation of the SSB. Table 3 shows these results.

Similarly, there was a significant increase in the extent of collabo ration – the average number of authors on a publication – for the com bined journal set from an average of 2.71 to 3.04 post-SSB. The effect size, captured by Cohen's f, was 0.15 – a moderate effect [47]. All journals, save for JAIS, exhibited increases in collaboration extent, with medium effect sizes in MISQ, ISR, ISJ, JSIS, JIT, AJIS, and IM, and small effect sizes in JMIS, EJIS, and DSS. These results are shown in Table 4.

## 4.2.2. Targeted submission and publication inequality

Because the SSB sent a signal across all regions that a particular set of journals were the “highest quality,” it is likely that journals would receive submissions from a broader set of authors and institutions. Before the SSB, institutions in different regions may have had less con gruity in their submissions to various journals, leading to varying pub lication patterns. Because of this, we expect that post-SSB, the SSB journals will have lower levels of publishing inequality. Publishing inequality means the extent to which publications in the journal are nonuniformly across articles authored by researchers at only a few in stitutions or AIS regions.

The Gini coefficient measures inequality; it varies from 0 – where institutions author equal numbers of publications to the journal – to 1 – where authors from a single institution author all the publications in a given journal [42,48]. Institutional inequality decreased in MISQ, ISR, JMIS, JAIS, EJIS, and ISJ but increased in JSIS, JIT, and DSS. IM did not exhibit a change in inequality. Table 5 shows these results.

The Lorenz curve is a probability plot of cumulative output (e.g., the proportion of published articles) against the proportion of entities (e.g., institutions or AIS regions) responsible for that output [42]. Fig. 1 shows the Lorenz curves for the journals in our sample. The diagonal line il lustrates the Lorenz curve if institutions' representation is uniform within the published articles of a journal, while the curved line repre sents the actual distribution of institutional representation. The greater the area between the diagonal and the true Lorenz curve, the greater the institutional inequality. For ISR, MISQ, JMIS, JAIS, ISJ, and EJIS, inequality decreased post-SSB (red line) vs. pre-SSB (green line) as the area between the curves decreased. Conversely, inequality increased in JSIS, JIT, and DSS.

We also assessed changes in inequality according to regional repre sentation, i.e., the extent to which publication counts reflect the three AIS regions' uniform representation. Table 6 shows decreases in regional inequality across nine journals. JSIS was an exception but also had the lowest inequality pre-SSB. Over time, we do observe greater North American dominance of authorship across the whole journal set. Pre-SSB, researchers at North American institutions accounted for 48% of all the publications across the journal set; post-SSB, this proportion rose to 61%.

## 4.3. Changes in research consumption post SSB

We proposed that changes in journal metrics such as citation counts reflect changes in research consumption patterns. We plotted the dif ference in average citations per year for each journal according to SSB status. Fig. 2 shows that pre-SSB, SSB journals had higher average ci tations than non-SSB journals. Post-SSB, articles published in the non

SSB journals accumulated more citations on average vs. the SSB.

We then employed the age-adjusted citation score as a proxy for the research impact of each article and aggregated these scores for each journal. Across all journals, the mean impact slightly decreased post-SSB (Cohen's f = 0.12). All journals save for DSS decreased in impact, albeit slightly in IM (f = 0.10) and JIT (f = 0.13) and moderately for the other journals. The research impact for DSS slightly increased $( \mathbf { f } = 0 . 0 7 )$ ). We also rank the journals by research impact in the pre-SSB and post-SSB creation eras. It is perhaps noteworthy that the non-SSB journals both increased in the research impact rankings following the creation of the SSB. Table 7 details the results of these analyses.

Multiple measures of research impact corroborate that the non-SSB journals did not suffer from being excluded from the SSB. Using the average citations per paper metric, DSS moved up from the ninth posi tion pre-SSB to the fourth position post-SSB; IM dropped slightly from fourth to fifth. Using the H-index metric, DSS rose from fifth to first, while IM dropped from second to fourth. These metrics have various weaknesses regarding the accurate capture of levels of research impact [43]. Even then, considering all these metrics, it is a safe interpretation to place DSS and IM as among the top eight journals regarding the citation impact of research. The analyses suggest that DSS and IM pro duce research perceived as valuable to be cited by the IS research community.

## 4.4.1. Journal self-citations

We collected data to determine whether journal-level self-citation patterns affect our findings regarding changes in measures of journal quality. To do this, we examined whether self-citation levels differed between the journals included in our study. Previous research concep tualizes journal self-citations in one of two ways. The first concept measures the ratio of the citations of an article within its publishing journal, compared with its total number of citations [49]. This definition captures what fraction of the citations of an article are from the pub lishing journal. For example, in our dataset, the DSS paper titled “Leveraging the capabilities of service-oriented decision support” had 167 citations. Of these, 7 were from articles also published in DSS. The journal self-citation rate for this paper was thus 7/167, or 0.04. Utilizing this measure, we found that JMIS (median 0.12), ISR (0.09), and JSIS (0.12) had significantly higher journal self-citation rates than DSS (0.08), IM (0.04), MISQ (0.07), EJIS (0.09), ISJ (0.08), and JAIS (0.00) (no significant differences among them). Conversely, JIT (0.00) had a significantly lower journal self-citation rate than all the other journals

The second conceptualization of journal self-citation refers to the extent to which an article references other articles in the same journal. Increasing the number of citations of articles published in the same journal is often encouraged by editorial staff during the review process [40]. This definition captures the count of papers cited by a given article that belongs to the publishing journal. For example, the JMIS paper titled “Sustainability of a Firm's Reputation for Information Technology” cited a total of 95 articles, and of these, 7 were papers previously pub lished in JMIS. By this self-citation measure, MISQ (median 7), JMIS (4), and IM (3) had the three highest average journal self-citations. The next group of journals includes ISR (2), ISJ (2), DSS (2), EJIS (2), JIT (0.5), and JSIS (0), which have no significant differences between them. JAIS (1) had the lowest average journal self-citation measure. Table 8 sum marizes the self-citation statistics for the journals.

## 5. Discussion

This section discusses our contributions to understanding the changes in research production and research consumption in the IS field associated with the increased the symbolic capital of the SSB constituent journals. We first summarize the answers to our research questions and offer several recommendations for IS researchers, academic institutions, and the AIS organization.

We identified a bifurcated response of the IS field to the SSB. First, regarding RQ 1, “Did the creation of the SSB change researchers' be haviors?,” we find that the SSB signal was not associated with a differ ential increase in collaboration for the SSB journals. However, the signal did increase the breadth of authors and institutions who published in SSB journals, leading to reduced publishing inequality in the SSB.

Regarding RQ 2, “Did the creation of the SSB change relative journal quality?”, we found unexpected results. As inclusion in the SSB is considered a key indicator of the quality of the constituent journals, we would expect to see higher citations in SSB journals compared with non-SSB journals. However, at least for DSS and IM, we find that this is not the case. These journals saw a rise in journal quality post-SSB, while some SSB journals saw reductions in quality metrics during the post-SSB period. This implies that the IS field ignored the SSB signal regarding the relative quality of DSS and IM compared with the SSB. Fig. 3 visually presents this bifurcated response. A more complete discussion of our findings and associated contributions is in the next section.

## 5.1. Contributions

The notion of symbolic capital is instructive in understanding the bifurcated response of the IS field to the signal sent by the establishment of the SSB. Symbolic capital is the amount of status, authority, prestige, and good reputation held by an entity [4]. Previous work demonstrates the importance of accruing symbolic capital at the individual level [15–17]. In academic disciplines, the primary way to enhance individual-level reputation and prestige is by publishing in journals with high levels of symbolic capital. Each journal derives its level of symbolic capital from multiple sources. The primary source of this symbolic capital is inclusion and relative placement within rankings created by credible bodies such as the Financial Times (FT50) and Association of Business Schools (ABS). and AIS Senior Scholars.

Authors who publish in these prestigious journals have a higher likelihood of tenure and promotion success. We extend the under standing of the accumulation of symbolic capital in academic research. To date, multiple studies have investigated how individual academics modify their behaviors to accumulate symbolic capital [18,54,55]. However, our study is the first to our knowledge that explores how journals acquire and distribute their stocks of symbolic capital.

The associated journal research impact statistics of age-adjusted citations, average citations per paper, and journal h-index place DSS and IM in the top 8 IS journals post-SSB.

The lack of negative effect on journal impact statistics indicates that many researchers value publishing in high reputation non-SSB journals and that the research is perceived to be of sufficiently high quality to cite. Thus, DSS and IM may derive their symbolic capital from hidden sources such as the network of scholars who publish in the journal or the perceived relevance of the research in the journal. Recent reports have shown that IS scholars perceive that some SSB journals have inordinately long review cycles, potentially resulting in the publication of outdated research. This, among other factors, may have reduced the perceived prestige of some SSB journals [56].

We have already noted that Ph.D. students are socialized to target the SSB journals to be competitive in the job market. ## 5.2. Implications

Our findings have potential implications for the ongoing debate about the impacts of the SSB. Previous research has encouraged the identification of a select group of elite journals. The SSB attempted to broaden the set of journals recognized as elite beyond those certified by external entities [see 2]. As has been described in the literature, multiple benefits accrued to journals placed in the SSB. For example, JSIS, which along with JIT, had had its official membership in the SSB deferred until 2011, has thrived, leading to higher submission volumes and a low acceptance rate. According to its founding editor-in-chief, “As an illus tration, last year [2017] we received a total of 291 submissions. 19 were accepted for publication, giving an acceptance rate of roughly 6.5%” [as quoted in 9]. In addition, since their official inclusion in the SSB, both JIT and JSIS have had papers win the AIS Best IS Publications Award [57]. Thus, inclusion in the SSB materializes concrete benefits for included journals which may account for the continued debates as to it composition.

Interestingly, DSS and IM, two journals routinely suggested as can didates for placement within the SSB, did not suffer in their citation impact as their impact levels increased. Therefore, it is questionable whether the Senior Scholars achieved their goal of defining a clear set of top journals. Our findings indicate that academic promotion and tenure committees should refrain from a blanket inference that research pub lished outside the SSB is of lower quality than that within the SSB and that at least some non-SSB journals are of commensurate quality with some SSB journals.

The finding of equivalent research quality using multiple metrics indicates that those seeking to establish successful tenure cases should utilize multiple measures of journal quality in addition to conferred stocks of symbolic capital. Likewise, IS researchers who participate on tenure committees should consider current measures of influence to better defend tenure cases to committee members who may not be familiar with the IS field and may be prejudiced toward sources of symbolic capital such as external lists and the SSB.

What drives the recognition of a journal as elite in IS research? If citations measure impact, DSS and IM have strong evidence that they should be considered as of equivalent quality and therefore legitimate candidates for inclusion in the SSB. Although our robustness check did not detect an effect in our sample of self-citation patterns, some journals may attempt to inflate their perceived quality by requiring extensive self-citations before publication. Suppose journals from outside our sample are found to manipulate rankings via self-citations. In that case, the citation rate will likely become of reduced importance in evaluating journal quality, which may lead to the emergence of other alternative measures used to assess the impact of a journal as a whole.

Potentially promising alternative measures of influence might include the examination of the publication patterns of top IS scholars. For example, what proportion of top IS scholars publish and or appear as the first author in a journal? If a large proportion of top IS scholars publish in one journal vs. another, especially as the first author, it is likely an indicator of targeting behavior driven by differential evalua tion of journal prominence within the field. Importantly, our findings suggest that any kind of evaluation of this kind is likely more appro priately performed within a region than by using the global network of the field, as the top scholars in one region may focus on a different set of journals than those in another region.

Critiques of existing journal lists have focused on the reinforcing effects of these lists on how research is conducted. When journal lists are self-reinforcing, they influence (i) the choices of venues to publish and (ii) the research methods and topics preferred by constituent journals [58], ultimately privileging the constituent journals and limiting the range of problems that the field aims to solve (p. 5) and (iii) the papers that are cited in literature reviews (both for their empirical papers and for standalone reviews). As Currie et al. [59] explain, the SSB provides a simplified basis for finding literature review samples but “may inad vertently prompt scholars to limit their search and exclude valuable journals from outside the [SSB]” (p. 3). Such a scenario likely benefits SSB journals with increased citations of their research but is potentially damaging to knowledge construction in IS if essential findings published in non-SSB journals are ignored. Thus, editors and reviewers must require authors to expand their literature searches beyond the SSB so that valuable work in the field is not overlooked.

Another critical question is whether evaluations of journal quality are equally privileged across demographics [60]. For IS, regional rep resentation is essential, but it is unclear that the field equally values scholars from AIS Regions 2 and 3 as they value Region 1. There are published critiques that see the socio-technical and design scienceoriented perspectives of IS as underprivileged because of underrepre sentation of Regions 2 and 3 scholarship in perceived elite IS journals [7,33,38,61]. Our analyses show that Region 3 researchers comprise 32% and 36% of DSS and IM researchers, respectively, compared to between 12% and 19% in the SSB, suggesting that these journals are highly valued at Region 3 institutions. These figures may indicate that Region 3 preferences are not as valued as other regions by the power structures in the AIS decision-making bodies.

In the last review of the SSB commissioned by the Senior Scholars, the majority of respondents recommended expansion and recommended DSS and IM as the top candidates for inclusion into the SSB. However, the Senior Scholars ultimately chose to maintain the SSB [59]. That the basket has not been expanded since a decade ago is perhaps not sur prising. As Avison and Fitzgerald [62] proclaim, “despite having such up-to-date and continually changing topics of interest, [the IS discipline] is ultimately quite conservative” (p. 182). But it is unclear the standards that the Senior Scholars use to arrive at their decisions.

The decision not to expand the basket may be seen as ignoring the feedback from the body of scholars, which makes the call for trans parency in decision-making by the Senior Scholars all the more urgent. The AIS Senior Scholars must provide transparency regarding the met rics that matter for a journal to be included in the SSB and the process in which feedback is collected, assessed, and acted on. IS has successfully lobbied and continues to lobby for more of its journals to be included in the Financial Times 50 list of ranked academic publications [63]. Still, the SSB has stayed constant over the same period. Does this reflect inconsistency?

## 5.3. Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. First, we obtained citation data from the Web of Science database, which likely yields lower cita tion counts than platforms such as Google Scholar. Future research could explore whether using a different database produces different results. We also only considered a few journals outside the SSB.

Some limitations arise from potential confounding factors. We assessed changes to the patterns of research production and consump tion in the IS field as represented by the features of incidence and extent of collaboration, institutional inequality, and research impact. While we saw a similarity in changes to institutional inequality and research impact according to SSB status, we did not observe equivalent sorting in collaboration. While we cannot directly attribute the sorting observation to SSB placement because of possible confounding factors, we can safely conclude that the journals we studied that were not placed in the SSB improved their age-adjusted citation rankings relative to the SSB jour nals. We can also safely conclude that most SSB journals exhibit lower institutional inequality in the post-SSB creation period. These results support our assertion that the IS research community – to an extent – exhibited a bifurcated response to the SSB signal.

## 6. Conclusion

In this study, we used bibliometric data retrieved from Web of Sci ence across a sample of ten journals, eight of which are in the SSB, while DSS and IM are not, to examine changes in author behavior and journal quality metrics after the establishment of the SSB. We included DSS and IM as representative of high-quality non-SSB journals because they are usually proposed as candidates for inclusion in the extension of the SSB [64]. We examined changes in the incidence and extent of collaboration, institutional inequality, and several measures of research impact.

We found a bifurcated response to the SSB signal by the IS research community. On one hand, IS researchers acted upon the signal regarding research production, as evidenced by the broader set of institutions publishing in most SSB journals after the SSB was created. On the other hand, IS researchers ignored the signal when considering research consumption, illustrated by the relative rise in journal quality metrics of DSS and IM – two non-SSB journals. Our findings suggest that negative impacts on excluded journals may be limited, at least to the extent that the research published in some non-SSB journals is perceived to be of high quality as measured by citations. We hope that our findings inform ongoing discussions on the SSB and those regarding the proper use of journal lists in tenure and promotion cases.
