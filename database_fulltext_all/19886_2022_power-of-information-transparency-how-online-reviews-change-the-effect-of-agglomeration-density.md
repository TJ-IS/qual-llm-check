# Power of information transparency: How online reviews change the effect of agglomeration density on firm revenue

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Firm agglomeration Online review Information transparency Information asymmetry Competition cost Platform

## A B S T R A C T

Agglomeration benefits firms because it minimizes consumers’ search costs and information asymmetry. How ever, the effects of agglomeration may change due to the considerable amount of information available in online platforms. Thus, drawing upon the theoretical lens of information transparency and agglomeration theory, we investigate how online reviews change the effect of agglomeration on firm performance. A total of 11,528 ob servations of 467 hotels show that agglomeration density positively affects firm revenue in the hotel sector. However, such an effect decreases with the increase in online review quantity (including volume and length). This change is manifested in two forms, namely, minimizing information asymmetry and increasing competition cost due to information transparency. Moreover, the positive effect of agglomeration density decreases due to the quantity of high-quality (few spelling errors and easy to understand) online reviews, but not with low-quality. We further discover that the substitution effect of online review quantity on agglomeration density is stronger for independent hotels than for branded ones, whereas the effect is similar among hotels with various qualities. Our findings provide implications for the management of online channels in changing the local agglomeration effectiveness.

## 1. Introduction

Firm agglomeration (i.e., spatial concentrations of organizations) is a common phenomenon across industries [1]. Firms often aggregate or even stay near their competitors to increase their opportunities to reach consumers and reduce the cost of obtaining resource, such as labor and suppliers [2]. This situation is particularly salient in the hotel industry [3]. For example, Home Inn and Hanting Hotel (two China chain brands of Home Inns Hotel Group and the Huazhu Hotel Group) prefer to imitate their rivals’ decision to enter a new market or locate in the nearby area [4]. Although firms suffer from competitive pressure in agglomeration because they provide similar products and services, the general belief is that they can gain considerable benefits over the competition costs to achieve competitive advantage [5–7].

To validate such an effect, previous studies have investigated the effect of firm agglomeration on its performance, including survival [8,9], price [10], profit [11], innovation [5], and revenue [7,12]. Ma jority of these studies have identified the positive effect of firm agglomeration. Agglomeration theory proposes several mechanisms, such as information asymmetry, to analyze the effects of agglomeration [13]. Facing information asymmetry, agglomeration helps consumers reduce information search costs, thereby lowering transaction uncertainty and risks prior to making consumption decisions [13]. As such, a firm prefers to remain close to other firms with high levels of advertising to share consumer demand [7]. Then, the increasing demand and con sumption lead to enhanced firm performance [7].

However, the effect of agglomeration may change given that a large amount of information has become easily available in online platforms, thereby minimizing the information asymmetry between firms and consumers while increasing the competition cost of firms. Firms increasingly rely on online reviews of platforms to inform consumers about their existence and the quality of product or service they provide [14]. Compared to the huge expense of advertising, online reviews of platforms are nearly costless but are able to break through geographical and time constraints, thus influencing consumer search patterns and search cost. Large amounts of online reviews enhance consumer awareness of “where is here” and minimize information asymmetry between consumers and firms [15]. Therefore, online reviews may mitigate the agglomeration effectiveness, because the former may sub stitute the function of agglomeration in terms of reducing searching costs by enabling transparent information. Moreover, online reviews of a focal firm are available not only for consumers but also for other firms located in the nearby area. Such transparent information increases the focal firm’s competition cost and changes its agglomeration effective ness, because competitors nearby can rapidly imitate and learn [16,17]. Previous studies have revealed that online reviews are important in dicators of firm performance, because they reflect the reputation and popularity of firms [18]. Nevertheless, how online reviews modify the effect of agglomeration on firm performance remains unexplored. This issue must be addressed, because managers can combine their preferred location decisions or offline agglomeration strategy with online strate gies to better promote firm performance.

In the current work, we examine firm revenue, because it is not only a key indicator of performance but also a primary concern of firm managers. In fact, this topic has received great attention in prior liter ature $[ 7 , 1 4 , 1 9 - 2 3 ]$ . This paper focuses on online review quantity and quality, which are critical dimensions of information [24]. Specifically, we investigate two important attributes of online review quantity, namely, review volume and review length. More and longer reviews convey more information, and these attributes have been frequently examined in the literature [21,25]. We also examine online review quality and construct a composite quality-quantity metric based on different aspects (including spelling error and readability) of review texts. The hotel sector is selected as the study setting because agglom eration is very popular and is a common context for hotel managers [3,7]. The hotel industry is also an ideal context because online hotel reviews are important for consumers to make purchase decisions on such high-priced and intangible experience products [26].

In summary, our core research question is as follows: How do online reviews (including quantity and quality) of hotels change the effect of agglomeration density on their revenue?

To answer this question, we draw upon the theoretical lens of in formation transparency and agglomeration theory to develop our model. A large number of online reviews, especially high-quality ones, repre sent a high level of information transparency. On the one hand, this minimizes information asymmetry between consumers and firms; on the other hand, this increases competition cost by increasing marketing learning. We collect tax data of hotels from three different sources: the Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts, online review data from the TripAdvisor website, and location data from Google Maps. From these, we construct a unique panel data and use the generalized method of moments approach to estimate the model. We confirm that agglomera tion density positively affects firm revenue in the hotel sector. However, such an effect decreases with the increase in consumer online review quantity in terms of review volume and length. Moreover, the moder ating effect of high-quality review volume is stronger than that of lowquality review volume. The positive effect of agglomeration density decreases due to the quantity of high-quality (few spelling errors and easy to understand) reviews, but not with low-quality reviews. Furthermore, compared with branded hotels, the negative moderating effects of online review quantity on agglomeration density are stronger for independent hotels but are similar among hotels with different levels of quality.

While previous studies investigate online reviews and offline loca tion separately, the present study is an early attempt to combine agglomeration with online reviews and investigate the changing effects of online reviews on the relationship between agglomeration and firm revenue. We contribute to the existing literature by providing a new perspective of online information. Specifically, we contribute to agglomeration theory by exploring the boundaries of agglomeration economies from an information transparency perspective. Agglomera tion theory proposes several explanations to understand the source of agglomeration economies [1]. However, our work reveals the process from agglomeration economies to diseconomies under information transparency environment by clearly demonstrating the mitigating ef fect of online review quantity on changing the agglomeration effect. We also add novel knowledge to the online review literature by highlighting the moderating roles of two important indicators of online review quantity (i.e., volume and length). Unlike previous studies on the direct impact of online reviews [18,19,26–30], we discover that online review quantity has a substitute effect on the traditional strategy (i.e., agglomeration or geographical clustering) in influencing firm revenue. We further find that such a substitute is effective for the quantity of highquality online reviews, but not for low-quality. These findings contribute to information transparency theory by simultaneously examining in formation quantity and quality and by revealing the dual roles (direct and moderating effects) of online reviews from the perspective of in formation transparency. Finally, our findings present new insights by investigating the roles of hotel chain affiliation and quality in differen tiating the effects of agglomeration density and online reviews on rev enue. We clearly indicate that independent hotels gain more benefits from agglomeration and online reviews (and its substitution effect) compared to branded hotels.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Advantages and disadvantages of agglomeration

Agglomeration indicates spatial concentrations of diverse or related organizations. Following McCann and Folta [1], the current study fo cuses on the spatial concentrations of related firms, which produce goods or services that are close substitutes within a particular industry. Previous studies have long recognized firm agglomeration and also proposed agglomeration theory with several mechanisms to understand its occurrence [1,3]. Agglomeration theory suggests that “net benefits of being in a location together with other firms increase with the number of firms in the location” [6, p. 219]. The term “net benefit” refers to the agglomeration effect, which is the offsetting result of two opposite forces (i.e., agglomeration gains and competitive costs) [10,31], thereby leading to advantages and disadvantages of agglomeration.

Through agglomeration, the geographic proximity of firms fosters the flow and transfer of information among stakeholders $[ 7 , 1 2 , 3 2 , 3 3 ]$ From the perspective of the demand side, agglomeration economies come primarily from low consumer search cost, which is “incurred by the buyer to locate an appropriate seller and purchase a product.” [34, p. 1677] Compared with spatial concentrated areas, consumers tend to spend higher search costs to obtain product and service information among geographically dispersed sellers [1]. Thus, firms in the concen trated areas will attract more visits (quantity) and realize higher revenue [7], considering consumer locational preference for clusters.

However, the information flow and transfer do not always bring positive externalities to all firms [3,35]; rather, this may also increase firm costs, leading to diseconomies of agglomeration. From the perspective of the supply side, proximity will increase the imitation and learning chances from competitors [16,17]. Some firms may benefit from it, but the focal firm’s competitive advantages may deteriorate, which in turn, increases the localized competition cost. Such a competitive pressure may lead to price wars, which has a negative effect on firm revenue.

First, studies on service industry focus on survival analysis [8,9,36], and only few have examined firm revenue [7,12]. Firm survival is mainly manifested as the exit of firms. Survival rate is not only influ enced by market demand, but also by operational cost or expense. However, firm revenue is an important indicator of performance, which mainly depends on market demand. Studies on revenue issue can guide the business process more precisely and deeply than those on survival study; however, they are rarely examined.

Second, previous studies have displayed various findings on the ef fect of agglomeration. The majority identifies the benefit of agglomer ation [5,6,9], but some studies have reported its negative effect due to the dominant force of competition [8,10,12]. A general agreement in dicates that agglomeration benefit dominates competition cost. How ever, some factors (firm size and brand) may change the positive effect of agglomeration [7].

Third, prior studies have investigated the effect of agglomeration and online reviews separately, but none of them examined their joint effect on firm performance, such as on revenue. Therefore, the present study addresses these deficiencies by exploring how online reviews change the effect of agglomeration on firm revenue in the hotel industry.

## 2.3. An information transparency perspective on the interaction between online review and agglomeration

With the rapid development and widespread use of online platform, online reviews, as a new information source, play an increasingly important role in affecting consumers’ decision-making process and improving firm revenue [18,30]. A large number of studies have confirmed the economic value of online reviews [14,18,20,27–29], while some studies have reported that online reviews function as brand [21,23] and advertising [37]. A few studies [16,38,39] have also high lighted the interplay between geographic location and online activity, as shown in Table 1. These studies contend that the positive impact of online review volume on performance is based on its awareness effect or herding effect [19]; however, the potential role of information trans parency has been ignored. Online reviews provide a source of rich in formation for consumers [21]. On the basis of the information nature of online reviews, we draw upon the perspective of information trans parency to examine their moderating effects.

Information transparency refers to the degree of accessibility and visibility of information to certain market participants [35,40,41]. Meanwhile, transparency means the removal of informational asym metries [42,43]. Most of the studies related to information transparency have focused on information disclosure in the capital market [44], in formation sharing in the supply chain [45], and information advantage in mergers and acquisitions [46]. Recent theoretical studies have investigated information transparency in the online market. Unlike “information transparency hypothesis” (a popular view suggesting that all participating firms benefit from the open information sharing in electronic markets), more and more evidence have been provided to support the view that information transparency can promote matching between consumers and sellers, but sellers do not always benefit from it [47,48]. However, the extant empirical research on such dual effects of information transparency remains scarce.

The current study combines information transparency with agglomeration theory to explore how the relationship between agglomeration and performance changes as information becomes more transparent. Although Marshall, one of the first researchers on agglomeration, did not emphasize the role of information in agglomer ation theory [43], two key mechanisms of the agglomeration effect are related to information: (1) the spillover of knowledge/technology de pends on the spillover of information [5,12], and (2) the reduction of consumer search costs also relies on the decrease of the cost of obtaining information through geographic concentration [7]. By contrast, con sumer online review is a source of information [21]. Therefore, both agglomeration and online review are related to information. Moreover, researchers have pointed out that information asymmetry is one of the main drivers for agglomeration [13,49]. Information advantage brought about by agglomeration is mainly obtained by reducing search costs and information asymmetry. Information transparency also means the removal of information asymmetry [42]. Thus, both theories emphasize the effect of minimizing information asymmetry. In addition, the im pacts of information transparency and agglomeration on firm are twoside. They both emphasize the benefits from the consumer side (by reducing search costs and information asymmetry) while also generating disadvantages for the focal firm from the competitor side (by increasing competition cost) [3,35].

We posit that online review changes the effectiveness of agglomer ation by increasing information transparency. Numerous online reviews of a firm represent considerable information that is transparent for consumers and other firms. Such transparency can benefit firms by attracting new consumers with market and product information. How ever, numerous online reviews also decrease the firms’ advantages of information to their competitors because nearby competitors can also access the information in a transparent environment [40].

## 3. Hypothesis development

This research focuses on online review quantity and quality but not review valence as the moderating variable. Review quantity and quality are two major measures of information transparency for which Williams [42] propose three criteria: information outcome (quantity), informa tion process (quality), and information infrastructure. The first two in dicators are most important, and the increasing quantity of information, especially that of high-quality, reflects higher information transparency. However, review valence is not related to information transparency [42]. In our context, review quantity refers to the amount of information released by consumers, such as review volume and length, while review quality refers to the usefulness of information to the user, such as review understandability [24]. Thus, the quantity and quality of consumer online reviews, regardless of high or low valence, reflects the degree of information transparency.

In our case, the quality of hotels, as high-priced and intangible experience products, is difficult to judge precisely by consumers prior to experience [26]. Hence, serious information asymmetry within the hotel sector leads to considerable information search demand of consumers before they make purchase decisions. In order to clearly describe the changes of the agglomeration effect in the online review environment, we discuss the mechanisms by developing a framework that integrates information transparency and agglomeration (Fig. 1).

In our context, revenue per available room (RevPAR) (i.e., the focal hotel’s revenue) is calculated by multiplying a hotel’s occupancy rate by its average daily room rate (ADR) [50]. The hotel’s occupancy rate is affected by consumer demand, while average daily room rate is influ enced by local competition. Agglomeration not only promotes local consumer demand (Part A in Fig. 1) but also strengthens local compe tition (Part B in Fig. 1). Thus, it is a double-edged sword for firm reve nue. The impact of agglomeration density on the focal hotel revenue is a net effect by offsetting two opposing forces (Part A–Part B), namely, agglomeration gains and competition costs $[ 3 , 1 0 ]$ . Consistant with the general findings of agglomeration effect (e.g., Chung and Kalnins [7]), we posit that the agglomeration gains dominance over the competition costs, thereby displaying a positive net effect. However, in an online review environment, both Part A and Part B will change, subsequently influencing the overall effect of agglomeration.

Specifically, in terms of agglomeration benefits (consumer side), the quantity of online reviews increases information transparency between consumers and hotels because large number of online reviews minimizes consumer search costs and information asymmetry [34]. Lengthy re views also enable consumers to obtain more detailed information about a product or service [21]. Therefore, consumers do not need to search information onsite, which weakens the positive effect of agglomeration density (Part A decreases). Compared to agglomeration, online review quantity increases market transparency and enables consumers to quickly and accurately find products or services that satisfy their ex pectations [51]. Information from consumer online reviews is consid ered more reliable than that provided by hotels (i.e., sellers) [52]. The information transparency between hotels and consumers caused by large number of online reviews reduces information search demand and consumer–seller information asymmetry. Thus, online review quantity weakens the positive effect of agglomeration on firm revenue through substituting the advantage of agglomeration to minimize consumer search costs and information asymmetry.

In terms of competition cost, online review quantities increase in formation transparency among hotels, thereby enhancing the chance of market learning and deepening localized competition [48]. A large amount of or long online reviews provide details regarding the focal hotel’s operations, thereby enabling other hotels to quickly emulate ri vals’ advantage. Firms observe and imitate the behavior of their peers to reduce risks and determine their own strategies and actions [53]. This situation strengthens the negative effect of agglomeration density (Part B increases), because it further increases the competition cost. In an online review environment, information is exchanged at an unprece dented scale and speed between sellers and consumers and among competitors [47]. By analyzing the online feedback of customers about competing products, hotels obtain insights to design better competitive strategies [54]. Thus, information transparency among competitors may lead to product imitation and homogeneity (which is one of the main reasons for increasing competition costs) by market learning. As the competition cost increases because of the information transparency of online reviews, the positive effect of agglomeration on firm revenue decreases. To conclude, with the decrease of Part A and the increase of Part B (Fig. 1) within an information transparent environment, the net effect (Part A – Part B) decreases.

As another important aspect of information transparency, informa tion quality influences the reception and processing of information [42]. High quality of information increases information transparency by enhancing users’ motivation and ability to process information [24]. Information users (including consumers and competitors of focal hotel) benefit from online reviews with high level of information quality (such as few spelling errors and easy to understand) that increase the effi ciency of their decision making $[ 2 4 , 5 5 ]$

Specifically, high-quality online reviews help consumers quickly and correctly capture the review content and meaning, thereby increasing the effective number of online reviews [24]. These useful reviews can reduce the search cost and information asymmetry to a considerable extent, and thus minimize the effectiveness of agglomeration density. By contrast, low-quality online reviews contain more “noise” and cause difficulties for consumers or competitors to comprehend the information on firms, thus playing less significant roles in reducing uncertainty in decision-making [55]. Compared with low-quality reviews, high-quality ones greatly improve information transparency between consumers and hotels, and thus have a stronger impact on the positive effects of agglomeration. Similarly, competitors can also obtain more valuable information and accurate insights from considerable high-quality online reviews, thereby effectively learning and imitating the advantages of the focal hotel. This high information transparency minimizes the infor mation advantage of the focal hotel and increases competition costs, which enhances the negative effect of agglomeration.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data

We collected data from three main sources, namely, Texas Comp troller of Public Accounts, TripAdvisor (a major U.S. online travel platform), and Google Maps. The Texas lodging sector plays an impor tant role in the state’s economy given that Texas is the second largest state. The Texas Comptroller of Public Accounts provides a complete record of quarterly tax data for the lodging sector in the area. It also includes basic information, such as hotel name, address, and capacity. Tax involves legality issues; thus, it is particularly authentic and trust worthy [21].

This study covers a representative sample of cities. Specifically, we selected the four largest and most popular cities of Texas, including Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, and Austin. Dallas is the third largest city in Texas. Houston is rated as the one of the “top 10 America’s most visited cities” by Forbes [56]. Fort. Worth, Austin, and San Antonio are listed in “Top 25 Destinations-United States” in TripAdvisor in 2019. We collected quarterly revenue data on 3534 establishments with 52,731 observations from 2006 to 2016.

We processed tax data set in three steps. First, we removed the outliers. By observing the hotel distribution in the four included cities, we found the abnormal situation that the number of hotels in Austin has risen rapidly since 2008. This finding is consistent with that of Zervas et al. [22], who reported that the entry of Airbnb (a prime example of sharing economy in the field of accommodation) has led to a surge in low-capacity, non-hotel establishments. Therefore, after excluding the data of non-hotel establishments with five rooms as cut-off points [22], the number of hotels in the same market became stable. Second, in accordance with the data provided by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis,<sup>1</sup> we used the GDP deflator index (based on January 1, 2006) to eliminate the impact of inflation in revenue series. Third, although we dealt with outliers by capacity, unreasonable cases with too many rooms and too little revenue remained. We deleted these outliers. Finally, we obtained a panel of 1324 establishments, spanning 41 quarters from 2006 to 2016.

On the basis of hotel addresses and names in the processed tax data, we crawled the latitude and longitude of each hotel through Google Maps. The latitude and longitude data helped us calculate the distance between the focal hotel and its neighbors. Furthermore, to supplement the revenue data with agglomeration density, we collected review data from the world’s largest travel review platform, TripAdvisor, which has been widely used in many studies [57]. For each consumer review, we crawled many items related to hotel and review. To match the tax data, we should integrate the review-level data into the hotel property level, which generates many new items. These items include total number of words, the average rating and volume of reviews during a quarter, and the cumulative average rating and volume of reviews. We obtained 1129 hotels with online reviews on TripAdvisor during the period 2006–2016.

Matching the revenue data with agglomeration density and online review data is a key part of data processing in this study. As shown in Fig. 2, we extracted city, hotel address, and hotel name information from the tax data, and the TripAdvisor data, after which we obtained two lists. We finally matched 467 hotels with online reviews by calculating the similarity of addresses and names in the two lists and a subsequent process involving manual checking. The remaining hotels cannot be successfully matched because the addresses in the two lists are consid erably different. Finally, we obtained 11,528 records in the four major cities of Texas (Austin, San Antonio, Houston, and Dallas-Fort. Worth)

## 4.2. Research variables

## 4.2.1. Dependent variable and independent variable

Our dependent variable, RevPAR, refers to revenue per available room for hotel i in quarter t, which is an industry standard performance measure [7,21]. We used a natural logarithm of RevPAR, which is a common method in many studies [10,21], to obtain a stationary sequence.

The independent variable used in the current study is the firm agglomeration density. The degree of firm agglomeration is often operationalized by counting firms within a certain geographic area [1]. The literature on agglomeration often uses two main measurement methods: one based on administrative division, such as U.S. metropol itan statistical areas [6], U.S. zip codes [7], and U.S. states [33], and another based on distance, such as a radius of one mile [58]. Previous studies [1] contend that distance-based measures are not limited by the governmental boundary delineations, and provide researchers an op portunity to explicitly examine externality attenuation. In addition, distance-based measures enable the agglomeration density of each hotel firm to have more variation and difference by considering each hotel as a core. Thus, we used distance-based measures to operationalize firm agglomeration density in the current study.

Specifically, firm agglomeration density refers to the number of neighbor hotels within a 5-km radius of hotel i in quarter t [59] and is denoted as AggDensity. Firm agglomeration density is calculated from the latitude and longitude information of the complete tax data. Thus, its measurement includes all legal taxable hotels, regardless of whether they are on the TripAdvisor list. Agglomeration density also depends on location. Although the fixed effect of “geographic location” is controlled in our model, the attractiveness of the location also evolves and de velops. However, the effect of this evolution is relatively weak, because our data are obtained in a short time span.

## 4.2.2. Moderating variables and control variables

We use the number of reviews for hotel i in quarter t to measure online review quantity as the moderating variables, denoted as ReviewVolume. The differences of levels of information quality in H2 are examined by classifying reviews into high and low quality based on text characteristics, and then constructing a composite quality-quantity metric. We use two metrics to reflect online review quality, including spelling error rate and text readability, based on prior literature [25,60]. Spelling error rate is the proportion of spelling errors in a review text. Readability is measured by Flesch Reading Ease. Then, by generating a dummy variable based on the median of all reviews spelling error rate/ readability for each hotel, each review is labeled as either “high quality” (fewer spelling errors/easy to understand) or “low quality” (more spelling errors/difficult to understand). Next, we separately calculate the number of high- and low-quality reviews for hotel i in quarter t, which are denoted as HQ\_ReviewVolume and LQ\_ReviewVolume, respectively.

For control variables, given the close connection between rating and quality, the average ratings in a quarter MeanRating are used as proxies for hotel quality. Moreover, because the demand of different locations varies, the hotel revenue and agglomeration density may be affected, thereby leading to endogenous problems. Thus, we use the mean of RevPAR of hotels within 5 km/3 km to measure the market-level demand, denoted as MeanRevPAR, to control omitted variables. In addi tion, we include a series of city-level macro factors (population, GDP, and unemployment rate), provided by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, to control the change of environment. Tables 2 and 3 present the critical variables, their operational definitions, descriptive statistics, and correlations.

## 4.3.2. Estimation

In our empirical models, we use a generalized method of moments (GMM) approach [61] to analyze the dynamic panel data model. We adopt several methods to alleviate potential endogeneity concerns. First, we control the effect of location factor by hotel fixed effect, given that the main endogenous source is that the resource endowment of the location determines the agglomeration density and market demand. We also use the mean of RevPAR of neighbor hotels to measure the marketlevel demand. Second, online review volume can be considered an endogenous variable due to the reverse causal relationship between volume and revenue [14]. According to the GMM framework and extant studies, endogenous variables can be instrumented with their lags [62,63]. The internal instruments are generally used across customer review settings and common in the existing literature [28,62–64]. However, given that the lagged term is a weak instrument, we use a series of tests (AR1, AR2 and Hansen J statistics) to justify the joint validity of instruments after GMM estimation.

## 5.1.2. Robustness check

We present a set of additional analysis to validate the robustness result. First, we use the total number of words of review text for hotel i in quarter t to measure online review quantity as moderating variable, denoted as ReviewLength. The results in Column 3 of Table 4 shows that agglomeration effectiveness declines as information from texts in creases, supporting H1. Second, we repeated the previous study by using the number of neighbor hotels within a smaller (3 rather than 5 km) radius to measure AggDensity. The results of Columns (1) and (2) in Table 5 show that even with the smaller scope, H1 remains supported. Third, we divided the hotels into different segments (e.g., luxury, mid scale, and economy) in accordance with the Smith Travel Research (STR) US Chain Scales report, given that the competition is stronger among hotels belonging to the same segment. ## 5.1.3. Additional analysis

To further explore the differences between these conclusions in various hotels, we divide the sample into branded/independent based on chain affiliation and high− /low-quality based on hotel ratings [65]. In addition, we observe the difference in the magnitude of interac tion term between branded and independent hotels. As shown in Fig. 3 (a), the coefficients (absolute value) of AggDensity × Ln(ReviewVolume) for branded hotels are less than those for independent hotels, thereby implying that the indirect impact of online reviews is also stronger for independent hotels. The results are also similar for Ln(ReviewLength). According to signal theory [66], brand chain—as a signal—conveys more information (e.g., product quality and tangible cues). From the consumer side, due to the advanced advertising and wide distribution of chain hotels [57], it is easier to obtain information from them than from independent ones within a traditional environment. When the envi ronment becomes more transparent, consumers are more likely to rely on online reviews to search information of independent hotels. Simi larly, from the seller side, despite being in a traditional environment, the related information is easier to obtain for competitors due to the higher information transparency of the branded hotels. Thus, neighboring competitors rely less on the information from online reviews of branded hotels than the independent ones. Furthermore, the change of compe tition situation is relatively weaker for branded hotels. For this reason, online reviews have a stronger mitigation effect by increasing infor mation transparency of independent hotels than those of branded hotels.

## 6. Discussions and implications

This study develops an empirical model of how online consumer reviews change the influence of firm agglomeration on firm revenue in the context of the hotel industry. Based on 11,528 observations of 467 Texas hotels consisting of tax data and online reviews, we analyze the dynamic panel model using a GMM approach. Our empirical results highlight four critical findings. First, consistent with the study of Chung and Kalnins [7], we confirm that firms’ agglomeration density positively affects their revenue, indicating that agglomeration benefit dominates over competition costs. More importantly, we further identify that the positive impact of hotel agglomeration density on revenue is negatively moderated by consumer online review quantity in terms of volume and length. This finding implies the substitution effect of online review quantity on agglomeration density. Moreover, the substitution effect of high-quality review volume is stronger than that of low-quality. Spe cifically, the volume of high-quality online reviews negatively moder ates the agglomeration effectiveness, whereas low-quality online review has no similar effect. Furthermore, such a substitution effect is greater for independent hotels than for branded hotels, but is similar between hotels with various qualities. The conclusion may be valid for other industries (such as restaurant and retail industry) where agglomeration and online reviews are both important.

## 6.1. Theoretical contribution

This study offers pioneering theoretical insights by combining agglomeration theory with the information transparency perspecti ve—two research streams that are developed independently of each other. Specifically, several important theoretical insights are provided.

First, this study contributes to agglomeration theory by exploring the boundaries of agglomeration economies from an information transparency perspective. Empirical findings show that the effect of agglomeration density on firm revenue decreases when information becomes available and transparent. This finding extends extant litera ture that only considers the influence of firms’ internal characteristics (i. $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } _ { \cdot \boldsymbol { s } }$ brand or size) on agglomeration economies [7,33] to the external environment (i.e., information environment). Moreover, although agglomeration density and online reviews are identified as antecedents of firm performance, the present findings capture their connection, namely, search cost and information asymmetry, and advance the pre vious literature by indicating the interactive effect of agglomeration density and online reviews on firm revenue. Online reviews influence the effect of agglomeration density in two aspects. In terms of agglom eration benefits, the information transparency of online reviews mini mizes consumer search costs and information asymmetry, thereby functioning as a substitute for agglomeration density. In terms of competition cost, such a transparency increases market learning op portunities and localized competition, thereby increasing the negative effect of agglomeration density. The result also provides insights on the net agglomeration effect by showing the change from agglomeration economies to diseconomies with the increase of information trans parency. This observation addresses an existing contradiction on the positive effect of agglomeration density found in several studies but not in others [8,67].

Second, the present study adds novel knowledge to the literature on online reviews by highlighting the substituting roles of two important indicators of online reviews quantity (volume and length) and by exploring their differential influences with various qualities. Previous literature determines the direct positive effect of online review volume and length on firm revenue [18,21]. However, the present study identifies the indirect but negative moderating aspect of online reviews in influencing revenue. Therefore, online reviews serve as a double-edge sword on firm revenue. This finding may partially explain the previ ous contradiction that online reviews benefit several firms but not others [15]. Moreover, this study advances online review literature by indi cating the differences between high and low-quality reviews in changing the effect of agglomeration density on firm revenue. This observation extends previous literature that focuses only on the volume and valence of online reviews [14,19,27–29]. We demonstrate that online reviews with high quality are more important than those with low quality for the possible reason that the former displays a higher level of information transparency than the latter.

Third, the present findings provide new insights into theory of in formation transparency. We extend this view of information trans parency to the online review context and empirically find that online reviews weaken the effectiveness of agglomeration density. Previous studies mainly focus on the product and price information that are proactively posted by firms [35,51], but overlook the reviews posted by consumers in the market. Previous literature postulates that firms should implement strategies to enable transparent product and price informa tion and consider the trade-offs of revealing and concealing different types of market information. However, our findings suggest that the strategy of managing online reviews from the consumer side is also important. Different from the traditional view that information transparency is always positive for all stakeholders [51], we identify the detrimental effect of online review quantity to the economic value of agglomeration density by minimizing the information asymmetry be tween consumers and firms and increasing the competition costs of the focal firm. Therefore, the findings highlight the duality role of infor mation transparency. In addition, we contribute to information trans parency theory by combining the metrics of information quality with quantity, which can be used in future research.

Finally, this research exhibits new insights by investigating the roles of hotel chain affiliation and quality in differentiating the effects of agglomeration density and online reviews on firm revenue. The effect of hotel characteristics on consumer behavior is substantial. Yet, related literature is scarce [68]. The present results support the view that online reviews play a more important role for independent and low-quality hotels than for branded and high-quality hotels, consistent with the findings reported in the literature [68]. We also add new knowledge to extant literature by discovering the heterogeneous effect of online re views across different hotels or firms. The moderating influence of on line reviews on the relationship of agglomeration density with firm revenue is stronger for independent than for branded hotels. For inde pendent and low-quality hotels, potential consumers face greater un certainty and need more information from the number of online reviews to make relevant decisions. Given that various hotels and firms provide different products and services, their characteristics may also differen tiate the moderating influence of online reviews, which requires further exploration in future research.

## 6.2. Practical implications

In our study, we provide some new suggestions for hotel managers according to our findings. First, given that review quantity moderates the effect of agglomeration on revenue, finding ways to manage online reviews to increase or decrease review quantity is important for hotel managers. In particular, firms that face adverse geographical conditions can put more investments into online channels and strengthen online review management to overcome geographical disadvantage, because online reviews weaken the importance of geography. For example, firm managers can encourage consumers to post more and longer reviews in travel review platforms by providing responses or implementing a dis count policy. Although consumer reviews improve information trans parency, these contents may be too subjective or may even have large errors. Related to this, hotel managers should proactively and promptly respond to reviews that may cause negative effects or those that have errors. Furthermore, given that firms do not always benefit from infor mation transparency, managers should formulate an information transparency strategy to gain information advantage and avoid fierce competition. For example, hotel managers can avoid information spill over as much as possible through the enforcement of some restraints (e. g., contracts) in order to maintain its own information advantage.

Second, given that the substitution effect of online reviews on agglomeration density is greater for independent hotels than for chain hotels, but is similar between hotels with various qualities, managers of various types of hotels should take different measures to gain benefit while avoid cost. For example, managers of branded hotels should recognize the limitation of online information in weakening the benefit of agglomeration. By contrast, independent hotels should encourage consumers to leave online reviews, appropriately manage these reviews, and take advantage of online reviews to reduce customer uncertainty and risk prior to their purchase when they agglomerate with other hotels.

Third, we determine that more neighbor hotels within a range indicate higher revenue for the focal hotel, thus demonstrating the ad vantages of agglomeration over its disadvantages. Therefore, hotel managers should formulate the strategy of location choice or agglom eration. For example, for new hotels, managers should enter certain markets at the right time, and take advantage of agglomeration. For incumbent hotels, managers should judge whether their hotels are more likely to be beneficiaries or contributors in the agglomeration area ac cording to the characteristics (e.g., chain affiliation) of their hotel and neighboring hotels. Facing the entry and exit of neighbor hotels in local markets, managers should adjust their judgments and respond appro priately in terms of price.

We also provided practical implications for platform managers. Given that the volume of high-quality online reviews works as a moderator, while the volume of low-quality online review does not, platform managers should encourage the generation of online reviews, especially high-quality ones. For example, platforms can develop a more comprehensive review ranking system based on the accuracy and un derstandability of review information (e.g., spelling errors and read ability). They can also adopt some economic incentive measures to encourage consumers to post high-quality reviews for the hotel after consumption. By optimizing the platform functions (e.g., votes and prepared review topics), managers may encourage consumers to post more understandable and valuable reviews.

## 6.3. Limitations and future work

First, the present findings are limited to a single source of online reviews and one industry. Future work can incorporate data sources from other industries (e.g., music) and platforms (e.g., Airbnb) to vali date the proposed model. Second, agglomeration density depends on the attractiveness of location to some extent. However, our research does not distinguish location from the influence of density. Although the ef fect of geographic location is controlled in our model by including the hotel fixed effect, the quality of the location constantly evolves and develops. Third, due to the unavailability of media data, we cannot analyze the differences among media (e.g., picture and video) reviews. Given the popularity of media reviews, this variable can be considered in future research.

Future research can take several possible directions. First, given that agglomeration and online reviews are also important in different prod ucts (e.g., search goods) or other sectors (e.g., restaurant and services) [57,69], future studies can apply and extend our model into other in dustries, and explore any industry-, product-, and service-specific dif ferences to deepen our understanding. Second, given the various consumer information and search channels in the online environment [70], such as expert reviews, blogs, and search engines, exploring their substitution role for traditional channels and comparing their difference can further extend research on information transparency research. Third, other outcomes (e.g., innovation) could be used to assess per formance. Innovation is an important performance indicator in the high-tech industry, where agglomeration is common. As innovation relies heavily on information and knowledge spillover, the interaction of online and offline activities may be observed. Therefore, one direction for future research would be to explore whether our findings hold for other outcome measures. Finally, the role of review content can be further explored in the future [71]. For example, if the content similarity is high, the value of the review volume may decrease. Hence, future research could pay attention to this direction.

His research has appeared in Tourism Management, International Journal of Hospitality Management, Decision Support Systems, Information & Manage ment, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, IEEE Transactions on SMC: Systems, Electronic Commerce Research and Applications, China Economic Review, and Service Science.
