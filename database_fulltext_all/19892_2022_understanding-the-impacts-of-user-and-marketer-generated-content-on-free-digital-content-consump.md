# Understanding the impacts of user- and marketer- generated content on free digital content consumption

Kexin Zhao <sup>a,\*</sup>, Peiqin Zhang <sup>b</sup>, Hsun-Ming Lee <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Information Systems & Operations Management, Belk College of Business, University of North Carolina at Charlotte, Charlotte, NC, USA <sup>b</sup> Department of Computer Information Systems & Quantitative Methods, McCoy College of Business, Texas State University, San Marcos, TX, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: User-generated content Marketer-generated content Free digital content consumption Social media Digital workout Exercise intensity

## A B S T R A C T

The literature has shown that user-generated content (UGC) and marketer-generated content (MGC) simulta neously influence consumers’ decision-making process. Yet it is unclear how UGC and MGC jointly affect the overall market reaction, when digital content is freely available to all consumers. To fill the gap, we integrate the UGC and MGC literature and research of determinants of physical exercise to develop a comprehensive conceptual model. Using a dataset collected from a leading online fitness brand, we find that both UGC and MGC affect a free fitness video's incremental view count. We further categorize UGC into three dimensions, where favorability represents the normative dimension, various textual features represent the informational dimension, and reply-density of the self-organized reviewer community represents the social dimension. While both normative and social UGC are positively associated with online fitness videos’ view count increase, the impact of informational UGC is insignificant. More importantly, product features, such as exercise intensity, moderate the impacts of UGC and MGC on free digital content consumption. When facing strenuous exercises, the consumer group relies more on peer users and less on marketers to make their content consumption decisions. The findings provide insightful implications to understand how consumers decide their engagement with free digital content delivered via social media channels.

## 1. Introduction

We have witnessed numerous digital transformations in the past two decades. One such example is content consumption, where people are consuming more and more content online, such as social media, books, music, news, TV, and fitness streaming videos. The Covid-19 pandemic accelerates such trend. Global digital content consumption has doubled in 2020, and average people can spend more than a quarter of their waking time on mobile screens [31]. Varieties of digital content are created and delivered to consumers, and competition for consumers attention is fierce [29]. Consumers face a lot of choices and uncertainties while choosing digital content. To make informed decisions, they are likely to seek a breadth of information prior to their consumption. Literature has shown that user-generated content (UGC) and marketergenerated content (MGC) simultaneously influence consumers decision-making process [23,54,55]. UGC refers to content created by consumers, such as numeric product rating, “Like”, detailed review comments, as well as discussions and social interactions between peer users. MGC refers to marketing and promotional messages created by

brand owners.

Businesses offer many different types of digital content at no cost using sampling strategies, freemium business models, and advertising sales [42]. When consumers incur very little costs to try digital content that is freely available to them. do we expect that both UGC and MGC would still matter? Digital content varies in terms of product features, which may affect how consumers utilize UGC and MGC. For instance, UGC affects used video games’ price decline differently based on games’ violence level and social orientation [67]. While prior studies have studied effectiveness of UGC and MGC in general [23,54,55], it is unclear whether and how UGC and MGC’s impacts will be contingent on product features. UGC can be represented through different formats. Based on the dual-process theory, Mikalef et al. [41] differentiate and compare normative UGC and informational UGC. Normative UGC is shown as summary rating scores aggregated from all past consumers, and infor mational UGC refers to detailed textual content of user reviews [41]. In addition, UGC can emerge from self-organized user communities formed around digital content. According to Scholz et al. [54], socialization among users increases online retailers’ conversion rate. We name thi type of UGC presentation formats as social UGC, which is derived from communications and social interactions among users of the same prod uct. Based on the due-process theory, normative UGC is more intuitive and apparent than informational and social UGC [41]. We intend to understand how normative, informational, and social UGCs work in parallel in influencing free digital content consumption.

To answer those research questions, we examine the impacts of UGC and MGC on free digital content consumption. The digital fitness streaming market provides us an ideal research context for the following reasons. First, both UGC and MGC can be observed, since digital fitness enterprises operate in a hyper-connected world and they co-create their brands with consumers on social media platforms [59]. Enabled by emerging technologies, digital workout becomes a revolution that re defines many aspects of the fitness industry [20]. Numerous techno logical solutions, such as wearable devices, mobile applications, and streaming service, enable businesses to deliver fitness products via Internet-connected equipment and digital channels. Notably many fitness enterprises deliver free exercise videos via social media channels, such as YouTube. While consumers “Like”, comment on, and virtually exchange ideas of fitness videos, fitness brands create and deliver mar keting messages through companies’ website and Twitter accounts.

Second, digital fitness routines vary in terms of exercise intensity. More strenuous exercise routines are riskier and require more effort and involvement from consumers. Consumers need to pay closer attention and scrutinize related information in a more elaborated manner before they participate in exercises of higher intensity. Therefore, we can examine whether impacts of UGC and MGC on digital content con sumption vary based on exercise intensity.

Third, the fitness industry represents an important yet under researched industry sector. Physical activity is beneficial to people’s physiological and psychological health and general quality of life [6]. According to a recent survey conducted by the Physical Activity Council, more than 70% of US population age 6 or older participate in various types of physical activities, such as sports, outdoor activities, as well as fitness and exercise.<sup>1</sup> With the growing number of consumers interested in healthy and active lifestyles, the fitness industry is booming and valued at nearly \$100 billion globally [5]. Despite its economic and practical importance, the fitness industry, especially the digital revolu tion that it is experiencing, has received little attention from academia.

We integrate the UGC and MGC literature and research of de terminants of physical exercise to develop the conceptual model. We study three types of UGC: normative, informational, and social UGC. In our research context, normative UGC is measured by a single favorability rating from all past consumers, and informational UGC is measured as various textual features of user reviews [41]. In addition, we use replydensity of self-organized user communities formed around a fitness video to quantify social UGC. We also consider MGC from two sources: the company’s blog and Twitter account. We collect panel data from a leading online fitness brand to empirically validate the proposed model. Data are collected from multiple sources, including the company’s website, blogs, and Twitter account, public media coverage of the company, as well as the YouTube platform. Text mining, social network analytics, and econometric analysis are utilized to analyze the dataset.

Overall, our findings provide insightful implications to understand how consumers decide their engagement with free digital content delivered via social media channels. We extend the UGC and MGC research into the digital fitness market, and confirm that both UGC and MGC affect free fitness videos consumption. We enrich UGC literature by proposing social UGC and revealing subtle differences between three types of UGC. While both normative and social UGC are positively associated with online fitness videos’ view count increase, the impact of informational UGC, such as subjectivity and readability, is insignificant.

When the digital content is freely available, consumers pay limited attention to detailed textual content of online reviews. Our research also highlights that the impacts of UGC and MGC are contingent on product features, such as exercise intensity. As expected, consumers engage less in physically challenging digital workout. More importantly, UGC and MGC have differential impacts on free digital content consumption based on exercise intensity. UGC’s positive influences are strengthened by exercise intensity. By contrast, influences from MGC are weakened by exercise intensity. Consumers rely more on peer users and less on mar keters when deciding to view more physically challenging workout videos.

We organize the rest of the paper as follows. First, we synthesize the UGC and MGC literature and the determinants of physical exercise research to propose the research framework. Next, we discuss each hy pothesis in detail. We then introduce our research setting, data collec tion procedures, methods of data processing and analyses, and hypotheses testing results. Finally, we discuss the implications for academia and practitioners, and opportunities for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. UGC and MGC literature

UGC and MGC have been studied extensively in the information systems and marketing disciplines. Nevertheless, only a small fraction of those studies has simultaneously investigated both UGC and MGC in driving consumers’ brand engagement or purchase decisions [54,55]. We briefly summarize relevant literature that examines joint impacts of UGC and MGC in Table 1, and compare prior studies with the current research to highlight our contributions.

Both UGC and MGC are important in consumers’ decision-making process [7,40,55]. Literature has mostly studied products or service that consumers need to pay to enjoy, such as vacation destination [7 35], physical goods [12,23,41,54], sports events [40], or movies shown in theaters [55]. It is unclear how UGC and MGC will jointly affect con sumers’ consumption of digital content. A lot of digital content, such as fitness videos, music, and sample TV series, is freely available to consumers via alternative pricing strategies [29]. It is intriguing whether consumers still pay attention to UGC and MGC when they incur zero financial expenses to use such content.

There are different types of UGC and MGC. Mikalef et al. [41] apply the dual-process theory to categorize UGC into normative and infor mational factors, where the former shows aggregate attitudes from all reviewers and the latter shows detailed comments from each individual reviewer. Due to advances of text mining techniques, informational UGC has been examined thoroughly. Various textural features, such as depth and valence, have been used to quantify informational UGC [7,23]. A few studies also include normative UGC, such as Likes [54] and numeric rating summary [41]. Social UGC, or social interactions among re viewers, has been largely overlooked. Goh et al. [23] differentiate directed and undirected communications among users, where undi rected UGC is more influential than directed UGC. Goh et al. [23] conduct the individual-level analysis, while we perform the productlevel analysis. Thus, we can identify online communities formed among reviewers of the same product, and explore whether those communities would affect consumers’ digital content consumption. Reply-density reflects strength of social interactions among users of the same fitness video, and is used to quantify social UGC in our research. Our comprehensive model also incorporates MGC coming from two different channels, blogs and tweets. Furthermore, we differentiate fitness videos based on exercise intensity, and explore whether UGC and MGC affect consumers’ digital content consumption differently based on heterogeneous product features.

## 2.2. Research of the determinants of physical exercise

To gain insight into consumers’ engagement with digital fitness videos, we review literature that examines factors motivating their participation in physical exercise in general. Dishman et al.’s [18] seminal work provides a nice literature review of the determinants of physical activities. They summarize exercise determinants into three categories. The first category is personal characteristics, such as age, physical competence, and lifestyle habits. The second category is envi ronmental characteristics, such as access to facilities and influences from family and peers. The third category is activity characteristics, including activity intensity and perceived discomfort. Due to data limitations, we do not directly observe consumer characteristics. Instead, we focus on both environmental and activity characteristics.

UGC and MGC are various types of external environmental factors that may increase consumers’ awareness and interests in workout created by online fitness brands. Social UGC is especially important in our research context, as interacting with others can change people’s perception and attitudes towards exercise [18]. Carron et al. have con ducted a meta-analysis to understand the impact of social influence on exercise behaviors and shown that “social influence generally has a small to moderate positive effect” [9,P.1]. Individuals are more likely to form exercise habits if they experience supportive interactions with people around them. In our case, people usually follow digital workout alone. Nevertheless, they can still virtually interact and socialize with other users via social media platforms. During the process of creating, reading, and commenting each other’s reviews, virtual communities are formed among digital workout consumers. Those virtual communities represent an important type of social influence, which might affect the focal individual's behavior [4,24,68].

Exercise routines vary in terms of intensity, and more intensive ac tivities require more efforts and skills from individuals. When exercise intensity increases, people are more likely to get injured, and feel discomfort or displeasure [18,44]. Thus, we add exercise intensity as a predictor of consumers’ engagement in digital workout. Moreover, im pacts of environmental factors may be differentiated by exercise in tensity. According to Duncan et al. [19], a sense of obligation drives strenuous exercises. When exercise becomes more strenuous, in dividuals need to carefully evaluate and internalize extrinsic motivations to be part of it. UGC tends to be more credible and trust worthy than MGC [34], and consumers may evaluate UGC and MGC differently based on exercise intensity.

## 3. Hypotheses

We propose a research model to investigate how UGC and MGC jointly affect consumers’ decision to view free digital content. Our dependent variable is the incremental view count of a fitness video, a proxy reflecting the decision to enjoy free digital content from the consumer group. Due to lack of data at the individual consumer level, our dependent variable only captures the overall market reaction to a video. We are unable to differentiate unique views contributed by different consumers from repetitive views contributed by an individual consumer. Social media platforms simultaneously distribute online streaming content and communicate consumer experiences. We differ entiate between three dimensions of UGC that could influence the consumers’ preference: normative, informational, and social di mensions. In addition, we consider MGC created by a brand owner to engage consumers through both blogs and Twitter. We also consider exercise intensity that can play a moderating role in the effects of UGC and MGC on digital content consumption.

## 3.1. UGC

UGC on social media is electronic word of mouth (eWOM) from consumers, which provides a direct impetus to influence consumers decision-making process [11,51]. Normative, informational, and social factors are three different dimensions of UGC in affecting the consumers product evaluation.

## 3.1.1. UGC: normative dimension

Normative UGC considers the aggregation of opinions and percep tions of past buyers [41]. One of the most common representation mechanisms of the normative dimension is favorability, which can be calculated in terms of likes and dislikes given to a specific product [36]. Since social media builds brands to be transparent, winning favorable responses from customers is essential to the success of branding [32]. Due to the voluntary nature of UGC, favorable opinions from fellow consumers are highly trusted and helpful for establishing the brand’s reputation [57]. Favorability indicates collective reputation regarding a product or a service [3]. Consumers are interested in trying products that are previously enjoyed and praised by peer users, so we propose that:

H1a. The favorability of UGC is positively associated with digital workout content consumption.

## 3.1.2. UGC: informational dimension

Informational UGC captures the quality of detailed online user re views [41]. The information quality of UGC is crucial in affecting con sumers’ decision-making [47]. Based on extensive literature in the eWOM area [22,67], the information quality can be measured by various textual features, including length, sentiments (positivity or negativity), subjectivity, and readability.

First, a shorter message, likely offering less detailed information, is perceived as less convincing and lower quality as opposed to a longer one [43]. When consumers spend time to evaluate alternatives, a lengthy text tends to be highly diagnostic and provides detailed infor mation that reduces uncertainty in the quality of a product or service [58]. Hence, we hypothesize that:

H1b. The length of detailed online user review texts is positively associated with digital workout content consumption.

Second, negative review content, conveying undesirable experience in consuming a product or service, can increase perceived uncertainty and risk associated with online purchase decisions [61]. With the mix of positive and negative information, negative information has stronger influence due to negativity bias, suggesting that people weigh negativity more heavily than positivity in terms of decision-making [21,49]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

H1c. The negativity of detailed online user review texts is negatively associated with digital workout content consumption.

Third, subjective messages reflecting on individual’s personal pref erences towards the products and service tend to be less informative and provide limited values to support consumers’ decisions [48]. UGC con taining subjective information poses as suspicious to customers, reducing its credibility [37]. Challenges associated with subjective messages may motivate consumers to discontinue their product search without committing to a purchase decision. Hence, we hypothesize that:

H1d. The subjectivity of detailed online user review texts is negatively associated with digital workout content consumption.

Finally, although texts with lower readability are less comprehen sible [33], a message with lower readability can be perceived as having more authority, which increases the message’s credibility and infor mativeness [22]. Past studies find that the messages with higher read ability are more likely to be rated as less helpful [33] and readability is negatively associated with products’ sales [22]. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1e. The readability of detailed online user review texts is negatively associated with digital workout content consumption.

## 3.1.3. UGC: social dimension

Peer effects are generated from social interactions. Past literature indicates that UGC provides companies an effective way to drive con sumer demand and product diffusion through peer-to-peer influences [1]. On content distribution platforms such as YouTube, a consumer can develop perceived-proximity to their peers via interactive online com munications [63]. One type of the highly interactive communications occurs when a consumer replies to a comment created by their peers. Soon after the reply is sent, the peer receives an automated notification, which establishes a bi-directional connection to form a virtual community among reviewers.

Interpersonal connections among consumers generate social in fluences in purchase decisions. Dewan et al. [16] study music con sumption in social networks and find that perceived-proximity increases the likelihood of listening to a song by 10.2%. Wilson et al. [63] point out that the social influences in a consumer network depend on the network structure, where dense networks increase consumers identification with their peers, help develop community norms, and enhance participation motivation in the communities. A more tightly intertwined community has a stronger influence on its members [30]. Therefore, we use reply-density of self-organized communities formed among reviewers of a video to quantify the social dimension of UGC and propose that:

H1f. The reply-density of self-organized reviewer communities is positively associated with digital workout content consumption.

## 3.2. MGC

Among normative/informational/social dimensions, only the infor mational dimension is applicable to MGC. MGC is created by the brand owner, and opinion aggregation or the normative dimension is not needed. Social UGC reflects interpersonal connections among reviewers of the same video. This research focuses on content solely created by the brand owner, and the social dimension is not applicable. Since all posts are created by the same brand owner, several MGC’s textual features are highly correlated, and there are limited variations in negativity/ subjectivity/readability across MGC posts. To avoid multicollinearity issues, we only keep length to quantify informational MGC. In addition, MGC volume is an important indicator to reflect the brand owner’s marketing effort [40].

Organizational blogs publish marketer-generated information to engage customers with business goals [62]. The blogs offer a lot of abilities to create a new business, ranging from distributing content that fits customers’ interests (such as new product ideas, event announce ments, and expertise demonstration), initiating customer communica tion, and gathering customers’ feedback for market research [14,15]. For example, Dewan and Ramaprasad [17] show that blogging for newly released songs is particularly effective for driving the sampling of music available online.

Microblogging (such as Twitter), pushing short messages to mobile users on the Internet, has become widely popular for fostering companyconsumer interactions or building consumer-brand relationships [50,66]. Different from traditional blogging, microblogging allows users to follow a brand and obtain interesting information about the brand without proactively seeking it [27]. Twitter is effective to promote a brand in the sports industry. For instance, soccer clubs can use Twitter to instantly disseminate match-related content during the season, such as star players’ statistics and team scores [46]. A study finds that a business engaging in brand communication on Twitter increases the consumers activities associated with the brand [65].

MGC is a brand’s communication, which can be created and shared through online social networks. Volume of MGC reflects engagement and efforts of the brand owner, as it takes time to create, edit, and post content on blogs and Twitter. Higher volume of MGC can lead to heightened customer participation with the brand on social media [40]. Therefore, we propose that:

H2a. The volume of MGC posted by the brand owner is positively associated with its digital workout content consumption.

MGC helps brand owners to create awareness and change attitudes among prospective customers [38]. The text length of an advertisement, implying strength in persuasion, has been found to have positive effects on consumer attitudes [39]. In social media, the project description length provided by the owner is significantly associated with the pro ject’s crowdfunding success [69]. Hence, we propose that:

H2b. The length of MGC posted by the brand owner is positively associated with its digital workout content consumption.

## 3.3. The moderating role of exercise intensity

Previous studies have indicated that consumers’ reliance on online content for purchasing decisions could vary across different types of products, even within the same category [67]. The informationprocessing motivation depends on product involvement, which refers to the consumers’ long-term interest (personal relevance) in a product class [47]. The needs/goals of the consumers can determine the levels of involvement, which help increase attention on the product-related in formation, as well as cognitive effort during comprehension [10,25].

In our study, the videos of high-intensity exercises are highinvolvement products that significantly reduce psychological pleasure [44,45]. Consumers who are interested in high-involvement products are motivated to spend time searching for product information to reduce the likelihood of making wrong decisions [13]. Because UGC enjoys reputation as dedicated and independent sources for consumers [11], the individuals engaged in the exercises with higher intensity rely more on UGC in their decision process. MGC is produced by the brand owners with explicit commercial interests. Therefore, individuals do not likely recognize MGC as an independent and trustworthy source for product information [23]. Strenuous exercise routines are riskier and require more efforts. People would need to have sufficient motivation to maintain high-intensity exercise and information from non-independent sources may be trusted less in this case. Therefore, we propose that:

H3. The effects of UGC on digital workout content consumption are stronger for higher-intensity exercises; however, the effects of MGC on digital workout content consumption are weaker for higher-intensity exercises.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

To empirically validate the proposed research model, we chose to examine a leading online fitness brand, Fitness Blender, as our research context for several reasons. Fitness Blender is one of the most-viewed fitness channels on YouTube and has more than 6 million subscribers in 2020 [2]. As a pioneering online fitness enterprise, Fitness Blender offers us the opportunity to collect a rich dataset to understand how UGC and MGC affect consumers’ engagement with digital workout. In addi tion to the official YouTube channel, Fitness Blender actively manages its company’s official website as well as Twitter account to promote and market its brand, and engages consumers with its fitness products. Using Fitness Blender as a focal case, it is feasible to investigate both UGC and MGC simultaneously. Furthermore, Fitness Blender creates and pub lishes hundreds of workout videos over the years, which cover a wide range of exercise intensities.

To collect data at the video level, we first obtained the YouTube video profiles from FitnessBlender.com using the crawler software developed by the authors. Then, we developed a desktop application to collect the UGC-related data, such as video view counts, like counts, dislike counts, review comments, and replies using YouTube Data API and the Python programming language. Most fitness applications pro duce weekly summaries to provide users workout suggestions [28]. Since workout tends to follow weekly plans, we adopt “week” as the temporal unit in this study to quantify people’s collective engagement in fitness videos. We ran the data collection application weekly and ob tained the data of the YouTube videos (total 542 videos) published by Fitness Blender from February to September in 2018.

All UGC measures were computed at the video level. There are many Python packages available to quantify informational UGC. We used VADER to compute the negativity score for the text of each review. Negativity scores vary between 0.0 and 1.0, where a higher score in dicates stronger negativity. We then measured the variable “Negativity” as the average negativity score of all the reviews for each video. We used Textblob to compute the subjectivity score of each review. Subjectivity scores are between 0.0 and 1.0, where a higher score means the review is more subjective. Similarly, subjectivity was measured as the average subjectivity score of all the reviews for each video. We used TextStat to compute the Flesch reading ease score for each review. Readability scores vary between 0 and 100, where a higher score means the review is easier to read. We also measured the readability as the average read ability score of all the reviews for each video. To quantify social UGC, we used NetworkX to compute reply-density for the reviewer community of each video. In the network, nodes are all the reviewers of the video and edges are included according to the link between two users connected by the reply of a review.

We collected MGC data on weekly basis from February to September in 2018. We used a browser to view the blogs posted on the company website (FitnessBlender.com) and recorded the text of each blog. Simi larly, we collected and recorded the Tweets posted on the company’s Twitter site (https://twitter.com/FitnessBlender). MGC variables were computed at the company level, and we intend to explore whether the brand owner’s overall promotional efforts can encourage consumers engagement with its individual products.

In addition to UGC and MGC, we also control for public media exposure, such as, news appearance and news length, which may also affect consumers’ decision to view online fitness videos. We searched for news articles by the keywords “fitness blender” through Google News. The news appearance for a company or an organization tends to occur sparsely and infrequently [56], and the company might often have no news at all for an entire week. Given Fitness Blender as a new brand, a large share of the news content has a positive tone [60]. As a result, news length is highly correlated with other textual features of news.<sup>2</sup> For these reasons, we focus on two critical features of the news items (i.e., news appearance dummy and text length) in our research as control variables.

The moderating variable, exercise intensity, is measured by four related factors: difficulty level, burned calories (upper calorie & lower calorie), and duration. We used principal component analysis (PCA) to create a composite exercise intensity index based on these factors. The first principal component (PC1) represents the maximum variance di rection in the data, and proportion of variance explained by PC1 is 80.97%. In addition, we estimated the model using each individual factor to represent exercise intensity, and the results were consistent.

We provide the detailed descriptions of all variables and their descriptive statistics in Table 2. Table 3 reports Pearson correlation matrix of all variables, and the matrix shows that most of the correlations between independent variables are less than the threshold of 0.6 [67] except for News and NewsLength, BlogVol and BlogLength, TweetVol and TweetLength, and Volume and PriorView. We further conducted a VIF test to detect the potential multicollinearity issue. The VIF values of all the independent variables are found to be below 4 that are less than the threshold of 10 [8,26]. Therefore, there is no multicollinearity concern in our estimation.

For example, videos’ popularity and users’ preferences for certain types of exercises might be correlated with review volume and also cause in crease of a video’s view count. If omitted variables not only impact a video’s incremental view count but also affect review volume, we might face the endogeneity issue. We address this issue using Two Stage Least Squares (2SLS) with instrument variables (IV) [22,64]. In the first stage, the endogenous variable (volume) is regressed on instrument variables and all exogenous variables to recover the estimated residual. In the second stage, we replace the endogenous covariate with its estimated residual from the first stage to the regression model to correct potential endogeneity concern. The IV approach requires that instrumental vari ables affect review volume, but do not directly affect incremental video view count. The two instruments that we find suitable are the average volume of all other videos belonging to the same type, and lagged review volume. They are denoted as volumeIV1 and volumeIV2 respectively. The average volume of all other videos in the same video category can be correlated with review volume because they both are driven by the underlying users’ specific unobserved variables, such as preference to wards certain workout subcategories. However, after controlling for exogenous variables, the incremental number of one focal video’s view counts is not directly affected by the average volume of the other videos. In addition, the review volume in the prior week is likely to be related with the review volume in the current week, but unlikely to be directly correlated with the error term that determines the incremental views for the current week. Indeed, our results show that the instrument variables volumeIV1 and volumeIV2 are all significantly correlated with review volume (β = 0.001 and 0.988 respectively, p = 0.040 and 0.000 respectively).

The sign of the coefficient of favorability suggests that more favor able online workout leads to an increase on total video views (β = 0.052, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , indicating that normative UGC positively influences users consumption of online workout videos and supporting H1a. This is consistent with prior literature, which has indicated that normative UGC, such as aggregated likes and star rating, influences consumers decision making [41,54]. The coefficient of reply-density has a positive and statistically significant relationship with incremental video views (β $= 0 . 1 1 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , supporting H1f. This suggests that higher replydensities among reviewer communities are associated with higher in cremental video views. In other words, social UGC increases users consumption of online fitness videos. Prior research has shown that a user’s behavior may be influenced by their online peers with similar interests [4,68]. Therefore, higher density of a video’s virtual commu nity can lead to more video view counts. Interestingly and counterin tuitively, informational UGC (i.e., all textual features of detailed user reviews) is statistically insignificant in the final model. Our results show that review length, negativity, subjectivity, and readability do not affect weekly increase of videos’ view count, which does not support H1b-H1e. One possible explanation is that, since Fitness Blender’s online workout videos are free and users can quit at any point, consumers are more likely to rely on aggregated indicators, such as favorability, to make their viewing decisions. They might not pay close attention to detailed reviews posted by other users.

In terms of MGC effects, the coefficients of blog volume and Tweet volume are positive and statistically significant (β = 0.015 and 0.005 respectively, p < 0.001 for both variables), suggesting that online fitness brands that have higher exposure via blogs and Tweets are associated with increased video views and supporting H2a. MGC volume may enhance consumers’ brand awareness and interests, which encourage user engagement with the brand [52]. MGC has been shown as the conventional source of information that consumers use to assess prod ucts [41]. Therefore, MGC can increase users’ consumption of online fitness videos. In addition, our findings provide evidence that Tweet length is positive and statistically significant (β = 0.007 and $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which suggests that Tweet that are longer and more detailed are asso ciated with increased video view counts. The coefficient of blog length has a negative and statistically significant relationship with incremental video views $( \beta = - 0 . 0 0 6 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which is counterintuitive. One plausible explanation is that lengthy blogs might be perceived as selfpromotion by the brand, make users tired of them, and alienate the users. Therefore, H2b is partially supported.

Our results indicate that exercise intensity level has a negative effect on incremental video views $( \beta = - 0 . 0 8 2 , \textrm { p < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which makes sense. The more intense the online workout is, the more likely users hesitate to participate in the exercise by following the video online.

As we have hypothesized, the influences of favorability and replydensity are stronger for higher-intensity exercises. In other words, the moderating effect of exercise intensity on UGC is statistically significant and positive. Specifically, the coefficient of the interaction between favorability and exercise intensity is positive and statistically significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 4 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , suggesting that exercise intensity level enhances the positive relationship between favorability and incremental video views. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that the intensity of an individual’s exercise is related to external influences [19]. Through social media, consumers reply on clues from their peers, such as videos’ favorability, to determine their engagement in intense exercise. Similarly, the coefficient of the interaction between replydensity and exercise intensity is positive and statistically significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 5 9 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , indicating that exercise intensity enhances the positive influence of reply-density on incremental video views. When the exercise is more intense, peer influence becomes even more impor tant. Consumers from a more tightly connected virtual community are more likely to engage in challenging online workout. Samendinger et al. [53] find that a virtual partner can significantly increase enjoyment for people performing higher, rather than lower, intensity of exercises.

Contrary to UGC, our results suggest that the influence of MGC is weaker for higher-intensity exercises. The coefficient of blog volume’s interaction with exercise intensity is negative and statistically signifi cant $( \beta = - 0 . 0 0 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , suggesting that intense level weakens the positive relationship between the number of blogs and incremental video views. This means that the organization’s blogs are less likely to motivate consumers to participate in more strenuous exercises. The coefficient of the interaction between blog length and exercise intensity is positive and statistically significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 1 , \ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , which suggests that intensity level weakens the negative impact of blog length on incremental video views. This means that the negative impacts of longer blogs are less influential when consumers rely less on the blogs to develop interests in engaging in higher-intensity exercises. Prior litera ture has shown that MGC is less trustworthy than UGC [34]. MGC is generated by brand owners to introduce their products or services, while UGC is generated by consumers who have actual usage experiences [34]. For more intense exercises, individuals are more likely to make partic ipation decisions based on other peers’ objective experience instead of marketers’ promotion. Therefore, our H3 is supported based on the above results about the interactions of exercise intensity with UGC and MGC.

With respect to control variables, public media exposure such as news and news length (β = 0.005 and 0.007 respectively, p < 0.05 and 0.001 respectively) are positively associated with incremental video views. It suggests that online fitness brands with news and lengthy news exposure can attract users’ awareness and increase their content con sumption. We also find that video views in the prior week are positively associated with incremental video views $( \beta = 0 . 6 6 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . It suggests that the trend of high video view counts in the previous week tends to continue. Furthermore, release days are negatively and signif icantly associated with incremental video views (β = − 0.277, p < 0.001), suggesting gradually diminishing interests in online workout videos. Monthly dummies are also negatively related to incremental video views, further confirming diminishing consumer interests in older videos. In addition, our findings conclude that review volume is posi tively associated with incremental video views (β = 0.220, p < 0.01). While informational UGC do not matter, sheer quantity of reviews en courages user engagement in online workout.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Implications for research

The key theoretical contribution of this study is to gain new insight into the influence of UGC and MGC on consumers’ decision-making in a hyper-connected world. We extend the research on UGC and MGC to the consumption of free digital content. We explore how consumers rely on UGC and MGC to make consumption decisions of fitness streaming videos particularly. Digital fitness products are intangible and experi ential, and it is difficult for consumers to evaluate before consumption. Therefore, various types of UGC (normative, informational, and social UGC) and MGC provide some important clues to guide consumers engagement decisions. Our research is one of the first studies to inves tigate various types of UGC and MGC in a comprehensive model.

Using data collected from a leading and well-known online fitness brand, we demonstrate that both UGC and MGC are important to encourage the consumption of digital fitness videos. We also suggest the need to include social UGC as one antecedent of digital content con sumption. To fully understand the effects of UGC, it is crucial to recognize virtual communities formed among users as a leverage to broaden and deepen the reach of products in the marketplace [59]. In this study, we extract reply-density of users’ comment network as a proxy for social UGC and show its positive influence on user engagement in digital workout. Our research also shows differences among the direct impacts of the different types of UGC. Text analytics enables us to quantify informational UGC, which reveals consumers’ responses to detailed textual content. Since digital workouts we examine here are freely accessible, users do not pay close attention to various textual features, differing from results reported in prior UGC studies [22,67]. Moreover, impacts of MGC are statistically significant. They are all positive, except that the length of brand owners’ blogs has a negative impact.

Since our dependent variable is users’ engagement in individual digital workout, we also include exercise characteristics in the model. Specifically, we use product involvement to justify that exercise in tensity matters in consumers’ responses to environmental messages. We propose to use intensity level to differentiate exercises, and empirically validate its moderating effect. For more intense exercises, our results suggest that positive influences from users, whether via normative or social UGC, are strengthened, while influences from MGC are weakened. When facing strenuous exercises, it is possible that individuals trust UGC more than MGC. Thus, it is more likely for them to make decisions incorporating UGC rather than MGC.

We contribute to knowledge development in the fields of UGC and MGC by revealing their joint impacts on the consumption of digital fitness content. We also propose and validate the moderating role of exercise intensity. We show subtle yet important differences among in fluences from various types of UGC and MGC. The findings of this paper open numerous avenues for future study on the emerging and thriving digital content business.

## 6.2. Implications for practices

This research also provides valuable managerial implications for digital fitness practitioners. E-commerce has presented fitness owners with a new means to improve marketing efficiency, engage their con sumers, and even deliver products and services to end users. Fitness owners should carefully manage the social media channels where their digital workout videos are posted. With the popularity of social media channels, UGC and MGC affect consumers’ behavior and decision making, which in turn, influence the e-business models that those en terprises need to implement to conduct business online. Fitness owners therefore need to be aware of the number of video views made by consumers, and more importantly, what factors can influence the view decisions of their consumers.

Our results indicate that favorability has a significant influence on free fitness videos’ view count. This implies that if a fitness owner can provide more resources to improve the favorability of its online con sumer reviews, it is likely to receive more video views. At the end of each video, the brand owners might remind and encourage their viewers to click the “Like” button. Increasing consumers’ engagement with digital workout videos can also lead to additional views and followers in the following weeks. Furthermore, fitness practitioners may setup an effective strategy to encourage consumers to create virtual workout communities so that users can interact and be motivated by other in dividuals with similar workout interests. The findings also demonstrate that MGC significantly impacts the video view count. According to the empirical results, fitness owners may utilize their online communication channels via blogs and Twitter to increase consumer awareness of their brands as well as digital workout products they offer. While Twitter has the 280-character limit, the fitness brand owners might also keep their blogs concise and right to the point.

The results show the differential impacts of UGC and MGC across product sub-categories. Despite having little trust from customers, MGC can be tailored and customized to promote the consumption of digital content with low product involvement. Meanwhile, UGC could be very valuable for forecasting consumption of digital content with high product involvement.

## 7. Conclusion and future research opportunities

This study intends to investigate how UGC and MGC affect free digital content consumption. Specifically, this research simultaneously examines impacts of three different types of UGC and MGC from two different channels on consumer engagement in digital workout content. We further explore the moderating role of exercise intensity. According to the results, we have found that normative UGC, favorability, has a significant positive impact on incremental video view counts of digital workout, suggesting that consumers would engage more with their favorable exercises. We also find that social UGC, reply-density, posi tively affects consumers’ participation in their workout activity. Replydensity captures peer influence from virtual communities formed among consumers of the same digital workout video. Online peers with similar interests influence a user’s behavior, as social network density is shown to increase video view counts [4,68]. Interestingly, contrary to many prior eWOM studies, informational UGC exerts no impact on consump tion of free digital workout videos.

MGC, such as blogs and Tweets, attracts consumers’ attention and increase their awareness, resulting in more views for individual videos. Our results suggest that the lengthier Tweets increase consumers engagement, leading to more video view counts. However, shorter blogs from marketers are preferred by consumers.

The results indicate that exercise intensity negatively influences consumers’ engagement with workout videos. Moreover, we find that exercise intensity enhances positive influences from users, including both normative UGC and social UGC. On the contrary, effects of MGC are reduced by exercise intensity. Those findings shed new light on UGC and MGC research.

There are some limitations in the study, which suggest future research opportunities. First, findings of this paper were based on the fitness video data collected from one fitness brand, Fitness Blender. Since our hypotheses are proposed based on well-established UGC and MGC literature and determinants of physical exercise studies, we believe that our model can be generalized. Future research would be beneficial by testing our theoretical framework using data collected from other online fitness brands as well as producers of other types of free digital content, and further validating external validity of our results. Second, there are some personal characteristics, such as $\mathsf { a g e } ,$ physical compe tence, and lifestyle habits, may also affect consumers’ engagement in digital workout videos. Due to the feasibility of data collection, we could not directly observe detailed consumer characteristics. Therefore, it would be interesting to investigate the impacts of consumer character istics when data collection is feasible. It would be interesting to explore whether different types of consumers will react to UGC and MGC differently. It might help digital content producers to further customize content and marketing messages to different consumer segments. In addition, publicity may also come from ${ \mathrm { T V } } ,$ magazine, as well as articles in the offline local newspapers [52]. Future research might assess other proxies of publicity, in addition to online news, to confirm our findings. Lastly, our dependent variable only reflects the aggregate decision to view free fitness videos from the consumer group. It is unclear whether consumers diligently follow those videos. It would be interesting to explore how UGC and MGC affect consumers’ actual workout in the future.
