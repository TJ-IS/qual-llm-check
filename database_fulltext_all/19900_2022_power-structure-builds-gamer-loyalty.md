Keywords: Online game Loyalty Relational cohesion Survey Structural equation modeling Team participation Norm compliance Flow

## A B S T R A C T

Online games offer substantial business opportunities, fueling intense competition among game makers. To build gamer loyalty, a critical challenge for game makers is preventing gamers’ misbehaviors that annoy other gamers. Such challenges should be considered when game makers are making their design decisions and resource allo cation decisions. We contend that building in-game relational cohesion should be an effective means of reducing such behaviors and strengthening loyalty. We also extend relational cohesion theory (RCT) by challenging the assumption that only equal power helps form relational cohesion in one-on-one contexts. In contrast, unequal power may provide an alternative means of enhancing relational cohesion in large groups of users (e.g., the entire user population). This study is the first to introduce and explore how unequal power—a novel and critical element of power structure—forms relational cohesion and strengthens online gamer loyalty. This study uses both behavioral-continuous and perceptual-anchored scales to collect responses from 1003 online gamers Theoretically, this study widens the applicability of RCT from one-on-one contexts to one-to-many contexts. Practically, this study can help game makers make design and resource allocation decisions to use unequal power, thus effectively building gamer loyalty.

## 1. Introduction

Online games are globally popular IT applications, which attract more than a billion users and garner more than US\$18 billion in revenue [27]. Such revenues attract strong competitions, e.g., more than ten thousand new games are released each year on Steam (a popular gaming platform) [28]. However, the building process of gamer loyalty would not go smoothly as gameplay is typically anonymous, which may trigger disinhibition [30]. Online disinhibition would motivate gamers to pur sue maximum enjoyment without due consideration for other gamers, i. e., in-game misbehavior. Misbehavior can include deception [38] or deviant behavior [3], thus annoying and/or disturbing other gamers and causing them to leave the game. Losing gamers could hamper game makers’ survival.

Past research has offered some remedies for gamers’ misbehavior, e. g., a guardian mechanism [2] or enhancing users’ self-control [36]. Although these remedies for correcting misbehaviors are effective, we contend that preventing misbehavior is another useful strategy. One approach to preventing misbehavior is by building relational cohesion among gamers (defined as a close and solid relationship with other gamers, [12]) because most gamers appreciate close and solid relations [10,18]. Building relational cohesion focuses on bilateral benefits [16]; this can trigger positive behavioral change [17], which may prevent gamers from engaging in misbehavior and further build gamers’ loyalty. Hence, we need to know the answer to the question: how can relational cohesion be formed among gamers to build their loyalty?

To answer this research question, we chose to revisit relational cohesion theory (RCT). as relational cohesion is the key element of this theory, justifying the suitability of RCT in our study. RCT posits that power structure is the origin of relational cohesion [17], while asserting that equal power (how balanced impacts of individuals on each other’s welfare) and total power (how individuals can impact each other’s welfare) help form relational cohesion in one-on-one contexts [44], thus motivating commitment behaviors [17]. In our context, commitment behaviors are closely connected to gamer loyalty [21]. All the above collectively connect power structure to relational cohesion and, thereby, gamer loyalty.

In an online game context, gamers often interact with a large number of gamers, not just in one-on-one situations. In this case, the gamer population has dominating power over any single gamer, which can result in unbalanced impacts on the single gamer (i.e., unequal power). This one-to-many relationship was not considered in RCT, so we offer an alternative theorization that unequal power can also lead to relational cohesion. The reason is that perceiving the dominating power (i.e., unequal power) in gameplay would boost gamers’ compliance inclina tion, because any single gamer cannot contend with such power (i.e., the power of the larger gamer population). Thus, the best countermeasure any single gamer can take in the face of such power is to form close and cooperative relations with other gamers, or relational cohesion. Accordingly, relational cohesion should also include relations between a single gamer and the larger gamer population.

Hence, RCT is contextualized and alternatively theorized in our study to identify context-specific sources of relational cohesion, and connect such sources to individual gamers’ relational cohesion and loyalty. During gameplay, flow is a major predictor of attitudinal loyalty [13,23]. Moreover, flow markedly grabs gamers’ attention and thus may divert gamers’ attention on other gamers and the power structure un derlying the between-gamer interactions. Hence, flow and its impact are also included in our model. We test the model using 1003 responses from online gamers using both perceptual-anchored and behavioralcontinuous measures. By conducting a number of robustness tests, we are more confident about the implications for practice and theory. We believe our study makes three contributions. First, our study informs future research using RCT in a one-to-many context should use unequal power as a key element. Second, our study extends the applicability of RCT, from the relationship between two or three individuals, to the relationship between a single individual and the wider community, thus amplifying the theoretical impact of RCT. Third, our study shows that relational cohesion requires attention to form, but gamers may focus their attention on playing games. In this sense, flow may hinder the formation of relational cohesion, suggesting that games can foster either within-gamer flow experience or between-gamer relational cohesion to strengthen gamer loyalty, while unlikely both.

## 2. Theory and prior research

## 2.1. Relational cohesion theory: power structure as source of relational cohesion

Various theories have been used to explain gamers’ behaviors. For example, psychological ownership theory and social identity theory explain the formation of gamer loyalty [21]. Dual system theory is used to explain social game addiction [10]. Self-determination theory is used to explain how various human motivations (including social motivation) could explain psychological ownership and thereby game addiction [32]. RCT is used to explain how relational cohesion could help form social networks and strengthen gamer loyalty [17,18]. All these theories are useful in explaining social influences in online games. Our focus is on explaining the formation of close and solid relationships with other gamers, i.e., relational cohesion among gamers, so RCT is a good fit for our research question: i.e., how relational cohesion can be formed among gamers to build their loyalty. Accordingly, we chose RCT as the underlying theory for our study.

RCT has been used in various contexts, e.g., it can explain how un certainty reduction and positive affect form relational cohesion in the context of repeated exchange [44], how personality traits form rela tional cohesion and subsequently online relationships [12], and how expectancy for relational growth fuels relational cohesion, thereby building strong online relationships [18]. Its wide application in online contexts supports the use of RCT in our online study context.

RCT posits that power structure (i.e., total power and equal power) motivates frequent exchanges and agreements, which further fuels positive emotions and relational cohesion, thus driving relationship commitment [17]. This supports the contention that power structure is a key driver of relational cohesion, motivating us to focus on power structure. Moreover, the literature has verified the long pathway from power structure to frequent exchange, positive emotions, and therefore relational cohesion [12,16]. Because replication of the entire pathway would unlikely substantially extend RCT. Hence, we chose to simplify the pathway directly from power structure to relational cohesion.

We then map the three key RCT elements into our research context: (1) power structure (including total power and equal power), (2) rela tional cohesion, and (3) commitment behavior. In the following, we explain the essence of each key element and connect each key element to the online game context, thus mapping RCT into our context.

First, power structure consists of total power and equal power [44]. Total power refers to the degree to which individuals can influence the welfare of each other. Individual persons in online games are gamers. Welfare in online games is the gaming outcome, which offers gratifica tion to gamers [13]. Accordingly, total power in online games is the degree to which gamers can influence the gaming outcomes of each other, which is most likely to occur when gamers participate in gaming teams. Gamers in the same gaming team win or lose team competitions together, and shared governance influences gaming team performance [40]. In short, gamers’ participation in gaming teams could determine each other’s gaming outcomes, reflecting the essence of total power. Therefore, we map total power as overall gaming team participation (defined as engagement in team gameplay) [19].

RCT has been developed and used in one-on-one situations [44]. We argue that the theory has not examined one-to-many situations, i.e., when a single gamer encounters many other gamers in the same game. Therefore, we modify RCT by proposing a new construct, unequal power, to replace equal power, reflecting the power differences between the one gamer and the many other gamers.

In contrast to equal power, the concept of unequal power reflects the dominant power of the large population of others over an individual person’s welfare. Contextualized into the online game context, the larger gamer population collectively has overwhelming power in influ encing the gaming outcomes of any single gamer by various means, e.g., speeding or hindering the process of “leveling up” (upgrading the ava tar’s in-game level), sharing or hoarding the advantageous in-game lo cations, and agreeing or refusing to form teams to obtain gaming achievements that are reserved only for teams. All these show the power of other gamers that could induce an individual to change their behavior to meet other gamers’ expectations and win their support, i.e., gaming norm compliance (defined as behavioral change to meet other gamers expectations) [31].

Second, relational cohesion is a key element of RCT [17]. Relational cohesion has exhibited a strong influence in online environments, including online games [12,18], indicating that RCT should fit the on line game context. Relational cohesion is close and solid relationships with other individuals. Important others in online games should be other gamers. Therefore, relational cohesion can be contextualized as close and solid relationships with other gamers, i.e., we map relational cohe sion into online games as in-game relational cohesion. We also use it as the core of our research model.

Third, commitment behaviors in RCT include gift-giving, staying, and contributing [17]. These behaviors reflect that commitment behaviors have essences of retention and contribution. Retention in an online game is to repeatedly play it, while contribution to an online game could be recommending the game to other potential gamers. Repeated gameplay and recommendation intention are central elements of loyalty [25]. That is, commitment behaviors could be contextualized in online games as gamer loyalty. Loyalty can be assessed in either the conative phase or the action phase [25], motivating us to further conceptualize commitment into two concepts: gamer conative loyalty and gamer action loyalty.

Online games offer an environment in which gamers concentrate their attention on mobilizing their gaming skills to complete gaming tasks, thus creating an in-game flow experience (defined as a gameplay experience of total concentration) [7,11]. Flow is an important aspect of online gaming, a factor that often motivates gamers to play [13,23]. RCT does not include flow, which was not considered an important aspect of the context in which RCT was developed. However, flow is an important part of the game context, justifying its inclusion in our model.

Table 1 lists the theoretical elements of RCT, the contextualized concepts, and their concept definitions. The contextualized concepts and definitions exhibit validity in four aspects: (1) the concepts have been used in the same context, showing the validity of using these concepts in the study context; (2) the definitions originate from the previous studies in the same context (i.e., online game context), indicating the validity of the concept definition; (3) the concepts were operationalized by using the items of previous studies, supporting the validity of measurement operationalization; and (4) the concept operationalization was assessed using loadings/cross-loadings, convergent validity, discriminant val idity, and common method bias, advocating its statistical validity.

After mapping the RCT concepts to the online game context, we further noted that the RCT has an implicit assumption, i.e., the in dividuals pay close attention to the power structure underlying their interpersonal relationships. Our study is unique in challenging this im plicit assumption. Online games offer an environment in which gamers concentrate their attention on mobilizing their gaming skills to complete gaming tasks, thus creating an in-game flow experience [7,11]. As ingame flow may divert gamer’s attention to power structure, thus sug gesting that flow may moderate or weaken the impact of the power structure, and justifying the inclusion of the moderator role of flow in our model.

## 2.1.1. Relevance of RCT in the context

Online games commonly include group play [10], identification with gaming guilds [21], and relational cohesion among gamers [18]. These features indicate the importance of close and solid relationships with other gamers as an important factor influencing gamer loyalty. Thus, these contextual features motivate us to use RCT as the foundation of our research model. RCT posits that total power and equal power are key drivers of relational cohesion, fostering individuals’ commitment be haviors [17].

We mapped total power into online games as the concept of overall gaming team participation because intensive team participation makes a gamer aware of how much other team members (i.e., the total power of many other team members) can impact their own individual well-being. Hence, according to RCT, overall gaming team participation should lead to in-game relational cohesion (H1a). We argue that RCT should include unequal power, because unequal power between a single gamer and the large gamer population would motivate gamers to seek to comply with other gamers’ expectations, i.e., gaming norm compliance. Hence, gaming norm compliance could influence in-game relational cohesion (H1b).

## 2.2. Power structure and its impact on relational cohesion

“Power” in RCT has been used interchangeably with “dependence” [16]. Both equal power and total power can independently increase exchange (agreement) frequency [17]. This implies that high total but unequal power may be another force to motivate exchanges or reach agreements. However, RCT literature has not yet examined this. Our study addresses this by contextualizing total power and unequal power in the online gaming context.

Power structure in our study includes overall gaming team partici pation and gaming norm compliance. Overall gaming team participation can encourage gamers’ compliance with team norms, which satisfy gamers’ social needs [31], identify gamers with their gaming teams [19], and formulate relational cohesion [18], justifying the link from team participation to relational cohesion.

RCT has clearly shown that total power induces individuals to pursue relational cohesion [17]. We contextualized total power in games as overall gaming team participation. According to RCT, this participation should help form in-game relational cohesion, i.e., a positive link as in H1a. Moreover, the link in H1a can also be inferred through alternative reasoning. Specifically, social motivations are important for encour aging online gameplay [32] because gameplay can satisfy gamers’ social needs [31]. In an online gaming context, team participation occurs through gamers forming or joining teams to complete challenging gaming tasks, e.g., killing a giant monster. This shows how same-team gamers may provide benefits that influence an individual’s willingness to do more to ensure such benefits. To achieve these benefits, gamers would continue to participate, which is critical to achieving a shared goal [37]. To ensure successful teamwork, gamers are required to communicate their strategies and real-time moves, i.e., frequently interact with team members and thereby satisfy their social needs. Collectively, the gaming teams represent a significant proportion of the gamer population. Therefore, overall gaming team participation would strengthen gamers’ emotional attachment to the large gamer popula tion, inducing the sense of a close and solid relationship with other gamers, defined as in-game relational cohesion [18]. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1a. Overall gaming team participation is positively related to ingame relational cohesion.

Norm compliance is an important factor influencing user behavior. Subjective norms are beliefs about what important others think one should do [6] and often impact one’s behavioral intention [39]. Social norms have strong social implications, and therefore, may influence relational cohesion, as in our study.

Norm compliance is contextualized as gaming norm compliance in our study. Norm compliance reflects social influences. Hence, it may be viewed as potentially overlapping with perceived interaction and social identity as the key constructs of socialization-enhancing strategy [21]. These concepts may be related, and therefore, we need to clarify their difference/distinction. Gaming norm compliance has a core element of changing one’s behavior to fit other gamers’ expectations [31]. This element is not included in perceived interaction (satisfaction on in-game communication with other gamers) and social identity (self-categori. zation, affective commitment, and membership-based self-esteem) [21,22], thus supporting the distinction between these concepts.

We also argue that in-game relational cohesion is distinct from both perceived interaction and social identity. In-game relational cohesion refers to a close and solid relationship with other gamers. Such a rela tionship is not part of the definitions of perceived intention and social identity, further justifying the distinction between in-game relational cohesion and the two concepts.

RCT indicates that power structure is critical for triggering a process leading to relational cohesion [17], showing the importance of power structure, including equal power [17]. Our study extends RCT by replacing equal power (as in one-on-one relations) with unequal power (as in one-to-many relations). We further contextualize unequal power in the online gaming context as gaming norm compliance. Norm compli ance enhances behavioral intention [39], indicating that norm compli ance is a behavioral driver. In online games, norm compliance strongly determines the desire to interact with friends [10]. The reason may be that complying with gaming norms makes gaming behaviors highly predictable, thus reducing uncertainty. Reducing uncertainty is a key pathway to relational cohesion [16]. That is, gaming norm compliance should be positively linked to in-game relational cohesion. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1b. Gaming norm compliance is positively related to in-game rela tional cohesion

The power structure in RCT comprises total power and equal power, which are regarded as independent ([17], p. 92). However, our study extends RCT by replacing equal power with unequal power. Moreover, our study innovates by proposing a link from total power to unequal power. The reason may be that the total power of many other gamers is considerable when compared to the power of any single gamer. The large total power implies the virtual insignificance of an individual gamer’s power, thus portraying extremely unequal power. This supports our link between total power and unequal power.

Applying the two concepts (total power and unequal power) in on line games, total power is contextualized as overall gaming team participation, while unequal power is contextualized as gaming norm compliance. In games, overall gaming team participation induces gamers to comply with team norms [31]; gamers joining teams are likely to find that any single gamer has significantly less power than any team. This could be evidenced by the link between knowing other members skills and team performance [40]. By extension, gaming teams have less power than the large gamer population. Therefore, any single gamer would have very little power when compared to the large gamer pop ulation, providing a motivation to meet other gamers’ expectations, i.e., comply with gaming norms [31]. This builds the link as predicted by H2. This link is new in using gaming norm compliance (norms in the larger gamer population), but not team norm compliance (norms in a single gaming team) as in past studies [31]. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2. Overall gaming team participation is positively related to gaming norm compliance.

## 2.3. Relational cohesion impacts gamer loyalty

RCT posits that relational cohesion triggers commitment behaviors, including gift-giving, staying, contributing [17], or investing in the relationship [16]. Such commitment behaviors in online games could include overlapping friendship networks or highly interdependent re lationships [12]. Our study extends RCT in its consequences of relational cohesion by adding conative loyalty and action loyalty, benefiting game makers in addition to gamer-gamer relationships, and thus further contributing to RCT.

Loyalty comprises conative loyalty and action loyalty [25]. Conative loyalty is the intention to repeatedly purchase/visit [41], while action loyalty refers to actual repurchase/revisit actions [25], showing their conceptual difference. Hence, we included both the attitudinal phase and the action phase of gamer loyalty (i.e., conative loyalty and action loyalty) in our research.

Loyalty reflects how users commit to repurchasing their preferred product/service in the future [5,25], sustaining game makers’ revenues and thus demonstrating the importance of strengthening users’ loyalty. Previous studies found that the antecedents of user loyalty include identification [42] and satisfaction [5], i.e., users’ positive emotions that strengthen their loyalty. However, these studies omit an important underlying mechanism, that is, positive emotions could first formulate perceived cohesion, which then motivates commitment behavior [17]. Therefore, we adopted relational cohesion as our core concept and explored how relational cohesion motivates users’ commitment-related behavior, i.e., gamers’ conative and action loyalty.

Relational cohesion helps users build a close and solid relationship with others [12]. According to RCT, relational cohesion can motivate commitment behaviors [17]. Moreover, relational cohesion has been known to exert positive impacts on online relationships [12] and therefore create social benefits, which are key to online user loyalty [42]. We argue that this loyalty could refer to both phases (i.e., conative loyalty and action loyalty) because a positive attitude is a strong pre dictor of both behavioral intention and actual action. Hence, we hypothesize:

H3a. In-game relational cohesion is positively related to gamer cona tive loyalty.

H3b. In-game relational cohesion is positively related to gamer action loyalty.

Conative loyalty can capture users’ loyalty intention and can be converted into action [25], showing the potential link between conative loyalty and action loyalty. Indeed, behavioral intention drives actual action [5]. Moreover, gamer conative loyalty demonstrates how gamers tend to repeatedly play [25]. Such repeated play should increase the frequency of gamers’ interactions with others. That is, gamers’ intention to repeatedly play (i.e., gamer conative loyalty) would enhance their actual engagement (i.e., gamer action loyalty). Hence, we hypothesize:

H4. Gamer conative loyalty is positively related to gamer action loyalty.

## 2.3.1. Using flow to theoretically extend RCT

Flow refers to an experience of total concentration with intrinsic enjoyment [7,11]. Flow differs from enjoyment in that it includes an element of concentration [7]. Flow has also been defined using attention focus, intrinsic interest, control, and curiosity [34,45]. Flow effectively boosts behavioral intention to use a system [11]. Flow differs from peak performance, because high performance is not a necessary element of flow. Flow also differs from immersion, which describes a feeling prompted by a media-created environment (i.e., gamer perception of “being there” in the online game, [11]), but does not necessarily require any concentration. All these show the conceptual distinction of flow.

In-game flow is a contextual feature of online games. Game content includes challenging tasks that require gamers to exhibit sufficient gaming skills, thus creating flow experiences and engagement [11], and subsequently gamer loyalty [13,23]. This shows that in-game flow is a significant contextual feature of online games. Past studies overlooked the impact of flow on gamers’ action loyalty, showing a knowledge gap.

To fill this gap, we reexamined the concept of flow, which reflects concentration on an activity [7,11]. This greatly motivates online users continual engagement. Accordingly, we propose that flow could induce actual actions, i.e., gamers’ action loyalty. In so doing, we contribute to RCT by indicating that flow can be a parallel predictor of commitment behaviors and therefore should be considered by future studies using RCT.

Flow is a highly enjoyable experience [45], which strongly motivates an individual to seek engagement in actions that trigger it [34]. This is certainly applicable to online games. Specifically, in-game flow fosters gamer loyalty [13,23], showing the relationship between flow and continuance intention, a key component of conative loyalty [25]. Hence, we hypothesize:

H5a. In-game flow is positively related to gamer conative loyalty.

Flow requires an individual’s focused attention [34] and thereby triggers a phenomenon called time distortion [1]. Specifically, flow makes one lose track of time and underestimate the time duration of an activity [1]. This encourages gamers to spend more time than planned in gameplay, boosting their actual game usage, which represents action loyalty [25]. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H5b. In-game flow is positively related to gamer action loyalty.

Game content includes challenging tasks that require gamers to exhibit sufficient gaming skills, thus creating flow experiences, and subsequently gamer loyalty [20]. This shows that in-game flow is a significant contextual feature of online games. However, previous studies focused on the direct impacts of flow, while overlooking its po tential moderator role, showing a knowledge gap.

To fill this gap, we reexamined the concept of flow, which reflects concentration on an activity [11,20]. This grabs an individual’s atten tion to focus on a single activity, meanwhile affects attention for other activities. We propose this new role of flow and argue that flow affects gamers’ attention to the power structure underlying the gamer popu lation. In so doing, we contribute to the RCT by indicating that flow can be a new moderator, which weakens the impact of the power structure on relational cohesion.

Power structure in online games should comprise two elements: overall gaming team participation and gaming norm compliance. The former informs gamers that other gamers (i.e., team members) can have an overwhelmingly large influence on their own gaming outcomes. This explains that overall gaming team participation can satisfy gamers’ so cial needs [31] and enhance relational cohesion [19]. To exert these impacts, gamers need to pay attention to other gamers. In this sense, flow concentrates gamers’ attention on gameplay, thus distracting them from noticing other gamers, and thus should negate the impact of overall gaming team participation, including the impact on in-game relational cohesion. Hence, we hypothesize:

H5c. In-game flow weakens the positive link between overall gaming team participation and in-game relational cohesion.

Power structure in online games should also include gaming norm compliance, which theoretically shows the element of unequal power. Gamers know that the entire gamer population has an overwhelming power over their own gaming outcomes, i.e., the relative power is un equal. This enforces gamers to change their behavior to meet others expectations, i.e., gaming norm compliance [31]. Changing behaviors to comply with others’ expectations can reduce uncertainty, a key pathway to relational cohesion and commitment, as posited by the RCT [16]. This uncertainty reduction pathway has an underlying premise, i.e., reaching agreement with other users [44], which in the game context requires gamers’ conscious attention to other gamers’ needs. However, flow re quires gamers’ total concentration [20], and therefore distracts them from reaching agreements with others, a necessary element of forming relational cohesion, as posited by the RCT [17]. Hence, we hypothesize:

H5d. In-game flow weakens the positive link between gaming norm compliance and in-game relational cohesion.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Sample and data collection process

We collected 1003 valid responses from online gamers, forming a large-scale study. Online surveys are valid data collection methods [29]

Hence, we posted invitations on two gaming-related forums (i.e., www. ptt.cc and www.gamer.com.tw) and social networking webpages (i.e., Facebook and Dcard). Posting invitations on various sites would reduce the bias owing to a single source of participants. Interested participants were directed to an online survey, which collected our study responses.

Fifty participants were drawn from all those offering complete re sponses, and each sent a gift certificate worth NT\$200 (approximately US\$6.60). Offering an incentive to increase the response rate is acceptable in the literature [9,42] and is also a common practice for local online surveys. Moreover, this is a modest reward, which may not induce substantial self-selection bias [15]. Our study requested that each respondent nominate a game they had recently played. The game was then used as the target to assess our study items. Overall, our partici pants most frequently nominated the following games: (1) League of Legends (n = 233)(2) MapleStory (n = 86)(3) Black Desert $( n = 6 6 ) , ( 4 )$ Hearthstone (n = 54),(5) Path of Exile $( n = 4 6 ) , ( 6 )$ ) World of Warcraft (n = 45),(7) The Magic Blade (n = 38), (8) Overwatch (n = 34), and 135 other games, with each nominated by fewer than 30 participants.

Our participants offered their responses between November and December 2018. We obtained 1107 intact responses. We set seven criteria for filtering out invalid responses, including (1) no recent gameplay (n = 3, 2) inability to nominate an existing game (n = 8, 3) gameplay indicated at more than 168 h a week (a week comprises only 168 h) (n = 1, 4) gameplay indicated for a number of years greater than the participant’s age (n = 1, 5) indicating an age greater than 80 years (n = 1, 6) failure to correctly answer the attention check (n = 81, 7) providing duplicate emails (n = 9). Among these criteria, the first two are to exclude participants who were not in our target group (i.e., gamers). The next four are to exclude participants who did not carefully respond to the study items. The last criterion is to exclude the partici pants submitting multiple responses. Applying these criteria generated 104 invalid responses. We discarded these responses, leaving 1003 valid responses or 90.6% (=1003/1107 = 90.6%) of respondents. Although online forms are known for their difficulty in testing nonresponse bias, we used the same testing method as in the literature [21] to examine non-response bias. Specifically, we compared the early (first 25% of) responses with the late (last 25% of) responses in our study concepts. The reason is that the late responses may resemble nonresponses, and therefore nonresponses can be extrapolated to the late respondents. Therefore, comparing early responses and late responses may reflect nonresponse bias. We did not find a significant difference $\left( p \geq . 0 5 \right)$ , supporting the lack of nonresponse bias.

## 4.2. Hypothesis testing

We used LISREL software to implement structural equation modeling (SEM) to test the study hypotheses. Fig. 2 illustrates the analytical results.

All hypotheses were supported. Overall gaming team participation and gaming norm compliance were positively related to in-game rela tional cohesion (β = 0.45 and 0.41, p < .05), supporting H1a and H1b. Overall gaming team participation was positively related to gaming norm compliance (β = 0.39, p < .05), supporting H2. In-game relational cohesion was positively related to gamer conative loyalty and gamer action loyalty (β = 0.46 and 0.07, p < .05), supporting H3a and H3b. As expected, gamer conative loyalty was positively related to gamer action loyalty (β = 0.18, p < .05), supporting H4. In-game flow was positively related to gamer conative loyalty and gamer action loyalty (β = 0.18 and 0.09, p < .05), supporting H5a and H5b. In-game flow moderated the relation between power structure and relational cohesion (interaction coefficient = − 0.05 and − 0.05, p < .05), supporting H5c and H5d.

The structural model performed acceptably, i.e., CFI = 0.93, NNFI = 0.92, IFI = 0.93, and SRMR = 0.068. Our model explains 15% of the variance in gaming norm compliance, 52% of the variance in in-game relational cohesion, 27% of the variance in gamer conative loyalty, and 10% of the variance in gamer action loyalty. These proportions are medium to large effect sizes [4], demonstrating the practical relevance of this study.

## 4.3. Robustness tests

## 4.3.1. Potential impact of flow on in-game relational cohesion

It is possible that flow may increase in-game relational cohesion. Accordingly, we tested this and found that the newly added impact of flow on in-game relational cohesion was not significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 4 , t =$ $1 . 5 1 , p > . 0 5 )$ . Similarly, the model fit did not significantly change, i.e., $\Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 . 6 1 < \chi ^ { 2 } ( \alpha = 0 . 0 5 , \mathrm { d f } = 1 ) = 3 . 8 4 .$ This indicates that flow does not significantly affect in-game relational cohesion.

## 4.3.2. Using League of Legends as a control variable

Using the most commonly nominated game (League of Legends) as a control variable, rather than using the most commonly nominated game genre: MMORPG, may better explain gamer loyalty. We conducted this replacement and analyzed the data again. We found that the model fit worsened, i.e., $\Delta \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 3 1 . 8 1 ( p < . 0 5 ) .$ , while all the hypothesis testing results remained the same. Moreover, the explained variance in gamer loyalty remained the same.

## 4.3.3. Mediation tests using bootstrapping method

Consistent with the literature [9], we conducted the bootstrapping method to examine the three possible mediations. We obtained evidence showing that all the mediations are significant, i.e., the confidence in tervals of mediating effects were $\geq 0 . 0 9 , p < . 0 5$ . Our study does not focus on mediations, so we did not develop hypotheses about mediations.

## 4.3.4. Impacts of control variables

In our study, female gamers exhibited lower action loyalty than male gamers, and age was positively related to action loyalty. These findings require that future studies adopt additional theoretical perspectives to examine them further.

## 4.3.5. Sensitivity test

We conducted a sensitivity analysis by removing the control vari ables. After this removal, all testing results remained the same, i.e., significant paths remained significant. Moreover, the changes in path coefficients were negligible, i.e., ≤0.01, supporting the robustness of the study findings.

4.3.6. Comparison of the impact of in-game flow and in-game relational cohesion on conative loyalty and action loyalty

To offer additional insight, we compared the impact of in-game flow and the impact of in-game relational cohesion on both conative and action loyalty. We found that in-game relational cohesion has a larger impact on conative loyalty than in-game flow $( 0 . 4 6 > 0 . 1 8 , p < . 0 5 ) .$ However, the impact of in-game relational cohesion on action loyalty does not significantly differ from the impact of in-game flow on action loyalty (p > .05).

## 5. Discussion

Our study examines the problem of maintaining gamers’ loyalty amid interpersonal conflict between gamers. Overall, our findings show two important sources of relational cohesion (i.e., overall gaming team participation and gaming norm compliance), which are expected to prevent misbehavior and interpersonal conflict, and thus strengthen online gamer loyalty. Our findings should be useful for game makers to make better game design decisions. Our study also revised our under standing of RCT, i.e., RCT posited that equal power was necessary to form relational cohesion [16]. However, our study showed that unequal power in the online gaming context could also form relational cohesion. By changing the understanding of RCT, we contextualized the theory’s el ements in the online gaming context, such as overall gaming team participation and gaming norm compliance. These offer novel means for strengthening online gamer loyalty.

By doing so, we also theorize on and verify a new effect of flow, i.e., how in-game flow can weaken the links between power structure (i.e., overall gaming team participation and gaming norm compliance) and relational cohesion. The reason may be that in-game flow implies gamers’ concentration on game content and ignorance of others’ exis tence. This adds our understanding, as compared to the typical depiction of flow as always positive.

## 5.1. Implications for theory

RCT has theorized power structure by using equal power and total power [17]. This is reasonable in one-on-one or dyadic relations [44]. However, relations may be between a single user and a large user pop ulation, which is unlikely to exhibit equal power. Without considering unequal power, RCT has limited explanatory power to account for re lations in large groups of users, e.g., the large gamer population. To enable RCT to explain such relations, we extend it by theorizing the dominant power of the larger gamer population in a game over any single gamer. This dominant power shows unequal power, which con trasts with equal power in RCT. Thus, we anticipate that future studies will include this theoretical element, unequal power, when using RCT to examine one-to-many relations. We believe that our extension of RCT can be used in other online communities and IT user groups, in which users are interdependent on one another, thus amplifying the potential usefulness of RCT for the IS discipline. For example, knowledge com munity users interact with other users in a large population of com munity members in situations where users’ compliance with community norms and relational cohesion may be important. Our study shows that there are two important sources of relational cohesion that may also apply in these other online contexts.

Online games have become popular, creating a profitable but competitive market and attracting the attention of gaming firms and researchers. Playing online games is well known for being a doubleedged sword; they may satisfy users’ wants and needs (e.g., achieve ment, social, and escapism, [32]) but may also cause game addiction [32]. The strong competition and potential negative effects could impede gaming firms from building a loyal customer base. To increase customer loyalty, previous studies have suggested enhancing decision support satisfaction [41], social identity [21,42], psychological owner ship [21], and flow [23]. These studies focus on the attitudinal phase of loyalty, but omit another important phase of loyalty, i.e., the action phase of loyalty. Such omission would hinder the aforementioned means from exerting their maximum impacts on the development of building a loyal customer base. To address this insufficiency, we explore the po tential sources of both gamer conative and action loyalty, thus contributing to the research stream on user loyalty.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research

Our study is a single study, which has a number of limitations. We were limited from including all unique aspects of each game. To main tain a clear focus, we chose only included game genre as a control variable. However, future research could entail a large-scale study, enabling the inclusion of all unique aspects of each game.

Gamers may differ in their role and influence, also creating less equal power relations. However, such differences may rarely result in the same dominant power as the large gamer population as a whole has on any single gamer. To maintain a clear focus, our study made the distinction between the two situations (i.e., one-on-one and one-to-many) accord ing to whether there is an apparently dominant power. Future studies could further explore whether our power inequality concept and its in fluence also apply to one-on-one situations.

We chose to take RCT perspective, as it is highly relevant. However, other theories may also explain phenomena in this context. Future studies should choose their underlying theory and explain more phe nomena in this context.

We used an online survey, which is known to be limited in testing nonresponse bias. Future studies could collect behavioral data to offer more evidence supporting our model.

Flow in our study may differ in its impact in virtual worlds that are built using virtual reality technologies. Future studies could replicate our study in virtual worlds or environments using virtual reality tech nologies to extend the understanding of the impacts of flow.

Our participants had a gender composition consistent with the sampling frame, i.e., the majority were male [8]. Thus, this context limited us from using a balanced sample. Future scholars may consider focusing on the distinctive preferences of female gamers.

To reduce the bias of collecting data from participants of a single source, our study posted invitations on various online sites frequented by our target population, which is a common practice (e.g., [31]). Defining a narrower target population and posting on a single site focused on that population (e.g., an Internet forum targeted at players of only one game, residents of one city, or gamers of a certain age group) could possibly enhance data validity (see [43]). Future studies could address this limitation by focusing on a narrow gamer population and posting on a single site targeting that population.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

Our study asked each participant to nominate a game to assess, accumulating a wide spectrum of games. Hence, our findings should be generalizable to most online games and would help game makers to make more effective decisions. Our findings may also be applicable to other online environments (e.g., online communities or social media) where norm compliance and relational cohesion play important roles. We also believe that our equal/unequal power structure could be applied to other online environments (e.g., online communities or social media), because users there also exert influences (i.e., have equal power) on each other or could form user groups/communities to exert dominant influence (i.e., unequal power) on any single user.

We found that overall gaming team participation is a key driver of relational cohesion and thereby gamer loyalty. Hence, game makers could include team elements into their game design decisions, e.g., designing gaming activities that require gamers to form teams. More over, incentives are known for boosting online users' engagement [33,42]. Accordingly, game makers could design additional incentives in gameplay, if gamers play in teams, e.g., increased threshold value of gaming experience points for avatar to “level-up.” Such requirements and/or incentives would accumulate gamers’ overall experience of team gameplay, and accordingly help build a loyal gamer base.

We found that gaming norm compliance helps form in-game rela tional cohesion. Hence, game makers should consider making a decision to emphasize on gaming norms. For example, game makers could explicate the gaming norms, publish them on their official websites, and post them on in-game boards or in guilds. This could inform and frequently remind gamers to comply with gaming norms, with the aim of enhancing norm compliance. Such compliance may help gamers form relational cohesion with other gamers.

We found that flow enhances gamers’ conative and action loyalty. Flow in our study offsets the impact of power structure on relational cohesion. This poses an alternative strategy for game makers to retain gamers who enjoy playing solo by creating a flow experience for them. Hence, game makers could regularly update their gaming content with the goal of increasing flow. For example, they could create new avatars or design new gaming quests. This could trigger gamers’ curiosity, which is an important element of flow [34]. Moreover, game makers could consider making their resource allocation decisions to design deterministic (rather than probabilistic) gaming mechanisms, e.g., fixing the in-game parameters of the physical/magic attack power, defense power, and effects of in-game crafts (weapons, armor, equipment, or decorations). Such deterministic gaming mechanisms would enable gamers to experience a sense of control in their gameplay. A feeling of control should fuel the flow experience [34], and accordingly, boost gamer loyalty.

## 6. Conclusions

This study contextualized RCT into the online gaming context, a popular digital context, to explore how to strengthen online gamer loyalty. Before our study, equal power was viewed as a key element in

RCT for explaining one-on-one relationships between gamers. This element was deemed unlikely to be influential in one-to-many contexts and may not guide game makers to make good game design and resource allocation decisions. Accordingly, we augmented RCT by using a new element—unequal power—to replace equal power, as in our one-to-many conceptualization in fueling relational cohesion. Our study contributes to future studies on RCT that they should include and contextualize unequal power in one-to-many contexts. We further verified that in-game flow may hinder the formation of relational cohesion, urging game makers to reconsider their design decision to posit their games either to offer intra-gamer flow or to form inter-gamer relational cohesion, while not both.
