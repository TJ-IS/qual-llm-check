## A B S T R A C T

In this article, we describe the decision support system that was developed for the assignment of courses to teaching modalities and rooms for the Fall semester of 2020 at the University of Connecticut (UConn). With the adoption of safety/mitigation standards imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic, the seating capacities of rooms were reduced by more than 70%, thus making virtually every existing room assignment for Fall 2020 infeasible. The demand for in-person instruction required the reassignment of a large number of courses to rooms, where not all requests for physical space could be accommodated. In order to maximize opportunities for in-person in struction, UConn introduced a teaching modality in which class meetings are attended on campus by only 50% of the enrolled students. As decision makers were given partial flexibility to assign teaching modalities to classes, the complexity of the assignment problem increased considerably, especially because the real-world instances involved hundreds of rooms and thousands of classes and required a quick solution turnaround in practice. In this article, we introduce this flexible assignment problem and describe the two mixed-integer programming for mulations that were used to solve the real-world instances of the problem; in particular, one of the formulations leverages structural properties presented in this work in order to represent the problem in a more compact way. We explain how we tailored our algorithms to solve the real-world problem, describe the dynamics of the interactive decision support system created in this initiative, and present insights derived from our study.

## 1. Introduction

In 2020, almost every aspect of everyday life was disrupted by the coronavirus (COVID-19), and strict physical-distancing rules to mitigate its propagation introduced a host of operational challenges. By October 2020, the University of Connecticut (UConn) was praised by White House coronavirus response coordinator Dr. Deborah Birx as having one of the highest in-person classroom participation rates.<sup>1</sup> This notable overall success of UConn resulted from unprecedented efforts by hun dreds of faculty and staff to plan for and maintain safety. While much of the credit belongs to our colleagues in public health for innovations in waste-water testing, for example, here we highlight the innovative use of optimization to make the best use of scarce classroom capacity, which had been drastically reduced to meet distancing protocols. Our work provided the opportunity for as many students and faculty to return to campus as possible, setting the stage for subsequent public health vic tories in keeping them healthy and safe once on campus. These effort represent one important early step that resulted in UConn’s recognition as a leading university in their COVID-19 response.<sup>2</sup> Despite the chal lenges introduced by social distancing, after all optimizations, subse quent scheduling adjustments, and student add/drops, approximately 34% of all UConn courses (19% of all seats) took place with some level of in-person instruction.

The number of cases and deaths in the State of Connecticut was relatively large at the beginning of the pandemic, so the local and state governments took several actions to reduce infection rates, such as the prohibition of large gatherings. As a consequence, UConn closed its six campuses in April. At that time, the timetabling activities and student registration for the Fall 2020 semester had already been mostly completed. However, physical-distancing constraints led to significant reductions in room capacity. Consequently, traditional in-person in struction for larger sections became physically impossible.

As many students and faculty communicated their desire to have inperson instruction, the university elaborated a safe reopening plan, which included creating new teaching modalities to maximize the number of student seats assigned to in-person classes. Particularly relevant for this work is a Split-Delivery modality, in which 50% of students attend a teaching session on-campus while the other 50% learn online, reversing on alternating course days. Instructors were given the opportunity to indicate their preferred teaching modality for each course, but due to space restrictions, requests for in-person instruction could be denied, and these classes could be relegated to online in struction instead. This aspect incorporated a new dimension to the problem, involving decisions about the teaching modalities of each course. Additionally, this led to a weighted assignment problem, where the weights are the number of seats assigned to physical rooms. In particular, the weight of a class assigned to Split-Delivery Learning is only 50% of the weight of the same class when assigned to in-person instruction, whereas online classes had a weight of zero. These modifi cations resulted in a challenging problem, mainly because teaching modalities had to be assigned at the planning level and virtually every room assignment made in the past was infeasible, so decision makers had no warm start solution. Additionally, the weight component trans formed a previous feasibility problem into an optimization problem, for which the manual identification of high-quality solutions was chal lenging and laborious given the dimensions of the problem. In partic ular, the assignment problem for Storrs, the flagship campus of UConn, involved almost 1,500 classes, 45,000 seats, and approximately 200 rooms.

With these changes, the timetabling decision support system (DSS) used by the university became unsatisfactory. Namely, significant modifications to the software were necessary, and as the vendor’s response times were expected to increase in the period, UConn initially decided to do all the timetabling activities manually for Fall 2020. The collaboration with the authors allowed the university not only to revisit this initial decision but also to incorporate some additional flexibility in the assignment of teaching modalities to courses, with direct impact in the assignment of rooms to classes.

In this article, we present the DSS that we developed to solve the assignment problem for UConn. Our solution consists of a two-stage framework, which maximizes student opportunities for in-person in struction in the first stage and minimizes assignment discrepancies in the second. We discuss how our DSS was conceived and adopted by UConn for the Fall semester of 2020 as an interactive system and presen several insights extracted from this work.

The manuscript is structured as follows. Section 2 we present a literature overview. The timetabling problem solved by UConn is dis cussed in detail in Section 3. We introduce the notation and the formal definition of the problem in Section 4. A greedy approach is described in Section 5, and our theoretical and algorithmic contributions are intro duced in Section 6. In Section 7 and Section 8 we present our results and insights, respectively. We conclude with directions for future work in Section 9.

## 2. Literature review

The timetabling problem has attracted considerable attention from the scientific community due to its numerous real-world applications [1–3]. Traditional timetabling problems address the assignment of courses to rooms and meeting times. In curriculum-based timetabling problems, the assignments should avoid conflicts between courses scheduled for the same term in the standard curriculum [4]. Conversely, post enrollment-based course timetabling problems assume that student enrollment is known, and the conflicts have been handled at the student level [5]; similar constraints are observed in the examination time tabling problem [6]. We consider a version of the problem where the assignment of classes to time periods is given as input; therefore, schedule conflicts had already been accounted for a priori, and our so lutions handle solely physical conflicts, i.e., courses meeting at the same time may not be assigned to the same room. Conversely, our models assign teaching modalities to classes, an aspect that has not been pre viously addressed in the literature. Therefore, this work contributes to the extant timetabling literature by incorporating a new assignment dimension.

Solution methods to the timetabling problem depend on the numbe of real-world constraints incorporated in the problem (see [6–8]). Metaheuristics are adopted when the problem does not admit efficient (e.g., linear) formulations, as exact solution approaches (e.g., based on mathematical programming) are unsatisfactory in these cases [9,10]. Nevertheless, mathematical programming techniques have also been proposed [11,12], with challenging scenarios being frequently handled by two-stage algorithms [13,14]; we also propose a two-stage algorithm based on mathematical programming formulations in this manuscript. Finally, our problem can be cast as a multiple-interval graph-coloring problem, in which each vertex is a class associated with meeting times and edges represent conflicts. Although hard in the general case, col oring problems can be efficiently solved in interval graphs [15].

The time frame for development and deployment of our solution was very limited (2 months), so we did not consider advanced aspects of software engineering (in contrast with [16], and more closely related to [17]). Instead, we focus on the functionalities of our DSS and on the results and insights extracted from this initiative. Particularly important are the mathematical programming formulations. Similarly to us, [18] and [19] present DSS for the timetabling problem with optimization models, but with different assignment dimensions (courses to time slots and to instructors, respectively). For an overview of the DSS literature on the timetabling problem, see [19].

Our work contributes to the literature of interactive decision support systems for the timetabling problem, which investigates solution tech niques produced by optimization algorithms and manually adjusted by decision-makers [20]. Interactive decision support systems play a key role in the adoption of optimization solutions [21], especially in settings where there is flexibility or ambiguity in terms of goals and constraints [22,23].

## 3. Timetabling at the university of connecticut

The timetabling problem solved by UConn each semester consists of the following decisions: 1) Number of sections offered for each course in each campus; 2) Assignment of instructors to classes; 3) Scheduling of each individual class; and 4) Assignment of classes to rooms. Once these steps are finalized, students work with faculty and staff advisors to find a feasible schedule that meets their educational goals and then register for classes in successive waves based on seniority. The vast majority of registration occurs for a standard Fall semester across eight weeks in March and April, with freshmen registration occurring over the summer and a few minor adjustments made to the schedules through the first two weeks of the semester in question.

The timetabling process typically starts about one year in advance. For the Fall semester of 2020, scheduling discussions started in the Fall of 2019 and had been completed before the COVID-19 pandemic started. In the early days of social distancing (March and April), advising and registration proceeded as usual, given that no one knew whether campus life could return to normal by September. Therefore, the efforts high lighted here consist of a revision to the final timetabling step (4) to reflect physical-distancing capacity reductions.

## 3.1. Assignment DSS at UConn

In recent years UConn has employed Astra Schedule<sup>3</sup> as a decisionsupport system to solve Step 4. Astra Schedule assigns classes to rooms while considering capacities constraints and avoiding the assignment of classes that meet at the same time to the same room; the software also allows users to restrict and prioritize assignments. Nevertheless, the problem is not solved after a single execution of the software. For example, some of the features provided by Astra Schedule could not be readily used by the university (e.g., restrictions based on technological capabilities within rooms), so the decisions required manual adjustments and evaluation of “what-if” scenarios. Overall, schools wish to have a “comfortable” schedule, with the vast majority of classroom needs being accommodated in the “home” building of a department or nearby dedicated lecture halls. However, when conflicts emerge, the approaches adopted by the schools may be very different. For example, courses with high demand cannot have their capacity reduced, so rescheduling may be the best mitigation action. Conversely, certain courses may only be taught in specific time slots, so capacity reduction or course cancellation may be the only acceptable alternatives in these cases. Additionally, a school may not have all its courses assigned to its home building, so a university-wide integrated approach is needed. Finally, historically the administrative staff has contacted the developers of Astra Schedule to request additional support or features for some specific conditions, an iteration that requires a non-negligible amount of time.

In order to give schools enough autonomy to accommodate the preferences of faculty and students, UConn adopts a distributed decision-making process, with the administrative staff and the local staff of each school working iteratively. The process starts with the schools, which devise assignment plans for the courses offered by their de partments. Next, the administrative staff consolidates the individual plans, and if conflicts are detected, the schools are communicated and adjustments are made accordingly. Finally, the process must end with an integrated and feasible assignment considered acceptable to all stakeholders.

This iterative process is time-sensitive and has very narrow margins for delays. However, under normal circumstances, this is not an issue, mainly because the university staff and the schools use the schedule employed for the same term in the previous year as a starting point, so they do not need to devise assignment plans from scratch. Typically, year-by-year changes account for growth trends, popular professors, known course sequencing conflicts, etc. These changes are not drastic, so the timetabling problem reduces to fine-tuning a pre-existing solution, thus reducing the performance burden from the DSS.

## 3.2. Changes imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic

After the announcement of physical-distancing guidelines for the Fall semester of 2020, UConn’s facilities and safety staff analyzed and esti mated the COVID capacity of each classroom, the maximum seating ca pacity assuming that students and the instructor should be at least 2 meters (6 feet) distant from each other. In general, COVID capacities represented between 20% and 30% of the original room capacity. These reductions had a drastic impact on the timetable for Fall 2020, as virtually all the assignments used in the past became infeasible. Nevertheless, through direct interaction with students and faculty, the university staff estimated that a considerable part of both populations had a strong preference for in-person instruction. Therefore, some ef forts to avoid having all courses going online for Fall 2020 were ex pected from the university.

Under these circumstances, a new plan was devised by the combined leadership of faculty and staff for Fall 2020. Faculty would convey a preferred teaching modality for each course, and the administrative staff would maximize in-person instruction by defining the teaching modal ities and assigning rooms to courses taking place on campus. These modifications resulted in an optimization problem that could not be directly addressed by the scheduling process and by the DSS used in the past. First, the time available for planning became an obstacle for the adoption of Astra Schedule, as both the number of requests made by the administrative staff and the reaction time of the vendor were expected to increase considerably. Additionally, the constraint requiring proximal teaching space was dropped to allow for a better solution, but this then required that the entire assignment problem for each campus had to be solved in an integrated way. Finally, whereas the usual process was al ways able to schedule all requests for classrooms, the COVID-safety capacity constraints would require that many requests for in-person course instruction could not be accommodated.

## 3.3. Introduction of split-delivery learning

UConn adopted several teaching modalities for Fall 2020. We grouped these modalities into three categories: Online Learning (OL), In-Person Learning (IP), and Split-Delivery Learning (SP). In OL courses, all the teaching activities take place online. IP is the most common learning method, traditionally, with all students attending all class meetings in a physical room. Finally, SP courses are a combination of IP and OL in which 50% of the students attend the classes in a physical room whereas the other 50% watch the classes online. OL classes were not considered in Step 4, as they did not require physical space. In the case of SP, even though instructors expressed willingness to perform a split delivery it was implicit that they would be willing to accept the IP modality. Thus these instructors gave us the flexibility to assign a half-capacity room, but not a mandate to do so. In either case, instructors that opted for SP and IP requests knew that the optimization might deny their request and set OL as the teaching modality of these classes. The “upgrade” flexibility provided by SP requests was a key novel aspect of the assignment problem for Fall 2020, as it increased the number of possible assignments and, therefore, enabled solutions with higher volume of in-person instruction. This flexibility feature was only incorporated into the process after the administrative staff decided to work with the authors.

## 3.4. Optimization goals

Given the information about teaching preferences and timing for each course, the goal was to maximize the amount of in-person in struction offered to students (100%, 50%, or 0% for IP, SP, or OL, respectively, for each course), weighted by student enrollment in each course. The decision support tool typically used to assign courses to rooms (Astra Schedule) could not optimize the assignment of teaching modalities during room assignment, requiring the work described here. Also, note that in contrast with other versions of the timetabling prob lem previously investigated in the literature, UConn did not consider the assignment of courses to time slots using decision support (neither in a normal cycle nor in our pandemic implementation). Thus, our optimi zation problem lacked one complication seen in the literature (endog enous time assignment) but added a new complication (endogenous modality selection).

As is often the case with interactive decision support, a small but non-negligible amount of manual “reassignment” took place after the optimization, using the optimization results as a basis for idiosyncratic adjustments based on emerging preferences not available at the time of optimization. Indeed, after the assignment decisions were made, student scheduling was re-opened (an “add-drop period”), allowing students to change enrollments across sections according to their preferences. With these movements, some courses no longer had a critical mass of students to justify their assignment to in-person instruction. These courses were moved online, thus allowing IP or SP requested courses to be given their places. Thus, our solutions provided a baseline packing of in-person instruction on each campus that was assuredly optimal with our data up to the time of the add-drop period, but then manually adjusted ac cording to changing student preferences.

## 5. Greedy heuristic algorithm

Before establishing a collaboration with the authors, the adminis trative staff at UConn decided not to use Astra Schedule (or any other decision-support system) to design a new assignment plan for Fall 2020 but to use a “manual” approach. This decision was made because the administrative staff was not confident that they would be able to configure and extract satisfactory results from the software within such a short period of time. Their approach of choice at the time is best approximated as a greedy algorithm.

The greedy algorithm assigns classes to rooms sequentially, in the (descending) order defined by their demand n(c). A class c is always assigned to the smallest room r it may fit in, i.e., r must have enough capacity to host $c ,$ and c may not conflict with any other class that has been assigned already to $r _ { * }$ This assignment strategy allows for better utilization of space and reduces discrepancies between the size of the rooms and the capacity of the classes.

Split-learning classes and flexible assignments were only incorpo rated into the new assignment problem after the beginning of our collaboration, so initially, the greedy approach would only try assign ments where classes required full capacity. Nevertheless, we use a twostage version of the greedy algorithm as the baseline in our experiments. In the first stage, the procedure above tries to make IP assignments for all classes. In the second stage, the algorithm produces SP assignments by repeating the same procedure to unassigned classes in $\scriptstyle { \mathcal { C } } ^ { \mathrm { S P } }$

The greedy algorithm runs in polynomial time, so we know from Proposition 1 that, in worst-case scenarios, it cannot give constant-factor approximation guarantees. We show in Section 8 that it delivers good results for the real-world instances, although not as good as those of our exact approach, described next.

## 6. Exact two-stage framework

Our solution approach is an exact two-stage algorithm. In the first stage, we identify an optimal solution using the objective function described in Section $4 ;$ two mixed-integer linear programming formu lations are proposed to solve this problem. The second stage uses a different objective function and additional constraints to find a solution that is optimal for the first stage and minimizes the discrepancy between room capacities and class sizes.

## 7. Development, deployment, and results

In this section we discuss how the project was conducted and present our results. The models and algorithms are implemented in Gurob version 9.0.0 through the Python $3 . 6 ~ \mathrm { A P I . } ^ { 4 }$ All executions are con ducted on an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-8565U CPU at 1.80GHz, 1992Mhz, 1 core and 8GB of memory, with the optimization software using the standard parameters of Gurobi; in particular, all presolve functionalitie were active in the experiments.

## 7.1. Pipeline of the decision support system

Each iteration of the decision-making process consisted of several manual steps, both to prepare the input files and analyze the solutions. The process starts with a spreadsheet file provided by the administrative staff that contains the input data for a campus. The schools used the same file to provide their data, so it contained additional information that was not relevant for the assignment problem, such as classes that should not be considered in the assignment problem (e.g., online cour ses). Therefore, filtering the input data is the first step of our DSS; the same filtering procedures were applied to all campuses.

The second step consists of the construction and solution of a mathematical programming formulation. The selection between Model (A) or (B) depends on the campus; as the instances of each campus were solved sequentially (e.g., we only worked on the Hartford instances after the assignments for Storrs were finalized), the model selection was made manually. Each model has a tailored pre-processing step; namely, Model (A) needs all pairs of conflicting assignments, whereas Model (B) needs ?? <sup>′</sup>. We show below that the pre-processing steps play a key role in the computational performance of these models.

After solving the problem, our algorithms generate a commaseparated values (CSV) file, which we use to update the spreadsheet provided by the administrative staff. Additionally, we also created a visualization dashboard, which provided an interactive tool that the staff could used to analyse the solutions. The university does not employ a database system to store this information in intermediate steps, so all the files were transmitted from the administrative staff to the authors and back by email.

## 7.2. Initial steps and the need for faster performance

Storrs is the flagship campus of UConn, and it is also by far the largest in terms of educational activity. Therefore, all the efforts were initially directed to the Storrs instance.

Our first solution was Model (A), but preliminary tests showed that the solution times were unacceptable for the Storrs instance. Perfor mance was crucial because the administrative staff wanted to keep a fast-paced iteration with the schools, who needed information about preliminary solutions to provide actionable feedback to faculty and program directors.

At first, even the construction of Model (A) was computationally expensive. This model needs the list of all conflicts between all pairs of classes, which requires pairwise comparisons involving almost 1,500 classes. In early versions of the code, this step would need approximately one hour of computation. To speed up the process, the conflict matrix would be computed a priori so that the code with the optimization model could load it directly. However, as the input data would frequently change over time, this pre-processing approach could only partially mitigate the issue. Additionally, Model (A) was also timeconsuming to solve, and this was the most critical issue of this formu lation. Both the number of variables and the number of constraints used in Model (A) are $O ( | \mathcal { C } | | \mathcal { R } | )$ ; the Storrs instance has over 8500 feasible assignments, so the resulting model relies on the pre-processing pro cedures performed by state-of-the-art commercial solvers to become tractable. Nevertheless, the reductions were not remarkable: 15% of constraints and 1% of the variables would be eliminated, and these preprocessing steps typically needed approximately two minutes of computation. Both the model and the input data were still in preliminary stages when the first tests were made, but with execution times going over 16 hours, the performance of Model (A) became a bottleneck. So lutions with optimality gaps below 5% could be obtained within 2 hours (including the pre-processing time). Nevertheless, the commercial solver struggled to close the optimality gap mainly due to the poor dual (upper) bounds, thus suggesting difficulties in extracting and exploiting struc ture from Model A.

## 7.5. Incorporation of second stage to handle discrepant assignments

Preliminary results uncovered an aspect that is not captured by the original description of the problem; some of the assignments produced by our models had a large discrepancy between the size of the course and the capacity of the room, e.g., classes with ten students assigned to rooms with 50 seats. While the optimization correctly identified this as ten student seats in its objective, it did not consider that a room with a closer match between capacity and expected usage would be more comfortable, for example. Additionally, on several occasions, the administrative staff mentioned that, if possible, the final plan should leave some time slots of large-capacity rooms unassigned, as the space could be used for administrative meetings, as study rooms, etc. Later in the process, we also observed that this arrangement was convenient for the schools in the post-optimization phase. They had the chance to reschedule courses to use available space in relatively large rooms for inperson instructions.

We mitigated these issues by incorporating a second stage to our DSS, which searches among the optimal solutions of the first stage for a so lution that minimizes discrepancies (see Section 6.3). We define the discrepancy of an assignment as the squared difference between the room capacity and the class demand, with the latter being scaled in SP assignments. This definition was adopted because solutions with several assignments having minor discrepancies were preferable to solutions with few assignments with substantial discrepancies. The final results show that the second stage had a significant impact. For the Storrs instance, the average discrepancy value per assignment in the solution of the first stage was $^ { 1 5 . 6 , }$ whereas the second stage brought this value down to 12.6, i.e., an improvement of more than 30%.

## 7.7. Post-optimization adjustments

The Storrs instance was the first to be solved, and took by far the largest amount of time among all campuses (several weeks). This asymmetry is justified not only by the fact that the Storrs instance was one order of magnitude larger than the others (see Table 1), but also because the entire decision-making process was new to all the partici pants. Once an optimized schedule was delivered, Department Heads began to work with faculty to make practical adjustments. As an example worked on first-hand by one of the authors, an Accounting professor saw that only one of his two sections of an upper-level elective was given a room in the optimization, while the other was assigned to move online. Not wanting to deliver the same course with two different modalities, he requested an in-person room for the second section, and an instructor from Management volunteered to give up her room-timeslot to accommodate this request. Such ad-hoc adjustments happened mainly at the school level and had little effect on the objective of maximizing in-person opportunities for students. For the School of Business, swaps changed only 1.67% of in-person courses assigned by the optimization on the Storrs campus, one data point indicating that the faculty and departments largely accepted the optimization results.

In our optimal solution for the Storrs instance, 233 classes (out of 1455) were not assigned to physical rooms, and the vast majority of these settled for online delivery. However, after the optimization, a few instructors worked with the registrar to find “anything that would work” for in-person teaching. Our efforts to deal with discrepancy by assigning smaller courses to smaller rooms and free up larger spaces played a crucial role in this stage, as the schools could reschedule courses to use the available space. At this point, the optimizer results (with minor adjustments) had achieved the goals set out for us: make as many inperson seats available to students as possible and fully respecting fac ulty preferences to be on-campus or not. The use of optimization suc ceeded in avoiding any political motivation for deciding which courses seats could be offered (without schedule disruption) and provided sig nificant help to the registrar in devising a baseline warm-start schedule for each entire campus.

Students were then given access to an add-drop tool to adjust their schedules as they would in any semester before the first day of in struction. After these movements, some in-person courses needed to be dropped from the schedule due to lack of enrollment, while other inperson courses needed to be added. Ultimately 8.8% of all courses were in this last group of in-person classes manually added to accom modate students into room-time-slots not assigned by the optimization process. Reflecting back, our initial conversations with the administra tion discussed the ugly possibility of throwing out the entire schedule and starting over. After persuading them that optimization could instead help salvage as much as possible of the existing schedule, the finding that over 91% of all courses could remain in the same time slot as a result of the optimization project was a victory. It typically takes about a year of planning and several weeks of working with students (to plan which courses fit in their schedules and fulfill requirements on pace to grad uation). Therefore, having only about 8.8% of the scheduling as manual re-work was a burden we were happy to take off the shoulders of our administration during these difficult times.

## 8. Insights about the adoption of SP learning

We analyze below alternative scenarios and show how a better “appreciation” of the SP modality, both in terms of adoption and on the weight it has in the objective function, could lead to (numerically) better results.

## 8.1. Adoption of SP learning and a fairer comparison with the baseline approach

Table 2 shows that the SP teaching modality had a relatively low impact on our solutions; namely, less than 2% of the classes (4% of the seats) were assigned to classes with split delivery. The main reason is the fact that only 84 out of the 1,532 classes elected for the possibility of SP assignments; Storrs had the vast majority, with 74 classes, whereas Hartford had 0, Waterbury 1, Avery 2, Stamford 3, and GBLC only 4. One possible explanation for the relatively low popularity of split delivery is the novelty of the concept; few faculty had previous experience with this teaching modality. Additionally, there was a concern of “double work” with SP delivery, as the instructors would have to teach on campus and still need to prepare the same course material for online delivery.

SP learning was only adopted by UConn as a result of a move to an optimization approach. Otherwise, the administrative staff would not have considered this modality, and their assignment would have been similar to the results of the first stage of the greedy algorithm, which involves only OL and IP delivery. In Table 3 we show the results for both algorithms when SP assignments are not allowed; the chosen modality for these classes was assumed to be IP. Observe that the relative per formance of the heuristic improves slightly with the removal of SP as signments. A comparison between the results for the greedy algorithm in Table 3 and the two-stage framework in Table 2 shows that our algo rithm increased the number of in-person classes and seats by 8% and 10%, respectively.

## 8.2. Same weight for SP and IP classes

Our objective function prioritizes IP assignments over SP assign ments. Alternatively, one could redefine this criterion by using the same weight for both types. In practice, this alternative definition of the objective function would have increased the number of classes and seats with “some level” of in-person instruction. We evaluated this alternative model using the same instances. For Storrs, the number of assigned classes and seats would grow from 1232 to 1234 and from 33,897 to 34,474, respectively, whereas Waterbury would have 25 additional seats (and the same number of classes); the number for all the other campuses would remain the same.

The impact of this modification in the objective function is nonnegligible, especially in the number of assigned seats, which increased by almost 600. In practice, though, feedback from students during the Fall of 2020 suggests that SP courses were not considered as good as their IP counterparts. Therefore, the overall impression was that the criteria used for the optimization reflected student preferences and, as observed by Thompson [3], better reflects the quality of experience perceived by students.

## 8.3. Higher adoption levels of SP learning

In Table 4 we show the results of a hypothetical scenario in which all classes requesting space for in-person instruction could be assigned for SP delivery. A direct comparison between Table 2 and Table 4 show that the total number of classes and seats with in-person instruction would increase dramatically. Furthermore, we observe that the optimal solu tion would have typically 10% fewer IP assignments when compared to the results in Table 2. Conversely, the number of SP classes would in crease considerably; similar trends can be observed for the number of seats. Note also that the number of covered classes (sum of IP and SP) goes to over 95% for Storrs, Avery, Waterbury, and GBLC. Such high percentages would not be achieved for Stamford and Hartford, but observe that the number of covered seats in these instances would be superior to 65% (versus 34% in the actual results).

These results show that the superiority of the exact approach over the greedy algorithm increases with more flexible assignments. Observe that the first stage of the greedy algorithm is not affected by the assumption that all classes may have SP instruction, so the myopic decisions of the first stage restrict the possibilities for assignments in the second stage. This observation is in agreement with the fact that the two-stage framework selects fewer IP assignments in these cases. In most in stances, the differences between the greedy algorithm and the exact approach are considerable (around 10% for classes and seats); excep tions were Avery Point and GBLC, for which the solutions produced by both approaches were similar.

## 9. Conclusion

In this paper, we presented an optimization approach to the assign ment of classes to rooms for Fall 2020 at the University of Connecticut, confronted with drastic reductions in room capacities due to COVID, alternating-use teaching modalities, and hundreds of unique course meeting patterns. The resulting NP-hard problem was solved several times as decision support for UConn schedulers and Department Heads, who used our results as an optimized master schedule that could later be adjusted for idiosyncratic complications and changing expectations as the global pandemic progressed. More importantly, our work delivered optimized results for all UConn campuses that were adopted in practice.

As for future work, stochastic extensions of the problem would probably have the most significant practical impact as long as reason able prediction models are available. For instance, information about expected enrollment can enable the design of a two-stage stochastic model; the first stage would have a preliminary assignment, and the second stage would support decisions on reassignments after the final enrollment numbers were known.
