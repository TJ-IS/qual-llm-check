# The dynamic customer engagement behaviors in the customer satisfaction survey

Saram Han <sup>a,\*,1</sup>, Christopher K. Anderson <sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business and Technology, Seoul National University of Science and Technology, 232 Gongneung-ro, Nowon-gu, Seoul 01811, Republic of Korea <sup>b</sup> SC Johnson College of Business, School of Hotel Administration, Cornell University, Ithaca, NY 14853, USA

## A B S T R A C T

Conducting customer satisfaction surveys (CSS) as a means of encouraging customers’ engagement with a firm is a widely used managerial practice. In an effort to convert this customer-to-firm engagement into customer-to-customer engagement, many firms have recently started to ask customers who reach the final question of the CSS to write an online review that will be automatically posted on a review site (e.g., Google or TripAdvisor). Using a unique longitudinal data set provided by a large global hotel chain, we aim to understand the drivers behind CSS participation and, ultimately, behind online review posting in response to a prompt at the end of a survey. We employ the Hidden Markov model (HMM) to analyze how a customer’s engagement state changes as a function of previous managerial responses, loyalty program membership, and satisfaction. The results reveal that customers have a greater tendency to transfer to higher engagement states if managers have responded to prior customer surveys. We have also found that the higher the customer’s loyalty program membership status is, the more likely it is that they will participate in a survey. Lastly, we show that customers have a greater tendency to post negative versus positive reviews. We then discuss the implications of this study for researchers and managers.

## 1. Introduction

Using customer satisfaction surveys (CSS) to collect customer feed back and control for service quality is a common practice in many in dustries. To make better use of the CSS system and increase the number of online reviews, online review platforms such as TripAdvisor, encourage firms to use review collection tools where the respondents of the CSS are solicited at the end of the survey to post a review to their website. Efforts have been made by firms to encourage customers to interact with the brand and with other potential customers through the use of post-stay surveys and the posting of reviews to sites such as Tri pAdvisor. However, low response rates create concerns regarding the value associated with review encouragement. Unless a customer has a high loyalty to the brand or has a particular request, it is common that customers ignore the firm’s survey request in the digital era where customers are surrounded by numerous email requests. A recent in: dustry report from McKinsey found that due to the low response rate, only 13% of the surveyed leaders answered that they are fully confident with their CSS results [1]. Thus, how to increase survey response rates (in turn, the review posting rates) is an important issue for marketers. Despite the growing interests in improving the CSS system, to our knowledge, the drivers of increasing the response and review posting rates through this system have rarely been investigated. In a typical CSS, however, there are not many known options for marketers to increase response and review posting rates. Encouraging customer survey participation by sending a follow-up email to non-respondents might be one option; however, email solicitations like these may annoy customers [2]. As such, there is a need to explore activities that may contribute to an increase in CSS participation rates.

Considering that a survey request and the response to it are part of human interaction, the underlying relationship between the survey interviewer and the respondent has been shown to be a crucial factor that determines the survey response rate [3]. In the current research, we focus on two antecedents that are meant to develop the relationship between the customer and the firm: the managerial response to the CSS respondent and loyalty program membership. To be specific, we answer whether the managerial response to a survey previously answered by a customer will increase their likelihood of future long-term survey re sponses and review postings. We further explore the short-term role of customers’ loyalty program membership status and their valence on survey responses and review postings, respectively. To address the latent and dynamic natures of the longitudinal customer engagement data, we apply a hidden Markov model (HMM). Our research contributes to the customer engagement literature by providing empirical evidence about its drivers using actual customer data and a robust statistical approach. Our research also sheds light on the industry where marketers seek ways to increase the effectiveness of their CSS systems.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Customer engagement

Recent research divides a customer's value addition into direct and indirect contributions [4]. Here, direct contributions consist of customer purchase–based value, which has, historically, been the target outcome in traditional customer relationship management (CRM). However, it has been more than a decade since both practitioners and academicians started to recognize that the value of customer contributions extends beyond transactional outcomes [5]. Thus, recent research has exten sively studied the concept of customer engagement (CE) as an indirect contribution to the firm. Such indirect contributions consist of cus tomer’s non-transactional actions that add value to the firm; for example, social media conversations that customers have about the brand and online customer feedback and complaints. In this paper, we follow the definition by [6], which defines CE as the behavioral mani festation by a customer toward a brand or a firm that goes beyond purchase behavior.

Researchers have suggested that ignoring non-transactional customer value in customer lifetime value (CLV) calculations could lead to a biased perception of a customer’s contribution to the firm [7]. As such, recent research has recognized that CE behavior should receive a level of attention equal to that given to consumption behavior in CRM [4]. Recognizing the importance of CE, many current marketing pro grams are not purchase focused; instead, they concentrate on improving CE via tools such as customer surveys or WOM agent campaigns [8]. [9] shows that such engagement-initiative programs provide firms with considerable financial returns. Therefore, evaluating the effectiveness of such engagement-initiative programs and how to improve them is important.

Despite the considerable interest in CE, there is a paucity of empirical research within the service literature that analyzes the drivers of CE [4,7]. Specifically, the literature lacks an individual-level examination of the patterns of CE evolution and, more importantly, what process might trigger these behaviors; this would aid practitioners when per forming an engagement-initiative program. Empirical findings in this area have the potential to make significant contributions in both academia and practice, as they would shed light on the fundamental marketing question: how can firms improve CE? Hence, unlike prior research, which addresses the role of CRM in terms of improving purchase-based outcomes, we highlight the effect of managerial efforts on indirect customer value (i.e., CE). In this paper, we use panel data that track a global hotel chain’s actual CE behavior, investigating the dynamics in an effort to understand how managerial interventions can influence CE level over time.

## 2.2. CSS as an engagement-initiative program

Because CE is a major source of competitive advantage, engagement marketing is now vital to the success of the service industry. As such, carefully designed engagement-initiative programs that provide communication channels wherein customers can easily participate in a conversation with/about the focal brand are receiving increasing amounts of attention within the industry [10]. A common managerial effort in many settings to facilitate CE through such a channel is the customer satisfaction survey (CSS). Two types of CEs can be observed under the current CSS system: customer-to-firm (C2B) and customer-to customer (C2C) engagements. While C2B engagement entails customer willingness to provide feedback and suggestions to the firm, C2C engagement refers to the behavior of transferring firm-related infor mation from one customer to another customers [8]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a typical CSS starts with a post-purchase evaluation by a customer, who has been contacted by the firm and asked to provide feedback about their recent experience [11]. C2B engagement is first observed once a customer decides to participate in the CSS. The CE in this instance might involve a form of co-creation, feedback, or complaint. Therefore, each customer response to the CSS provides an opportunity for a manager to rectify any service failures or to let the customer participate in the co-creation process [4].

In order to extend this C2B engagement so that it becomes a C2C engagement, recent online CSS questionnaires facilitate the posting of customer feedback to public review platforms (e.g., Google or Tri pAdvisor). In practice, this tactic could either take a sponsored form (where consumers are offered compensation) or a self-motivated format [12]. Note that in our research environment, it is the latter case which keeps our model from being affected by other monetary factors. The second manifestation of CE takes place when the customer agrees to post an online review (i.e., C2C engagement).

The benefits of engagement through the CSS are four-fold. First, the mere request for participation in a survey has been known to have a positive financial impact on a firm [13]. The ability of customer surveys to increase profit has been highlighted by many studies. For instance, [14] found evidence that simply contacting customers to listen to thei voices is effective at fostering repeat purchases. [15] demonstrated that asking customers about their purchase intentions through a survey in creases their repeat-purchase tendency and reduces the interval between purchases. Similarly, [11] indicates that conducting a customer survey that includes open-ended questions positively affects purchase behavior. Second, responses to the CSS provide managers with an opportunity to make amends for any service failures [16]. Recent findings corroborate the idea that appropriate redress for service failures reported via a survey or online review allows a firm to achieve a better customer relationship [17]. The idea behind these findings is that a recovery effort by a service provider in the event of failure rectifies the customer’s perception that they experienced unfair treatment; this, in turn, im proves their satisfaction levels [18]. For example, [19] found that the average rating of a hotel increased after managers started to respond to negative reviews. Third, this process allows firms to increase the numbe of reviews they receive; this is known as an important driver that in creases firms’ financial outcomes [20]. Considering that the majority of customers do not post online reviews, online reviews from customers who would not normally have posted are beneficial resources for man agers [21]. Fourth, CSS responses are customer feedback that help improve or develop the firm’s offerings through co-creation. Previous studies have suggested that customers’ shared knowledge can enhance service experiences [22] by developing new service or improving existing service. Indeed, [23] demonstrate that customers’ service participation contribute to the customer satisfaction through creation of perceived economic value. Lastly, such a CSS system allows verified customers to post genuine reviews; this increases the credibility of the information provided, thereby benefiting the firm in the long run [24]. As consumers posting on online review platforms voluntarily decide to submit their reviews, the online review platforms themselves have suf fered from fictitious reviews that are not written by actual customers [25]. Under the CSS system, only “verified customers” who have actu ally experienced the product/service can be invited to the survey (and can post an online review). In this way, a more authentic and reliable source of reviews can be generated.

It is evidently important for customers to participate in a survey in order to have the opportunity to post a review on social platforms. Therefore, CSS solicitation, as part of a marketing campaign, has been widely implemented as a growing number of firms realize the power of CE [26]. If CE encouraged through the CSS system is a strategic resource, companies need to understand the drivers behind the two opinion channels (i.e., C2B and C2C) that effectively stimulate CE behaviors. According to the data used in the analysis, CSS participation rates are usually low (49.70%), and the tendency to post an online review at the end of the survey is tenuous (0.36%). This phenomenon is in line with previous research, which suggests that the majority of customers are more likely to silently quit their relationship with a firm rather than providing it with feedback or telling their peers about the product/ser vice [21].

Considering that interacting with customers who participate in a CSS is readily within management’s control, it is important to understand the engagement patterns of customers who receive the CSS. As yet, however, academics and practitioners alike lack sufficient understand ing of the dynamics of CE and how they affect the CSS and its ante cedents. Although there are studies that discuss the expected financial outcome of conducting a survey [9,11] or compare the organic and solicited reviews posted after the CSS [26], no research has yet consid ered how to stimulate customer participation in the CSS (which, in turn, results in an enhanced probability that they will post a review).

Specifically, in this study we address the following managerial questions. Do managerial responses to CSS respondents improve CE? Are there any other controllable factors that increase customer engagement with CSSs? Where a customer has responded to the CSS, which valence (i.e., positive or negative) is more likely to be voiced over social media (i.e., C2C engagement)? These questions are crucial to effective CE management and should be considered by any manager that wishes to conduct a CSS. Therefore, the objective of this study are to evaluate the effect of externalities of common marketing practices (i.e., managerial responses and membership/loyalty program participation) on enhancing CE and understand which valences are more likely be conveyed over online review platforms. The managerial response refers to the email response of the manager to the survey participant, and the membership status indicates the status that a customer earns from the loyalty program of the hotel chain. We further discuss each of these in the hypotheses section.

## 2.3. C2B and C2C engagement behaviors

This study contributes to the CE literature that deals with the two behaviors that form the outcome variables of our proposed model. The existing body of literature, which discusses CE as the variable of interest, divides CE into two streams depending on to whom the customer voice is directed (i.e., the target of CE): C2B [6] and C2C [27] engagements [9].

C2B (or customer-to-firm) engagement is how customers voice their opinions to a firm; it includes complaints, feedback/co-creation, and compliments [27]. Historically, studies that focus on C2B engagement have used either profit or satisfaction as the dependent variables and examine the impact of the customer–manager interaction [28]. For example, [29] discuss how the attributes of the managerial responses collected from TripAdvisor are associated with the future review ratings. However, these scholars have paid little attention to how to increase the C2B engagement itself. Considering that most customers do not share opinions (with the firm), there is a lack of understanding of how to encourage customers to interact with a business. The only notable exception is [30], which uses longitudinal data of customer voices and managerial responses on Twitter to study C2B engagement behaviors over social media. Within the context where customer–manager communication is publicly visible to other people, they found that managerial responses to the customer voice encourage customers to voice more of their complaints. Although the findings by [30] are insightful, their research environment is significantly different from ours. Rather than studying individuals who already have enough utility to participate in publicly open communication platforms, the customer’s voice in our study is initially targeted at the manager in a private manner, but has the potential of being extended to a C2C engagement (i. e., an online review). Therefore, our research setting allows us to investigate how to increase the online review posting utility of those who otherwise would not have posted online reviews, as their initial motivation to participate in the survey lies merely in voicing their opinion to the manager (compared to other customers).

One common thread among CE literature is that an understanding of the customer value creation should not be restricted to the parties in an exchange (service providers and customers) [9]. Customer value is derived from both the C2B and the C2C engagement, a concept that has long been discussed in marketing [8]. Specifically, literature on WOM discusses how the communication among customers impacts the customer purchase behavior or the financial outcome of the firm [31]. For example, [32] has found that WOM has twice the impact on customer equity as other marketing campaigns. Due to the proliferation of internet usage, recent empirical research in this stream extensively studies the essential role of online WOM in the business environment [33]. [34] found that C2C communication via online communities re duces product uncertainty; this, in turn, increases the intention to immediately purchase. Similarly, [35] measures the effect of each cus tomer’s ability to spread WOM recommendations using various social media connections, providing evidence of its positive financial impact on sales and return on investment. Likewise, recent research have found a positive relationship between the online CE and the firms’ financial performance in the movie industry [36]. As such, firms now proactively seek ways to motivate customers to post online reviews about their product/service experience. Although interest in C2C engagement is growing, there is a paucity of research investigating the dynamics of CE by longitudinally tracking the data at an individual level. To accurately gauge CE, we need to understand how an individual customer’s engagement behavior evolves during repeated interactions and in response to the firm’s actions. Therefore, we need a dynamic analysis at the individual customer level that explicitly accounts for the underlying relationship [28].

To fill this gap, this study takes a novel approach. First, from a data perspective, we analyze a unique panel data set obtained from a hotel chain in order to investigate the dynamic CE behaviors. We use this data to track actual customers’ survey participation, the managerial re sponses to the survey participants, online reviews posted as a response to the final question in the CSS, and other transaction-level characteristics. Prior CE studies have dominantly used the intention (e.g., intent of WOM) and compare it to the behaviors as a dependent variable [18]; this approach has been criticized due to both the weak relationship it creates between intentions and behavior and its low managerial implications [37]. Our data allows us to overcome the defects inherent in the use of intention variables, as we examine the impact of the predictors on actual CE behaviors (i.e., via CSS participation and online review posting). Second, from a methodological perspective, our model complements the growing body of marketing literature that highlights the usefulness of HMM when considering customer dynamics in CE [7]. We model a customer’s underlying engagement and track its dynamic evolution over time. In this model, we assume that there exist different levels of un observed engagement states for each customer at different times which we can infer from the observable customer behaviors. Although it has been recommended as a way to account for the dynamics in CE analyses [38], researchers have rarely considered the time-varying effects that can be identified using the HMM [7]. Using the HMM, we find that CE transfers across distinct states over time, and that the engagement evolution is attributable to a customer's prior relationship experience with a particular hotel. However, note that due to the limitation of our data, we included customers who have at least certain number of ob servations. Therefore, our findings cannot be generalized to the cus tomers who are at the early stage of relationship with the brand.

We contribute several new findings to the collective knowledge of CE. The results of this study show that: (1) throughout the repeated transactions, a customer could be either in a low or a high engagement state; (2) a customer has a greater tendency to transfer to a higher engagement state when more of their prior CSS feedback is responded to by managers; (3) the higher the customer’s loyalty program status, the greater tendency to participate in a survey; and (4) customers have a greater tendency to post a negative review than a positive review. Our model can help managers decide whether to respond to customers, reevaluate the membership program as a tool with which to leverage CE, and/or understand which voices are more likely to be posted as an online review within the CSS system.

In the following sections, we present the predictors of our model and develop the hypotheses underlying our empirical analysis. In the next section, we describe the data set and specify the model. We then discuss the results of the hypotheses tests and the empirical findings. We conclude by assessing the implications of our study for both researchers and managers.

## 3. Hypotheses

In this study, we propose three critical drivers of CE behaviors in the context of CSS: managerial responses, loyalty program participation, and satisfaction. As past customer–manager interactions constitute an important part of the customer experience, we suggest that these experiences might gradually impact CE behaviors throughout the customer journey [39]. Loyalty programs in the service industry serve as a means to keep a customer base; however, less attention has been paid to their CE value. We illustrate the positive relationship between loyalty membership and CSS participation, as well as discussing how review valence affects the posting of online reviews.

## 3.1. Impact of prior managerial responses on CE

As discussed in the introduction, in a typical interactive CSS system, managers can privately respond to customer feedback. When multiple interactions between the customer and a firm occur, the CE to a given CSS request may be affected by previously received managerial re sponses [40]. We extend the existing equity and social exchange theory to understand the causal mechanism between the managerial responses and CE behaviors. Equity theory suggests that people express their voice when the output they receive is equal to or greater than their input [41]. By receiving responses from the service providers (i.e., output), cus tomers understand that they have an important role in improving a firm’s service performance (i.e., input). This is in line with social ex change theory that suggests that when two parties interact, they eval uate various forms of costs and benefits throughout the interactions to determine whether this exchange is equitable [42]. For instance, sup pose that you send feedback to a hotel through an email. You might be further encouraged to give another feedback to the firm in the future if your past feedback is responded by the manager (i.e., a benefit out weighing the cost) as compared to the case where the manager appears to ignore your feedback (i.e., a cost without having any benefits). Indeed. prior studies demonstrate that customers' online WOM moti: vation tends to increase when they recognize that their voice has had an impact on the firm and on other potential customers [19]. For example, in analyzing the online review data collected from major online trave agencies in China, [43] found that the number of reviews increases when managers respond to the posted reviews. Similarly, [17] found that when a dissatisfied online reviewer receives a managerial response, they tend to post a more positive review about the same hotel after the subsequent stay. These findings suggest that a customer's future feedback might be influenced by the manager’s reaction to their previous feedback. This may also hold true in private customer-managei communication. A lack of response gives the impression that the feed: back system is not important to the firm, causing the customer to put less effort into their response to the CSS. In contrast, responding to cus tomers with appropriate answers gives customers the impression that their feedback is important and that completing the CSS is worth thei effort. As a result, such managerial responses can serve as positive reinforcement, encouraging customers to repeatedly engage with the firm. Therefore, we expect that customers who receive a greater number of managerial responses will have a greater tendency to engage than those who receive fewer managerial responses.

Hypothesis 1. (H1). A customer has a greater tendency to transfer to a higher engagement state when more of their prior CSS feedback is responded to by managers.

## 3.2. Impact of loyalty program membership status on survey participation

Many firms use loyalty programs as a means of enhancing CLV [44]. Typically, a loyalty or reward program incentivizes a firm’s customers to increase their relationship status with the firm by providing price dis counts or product upgrades based on their previous transaction history. In this way, customers with higher levels of membership status are rewarded. As the membership tier grows, the benefits of staying with the firm increase; this, in turn, increases the switching cost for the customer and makes them more loyal to the firm [45]. Extensive research has found evidence that loyalty programs are effective at creating a profit able relationship with customers. For example, prior studies have reported that loyalty programs are effective at increasing customer retention rate [46] and customer share [44]. Recent studies have found that higher-tier members have a significantly greater intention to pur chase from the focal brand than lower-tier members [31,47]. However, none of this research stream discusses the indirect effects on customers, such as CE behavior, that the loyalty program can have.

Understanding that customers in different membership tiers exhibit different levels of engagement behavior allows managers to reevaluate the expected outcomes of their loyalty program. Customers who have a weak relationship with the firm may have low motivation to send a feedback as it is easier for them to simply switch to another brand rather than trying to change the firm or developing a rapport with the firm from scratch. For example, [48] shows that customers who have a high level of commitment are more willing to provide constructive feedback to the firm than their counterparts with low levels of commitment. Therefore, in this study, we posit that loyalty programs are effective drivers that increases CSS participation rates. Psychological attachment to the product/brand, which indicates that an emotional bond exists between the customer and the product/brand, is a driver that enhances a customer’s willingness to provide feedback to a firm [27] or to recom mend their experience to a friend or a colleague [49]. Converging evi dence suggests that this psychological attachment is a key element of the loyalty program [50]. For example, using a meta-analysis, [45] found that higher-tier members of a loyalty program have greater psycholog ical attachment to a firm than do the lower-tier members. As the loyalty program membership reflects a customer’s psychological attachment, higher-tier members should be more active in interacting with the firm [6]. This leads to the second hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. (H2). The higher a customer’s membership status within a loyalty program, the greater their tendency to participate in a CSS.

## 3.3. Impact of satisfaction on review posting

Customers may decide to respond to a request to post an online re view that is located at the end of the CSS, but only when they have strong-enough motivation to do so. A customer’s satisfaction level has long been known as a key factor in the process of deciding whether or not to post a review [51]. A greater tendency to share a negative WOM (as opposed to a positive one) has been repeatedly found in previous research [24,52]. [53] argue that, based on prospect theory, customers tend to be more sensitive to their losses than their gains; this leads them to be more responsive to negative events (in other words, they experi ence loss aversion). Moreover, negative information is more perceptu ally salient than positive information; this results in customers remembering negative information longer than they do positive infor mation [54].

We expect that this comparatively greater tendency to share negative WOM should hold under the CSS system. In addition to the intrinsic motivation, we suggest that the unique nature of the CSS facilitates the tendency to post negative reviews. Due to the widely recognized power of customers’ online reviews to impact the business [55], customers may be aware that their online reviews can influence the way a company and its image are perceived. Grounded in learning theory [56], customers who want to induce a change in their relationship with a firm may seek a stimuli that either rewards or punishes the firm’s behavior. In the context of CSS interaction (where the online review poster’s identity is not anonymous to the manager) customers may want to treat this op portunity as an instrument that can have a strong impact on the way the firm treats them. In comparison to the many positive reviews available online, however, negative reviews are rare and unexpected; therefore, negative reviews are more salient than their positive counterparts, and are thus perceived as being more influential on future customers [57]. As customers perceive punishment as having a greater impact on a firm than reward, customers who have had a negative experience are more likely to use this power than those who have had a positive experience. Therefore, we suggest the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. (H3). Less satisfied customers have a greater tendency to post an online review than more satisfied customers.

## 4. Data

Our unique data are comprised of customer satisfaction surveys relating to 515 hotels located in the US from one of the largest mid-scale international hotel chains in the world. The data were collected over a period of 48 months, from Jan 2015 to Dec 2018. This firm has an ongoing survey-based customer satisfaction tracking program operated by an outsourced market research company. Within seven days following check out, the hotel chain’s CSS provider automatically emails a survey request to all customers who have chosen to provide their contact information. The data include responses from over 235,111 unique customers, and from 477,010 survey responses out of 834,237 booking occasions. In total, 3,589 of these respondents also posted re views at the end of the survey. For every customer visit to the hotel, the company tracks the following details: transaction-specific information; the membership status of the customer at the point of purchase; whether the survey is responded to; the self-reported satisfaction ratings sub mitted by the customer regarding their recent experience at the hotel; whether the customer posted an online review at the end of the survey; and whether and how the manager responded to customer feedback.

Every respondent receives a maximum of 20 questions, and the number of questions varies depending on the services each customer received. We chose one of these questions as the satisfaction variable (SAT): “How satisfied were you with the OVERALL experience?” The rationale behind our decision to select this variable as a satisfaction measure is: 1) it measures the same general satisfaction with the hotel experience as the online review rating question; and 2) this satisfaction question is the first satisfaction question that customers receive, thereby averting any potential measurement bias caused by the question-order effect [58]. Each customer’s decision to open the email and complete the survey is based purely on their self-motivation. because the hotels do not provide any financial incentive to do so. We confirmed that all ho tels’ name include the umbrella chain’s brand name. In doing so, we can assume that customers recognize the visited hotel as being part of this chain. Thus, consumers may perceive the received survey request and managerial response as being sent from the agent employed by the same chain. When a customer makes only a few visits, their visit patterns are less likely to be of statistical significance [59]. We therefore narrowed the respondents down to customers who booked from the hotel chain at least four times but not over 50 times during the calibration period. Due to the long computation time required by the Bayesian estimation, we randomly selected 5000 individuals who participated in the CSS at least once. While the number of samples (5000) has been chosen somewhat arbitrarily, it was done so that the computational time for parameter estimation was reasonable while also ensuring sufficient review-posting cases that would allow our model to have statistically acceptable power. The CSS response and review-posting rates across the observations of the original data are 51.90% and 0.39%, respectively, which are similar to those of our final data set (survey participation rate: 49.70%; review posting rate: 0.36%). Therefore, these values allow us to confirm that the CSS participation patterns in the final data set are similar to the original data set. As our data-filtering process has the potential to be affected by selection bias (as it is more representative of the highly engaged cus tomers) we account for individual-level engagement propensities in our HMM. This procedure vielded a data set with a total of 35.002 hotel visits and 17,392 survey-participation occasions.

## 4.1. Dependent variables

In order to understand the dynamics in CE behaviors, the priorcustomer CSS responses and the managerial responses are both assumed to affect the customer’s latent engagement state over time. Following [60], we develop the HMM (which we will describe in the next section) in such a way that the customers’ multidimensional behavior (i.e., CSS participation and online review posting at the end of the CSS) is reflected in each of the latent CE states. For example, a customer who is engaged with a firm will be captured by a state that is characterized by a high tendency to engage in survey participation and online review posting. In contrast, we expect that a low engagement period will be captured by a state that is characterized by a low probability to engage in CSS participation and online review posting. Therefore, in our HMM, we calculate that the customers’ latent CSS engagement is determined both by their sequential survey participation and their online review postings.

For the binary event of CSS participation, we use the survey response incidence; that is, whether or not the customer completed the survey after receiving the email about the survey request. The binary event of posting an online review is a variable that indicates whether a survey respondent answered the final two questions, which ask the respondent to evaluate the hotel on a five-point scale and write a review that will be automatically posted on either TripAdvisor or Google’s online review platform.

## 4.2. Explanatory variables

In addition to the two dependent measures, the data also contain a host of transaction-specific variables. When multiple interactions be tween a customer and a firm occur, the engagement decisions regarding a given CSS request will be affected by the managerial responses received previously [40].

However, recollections of any prior interactions with a firm may dissipate over time, as people are typically more aware of recent events than they are of events that have happened in the past [40]. Customers update their engagement levels using information gathered during new interaction experiences with a firm; this relatively more-recent infor mation may diminish the effect of prior managerial responses [61]. Hence, we need to account for the effects of forgetting by assuming that recent managerial responses are more salient than those received long ago. As such, we apply the following variable transformation, which is used in the marketing literature [40,61]. We use the index $i = 1 . . . , N$ for the customers and $t = 1 . . . T _ { i }$ for customer i‘s events. Let $\mathrm { V I S I T } _ { i t } , \mathrm { P A R T } _ { i t } ,$ and $\textstyle \mathrm { M R S P } _ { i t }$ serve as the binary dummy variables indicating the hotel stay, the survey participation, and the managerial response at time t, respectively. The continuous variable $\mathbf { S A T } _ { i t }$ is the customer satisfaction reported in the survey at event time t; this ranges from 1 to 10, with 1 being very dissatisfied and 10 being very satisfied. Let $t _ { i j }$ denote the calendar time of a particular event j for a customer i.

In this way, we define the prior CE experiences and the managerial responses according to their incidence and timing, so that the effect of each event is discounted over time. We estimate $\lambda _ { \nu } , \lambda _ { r } ,$ and $\lambda _ { m r } ,$ which are the decay parameters for prior hotel visits, survey responses, and managerial responses to the surveys, respectively. These decay param eters are restricted to be $0 < \lambda \leq 1 ;$ in this way, we can determine how long the effects last. For example, a decay parameter close to 1 indicates that the effects are minimally discounted over time. In contrast, if a decay parameter is close to 0, then only those events that have occurred recently can impact the current engagement behavior, and the effect decays quickly. Prior events are thereby weighted by their proximity to the current CE event.

## 5. Model and analysis

As our data are not generated by a laboratory experiment (where the confounding effects are carefully controlled) we need to use carefully designed individual-level statistical models in order to generate valid inferences [13]. In this section, we describe how our HMM identifies the CE behavior.

HMM and the latent class model share common features, as both are probability models [62]. In a probability model, observed customer behavior is a function of the latent individual state in which a customer currently belongs [7]. Therefore, these models classify individuals into a set of latent segments in which the probability of the manifestation of the observed dependent variable varies across different states. Such a probability model overcomes the potential problem whereby a model fails to include an important unobserved variable that might potentially have an impact on customer behavior. However, compared to the latent class model (where membership of the latent state is permanent and does not dynamically evolve) HMM assumes that the membership state is not only individual-specific, but also time varying. Here, the transition from one membership state to the other is affected by a number of time varying covariates. As such, while the traditional state-dependent model, which assumes future behavior as a function of lagged behavior, can be sensitive to temporal effects and underestimate those effects that contribute to the gradual evolution of a state transition, HMM accounts for both. Due to these benefits, an increasing number of marketing researchers have been applying HMM to building and testing theories designed to account for the data’s dynamic nature [7].

HMMs are attractive methods in CE research as they identify the underlving discrete attitudinal latent states of the CE and track its development [63]. Despite the growing popularity of HMMs in mar keting, they have rarely been used as an engagement model [7]. In thi study, we use an HMM to understand the evolution of a CE based on a CSS setting in which public C2C engagement is likely to occur after the private C2B engagement (See Fig. 2).

For each period, we observe two possible engagement behaviors, which form the dependent measures in our model, as follows: (1) the binary event of CSS participation; and (2) the binary event of posting an online review that follows after the CSS responses. Our main interest centers on explaining the CE following the prior accumulated relation ship experiences gained through the CSS system. In our HMM, we as sume that, while these two observed engagement behaviors are correlated through the latent engagement states, they are conditionally independent, given each latent engagement state [60].

Using the proposed HMM, we allow customers to transition between engagement states over time as a function of the time-varying covariates [62]. These variables, which drive the state-transition probabilities, are known to have a long-term impact. Before making any behavioral de cisions, customers gradually develop their engagement to a firm based on their prior relationship experiences with the hotel chain. Once an engagement state passes a certain threshold, the customer moves to another long-lasting engagement state with the firm; this, in turn, in fluences all subsequent engagement behaviors. The probability of the survey participation and online review posting would then depend on which engagement state a customer belongs to at a given time point. Therefore, the variables associated with those of the prior customer –brand relationship experiences (instead of the state-dependent behav iors) that have a long-term impact should be included as covariates driving the state-transition period [62].

Contrary to the covariates in the state-transition matrix, there are also short-term effects that have an immediate impact on the outcome variables, given the current engagement state. In our study, the cus tomer’s membership tier and the reported hotel satisfaction are assumed to have an immediate impact on survey participation and on online review posting, respectively. The rationale behind our decision is that these variables are transaction-specific customer characteristics; as such, they may temporarily influence the probabilities of the engagement behaviors that follow every hotel purchase, but not the decisions after wards. For example, a customer may engage with a firm if the current membership tier is high, but will be less likely do so after subsequent hotel stays if their membership suddenly lapses. That is, the loyalty program has only a transaction-specific effect, rather than a long-lasting one. Likewise, the reported satisfaction with the current hotel visit (i.e., transaction-specific satisfaction) only influences the online review posting motivation of the current time point; it does not influence posting motivations in the long term. Hence, in our model, the observed engagement behaviors are a function of both the time-varying long- and short-term covariates.

As the decision to participate in the survey precedes the decision to post an online review, we model the online review posting behavio conditional on the survey participation behavior, as suggested by [60].

State-dependent Behaviors (Short-term Effects). At every hotel visit $t ,$ we observe the customer $\textit { i } ^ { \prime } \textit { s }$ behavior in terms of whether they participated in the survey $( Y _ { i t } ^ { . s u r v e y } )$ and whether they posted an online review through the CSS system $( Y _ { i t } ^ { ~ r e v i e w } )$ . Let the probability of customer i participating in the survey after the hotel stay at time t, given the latent engagement state $s ,$ be $p _ { i t | s } \dot { s } ^ { u r v e y } = P ( Y _ { i t } \dot { s } ^ { u r v e y } = \tilde { 1 } | S _ { i t } = s )$ . This is modeled as a function of the propensity to participate in the survey that varies across different engagement states and membership tiers and in dividuals. The parameter $\widetilde { \beta } _ { 0 s } ^ { s u r v e y }$ represents the average propensity to participate in the survey across each engagement state s. Individuallevel heterogeneity that varies across customers is controlled by $\omega _ { i } ^ { s u r v e y }$ . Therefore, we model the survey participation probability as the following logit model,

$$
p _ {i t | s} ^ {\text { survey }} = \frac {e ^ {\widetilde {\beta} _ {0 s} ^ {\text { survey }} + \omega_ {i} ^ {\text { survey }} + \gamma_ {s} ^ {\text { survey }} \text { TIER } _ {i t}}}{1 + e ^ {\widetilde {\beta} _ {0 s} ^ {\text { survey }} + \omega_ {i} ^ {\text { survey }} + \gamma_ {s} ^ {\text { survey }} \text { TIER } _ {i t}}}.\tag{1}
$$

Likewise, the online review posting probability ${ p _ { i t | s } } ^ { r e \nu i e w } = { P ( Y _ { i t } } ^ { r e \nu i e w }$ $= 1 | S _ { i t } = s )$ is also assumed to be independent given the engagement state, s. However, as the online review posting decision is conditional on survey participation, the probability that a customer i will post an online review following a hotel visit $t ,$ given the engagement state $s ,$ can be modeled in the following manner,

$$
p _ {i t | s} ^ {\text { review }} = \left( \begin{array}{l l} \frac {e ^ {\widetilde {\beta} _ {0 s} ^ {\text { review }} + \omega_ {i} ^ {\text { review }} + \beta_ {s} ^ {\text { review }} \text { SAT} _ {i t}}}{1 + e ^ {\widetilde {\beta} _ {0 s} ^ {\text { review }} + \omega_ {i} ^ {\text { review }} + \beta_ {s} ^ {\text { review }} \text { SAT} _ {i t}}}, & \text { if } y _ {i t} ^ {\text { survey }} = 1, \\ 0, & \text { if } y _ {i t} ^ {\text { survey }} = 0, \end{array} \right)\tag{2}
$$

where $\widetilde { \beta } _ { 0 s } ^ { r e \nu i e w }$ is the average propensity to post an online review at the engagement state s and $\omega _ { i } ^ { \mathit { r e v i e w } }$ captures the unobserved individual-level heterogeneity just as the survey participation model in Eq. (1) does. The short-term effects of satisfaction on the customer’s online review posting behavior are captured in $\beta _ { s } ^ { r e v i e w }$ . The online review posting probability is only calculated when the customer decides to participates in the survey following the hotel visit t. Therefore, the probability becomes zero if the customer decides not to participate in the survey.

Engagement State Evolution (Long-term Effects). As discussed earlier, we assume that the customer’s propensity to participate in the survey and their online review posting are both affected by prior customer –manager interactions. That is, a shift from one engagement state to the other occurs when the customer’s utility for engagement changes significantly due to the prior customer–manager relationship experi ences gathered before making the current engagement decision. Spe cifically, we model the transition process between engagement states during the observed period as a Markov process, where the transition is only dependent upon the previous engagement state, but not upon the entire previous history of engagement-state transitions. The transition matrix for the hidden engagement states is as follows:

$$
Q _ {i, t - 1 \rightarrow t} = \left(\begin{array}{c c c}q _ {i t 1 1}&\dots&q _ {i t 1 S}\\\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\q _ {i t S 1}&\dots&q _ {i t S S}\end{array}\right)\tag{3}
$$

where $q _ { i t s s ^ { \prime } } = P ( S _ { i t } = s ^ { \prime } | S _ { i t - 1 } = s )$ is the conditional probability of an individual i moving from an engagement state s at time t − 1 to another engagement state s<sup>′</sup> at time t, and S is the number of latent engagement states, which we determine based on the model fit. We model the cus tomer’s propensity to move from one engagement state to the other

using a multinomial logit model,

$$
q _ {i s s ^ {\prime} t} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {e ^ {\mu_ {i s s ^ {\prime}} + x _ {i t} ^ {q} a _ {s s ^ {\prime}}}}{1 + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {S - 1} e ^ {\mu_ {i s j} + x _ {i t} ^ {q} a _ {s j}}}, & \text { for   } s \in \{1,..., S \} \text {   and   } s ^ {\prime} \in \{1,..., S - 1 \}, \\ \frac {1}{1 + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {S - 1} e ^ {\mu_ {i s j} + x _ {i t} ^ {q} a _ {s j}}}, & \text { for   } s \in \{1,..., S \} \text {   and   } s ^ {\prime} = S. \end{array} \right.\tag{4}
$$

where $s \in \{ 1 , . . . , S \}$ and $s ^ { \prime } \in \{ 2 , . . . , S \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 1 \}$ . The parameter $\mu _ { i s s ^ { \prime } }$ is the individual-specific propensity to transition from engagement state s to s<sup>′</sup>. The vector of parameters $\pmb { a } _ { s s ^ { \prime } }$ captures the effects of the time-varying covariates, $x _ { i t } { } ^ { q } ,$ which are the set of variables introduced in Table 1. Consequently, $\pmb { x } _ { i t } \pmb { q } _ { } \pmb { a } _ { s s ^ { \prime } }$ represents how prior customer–manager rela tionship experiences contribute to the increase, decrease, or stickiness of the engagement-state transition probability. It should be noted that our modeling approach assumes that these customer–manager relationships occur, and are observed, prior to the next survey request, which excludes the possibility of a reverse causality affecting our findings.

## 6. Results

Here, we describe results obtained by estimating the models described in the previous section. Similar to prior research applying the HMM in marketing [62], we adopt the Bayesian approach to the esti mation process due to its straightforward interpretation of the standard errors. Results are obtained using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, with three parallel chains run with 15,000 iterations each; we discarded 10,000 previous iterations as burn-in. We implemented the model using Stan [64] and confirmed that the model converged using [65]’s potential scale reduction factor (PSRF). To determine the number of latent states, we estimated the model by varying the number of hidden states from two to four, and determined the number of states recom mended by the model selection criteria. As both Watanabe–Akaike in formation criterion (WAIC) and leave-one-out cross validation (LOO) do not decrease further after two hidden states [66], we chose the more parsimonious specification with two states.

These results clearly imply that, when all hotels in the same brand adopt the strategy to reply to all CSS feedback, the brand can not only expect the already highly engaged customers to increase their engagement level, but that customers in a low-engagement state may become highly engaged (approximately 25.4%).

## 6.2. Impact of membership status and satisfaction on state-dependent probabilities

Are customers in a higher membership tier more likely to participate in the survey (Hypothesis 2)? The state-dependent parameter $\gamma ^ { s u r v e y }$ strongly supports our hypothesis for customers in a low-engagement state $( \gamma _ { 1 } ^ { ~ s u r v e y } ~ = ~ 1 . 1 5 3 )$ , but not when the customer is in a highengagement state. For example, in the low-engagement state, a customer with the highest tier of membership is 8.75% more likely to participate in a CSS than a customer who has not enrolled in the membership program. However, such an effect does not hold true when the customer is already in a highly engaged state. The result implies that the membership program is effective at encouraging non-engaged cus tomers to participate in the CSS, but not at making already-engaged customers participate to a greater extent.

Finally, we turn to the question of the impact of satisfaction $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ negative versus positive) upon the propensity to share opinions via online review platforms. As the parameter estimates of $\beta _ { 1 } = - 0 . 6 9 4$ and $\beta _ { 2 } = - 0 . 1 1 6$ indicate, we find strong support for Hypothesis 3: that negative satisfaction is more likely to be posted as an online review. For example, for an average customer in our data who is in a high engage ment state, the likelihood to post an online review of someone who provide feedback with SAT = 1 is 0.465% higher than the likelihood of someone who provide feedback with the highest satisfaction (i.e., SAT = 10). Considering the average review-posting rate across the customers in our data is only 0.38%, this result suggests that those who participate in the survey in order to complain are significantly more likely to post their voices on a public platform than are those who respond to the survey in order to compliment the hotel.

## 7. Discussion

Our research is among the first to empirically study CE behavior using a widely implemented CSS in the service industry. Unlike prior empirical research, which evaluates the performance of marketing campaigns based on transactional outcomes [67], our research expands upon prior studies of CE and investigates its drivers using actual consumer-panel data. Research into CE suggests that an appropriate CE management strategy should start by identifying the engagement behavior; for example, by considering a firm’s most- and least-engaged customers and the valence of the engagement [6]. However, empirical findings in this area are lacking. Thus, our examination of how to identify CE behaviors and the drivers that leverage these behaviors provides important insights in the marketing literature.

Applying HMM to actual panel data, we tested three hypotheses. First, we found that the number of managerial responses that customers receive can increase the probability of future survey participation and online review posting within the CSS context. Second, it became evident that a membership program has the effect of increasing the survey response rate, but only for those who are currently less engaged. Last, congruent to the findings in WOM literature [52], this study shows that the negative relationship between customer satisfaction and online re view posting behavior also holds true within the CSS context.

These findings have significant implications for firms that have an interactive feedback system whereby managers can respond to cus tomers individually and privately following their reception of consumer feedback. In contrast to traditional customer surveys, where the only purpose of conducting the survey is to understand customer perception at an aggregate level, the interactive features of CSS systems allow firms to create a personalized relationship with individual customers. Addi tionally, this system also allows more customers (who otherwise might not be included in the sample) to voice their opinions via online review communities. Making full use of this system allows firms to improve their own value by increasing the survey-participation rate and pro pensity to post an online review. Hence, our findings provide critical insight into customer voicing behavior that is useful for organizations that are seeking ways to improve CE.

First, our findings contribute to marketing practitioners’ knowledge by advancing their understanding of the effect of written managerial responses to customer feedback on customer engagement behaviors. A typical problem that managers suffer from is how to decide whether responding to customers is worth their investment. This study suggests that the crucial role of this managerial effort as a key driver of CE value is not negligible; therefore, it should be positively considered by deci sion makers. Hence, if increasing CE is one of a firm’s goals, managers should develop a system that allows them to respond quickly to those customers who provide feedback. This may include setting up an alert for incoming customer feedback, or hiring employees whose re sponsibility it is to respond to customers. Interestingly, it is not only the responses by managers from the hotel in question, but also those from other hotels where the customer has participated in a CSS, that have an impact on CE with the focal hotel. This result implies that, when responding to survey participants, an integrated brand-level approach (rather than a response at a single-property level) is required in order to maximize CE.

Second, this study allows managers to reevaluate the engagement value of their loyalty membership program. Loyalty membership pro grams have long been discussed as drivers of customer loyalty that can be measured by financial outcomes [68]. As such, repeat purchase behavior and share of wallet have been suggested as metrics that could be used to evaluate the performance of a loyalty program [69]. Several studies that are suspicious of the benefits of a loyalty program [70] contend that, taking the cost of operating the program into account, the profitability of the loyalty program could be smaller than is generally suggested. However, based on the results of our research, deciding to abolish or reduce investment in a loyalty program based solely on its financial impact is a short-sighted approach to CE. Considering that the survey participation rates increase considerably when customers enter a higher membership tier, managers can expect not only a higher proba bility that customers will engage in online review posting, but also in the more enthusiastic co-creation of feedback that is in fact initiated by these loyal customers [27]. Therefore, there may be more incentives than previously expected for managers to keep the membership program and promote the lower tier members toward upper tier status.

Finally, our findings support the famous business adage “a happy customer tells a friend, an unhappy customer tells the world,” which has been widely documented in marketing textbooks [71]. Our results reveal that customers who complain via CSS are more likely to post their voices as online reviews; therefore, this research has important implications for social media managers who monitor the online reviews posted by a hotel’s guests. In order to reduce the probability of negative reviews being posted, service providers should put greater effort into carefully listening to their customers’ complaints before negative views are exposed on public platforms.

A result unrelated to the core purpose of this paper is also worth mentioning. The long-term effects shown in Table 5 suggest that increasing the customer’s (reported past) satisfaction is critical to leveraging engagement. The significantly positive estimates of the pa rameters $a _ { h i g h  h i g h } { } ^ { 5 } , \ a _ { l o w  h i g h } { } ^ { 6 } ,$ and $a _ { h i g h  h i g h } \ l ^ { 6 }$ suggest that a customer who is highly engaged is likely to further increase their engagement level when the level of prior cumulative satisfaction is high. The lower the cumulative satisfaction, the lesser the likelihood is that a customer will stay at a high engagement state and, therefore, the greater the likelihood that they will transfer to a low engagement state (see the negative and statistically significant estimates of the parameters $a _ { l o w  h i g h } { } ^ { 3 } , a _ { h i g h  h i g h } { } ^ { 3 } ,$ and $a _ { l o w  h i g h } ^ { ~ 4 } )$ . This result is congruent with prior research that shows the positive impact of customers’ cumulative satisfaction on customer–firm relationship duration [72]. While many firms have recently started to implement engagement-initiative pro grams, they often ignore the fundamental truth: customers need to be satisfied with the offered value in order to be engaged with the firm.

Several limitations of our study call for further research. First, due to the sample selection process, our analysis may not be generalizable to customers who have a limited relationship with the hotel chain. It may be useful to compare our results with data taken from newly acquired customers or customers who post reviews online without taking the survey. Second, our data are from a single hotel chain. While said data are sufficient to generalize, and the hotel brand used is one of the most popular chains in the world, customer behavior with respect to other hotels, which may have different quality standards or reputations, might vary. Third, analyzing the textual contents of written feedback and of managerial responses may reveal interesting findings. For instance, customers may change their engagement behaviors based on the politeness of the managerial response. It will also be interesting to see whether responding to a specific topic raised in customer feedback in creases or decreases engagement behavior. Lastly, there are many other common marketing practices, besides the variables studied in this research, that have potential engagement value. For example, sales promotion is a typical tool, the performance of which is evaluated based on purchase-based outcomes. A better understanding of its engagement value, however, might allow decision makers to improve their market ing strategies. We hope our study will inspire further research in areas where traditional business tactics are used that have the potential of creating CE value.
