## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online social game Freemium model Premium features Social networks Social influence

## A B S T R A C T

An increasingly popular trend in digital businesses is the adoption of the freemium model, in which premium features are provided along with free features. Because this model separates use (spending time) from purchase decisions (spending money) and people treat spending time and spending money differently, it derives two distinct types of social influence: social influence from peer spending time and peer spending money. This multiplex social influence, however, has not received sufficient attention. We investigated a popular online social game and studied how multiplex social influence jointly affects players’ spending time and spending money in a digital freemium environment. We uncovered spending time has a more substantial effect on players’ spending money than does spending money. The dependence between two different types of social influence provides evidence of multiplex social influence and its asymmetricity. We further tested and discovered that personal characteristics and network measures moderates multiplex social influence. Because the freemium model pro liferates in many online environments. the current study enlightens the value of free users' social influence in the operations of businesses with freemium models.

## 1. Introduction

The digital freemium model is a business model wherein a firm’s product or service has free features that provide basic functionalities and premium features with an additional fee [1]. The freemium model has been widely utilized in the Internet context, including software, mobile apps, digital games, e-newspaper, and web services, where digital products and services are free to download but typically include offers to pay for ad-free, richer features, additional contents or services, like in app purchase available in IOS or Android markets [2,3]. The common component of these businesses is consumers’ ability to continually use a free product or service and choose to purchase additional features or benefits that complement free usage repeatedly. The industry believes the freemium model is the future of online service providers [4]. For example, in the digital gaming industry, in-game spending, which pri marily consists of gamers buying digital collectibles like character skins, loot boxes, and other virtual gear, accounted for 80% of the total digital game revenue of \$120 billion in 2019.<sup>1</sup>

Despite its popularity and potential benefits, freemium has inherent challenges, not the least of which is the number of customers who convert to buying the digital product’s premium features [5]. Academic and practical literature has found that freemium firms commonly have moderate customer conversion rates between 1% and 10%, averaging around 2% to 4% [6]. Specifically, many SaaS and Web app firms claim a 3% conversion rate [7]. In online product and service markets, while there are a few outliers like Slack and Spotify claiming a higher con version rate than 20%, a good conversion rate is recognized as 4% as Dropbox and Evernote has achieved, and the typical rates are more like 1% [8]. Kumar further argues that a very high conversion rate isn’t necessarily good in the freemium model and one major benefit of a freemium model is to generate traffic: “the best long-term strategy is generally to aim for a moderate conversion rate coupled with a high volume of traffic (in my research, I’ve found that most companies range from 2% to 5%)” [1].

Therefore, encouraging users to pay is critical for the success of on line businesses with freemium models. Service providers such as GitHub,

Canvas, and Slack have adopted the freemium business model because it allows price-sensitive users to obtain use experiences via free trial of the services. Those free trial experiences create positive word-of-mouth, leading to more users who may later convert to pay users as their needs become commercial [9]. This phenomenon indicates that a promising strategy for converting free into paying users and increasing revenue is to leverage the power of social interactions between users and their friends. It also indicates the existence of multiplex social influence, which means that peers’ usage may influence the focal user’s usage and the focal user’s payment behavior, and vice versa.

Many studies have documented the effects of social influence on individual decisions in different social contexts [10–12]. However, even in studies modeling heterogeneity of actors [11,13] an underlying assumption is that only “same-type” social influence exists between a pair of actors: peers’ purchase influencing focal actor’s purchase, peers financial investment influencing focal actors’ investment, or peers’ IT adoption influencing focal actors’ IT adoption [12,14]. Consequently, the multiplexity of social influence has been largely ignored, resulting in limited knowledge to date about whether or how this aspect of social influence may work. The multiplexity of social influence refers to the extent to which two actors are bound to each other in different social interactions, and peers’ behavior of different types may influence focal actors’ behavior.

In the freemium business model of online games, playing and paying are two different behaviors because people treat the spending of money and time differently. Okada and Hoch [15] discovered that time spending is associated with higher risk, higher return decisions, while money spending is more risk-averse because of the ambiguity in the value of time. Okada [16] further tested and proved that people spend more time than money on hedonic products. In contrast, moneyspending is usually risk-averse and follows the principle of utility maximization (i.e., utilitarian consumption) [17]. Furthermore, there is evidence that money spending in online multiplayer games is conspic uous consumption that can signal people’s distinctive social status to obtain social capital [18]. Therefore, the items acquired by money spending should have the function to make the players unique in specific perspectives (e.g., be more powerful, have more capability, or different appearance) compared with players who do not spend money. Hence, players’ paying and playing in online games convey their hedonic or utilitarian attitude toward the game. Further, they exert multiplex social influences on their friends: the social influences of peer spending time and peer spending money, respectively.

It is still unclear how the focal users’ behavior of money spending and time spending can be influenced by peers’ money spending and time spending behaviors. Park et al. [19] only examined the influence of the same type of consumption on focal player’s behavior, i.e., peer players hedonic (utilitarian) consumption on focal player’s hedonic (utilitarian) consumption. Still, they didn’t examine the interaction between the two types of consumption, and those two consumptions are all made by ingame currencies. Ulku et al. [20] studied how the amount of time spent on queuing influences the amount of money spent on subsequent purchasing. However, they did not examine how money spending in fluences time spending due to the research context.

This study aims to fill this research gap to study multiplex socia influence by leveraging rich archival data from the online gaming in dustry. More specifically, we aim to investigate not only the social in fluence from peer’s same type of consumption (i.e., influence of peer’s money (time) spending on focal player’s money (time) spending) but also the social influence from peer players’ different type of consump tion (i.e., influence of peer’s money (time) spending on focal player’s time (money) spending). In addition, we investigate the heterogeneity of multiplex social influence contingent on players’ characteristics and network position. Although there are other possible social influences, the multiplex social influence we study is critical for the freemium business model and, at the same time, general across all applications of the freemium business model.

This study makes several theoretical contributions. First, it verifies a plausible mechanism for the multiplex social influence of peers spending of time and spending of money in the freemium environments. Specifically, we find empirical evidence that social influence by peer spending of time $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , use of free features) tends to be considerably stronger than that of peer spending of money (i.e., purchase of premium features), suggesting social influence from hedonic consumption is stronger than that from utilitarian consumption. Meanwhile, we find that peer spending of time influences both focal player’s time spending and money spending but not vice versa, suggesting hedonic motivation is the main driving force and free users indirectly contribute profits to the service providers. These findings help to answer the fundamental question of why service providers are willing to maintain a significant proportion of free users in their online businesses.

Second, multiplex social influences of time spending and money spending are heterogeneous. For example, our findings revealed that time spending decisions are also influenced by peers’ money spending decisions for high-pay players, suggesting that high-pay players are more utilitarian-driven. Conversely, for low-pay players, peers’ money spending decisions do not affect them, implying that they are purely hedonic-driven. Moreover, our results suggest that, for money spending decisions, only high-play users are positively affected by social influ ence, while low-play users are not affected. Based on these findings, we discuss notable implications concerning how providers promote the consumption of both free and premium features in online freemiumbased platforms through social networks.

Finally, we propose a tractable empirical model that overcomes one of the major modeling difficulties—the endogeneity of social influence, which is common in research on social influence with observational data. This study provides an approach to identify multiplex social effects in observational data compared to experimental data [21]. We studied users’ repeated but infrequent consumption decisions, a phenomenon more complicated than the one-time binary “pay-or-not-pay” decision in social influence studies [13,22]. From a managerial perspective, analyzing repeated consumption decisions provides more meaningful guidance for managing consumer lifetime value.

## 2. Theory development

## 2.1. Multiplexity of social influence

Actors in a social network may interact with each other in different types of relationships. In many online networks, such as Linkedin, users can form explicit friendships due to business and knowledge relations and exchange content and communicate with each other [23]. Cheung et al. [24] discovered that consumers’ purchase intention is influenced by two types of social information cues: peer consumer review and peer consumer purchase. Di Stefano et al. [25] suggested that people who share connections in different relationships cooperate more quickly in a social dilemma. Yang and Wang [26] discovered various social agents, peers, parents, and social industries have different influences on peo ple’s privacy attitudes. Xiao and Xue [27] found that peer investors influence is more substantial than fundraisers’ marketing efforts.

Using a similar context, Park et al. [19] investigated the influence of the same type of peers’ consumption on focal player’s behavior, i.e., peers’ hedonic (utilitarian) consumption on focal player’s hedonic (utilitarian) consumption. Therefore, following the findings of those studies, we believe that in online social games adopting the freemium model, peer time spending should positively impact the focal player’s time spending behavior. Similarly, peer money spending should have a positive impact on the focal player’s money spending behavior. How ever, despite the importance of digital environments, limited literature examines the effect of social influence on both users’ spending money and time.

Since different relationships or interactions can influence people’s behaviors, scholars have explored general social patterns in multiplex social influence, including trust, friends, voting, and reciprocity. They have found that users who interact in different dimensions tend to in fluence each other. For instance, scholars have found that negative so cial interactions (e.g., hostility, armed aggression, and punishment) differ from positive interactions (e.g., friendship, communication, and trading) with lower reciprocity, weaker clustering, and fatter-tail degree distribution in an online game context [28]. A multiplex social network analysis of an online health community by Zhao et al. [29] demonstrated that relations triggered by blogs, message boards, and private messages lead to subsequent social ties of other types. The findings imply that multiplex social influence may jointly affect user behaviors such as use [30].

People tend to treat the spending of money and time differently [15]. Okada [16] found that consumers prefer hedonic products more than utilitarian products when deciding spending time. On the other hand, Liu and Aaker [17] found that people switch to a utilitarian mindset when being asked to donate money instead of time. Hence, peers spending of time and spending of money reflect their different attitudes toward game playing and therefore exert two different types of social influences. Considering the potential that word-of-mouth may attract new users of digital services and users of free services may later convert to users who pay, cross-type multiplex social influence may exist. First, peers time spending may influence the focal user’s money spending because it may signal the game’s hedonic quality and deliver their attitude that they play the game for hedonic purposes [16]. Under such influences, a focal player may also decide only to play for hedonic purposes, spending more time but not money. Second, since social in teractions intensify competition [31] when connected peers spend more time playing the game, it is possible to create competition among players. Therefore, it is harder for the focal player to signal his/her social status by only spending more time, thus turning to spending money to buy conspicuous items [18]. We believe that either mechanism matters depending on the characteristics of players because social influence is believed to be heterogeneous [24,32,33]. The second mechanism may take effect for some intensive players, and peers’ time spending in creases their money spending.

Furthermore, the cross-type multiplex social influence may be asymmetric, or in other words, peers’ money spending does not influ ence focal’s time spending in general. In contrast to time, money is a relatively more limited resource that not every player has a budget. However, the effect of peers’ payment may also depend on the charac teristics of players. Ulku et al. [20] found that customers are inclined to make time spending more worthwhile by compensating the cost of time via money spending because time is already sunk when consumption decisions are made. Our context is different in that the cost of money spending is already sunk, and to amortize it, the players have to play more to realize the value of money spending to show social status. Therefore, for players who have the budget to spend money in the game, their time spending may be influenced by peers’ money spending behavior because without spending more time in the game, it is difficult to deliver signals of their status obtained from conspicuous items. On the other hand, when peers show strong signals of their social status, a focal player with a low budget of money may reduce their spending of time because of social pressure [34].

Moreover, players’ position in the network is an important factor moderating the effect of multiplex social influences. For example, the contagion of behavior is faster in networks in which individuals receive influences from multiple peers, and the contagion is more effective in clustered networks [35]. Therefore social influence is associated with network features such as network cohesion, tie strength, and clustering [36].

## 2.2. Identification of social influence

The primary challenge in verifying social influence is to disengage social influence from confounding factors, such as homophily, corre lated unobservables, and simultaneity [37]. Homophily is the tendency that “contact between similar people occurs at a higher rate than among dissimilar people” [38]. For example, in a massively multiplayer online role-playing game (MMORPG), players tend to play with those who have similar age and similar level of game experience [39]. Therefore, con nected people tend to exhibit similar behaviors because of similar per sonal characteristics, often unobservable to researchers. Distinguishing homophily from social influence is important because the two effects are associated with different managerial strategies for network-triggered interventions [40]. In addition, correlated unobservables and simulta neity, which occur if we regress individual behavior on average char acteristics and behaviors of their peers, could also result in endogeneity concerns.

Social influence studies have reported various statistical and econometrical techniques to resolve the identification challenges in so cial influence models. Aral and Walker [41] and Bapna and Umyarov [13] have exploited randomized field experiments to distinguish social influence from confounding factors such as homophily. Aral et al. [40] and Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [42] have applied propensity score matching to quantify social influence, which could be profoundly overestimated without taking homophily into account. In addition, a fixed-effect technique [43] is the ideal solution to control for individual time-invariant unobserved factors that may lead to inconsistent esti mates when panel data are available. Finally, the instrumental variables technique can be applied to identify social influence based on some assumptions [44,45]. Since no ideal model exists, one should carefully choose methods depending on research contexts and goals.

## 3. Research context and research questions

Our research context is a large-scale commercial online role-playing social game operated by a large game operator in Asia. The corporate partner graciously provided us with all the detailed records of the anonymous individual player in their databank. However, the game’s name is made anonymous due to the confidential requirement of the game company. The game was released in July 2010 and closed in

January 2018. By the end of 2011, it had 80 million registered players and peak concurrent users of more than 0.7 million. The raw data contain complete records of user behavior from January to March of 2011. Specifically, potential players must register accounts to start gaming. After logging into the game, players play a role as a character (avatar) from various hero classes (e.g., warriors, archers) to perform pre-given tasks, such as slaying monsters, exploring new territories, and finding treasures to earn reward points. By accumulating points, player character levels advance from 1 to 40. After a player completes a task, the system evaluates the performance and delivers reward points or virtual goods to the player.

Apart from game tasks, players can engage in social behaviors. Notably, the game implemented a social network–based friendship system, by which players can send friend requests to others within the game environment. After approval, they can communicate and collab orate to perform more challenging tasks, such as fighting monsters. Through the friend system, players can observe their friends’ actions more efficiently when they are connected. This feature makes the game more social than traditional video games. Moreover, players use equipment such as weapons and armor to make their avatars stronger (e. g., health points, strength, agility, and intelligence) to accomplish tasks more efficiently. With the freemium business model, the social game is free-to-play, but players have the option of spending real money to obtain premium equipment such as rare swords and shields.

To sum up, in this freemium social game, players can play the game free of charge but are charged for premium features (in the form of purchases), providing extra functions of the game. Playing the game for free represents players’ behaviors of time spending, and pay for free mium charges represents players’ behaviors of money spending. People regard spending money and spending time differently. Therefore, peers money spending and time spending translates into two types of in fluences. Social influence, driven by observed interactions between peers, provides a possible direction for encouraging in-game consump tion. In this study, we focused on two types of consumption behav iors—the consumption of free features, measured in playing time, and the use of premium features, measured in payment. To achieve this goal, we proposed an empirical model to answer the following research questions: whether the social influence from peer’s same type of con sumption and the social influence from peer’s different type of con sumption exist. In addition, we also investigate the heterogeneity of multiplex social influence contingent on players’ characteristics and their different network position.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Raw data and network construction

We employed a panel of data consisting of granular records of players’ activities appearing in the game within a time window of the first 12 weeks of 2011. We excluded players who had never logged in to the game during the time window since they are irrelevant to the focus of this study. Overall, we got 597,657 active players for this analysis. The attributes in the raw data are measured at a daily level, while we believe weekly panel data are preferred since it gets rid of the day-of-theweek effect and sparse daily actions.

To depict social dynamics in the game community, we constructed a friendship network for each period based on the dyadic friend connec tions extracted at the beginning of each week. Since social connections are symmetrical in this context, we defined the friendship network as an undirected network. Based on the friendship network, we applied two widely used network measures—degree centrality (i.e., number of friends) and clustering coefficient (i.e., proportion of connected friends among any pairs of friends).

## 4.2. Main variables and summary statistics

## 4.2.1. Dependent variables

We observed two types of behaviors of game players— $P l a y T i m e _ { i t }$ (in minutes) reflects how long player i spent on the free-to-play online social game at time t; Payment (in Chinese currency, RMB) reflects how much money the player i spent to buy virtual currency at time t. To better describe the modeling issues without losing their general relevance, we proposed playing time as a measure of consumption of free features and payment as a measure of consumption of premium features of the online social game.

## 4.2.2. Main Independent variables

To capture multiplex social influence, we measured peer usage and purchase as the log-transformed average of consumption levels of the focal players’ immediate friends in the social network (LogFrdPlayTime and LogFrdPayment ).

## 4.2.3. Control variables

We constructed a set of individual- and group-level control variables that may influence the focal players’ consumption. First, we constructed an undirected friendship network and then calculated degree centrality and clustering coefficient. Specifically, degree centrality (Degree ) denotes the number of friends of player i at time t, which captures the player’s local network centrality, which is a standard measure of individuals structural social capital [46]. The second local network measure we adopted is the clustering coefficient (Cluster ), which denotes the pro portion of connected friends among any pairs of friends of player i at time t. The clustering coefficient measures the extent to which a set of individuals are interconnected [47]. In addition, since a single player can control multiple avatars with different character levels, we created character variables such as number of characters (NumCharacter ) and average character level (AvgLevel ) as control variables. Moreover, in this game community, players can join groups to participate in group-based competitions occasionally organized by game operators. To control for the effect of groups, we create character-group variables, namely number of characters joining groups (NumGroup ) and number of characters serving as leaders in groups (NumLeader ). In addition, we used player tenure (Tenure ) as a control variable for their experience in the game. Finally, based on the friendship network, we further constructed average peer characteristics,<sup>2</sup> including NumCharacter(Frd) , AvgLevel(Frd) , NumGroup(Frd) , and NumLeader(Frd) , as control variables.

Due to high skewness, we take a log transformation for most of the continuous variables. We present descriptions and summary statistics of the main variables in Table 1.

## 4.3. Modeling specification: fixed effects type II Tobit model

In this study, we faced two modeling issues: the nature of players decision problem—infrequent consumption, and the other one is from the general identification challenges in social effect models—endogeneity. Regarding infrequent consumption, players are likely not to spend any time or money on the game in the period, making consumption decisions “infrequent.” For a linear regression model, a high proportion of excessive zero-valued observations may cause considerable estimation bias. We, therefore, applied the type II Tobit model empirical framework to deal with infrequent consumption decisions, which we will describe in detail below.

In contrast, endogeneity is a common empirical issue in social influ ence models, which are mainly caused by confounding factors, such as homophily, correlated unobservables, and simultaneity (see [37] as a review). All these confounders can lead to endogeneity bias if they are not taken into consideration in the empirical model. We mitigated endogeneity caused by homophily and time-invariant correlated un observables with the fixed effects technique. We further applied the instrumental variable method to resolve time-varying correlated un observables and simultaneity.

We proposed a fixed-effects type II Tobit model with instrumental variables technique to address the above modeling issues. Specifically, in the panel data model, each observation unit is an individual-week pair. Let y denote the dependent variable, the log-transformed con sumption levels of the focal players (LogPlayTime or LogPayment ). We estimated the models for two dependent variables separately. We pre sented a fixed-effects type II Tobit Model for observed outcomes d and y as follows.

$$

$$
(v _ {i t}, \nu_ {i t}) \sim \text { Bivariate   Normal } \left(\binom{0}{0}, \left( \begin{array}{c c} 1 & \rho \sigma \\ \rho \sigma & \sigma^ {2} \end{array} \right)\right)\tag{2}
$$

$$
\mathrm{d} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} 1 \text {   if   } \mathrm{d} _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {*} \geq 0 \\ 0 \text {   if   } \mathrm{d} _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {*} <   0 \end{array} , \mathrm{y} _ {\mathrm{it}} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} 0 \text {   if   } \mathrm{d} _ {\mathrm{it}} = 0 \\ \mathrm{y} _ {\mathrm{it}} ^ {*} \text {   if   } \mathrm{d} _ {\mathrm{it}} = 1 \end{array} \right. \right.\tag{3}

The first equation on d (i.e., participation equation) in expression (3) represents a binary model for participation decision. The second equation on $y _ { i t } ( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , intensity equation) is a linear regression model for consumption intensity for positively valued observations. We defined $d _ { i t }$ as whether player i participates in the game by at least 1 min or pur chases virtual currency by at least 1 RMB at time $t ,$ as outcomes of the participation decision. The latent variable $d ^ { * }$ denotes the unobserved utility from participation, while the latent variable $y ^ { * }$ denotes the utility from consumption, which is only observed when player i participates. The observed outcome variable $y$ is equal to $y ^ { * }$ when $d ^ { * }$ is positive and zero otherwise. Importantly, we included player fixed effects $( \delta _ { i }$ and α ) in both equations, to control for time-invariant unobserved heteroge neity. The model allows the error terms of the two equations to be correlated, thus capturing the estimation bias caused by excessive zerovalued observations. We specified the covariate matrices $z \gamma$ and $\pmb { X } \beta$ as follows.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathbf {Z} _ {\mathrm{it}} \gamma & = \gamma_ {1} \text {Characteristics} _ {\mathrm{it}} + \gamma_ {2} \text {NetworkMeasures} _ {\mathrm{it}} + \gamma_ {3} \text {PeerCharacteristics} _ {\mathrm{it}} \\ & + \gamma_ {4} \text {Tenure} _ {\mathrm{it}} \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathbf {X} _ {\mathrm{it}} \beta & = \beta_ {1} \text { LogFrdPlayTime } _ {\mathrm{it-1}} + \beta_ {2} \text { LogFrdPayment } _ {\mathrm{it-1}} + \beta_ {3} \text { NetworkMeasures } _ {\mathrm{it}} \\ & + \beta_ {4} \text { PeerCharacteristics } _ {\mathrm{it}} + \beta_ {5} \text { Tenure } _ {\mathrm{it}} \end{array} \tag {5}
$$

The participation equation contains a set of covariates, including the player’s observed own characteristics (including character variables and character-group variables), player tenure, network measures (including degree centrality and clustering coefficient), and average peer charac teristics. Character variables and character-group variables describe the players’ level of experience and plausibly relate to their commitment to the social game. We also proposed that the players’ participation deci sion would be associated with degree centrality and clustering coeffi cient, as players with different local network structures would have different participation propensities. Finally, we have also included average peer characteristics to control avatar difference and player tenure (in a quadratic form) to control for unobserved temporal variations.

Regarding the intensity equation, we included lagged log-transformed peer usage and purchase, namely LogFrdPlayTime <sub>−</sub> and $\begin{array} { r } { L o g F r d P a y m e n t _ { i t - 1 } \ : ( \mathrm { i . e . , } } \end{array}$ average playing time or payment of friends), as the main independent variables to capture the consumption of friends in the previous period. They are endogenous variables due to the nature of social effect models. We applied time lagging for the peer consumption variables to mitigate endogeneity caused by the reflection problem [48] since lagged peer consumption should not be affected by the focal player’s current period consumption. More importantly, time-lagging ensures that the estimated social influence is not driven by the network effect that players receive more utility by merely playing together rather than observational learning. It is reasonable for us to assume in the context that transmission of social effects exhibits a oneperiod lag as weekly consumption levels of friends will not be realized by the focal players until the end of the period. Notably, we assume that friends’ consumption affects the focal players only in their intensity decisions.<sup>3</sup> Furthermore, we included network measures, average peer characteristics, and player tenure as control variables.

## 4.4. Instrumentation for play and pay

The estimated peer effect may suffer from endogeneity if timevarying unobserved factors affect the connected players’ usage and purchase simultaneously. Therefore, we further applied a specific instrumental variable technique suitable for social network data. For example, Bramoull´e et al. [44] proposed an instrumentation strategy for peer effect utilizing average characteristics of friends of friends who are not immediate friends (the so-called “second-hop” friends) of the focal individuals as instruments. Similarly, de Matos et al. [45] adopted the average behaviors of second-hop friends of the focal individuals as instrumental variables.

The endogenous variables in the model are peer consumption vari ables (LogFrdPlayTime and LogFrdPayment ), defined as average consumption of the focal players’ direct (i.e., first-hop) friends. Our instrumentation strategy exploits social connections that provide vari ations in average characteristics or behaviors of second-hop friends that do not directly affect decision outcomes. Specifically, we utilize in struments LogFFPlayTime<sub>it− 1</sub> and LogFFPayment<sub>it− 1</sub>, which measures lagged consumption of the second-hop friends of the focal player. As second-hop players do not have social connections with the focal players, they will not directly influence the current period consumption decisions of the focal players (i.e., exogeneity assumption). Regarding the relevance assumption, second-hop players connect with first-hop friends of the focal players, so their behaviors are correlated. As a result, the proposed instrumental variables should have considerable predictive power on the endogenous variables $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ relevance assump tion). We ruled out the existence of the “weak instrument” issue by conducting F-test for the goodness-of-fit of first-stage regressions based on the Sanderson-Windmeijer F-statistic [49]. In Table 2, we present descriptions and summary statistics of the instrumental variables.<sup>4</sup>

## 4.5. Estimation strategy

The model of intensity decision suffers from selection bias if one does not correctly account for the participation decision simultaneously. Hence, the estimation strategy of the type II Tobit model follows a similar logic of estimation of the sample selection model [50]. However, commonly used sample selection models are based on cross-sectional data and do not apply to the panel data model with fixed effects. Therefore, we referred to the recent econometrics literature on esti mating panel data models with selection [51]. Briefly, we adopted a twostep estimation procedure proposed by [52] and extended the model by instrumenting for peer consumption variables. The estimation relies only on a flexible distributional assumption and has various advantages over other methods. Briefly, in the first step, γ parameters in the participation equation are estimated in a fixed-effects logit model. To obtain β parameters, in the second step, we regressed the firstdifferenced intensity equation between within-individual pairs of pe riods with “identical” propensities of participation, using weighted least squares (WLS) estimation. The results of a series of Hausman tests sug gest that the null hypothesis that the endogenous regressors can be treated as exogenous is rejected in all the specifications. For comparison purposes, we also conducted the weighted least squares (WLS) estima tion for the same type II Tobit model but did not include instrumental variables. For more details about the estimation procedure, please refer to [52].

## 5.2. Effect of multiplex social influences on purchase

Table 4 presents the results of the effect of social influence on focal plavers' payment. Importantly. we identified evidence of the effect of multiplex social influence on the focal player’s payment decision. With instrumentation, friends’ playing time $( c o e f . = 0 . 1 4 9 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ has a significantly positive effect on the focal player’s payment, while friends payment $( c o e f . = 0 . 0 6 0 5 , p = 0 . 0 3 7 )$ has a much smaller and less sig nificant positive effect on the focal players’ payment. Hypothesis 2a and hypothesis 3a are both supported. This result indicated that game op erators might effectively increase the payment of the focal player by encouraging their friends to spend more time on the game because players can more easily observe the playing behavior of peers from the completion of tasks or the intensity of social interactions in the game community than their less-transparent paying behavior. For instance, players can infer peers’ playing time by simply viewing the status of their avatars, whereas they can gauge friends’ payment only by checking the value of their virtual equipment. Therefore, with the freemium model, free players’ consumption indirectly provides revenue to the operator of the online social games. Moreover, the cross-type social in fluence is even more significant than that from the same type of social influence from peer payment.

In addition, the control variables revealed valuable associations. Players with high average character levels or with peers with high average character levels are more likely to pay for premium features (“First” column), meaning that high-level players or players with highlevel friends tend to be paying customers. Players who join or serve as leaders in groups or have friends doing so are less likely to make a payment, probably because group membership provides benefits that could substitute premium features. However, the coefficients in the first equation are interpreted as within-individual effects, and we did not make any theoretical predictions regarding effect directions.

## 5.3. Does social influence affect different types of players differently?

The consumption levels of heterogeneous users in the freemium model may be differently affected by social influence because prior studies have suggested that some individuals are more susceptible to social contagion than others [22]. To check for heterogeneity in players vulnerability to multiplex social influence, we explored the role of rev enue leaders who provide a large fraction of playing time or payment to the game operator [54]. Specifically, for the regression analysis on playing time, we constructed three groups of players: high-pay Players consists of players who have paid at least 93.45 RMB, which is the median value among players who have paid at least 1 RMB; low-pay Players consists of players who have paid at least 1 RMB but less than 93.45 RMB; No-pay players consists of players who have paid nothing<sup>6</sup>. In addition, for the regression analysis on payment, we classified players who have played more than 21.87 min, which is the median value, as the high-play players and the remaining as the low-play players<sup>7</sup>. ## 5.4. What’s the role of network measures?

Previous literature on social influence and contagion has revealed that individuals with high local network connectivity are more likely to be influenced by connected individuals because of insensitive commu nication and interactions [47]. Specifically, degree centrality, measured by the number of friends, reflects the number of links that carry social interactions and should be positively associated with social influence from friends; clustering coefficient represents connectivity among in dividuals via multiple redundant paths. Thus it also leads to a more significant social influence from friends. To verify the moderating effects of degree centrality and clustering coefficient, we re-ran the second stage estimation with interactions between the peer consumption vari ables and network measures included in the model, as shown in Table 6.

We first focus on the moderating role of degree centrality. For the playing time decision, degree centrality positively moderates the impact of friends’ playing time. However, it negatively moderates the effect of friends’ payment, although the overall impact of friends’ payment is insignificant (in Section 5.1). For the payment decision, degree cen trality positively moderates the effect of friends’ playing time. The result indicates that focal players with more friends are more likely to be positively influenced by friends’ playing behavior when making both decisions. For the moderating role of clustering coefficient, we find that it also positively moderates the impact of friends’ playing time on the focal player’s playing time, meaning players with high local network connectivity are more likely to be positively influenced. While clustering coefficient does not significantly moderate the impact of social influence on the payment decision of the focal player. In summary, hypotheses 5a and 5b are supported.

## 5.5. Robustness checks

## 5.5.1. Additional instrumental variable specifications

The validity of our instrumental variables relied on the exogenous assumption that second-hop friends’ lagged behaviors would not be correlated with current period errors in the regression model. In this context, the fact that the focal players cannot observe their second-hop friends’ characteristics provides additional excluded instruments for identifying social effects [44]. Therefore, we constructed the average characteristics of the second-hop friends of the focal players as an addi tional set of instrumental variables, namely NumCharacte $( F F ) _ { i t - 1 } ,$ $A \nu g L e \nu e l ( F F ) _ { i t - 1 }$ , NumGroup(FF) <sub>−</sub> , and NumLeader $( F F ) _ { i t - 1 }$ . The de scriptions and summary statistics of these variables are presented in the final four rows of Table 2. Weighted-IV estimates with the full set of instruments for the intensity equation are presented in Column $^ { * * } \mathrm { I V } 2 ^ { * }$ of Tables 3 and $^ { 4 , }$ revealing that the estimates for social influences are consistent but have greater effect sizes than IV estimates based on the original set of instruments.

In addition to the peer consumption variables, average peer charac teristics variables in the intensity equation may be endogenous. If endogenous network formation occurs, the focal players’ social con nections and the average characteristics of their peers are not randomly generated. Therefore, we specified average peer characteristics as additional endogeneity variables and conducted the IV estimation with the full set of instruments to address this. The results are presented in Column $\ " \mathrm { I V } 3 \ "$ of Tables 3 and 4. The estimated social effects exhibit trends consistent with those in the main analysis.

## 5.5.2. Testing the merit of type II Tobit model

One of the advantages of the type II Tobit model over the linear regression model is its ability to overcome the infrequent consumption issue. However, its major drawback is model complexity. If no estima tion bias due to excessive zero-valued observations is present in this context, simple linear models could be a proper alternative to the Tobit model. Therefore, to show the existence of such estimation bias, we conducted a fixed-effect linear model using nonzero observations. We further included time dummies in the linear model to control for any time trend and seasonality. According to the $\mathrm { r e s u l t } , ^ { 8 }$ both average playing time and payment of friends encourage the playing time of the focal players. Furthermore, social influence has no significant effect on the focal players’ payment. More essentially, the estimated effect sizes are quite different from those prior, indicating that the linear model yielded an estimation bias for the infrequent consumption issue, which was not adequately addressed.<sup>9</sup> Therefore, we relied on the type II Tobit model, despite its complexity.

## 5.5.3. Testing the impact of time lagging

In the main analysis, we adopted lagged peer consumption variables to avoid the reflection problem and determine the impact of an alternative mechanism network effect. We utilized the previous period of peer consumption to approximate the social effects of the current period. However, the game environment may evolve extremely quickly, and therefore the previous period environment may not be a reliable approximation. As a robustness check, we applied the same empirical model without time lagging. According to the estimation result,<sup>10</sup> the effect size of the positive social influence on the focal players’ playing time was considerably greater, probably due to reflection problems and the network effect. Notably, the effect of peer consumption on the focal players’ payment differed from the result obtained in the main analysis. From the IV specification, the effect of the playing time of peers on payment was insignificant, indicating contemporaneous friends’ playing time may not affect the focal player’s payment decisions. Therefore, we relied on the model with time lagging in the main analysis.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

Using data from a large-scale online social game, we investigate the multiplex social influence of peer consumption of free and premium features, measured by average playing time and the payments of socially connected friends, on focal players’ consumption decisions. The result yield noteworthy insights for digital freemium environments with social networking features. The proposed empirical model can be applied to various studies on social effects in online business operations.

Freemium has become the dominant business model among internet start-ups and smartphone application developers. However, when companies cannot convert enough users into paying customers, they suffer and fail. Therefore, converting users from free to paid users of (premium) product features is the key to success in the free value-added competition. This study explored this mechanism from the perspective of the multiplexity of social influence: peers’ play or spending of time and peers’ pay or spending of money. The results confirmed that peer spending of time and peer spending of money play significant roles in the focal user’s purchase decisions.

From an academic perspective, this study sheds light on the critical role of multiplex social influence in digital freemium businesses, which is a critical but understudied problem. We extended the focus to a more complex situation in which consumers make infrequent consumption decisions over time. Based on the proposed empirical model, which overcomes identification challenges in social effects models, we identi fied the positive multiplex social influence on users’ paying behaviors in the freemium context. For peers’ influence of the same type of behavior, our result is consistent with those of relevant studies on the adoption of technologies or products. For peers’ influence of different types of be haviors, we discover that, in general, peer spending of time influences the focal player’s spending of money but not vice versa. This finding has two important implications. First, people treat spending money and spending time differently. Their reaction to peers’ spending money and spending time is also different. Second, it indicates that different types of social influence are not independent, and there is strong evidence of multiplex social influence.

Furthermore, we reveal that multiplex social influence is heteroge neous and contingent on personal characteristics and network measures. First, our results indicate that social influence is more effective in inducing high-pay players to play more. By contrast, it is less effective in encouraging low-play players to pay for premium features. Second, our results show that high-pay and low-pay players’ playing time is affected by friends’ payment in different directions, which indicates that the multiplex social influence is also contingent on players’ characteristics. Third, we have verified the moderating role of degree centrality and clustering coefficient, consistent with previous literature [47]. To sum up, this study contributed to the limited literature on the digital free mium model by providing solid empirical evidence that verifies the causal effect of multiplex social influence. Moreover, it offered practical guidance for the administration of online businesses with a freemium model.

Game operators can leverage these conclusions to enhance the con sumption of social games in terms of both playing time and payment. In general, the results suggested that game operators should pay attention to different types of social interactions. Undoubtedly, both practical experience and empirical evidence indicate that high-pay players are the most valuable assets of social games. The primary practical strategy for game operators is to identify and maintain a fraction of high-pay players by providing specific promotions. This is the general operational strat egy for contexts with a freemium business model. Meanwhile, high-pay players are more strongly affected by social influence when making playing decisions. Hence, the second practical strategy is to offer tar geted promotions to friends of high-pay players to encourage them to play more.

Finally, multiplexity increases the number of approaches to under stand social influence on user behavior. When practitioners are inter ested in understanding user behavioral patterns in the presence of social networking, multiplexity allows the leveraging of information from one relationship to predict user connections in other types of relationships [30] and their purchasing decisions [55] More specifically, our results show that both peer spending of time and money influence the focal player’s spending of money. This finding has two folds of practical im plications. Firstly, in terms of managing players’ relationships, it would be helpful to recommend in-game friendships among players who spend money. In addition, it is also worthwhile not only to keep users who only spend time in the game but also to encourage them to spend more time in the game. Secondly, game operators could differentiate the design of items that can be acquired by spending time and those that can be ac quired by spending money. Items acquired by spending time could satisfy players’ hedonic needs but not utilitarian needs. In comparison, items acquired by spending money should satisfy players’ utilitarian purposes and their hedonic purposes. For example, the appearance of armors acquired by spending time could appear interesting but not distinctive. Meanwhile, armors acquired by spending money should be distinctive and more powerful and have a distinctive appearance.

This study had several limitations. First, we did not include time dummies in the fixed effects type II Tobit model. The estimation approach we applied imposed restrictions on the time-series properties of the model; that is, it did not allow time dummies in the participation Eq. [51]. Because we used a time window as short as 12 weeks, not including time effects was not a severe issue for estimation. Moreover, we included a quadratic form of player tenure in the model, which controlled for the time trend. Second, the empirical model captured local network features by including two of the simplest network meas ures—degree centrality and clustering coefficient. However, it may be better to apply more complex network measures to ascertain the effect of network structures on the behavior of individuals. Nevertheless, this limitation should not hinder the conclusions because various model settings were applied to mitigate endogeneity and estimation biases. Third, the efficiency of the type II Tobit model is low, but its estimation is consistent. It would be better if new methods that can achieve both efficiency and consistency were applied. Last, while the study has explored two essential behaviors, i.e., product use and pay, in the freemium model, other important behaviors may emerge as the free mium model evolve. Future work should pay attention to emerging behaviors and the multiplex social influence in the evolving freemium models.
