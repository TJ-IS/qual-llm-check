## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Eye-tracking Machine learning Mental attention Cognitive demand Objective difficulty

## A B S T R A C T

Predicting accuracy in cognitively challenging tasks has potential applications in education and industry. Task demand has been linked with increases in response time and variations in reaction time and eye-tracking metrics, however, machine learning research has not been used to predict performance on tasks with multiple levels of difficulty. We report data on adult participants who performed tasks of mental attentional capacity with six levels of difficulty and use machine learning methods to predict accuracy scores considering metrics associated with task difficulty, reaction time and eye movements. Results show that machine learning models can robustly predict performance with reaction times and difficulty level being the strongest predictors. Eye-tracking indices can also predict accuracy independently, with the most important features of the model driven by the number of fixations, number of saccades, duration of the current fixation and pupil size. Practical and theoretical impli cations of the results are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Eye movements and attention are inherently related. What we are looking at often corresponds to what we are attending, and while that is not always the case, our eye movements seem to play an important role in our higher order cognitive processes. Mental-attention is a higher order cognitive resource that reflects the effortful application of atten tion to maintain and manipulate information [1], it is considered the maturational component of working memory [2], and is related to in telligence [3] and scholastic performance [4]. Eye-tracking is an invaluable tool for studying attention and related cognitive processes [5]. Research shows that eye-movements reveal information about mental processes, which may not be easily accessible through behavioral performance measures alone (see [6,7] for reviews). Machine learning approaches have been applied to eye-movement data to classify per formance on all sorts of mental tasks such as learning new words [8], comprehension of physics concepts [9], verbal fluency and literacy tasks [10,11] and analogical reasoning [12]. These studies have used classi fication algorithms to predict low and high performance in cognitive tasks. Various parameters associated with reaction time have also been found to predict not only performance in a task, but also cognitive decline in skills and abilities [13–15]. To our knowledge no study to date used regression predictive algorithms on factors that include eyetracking and reaction time data to predict accuracy on tasks of higher order cognitive functions with multiple levels of difficulty. We examine predictability of accuracy on parametric measures of mental attention with six levels of difficulty using machine learning approaches on re action time and eye-movement data from adult participants.

Reaction time and its components have long been found to be asso ciated with both performance in specific tasks and general intelligence [15]. Notably, intraindividual variability of reaction time has been used to predict performance in pilots in flight-simulation [13], as well as to predict long-term cognitive decline [14] with the use of classic statistical methods. Reaction time is related to difficulty level, as it takes in dividuals longer to complete more difficult tasks (e.g., [16]). Difficulty level has been related to performance decline (e.g., [16]), yet difficulty levels of cognitive tasks are not often used to improve forecasting per formance. A promising technique that might provide improved modeling of performance is eye-tracking, as wearable eye-tracking de vices are becoming more accessible.

Eye-tracking has a long history. Discoveries of eye-movement facts (e.g., saccade latency) in the late 1800s lead to applied research in the mid 1900s (1930–1958), and improvements in the eye movement recording systems later that century (1970–1998; [17]). Modern eyetracking systems are based on devices that track the position of the pupil and record movements of the eye. Further data processing allows to identify various indices such as fixations $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ short periods of time, during which the gaze is held on one point in the visual field), saccades, (i.e., eye movements between fixations) and their characteristics that have been connected to cognitive phenomena by years of research [17]. Eye-tracking is widely applied in many domains in neuroscience and psychology. For instance, studies show eye-movement parameters distinguish performance among typical and atypical samples, providing an additional diagnostic measure for many neurological and psychiatric disorders ([18–21] for reviews and meta-analyses). Eye-tracking data has been used with standard inferential statistics (i.e., not using machine learning) to predict performance in a variety of higher order cognitive tasks, such as conceptualisation of physics [22]. Specifically, Rozenblit and colleagues [22] used laborious qualitative analysis of gaze path by independent observers to predict performance of students in a me chanics visualisation task and identified differences between high and low performers. In another study, longer fixations and shorter saccades have been found predictive of accuracy in understanding physics con cepts presented in different modalities [23]. Attention during educa tional videos of different formats was assessed with intersubject correlation of eye movements and then used to successfully predict test scores of participants in another study [24]. In recent years eye move ments research has begun to utilize machine learning approaches in all areas of vision science, as these have potential application in facilitating decision making in forecasting individual performance and choices.

Machine learning is a class of methods of data analysis that allows to automatically and efficiently identify patterns in large amounts of data. Machine learning methods are most widely applied to two broad categories of problems: classification (e.g., score belongs to either A or B) and regression (e.g., score is a specific value) problems. Machine learning methods have been introduced into cognitive science and successfully implemented to classify performance on cognitive tasks from eye movement data. A linear support vector machine was used to classify trials in which a stimulus was either present or absent with 95% prediction accuracy, in a sample of seven participants [25]. An eyemovement study reported 85% accuracy for classifiers of learning of complex words from online course materials in a sample of eight par ticipants [8]. A Naive Bayes generative model and least-squares support vector machine was used in a discriminative model to classify successful and unsuccessful trials in comprehension of physics concepts with 80% accuracy in a sample of 400 participants [9]. Optimal strategies fo analogical reasoning were successfully classified from eye movements using a support vector machine with 93% accuracy in a sample of 28 participants [12]. A support vector machine has also been implemented to classify literacy levels from fixation time, first-pass rereading time, second-pass fixation time and dwell time with 84% accuracy in a sample of 67 participants [10] and to classify programmer's levels of expertise and perceived task difficulty with 99% and 62% accuracy respectively for a sample of 38 participants [26]. With a sample of 70 participants, the k-nearest neighbors algorithm and the bagged tree classifier were used to identify high performers in Ruff Figural Fluency test, where participants were asked to draw as many new figures from a set of dot configuration, with 71% accuracy on both methods [11]. It is important to note that all of the aforementioned studies addressed classification problems, generally differentiating between high and low performance but not attempting to predict individual accuracy. To our knowledge, this study is the first to use a predictive model to identify performance scores for cognitive tasks with multiple levels of difficulty, such as tasks of mental attentional capacity.

Mental-attention is a developmental construct that corresponds to the ability to maintain and manipulate information in mind, first introduced by Pascual-Leone [1]. The model of endogenous mental attention follows a spotlight analogy representing mental attention as the effortful energy which is nested within working memory, which is then nested within the field of activated schemes, and which is in turn nested in the repertoire of schemes of an individual [27,28]. This effortful energy is limited and grows with age and tasks of menta attentional capacity have been used extensively in research in psychol ogy ([3] for review) and education ([4] for review). Research shows that when the mental attentional demand of the task is above the mentalattentional capacity of the individual then the performance on the task declines or drops to chance levels [29,30]. Tasks of mentalattentional capacity have multiple levels of cognitive demand allow ing for comparison of performance among levels of difficulty [3]. The ability to mobilize attentional resources in visual contexts with varying levels of perceptual noisiness and cognitive difficulty is vital for many industries, associated with increased risk for human life and well-being, such as driving, control of air traffic, operation of heavy machinery and identifying abnormalities in health-related examinations. Mobilizing attentional resources is also vital for learning and scholastic success. Thus, knowledge and decision-making systems that can forecast per formance have applications in industry and educational practice. Mea sures of mental attentional capacity are designed to mimic situations that require sustained attention for extraction and maintenance of relevant information with simultaneous inhibition of irrelevant dis tractors. More importantly, parametric increase in difficulty levels of the task allows us to capture the moment when load increases beyond the capacity of the individual, after which errors are drastically more likely to occur. This feature of the measures, combined with their visual mo dality, makes them a suitable model task to predict performance based on eye behaviour during the task. Further, we assess mental attention with the color matching tasks [2], which come in two versions that share the same scaling (e.g, trial length, number of trials) and metric (e.g., comparable scoring method) but differ in context (i.e., use different base images). This allows for minimizing methodological confounds associ ated with task design and offers the opportunity to examine model consistency in different contexts.

Despite the wealth of potential applications in user interfaces [31], educational practice [8] and medical advancements [32], knowledge on specific parameters important for predicting performance in visual tasks with variable mental attentional demand is lacking. In a data driven approach, we use machine learning methods to predict accuracy scores for mental attentional tasks considering features (i.e. measurable prop erties or characteristics) associated with task difficulty, reaction time and eye movements and explore the relative importance of these fea tures in predicting performance of individuals.

## 2. Methods

In this study participants were presented with the Colour Matching Task in two versions with and without interference that each includes six levels of difficulty. Their eye-movements and behavioral responses were recorded with EyeLink Portable Duo SR Research eye-tracker and exported for constructing a machine learning model. The machine learning pipeline consisted of several steps: feature engineering, model training with a grid search procedure to examine various models with different sets of features and cross-validation, as well as estimation of feature importances. In this section we present participant information, a detailed description of the tasks, stimuli, data acquisition, pre processing methods and the machine learning pipeline.

## 2.1. Participants

Sixty-two adult participants with normal or corrected vision with contact lenses and no prior self-reported head trauma or cognitive im pairments were recruited through advertisement for research partici pants on the University campus and through the University mailing list.

Five participants were excluded from further analysis: two participant due to technical errors during recording, three performed at an accuracy lower than chance in the easiest levels of the task, where we generally see 90–100% performance in adults. The final dataset included 57 participants (34 females; 23.0 ± 3.88 years). The local research ethics board approved methods and procedures; all participants signed informed consent.

## 2.2. Experimental tasks and stimuli

Participants were invited to complete the Colour Matching Task (CMT) in two versions. Training was provided to familiarize participants with the rules of the task and ensure that participants understand the timing of the task.

## 2.2.1. Colour matching tasks

CMT was administered in two versions (CMT-Balloons and CMT Clown; Fig. 1; [2]). In both versions, the goal of the player is to compare the colours they see in the picture to the colours they saw in the previous picture and press a button to indicate whether the relevant colours are the same or different as quickly and as accurately as possible, disregarding the position of the colours. The levels of difficulty corre spond to the number of relevant colours to hold in mind and compare, plus a constant for executive schemes needed in each version of the task. The load for executive schemes is estimated to be one for CMT-Balloons and two for CMT-Clown. Thus, mental demand is n + 1 for CMT Balloons and n + 2 for CMT-Clown, where n is the number of relevant colours. Two colours (green and blue) are irrelevant and serve as dis tractors, and participants are instructed to ignore them. The CMT-Clown requires an additional executive scheme for effortfully identifying the relevant colours among the increased number of distractors such as the face and other configured body parts [2]; [33]. There are six difficulty levels indexed by the number of relevant colours. A total of 24 task blocks are presented in the task. Each task version contains 4 runs of 6 blocks (32 s duration), which contain 8 stimuli and uniform difficulty level, with blocks of different levels placed in pseudo-random order. Every task block ends with an inter-block stimulus (when no response is required, since only irrelevant colours are present) that indicates a change in difficulty level. Presentation of the stimuli lasts for 3 s, during which the participant needs to make a response (same/different) by pressing a button. The experiment was programmed and presented in Experiment Builder (SR Research). Every participant completed all 24 blocks and reaction times and accuracy were recorded. Accuracy was calculated as percentage of correct responses for the trials that required a response per difficulty level. Reaction time for all responses was calculated as the time elapsed between the start of stimulus time and the time of button press. Reaction times were averaged per mental demand level from both correct and incorrect trials. Accuracy was the target variable. Accuracy was calculated as the percentage of correct responses out of all trials for every mental demand level. Every participant had 6 accuracy scores for each version of the task. Missed trials were counted as incorrect responses.

## 2.3. Eye-tracking: data acquisition and variables

Eye-tracking data were acquired using EyeLink Portable Duo SR Research eye-tracker with 1 ms temporal resolution (at 1000 Hz fre quency) in remote head-free-to-move mode. Raw sampling data was preprocessed to identify eye tracking indices with the use of Data Viewer software (SR Research). Relevant eye-tracking indices were extracted for each participant and used as features in subsequent machine learning analyses. These included fixation duration of current fixation, total number of fixations in a trial, average pupil size during the current fixation and whether a blink was present before or after the fixation, amplitude of the current saccade in degrees of visual angle, presence of a blink in the current saccade, average velocity of the next saccade and the angle between the horizontal plane and the direction of the next saccade, and duration of all selected blinks in a trial (in milliseconds). These features were selected for analysis with future wider application of performance prediction in mind: standard reliable algorithms for identifying these features have been developed and are incorporated into data analysis and visualization software for most commercial eyetracker devices.

Saccades and fixations were identified with the use of the saccade detection algorithm developed by SR Research within the Data Viewer. To detect a saccade, for each data sample, the parser computes instan taneous velocity and acceleration and compares these to the velocity and acceleration thresholds. If either is above threshold, a saccade signal is generated. The parser checks that the saccade signal is on or off for a critical time before deciding that a saccade has begun or ended.

## 2.4. Machine learning pipeline

A common machine learning pipeline was used to train and test various models. This pipeline basically consists of feature engineering, model training with a grid search procedure to examine various models with different sets of features [34]. After the grid search, we built a final model on the top performing machine learning algorithm. The pipeline also contains a leave-one-out cross-validation procedure and a set of different machine learning algorithms (Fig. 2). The details of each step are provided below.

## 2.4.1. Feature engineering

In machine learning, features correspond to measurable properties or characteristics. In our study, for each participant and each trial we considered features associated with task difficulty $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ difficulty level), reaction time, and eve-movements. Eve movement features relate to blinking, fixations, and saccades for every level of the task. We extracted features describing individual saccades and fixations. Four features were extracted for fixations: fixation duration of current fixation, total num ber of fixations in a trial, average pupil size during the current fixation and whether a blink was present before or after the fixation. Five fea tures were extracted for saccades: amplitude of the current saccade in degrees of visual angle, presence of a blink in the current saccade, average velocity of the next saccade and the angle between the hori zontal plane and the direction of the next saccade. Additionally, we extracted the average duration of all selected blinks in a trial (in milli seconds). Mean, median, standard deviation, minimum and maximum were extracted for all features. The models were built using Python (3.6

Python packages, version numpy 1.17.2, sklearn 0.21.3, pandas 0.25.1).

## 2.4.2. Machine learning models

We examined various machine learning models that are applicable to regression problems and are standard in the field: Linear Regression [36], Lasso Regression [37], Ridge Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors Regression [38], XGBoost Regression [39], Random Forest Regression [40].

## 2.4.3. Cross-validation

We used leave-one-out cross-validation [35] with $R ^ { 2 }$ to estimate performance of each model. Each participant was characterized by a vector with 76 features described above. We calculated means per dif ficulty level for every participant for every feature and applied machinelearning models to 6 points per participant with a total of 324 points in the analysis. It is important to distinguish the use of the $R ^ { 2 }$ metric in our analyses. In classic statistical methods $R ^ { 2 }$ is calculated for a linear regression using the same sample, on which regression parameters are estimated, however, in the case of Machine Learning methods $R ^ { 2 }$ can be calculated as difference between 1 and the ratio between the mean squared error and the output variance [41]. The error and the variance are calculated using an independent validation set, which is not used to estimate parameters of the predictive model. Thus, such a ratio essen tially compares the predictive model with the trivial constant prediction up to the fact that the constant prediction is computed on the test set rather than the training set (output variance can be considered as a squared error of the prediction on the independent test, when the pre diction is given by the constant equal to the output mean value). Thus, in our analyses this $R ^ { 2 }$ estimates to what extent the prediction by some complex model is better than the simplest possible constant prediction. While our main goodness-of-fit measure was $R ^ { 2 } ,$ we additionally calcu lated RMSE (Root Mean Square Error) and $R ^ { 2 }$ adjusted values for each model in order to examine error rates of the models and provide more detailed information on model performance. RMSE is the square root of the value obtained from the average squared difference between the predicted and the actual value of our interest variable $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ accuracy).

Then, a set of established machine learning algorithms were trained and cross-validated based on $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ score. Finally, we selected the best model and the most important set of features.

This metric can be used to judge the efficiency of the model by assessing its error rate. The lower RMSE values are, the higher the accuracy of the model is. $R ^ { 2 }$ adjusted is used in classic regression models to account for the phenomenon of the $R ^ { 2 }$ automatically increasing when extra explanatory variables are added to the model. In this case we used the formula proposed by Mordekai Ezekiel: $R ^ { - 2 } = 1 - ( 1 - R ^ { 2 } ) ^ { \ast } ( \mathbf { n } - 1 ) / ( \mathbf { n } - \mathbf { p } - \mathbf { \bar { \alpha } }$ 1); where p is the total number of explanatory variables in the model (not including the constant term), and n is the sample size. While this metric is important for cases when error and the variance are calculated using the same dataset as the one used for training the model, the pitfalls of $R ^ { \breve { 2 } }$ are compensated when cross-validation is applied.

Eye tracking features, difficulty level and reaction time were used to predict accuracy in regression models separately for CMT-balloons and for CMT-clowns, and then for combined data from the two versions of the task. To evaluate eye movements independently and examine the influence of reaction time and difficulty level regression models were repeated with reaction time and difficulty level removed.

Hyperparameters, the parameters of the model for learning process control, were tuned with a procedure based on the one-leave-out cross validation [31]. Specifically, each time one participant's point (i.e. one sample from the training set) was removed from the training set a pre diction was made for that participant's point with the fixed set (i.e., the complete set without the removed point).

The final stage of analysis was calculation of feature importance scores. Importance scores are calculated to assess the relative input of different features in the model. Computation of importance scores varies between different machine learning methods. As our best performing model is based on gradient boosting technique, a feature importance score indicates how valuable each feature was in the construction of the boosted decision trees within the model. Importance is calculated for a single decision tree by the amount that each feature split point improves the overall performance measure of the model $( R ^ { 2 } ) _ { : }$ , weighted by the number of observations the node is responsible for. Feature importance scores are then averaged across all of the decision trees within the model.

## 3. Results

All tested models provided comparable results with the XGBoost regression model providing the most accurate prediction of accuracy from selected features (Table 1). XGBoost regression is a popular and efficient implementation of the gradient boosted trees algorithm. Gradient boosting is a supervised learning algorithm, which attempts to accurately predict a target variable by combining the estimates of a set of simpler, weaker models [39]. In our case this model outperforms models based on simple linear regression algorithms, such as Linear, Lasso and Ridge regressions.

## 4. Discussion

We examined the contribution of eye-tracking, reaction time and difficulty level parameters in predicting accuracy in higher-order cognitive tasks that assess mental attentional capacity. We highlight four main findings: 1) the prediction model with combined scores can robustly predict performance on parametric measures of mental atten tional capacity, 2) reaction time and difficulty level contribute sub stantially as predictors of accuracy, 3) eye-tracking indices can also predict accuracy independently, albeit with lower effect size, 4) eyetracking indices with the most importance include the number of fixa tions, number of saccades, duration of the current fixation, variance of saccade duration and pupil size.

Difficulty level and reaction time are the most important features. The top ten eye-tracking features that contribute to the models are: mean number of fixations in a trial, standard deviation of saccade duration, the mean number of saccades, mean current fixation duration (in milliseconds), mean number of blinks following fixation and mini mum, maximum and mean of pupil size for combined CMT-Balloons and CMT-Clowns model. For CMT-Clown the top features were the mean number of fixations in a trial, mean number of saccades in a trial, standard deviation of saccade duration, mean of fixation duration and standard deviation of fixation duration. For CMT-Balloons the top fea tures were the standard deviation of fixation duration, the mean number of fixations in a trial, the mean number of blinks following fixation, the maximum of fixation duration and mean pupil size.

Our machine learning model demonstrates for the first time that accuracy on complex parametric measures of mental attentional ca pacity can be predicted with high effect size. We obtained 0.828 for the combined task versions and 0.783 (CMT-Balloons) and 0.812 (CMT-Clown) for the versions separately. These effect size values are compa rable despite the difference in the context of the task $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ , participants process colors presented on balloons in one version and clowns in the other). Critically, some factors seem to favor one version of the task over the other. For example, reaction time was slightly better in predicting CMT-Balloons when considered independently, whereas difficulty level and eye tracking were better in predicting CMT-Clown when considered independently. This finding is valuable for future research and design of decision support systems that consider contextual differences within the same domain. Whether contributing factors change by domain (e.g., verbal or numeric) is an interesting question for future research.

Reaction time components were the strongest independent predicto in our models. The relation between accuracy and reaction time has a long history of research in the field of cognitive science [43,44]. Importance scores for both CMT-Balloons and CMT-Clown and the model based on the combined dataset show that the feature that con tributes most to the accuracy of the model is standard deviation of re action time (Fig. 4). This is consistent with prior studies, as higher intraindividual variability reaction time has been found to predict lower cognitive performance [13–15]. Additionally, it is important to note that mean reaction time, which was also a strong predictor in our models, is closely associated with difficulty [16]. Levels with higher difficulty contain more relevant schemas and thus require more time to process and make a response, when task demand is within the mental attentional capacity of the individual [2]. However, when the level of difficulty is above the individual's capacity, reaction time drops, as participants resort to guessing. Although machine learning models rarely use reac tion time to predict accuracy in healthy samples, some studies with clinical samples showed the potential of reaction time as a predictor in performance on cognitive tasks with a successful random forest classifier for mild cognitive impairment in older adults [45]. Reaction time in physical fitness laboratory tests also proved to be an important predictor in detecting successful aging [46] and a simple reaction time-based classifier reliably differentiated between different cognitive impair ments [47]. We conclude that both mean, standard deviation and extreme values (i.e. maximum) of reaction time can be successfully used with machine learning approaches to predict performance, where recording of reaction time is possible.

Our models demonstrate that difficulty level is also a strong predictor for accuracy, however this effect was stronger for the high-interference version (i.e., CMT-clown). Although many tasks have assigned difficulty levels, difficulty level is rarely quantified for a given task $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , identify the number of schemes that need to be processed for successfully completing a task). This is often the case for tasks offered to schoolchildren and students, but it is also true for many tasks we do not perceive as having an objective level of mental attentional demand, such as online shopping or searching for relevant information on a news screen. Our study underscores the importance of theoretically driven task analysis that can be used for qualitatively and quantitatively assessing task demand (e.g., [27]). Theoretically, this point is especially important as research has demonstrated individual differences in cognitive abilities for adults ([48,49] for reviews) and children [2,27–29,33,50]. Although small effect sizes are observed when eye movements to CMT tasks are considered separately, the model considering all eyemovement data together demonstrates a medium effect size in inde pendently predicting accuracy. Eye-movements have been shown to reflect task difficulty and cognitive effort [31], yet mechanisms of this relation are not entirely understood. The features that were of the most importance for our models have also previously been found to change with cognitive effort. Specifically, the number of saccades and fixations often varies with task difficulty, with the number of fixations and sac cades increasing in tasks with visual components [53], which we also find in many real-life situations. Indeed, in order to successfully com plete such tasks, participants need to saccade and fixate on relevant items and the increase in items can reflect the need for increase in saccades and fixations. Efficiently increasing the number of saccades and fixations thus can lead to higher accuracy. Duration of fixations is often used as a measure of attention to specific areas of interest in both perceptual and higher order cognitive tasks, such as in reading and so cial cognition studies. Decrease in mean fixation duration may be indicative of increase in task difficulty [54]. We propose an additional explanation for the efficacy of this measure in predicting performance: participants who were able to fixate on more relevant items in the image (i.e. with shorter fixations) were also more successful at solving the task. Pupil dilation also has a long history of association with cognitive dif ficulty [55–57] with increased pupil size indicative of higher mental workload [58,59], which would be helpful in predicting the decrease in accuracy with increase in objective task difficulty. The relation between eye movements and cognitive demand is relevant for many real-world applications including development of user interfaces for software and sites $[ 6 0 , 3 1 ]$ , development of educational online courses that adapt to the learner's needs and capacity [8], and development of sophisticated and safe surgical equipment [32]. Importantly, here we show that eye movement features improve models predicting higher order cognitive performance.

## 5. Conclusion

We examine metrics derived from behavior (reaction time), a-priori $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . ,$ , difficulty level) and eye-tracking to predict accuracy using machine learning approaches. The strongest prediction model is derived using all metrics and all tasks. Separate models for CMT-balloons and CMTclowns show comparable ${ \bf { \bar { R } } } ^ { 2 }$ values, despite the difference in the vi sual context of the task. Reaction time, difficulty level and eyemovement metrics also predict performance independently, albeit with lower effect sizes. Theoretically, results point to common and distinct features associated with these metrics that can inform theories of cognition and vision science. Practically, our result is useful in designing decision support systems to forecast performance using short visual tasks of mental attentional capacity. Research in this area can also benefit as the predictive algorithm may be applied to other contexts where color processing is required. Specifically, future studies can investigate whether the algorithm can apply in samples with different age groups and in different contexts and domains, such as images with numbers or letters.
