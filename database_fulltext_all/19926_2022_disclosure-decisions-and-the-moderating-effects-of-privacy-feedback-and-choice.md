## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Privacy protection Privacy feedback Disclosure Perceived privacy Justice theory

## A B S T R A C T

To address data concealment challenges, this study considers how perceived privacy and justice might drive users’ information disclosure intentions. Two contextual factors (privacy feedback and choice) also might function as moderators in the proposed relationships. The model was tested using a controlled lab experiment featuring a new app dedicated to m-commerce where both privacy feedback and choice were manipulated.

The results affirm a critical role of perceived justice in determining perceived privacy, which shapes disclosure intentions. Providing privacy feedback also enhances the positive effect of perceived justice on perceived privacy and the positive effect of trust propensity on disclosure intention, and alleviates the negative effect of privacy experience on perceived privacy. In addition, providing choices can help users make more conscious privacy decisions.

Accordingly, this study contributes to extant literature by extending a single-context theory view and exploring moderating effects of both privacy feedback and choice on information disclosure decision processes. This research also establishes the real effect of feedback by differentiating people who choose (or not) to receive privacy feedback. In addition, this study is applying justice theory to design privacy feedback, through a theo retical link between feedback functions and three justice dimensions (i.e., procedural, informational, and interactional). Finally, we replicate previous findings from past privacy literature using a new setting and a new measure for privacy, further establishing confidence in those relationships

## 1. Introduction

Information privacy is a prominent concern for modern consumers [1], particularly considering the possible costs associated with potential data misuses or data breaches. In the first half of 2020 alone, data breaches compromised 36 billion records worldwide [2]. In addition, more than 80% of mobile app users in China have suffered information leakage and privacy invasions [3]. In response to these dramatic trends, users worldwide increasingly seek to protect their privacy by concealing some personal information. However, such privacy-protective behavior hinder firms’ efforts to gather and capitalize on users’ data. Accordingly, companies seek effective methods to encourage users to disclose per sonal information.

One option involves transparency: When service providers proac tively inform users about their privacy practices, such as with privacy statements, users tend to be more willing to disclose personal information [4]. Yet, such measures also have two main drawbacks. First, they generally appear before the user begins to engage with the system, leaving users unaware of the privacy practices that may follow their information disclosure. Second, their effectiveness is debatable: few users read or understand them [5,6], leading users to accept them without being fully aware of their resulting level of privacy. Thus, pri vacy statements might not lead to truly enhanced privacy for the users, and new approaches to inform users thus appear necessary.

Privacy feedback involves providing users with information about privacy practices while they engage in service usage [7], an option that might mitigate privacy concerns and enhance disclosure [4,8,9]. As, to our knowledge, privacy feedback has not been implemented yet in real apps, its effect on information disclosure needs to be evaluated and isolated, a gap this research aims to fulfill. As is true of privacy state ments, if privacy feedback (examples of such a feature are displayed in Appendix A and Fig. C1) is indeed presented but not considered by users, its effect would be minimal. To establish the true effect of privacy feedback, it is necessary to differentiate people who care about feedback and those who do not, a preference that might be revealed if users have the choice to receive privacy feedback or not.

The first theoretical foundation of this paper is justice theory, which refers to perceptions of fairness in decision-making and resource allo cation environments [90]. Justice theory was used to investigate the influence of privacy interventions on users’ behavior in previous liter ature [e.g. 14,15] (Appendix B provides a review of relevant references). Until now, however, no study applies justice theory to guide the design of new privacy-protective measures. As it is a relevant theory to un derstand privacy-protective approaches, we adopt justice theory as a framework for designing the privacy feedback measure in this study. Given justice relates to privacy-related beliefs [36,37] and disclosure behavior [14], we also consider perceived justice as a core belief that would influence privacy-related decision-making and, in the end, disclosure intention.

We further develop our model based on a second theoretical lens i.e. theory contextualization, following the ‘context as moderator’ approach (level 2b) [71]. Incorporating contextual moderators into known privacy-related theory will make our understanding of the relationships between beliefs and behavior more context-sensitive thus making those theories richer. While context has already been found to moderate the nature and extent of privacy-related relationships in past literature (e.g. [10,91,92]), the moderating influence of contextual features such as privacy feedback and choice has not been theorized in a cohesive way until now. Privacy-protective measures usually appear as independent variables in prior literature. However, how these factors might influence users’ decision-making processes, as contextual moderators, is un known, a gap we aim to address here.

Based on these two theoretical lenses and previous work in the area, we propose a model of relevant antecedents to information disclosure intentions. To complement justice theory, key constructs in privacy research, already shown as critical antecedents to disclosure, are also incorporated. Beyond perceived justice and perceived privacy posited as core constructs and mediators in our model, we introduce two individual antecedents: trust propensity, a personality attribute that might affect justice perceptions and behavior directly or indirectly, and privacy in vasion experience, which likely shapes privacy-related attitudes and behavior [10–12].

Considering the need for a systematic investigation of privacyrelated issues that can propose guidelines for designing privacyprotective measures that increase users’ justice perceptions and disclo sure, we raise and attempt to answer three main research questions:

(1) What are the relevant privacy-related and justice related ante cedents to users’ disclosure decisions? To what extent do perceived privacy, perceived justice, and individual attributes (e. $g . ,$ trust propensity, privacy invasion experience) drive users disclosure decisions?

(2) What moderating effects do privacy feedback and choice have in such decision processes?

(3) Can measures (e.g., privacy feedback) designed to improve users perceptions of justice increase users’ intentions to disclose per sonal information?

To test the proposed model, we undertake a controlled lab experi ment and manipulate the presence vs. absence of each of our two contextual moderators i.e. privacy feedback and choice. The privacy feedback treatment reflects our theoretical understanding of justice theory. Participants are also given the choice (or not) to get privacy feedback. Considering the popularity of m-commerce in China, and scant research into privacy feedback in this context, we feature Chinese m-commerce as the context for our study.

In turn, this study contributes to existing literature in several ways. First, we explore and confirm the moderating effects of both privacyprotective measures (feedback and choice) on information disclosure decisions, adding to the sparse research works that include contextual moderators in their models (e.g., industry, website attributes, requester properties, type of data, amount of data, disclosure incentives [21–25]). In identifying privacy-protective measures as contextual moderators, we extend the single-context theory view in IS [26] as it relates to infor mation disclosure.

Second, in recognition of the importance of perceived justice for users’ disclosure decisions [13], we design a privacy feedback solution that theoretically links its functions with three dimensions of justice (procedural, informational, and interactional), then investigate how it might influence disclosure decisions by moderating some relationships in the decision-making process. Justice theory informs prior studies of the effects of existing privacy-protective measures [14,15], but we know of no studies that have applied it to design new privacy-protective measures or that use rigorous empirical research to determine their ef fects on disclosure decisions. In so doing, we address calls for more design-based research aiming to achieve better protection for user [16–19], while enabling firms to encourage information disclosure.

Third, we added a second factor to consider whether participants may choose to access and read the privacy feedback or not. We know of no other studies that acknowledge that users rarely read privacy state ments while also investigating the real effects of this privacy-protective approach. Studies on the effectiveness of privacy-protective methods generally ignore users’ interest in such features and how this (dis)in terest influences the effectiveness of privacy-enhancing features. In commenting our model outcomes, we rely on the notion of central and peripheral routes from the elaboration likelihood model [20]. When users lack choice, they likely focus on surface features, such as the very presence of feedback (peripheral cues), which may lead to temporary behavioral changes. But, when users must choose whether to access privacy feedback, they may be more motivated to pay attention to the content of that feedback and exhibit some behavioral changes that resist behavioral change challenges (central route).

Fourth, we replicate results from previous literature using a new setting (m-commerce in China) and a new proxy to measure privacy i.e. perceived privacy instead of privacy concerns. By doing so, we give further confidence in the explanatory power of relationships already established in previous work such as, for example, the links between justice and privacy, between privacy and disclosure or between privacy invasion experience and privacy. Given the scarcity of replication research in IS, the contribution of this replication effort is worth mentioning.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Perceived privacy

Privacy concern has often been used as a proxy to measure privacy in previous research, such as in explorations of the effects of users’ privacy concerns on their willingness to provide information [11,15,21,27,28,29,30,31,32,95,96,97,98]. As concerns only refer to negative aspects related to privacy, in this paper, we consider instead perceived privacy (i.e. people assessment that external agents have limited access to information about them) [33] to measure users’ overall attitudes to privacy. In doing so, we aim to test some hypotheses already tested in past literature using new settings and new measures of attitudes toward privacy. Replication is an important component of scientific method [100], which is regarded as a central and valued part of IS body of knowledge [101]. As progress in IS research currently relies too much on seeking exploratory and confirmatory evidence, some scholars called for increased replication research [102], an effort we are seeking to provide in this paper.

Past empirical research on perceived privacy mainly focuses on its antecedents, such as perceived control, perceived risk, privacy concerns, or trust [33,34,80], without referring to privacy invasion experience.

Furthermore, prior findings reveal that perceived privacy has significant positive effects on ad attitudes and purchase intentions on social plat forms [35]. We know of no studies that test the relationship between perceived privacy and information disclosure intentions though. To complement previous literature therefore, we explore how perceived privacy might influence information disclosure decisions.

## 2.2. Justice theory

Justice theory provides an appropriate theoretical framework for analyzing privacy issues $[ 3 6 , 3 7 ]$ , because perceptions of justice strongly influence privacy concerns and disclosure behavior [13,72,93]. Justice relates to people’s needs for fairness and transparency, which are highly relevant in privacy-related settings, so justice arguably should increase willingness to disclose personal information [14]. That is, users’ per ceptions of justice might motivate them to disclose personal informa tion, even if they perceive a threat in doing so [38,39].

Justice theory also can predict the effects of various privacy in terventions on users’ behavior. For example, Xu et al. [14] consider the effects of three privacy intervention approaches (compensation, in dustry self-regulation, and government regulation) and two justice provisions (distributive and procedural) on users’ intentions to disclose personal information to location-based services. Zhao et al. [15] also apply justice theory to investigate the influences of four privacy in terventions (incentive provision, interaction promotion, privacy control, and privacy policy) on users’ justice perceptions in a location-based social network. References on how justice theory was considered in the past in relation to privacy are presented in Appendix B.

Although justice theory provides a highly relevant theoretical basis for studying approaches to privacy protection, no investigation has leveraged justice theory to guide the design of new privacy-protective measures so far. If an organization’s approach to privacy protection is driven by justice, such that users perceive that their information is being handled fairly, then they may experience greater trust and fewer privacy concerns [40,41]. Accordingly, in this study, we adopt justice theory as a framework for designing a privacy feedback measure.

Justice theory identifies four types of justice: distributive, proce dural, interactional, and informational [42]. The first three are widely used in privacy research $[ 1 4 , 1 5 , 4 3 ]$ . In our study, privacy feedback re fers to information that service providers offer to users, so it has little relationship with distributive justice, which mainly emphasizes the benefits a user receives from a service provider. Consequently, we focus on procedural, interactional, and informational justice.

## 2.3. Privacy protection measures

## 2.3.1. Privacy feedback

Until now, to inform users about their privacy practices, service providers have mainly posted privacy statements that describe a priori, and before app usage, how they collect, process, or exploit users’ per sonal information. Empirical studies establish the effectiveness of pri vacy statements for reducing users’ privacy concerns and privacy risks and encouraging information disclosure $[ 1 4 , 1 5 , 2 9 , 3 1 , 4 4 - 4 6 ]$ . Mobile apps that rely on permission-based approaches and privacy settings also enable users to control access to sensitive information and functions [46,47]. We know, however, that users barely read such privacy state ments, making this solution hardly effective in practice.

When service providers proactively inform users about privacy practices, users may be more willing to disclose information [4], as suggested by investigations of the effects of feedback on users’ privacy attitudes and behavior in mobile location-sharing systems [48–51], online social networks [52], and contextual instant messaging services [8]. However, none of these studies refers to m-commerce, nor do they use rigorous empirical methods to explore the effects of feedback. In addition, whereas most m-commerce apps provide various feedback, such as purchase order information, logistics information, or refund progress, they rarely describe privacy practices. Privacy-related feed back may therefore be key to mitigate privacy concerns at the stage of app usage, as opposed to strategies that would inform users about pri vacy practices before engaging in the app (such as privacy statements) or after engagement (such as follow up communication messages from the firm, based on data collected from app usage and purchase). Up to now, however, privacy-related feedback is rarely present on mobile apps in practice. It is the aim of this paper to investigate its influence on the privacy-related decision-making process and, ultimately, corresponding disclosure intentions.

Noting the salience of privacy issues for m-commerce in China, and using prior insights into feedback [7], we attempt to design a privacy feedback measure, specific to m-commerce settings, that would inform users about when, what, and by whom their personal information is being collected and how their disclosed information gets treated. With an empirical study, we also evaluate the effects of privacy feedback by uncovering its moderating influence on users’ disclosure decision making processes in such an m-commerce environment.

## 2.3.2. Privacy choice

Privacy feedback might not be fully effective if users do not read it or fail to understand it. To determine if privacy feedback is something that users care about, we suggest offering them a choice between accessing and reading privacy feedback or not. Perceived control (proxy for choice) affects privacy concerns [15,44,53] and perceived privacy [33,34]. Therefore, in addition to privacy feedback, we study the po tential moderating effect of choice on users’ information disclosure decision-making processes to uncover its role.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Our model aims to explain individuals’ intention to disclose personal information (Fig. 1). Key constructs in privacy research (perceived pri vacy) and justice theory (perceived justice), already shown as critical antecedents to disclosure intentions in previous literature are posited as key beliefs influencing disclosure intention and added as mediators in our model. Factors internal to the decision-maker $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , trust propensity, privacy experience) are central to the decision-making process and conceived as critical individual antecedents. Contextual factors external to the decision-maker (e.g., privacy feedback, privacy choice) enter as moderators of the model relationships, following theory contextualiza tion and the ‘context as moderator’ approach.

## 3.1. Perceived justice and disclosure intention

Consumers’ perceptions of how justly companies handle their per sonal information could motivate them to disclose personal information, even if they perceive a privacy threat [38]. Justice relates to needs for fairness and transparency in privacy-related actions, so if service pro viders improve the transparency of their data practices, users may feel more comfortable providing them with information.

Hypothesis 1. Perceived justice positively affects disclosure intentions.

## 3.2. Perceived justice and perceived privacy

Perceived justice reflects summary assessments of three justice di mensions (procedural, informational, and interactional) that can create a positive mental state [54]. For this study, we define perceived justice as a mental state that likely influences overall self-assessments of pri vacy. The degree to which users have faith and confidence in an orga nization’s privacy practices should significantly influence such selfassessed perceptions of privacy [33], such that companies can alle viate users’ concerns by establishing a just environment [15,41,72,94]. In this study, instead of concerns, we use perceived privacy as a proxy to measure privacy and aim to replicate previous relationship between justice and privacy by testing the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. Perceived justice positively affects perceived privacy.

## 3.3. Perceived privacy and disclosure

Users may refuse to provide their personal information if they perceive a lack of privacy, such that withholding information gives them a means to protect it. When users perceive greater privacy protection, they should feel more comfortable disclosing personal information [55]. If privacy appears sufficient indeed, they can trade off information disclosure against more services. Therefore, in an effort to replicate previous literature in the area (e.g. [11,21,95,96,97,98]), we aim to test whether privacy (measured through perceived privacy in this paper) will have a positive relationship with information disclosure:

Hypothesis 3. Perceived privacy positively affects disclosure intentions.

## 3.4. Trust propensity

We introduce trust propensity as a first individual antecedent because of its relevance in the privacy-related decision-making process [39] and its ability to affect both justice perceptions and subsequent behavior, directly or indirectly [56,57], as already verified in the pri vacy realm (e.g. [29]). As a personality attribute, trust propensity is defined as a stable individual trait, reflecting a generalized expectation of others’ trustworthiness, which thereby influences the likelihood that a person will trust others [58]. People with a high level of trust pro pensity believe that most people are fair and have good intentions [59]; anticipate they will be treated fairly by others, even under conditions of uncertainty [60]; and have more positive perceptions of justice than people with lower levels, even if exposed to identical fairness informa tion [56]. People who are more willing to trust others are more likely to perceive justice, in confirmation of their positive expectations.

Furthermore, trust propensity can reduce uncertainty and encourage online participatory behavior [61], suggesting that people with higher levels of trust propensity are more willing to disclose information to others, including strangers [62,63]. Finally, trust propensity can effec tively predict consumers’ trust in e-shopping situations [61], which in turn is an important predictor of disclosure intentions [21,27].

Hypothesis 4. Trust propensity positively affects (a) perceived justice and (b) disclosure intentions.

## 3.5. Privacy invasion experience

We introduce privacy invasion experience as a second individual antecedent because of its demonstrated influence on privacy-related decision-making processes [21,47]. To evaluate new information, peo ple tend to draw on previous experiences [64]. If a person has suffered a privacy invasion in the past, he or she likely is more concerned about information privacy [1]. Existing literature concurs that prior experi ences largely shape privacy concerns [10–12,99], especially in the mo bile environment [65,66]. Moreover, future expectations based on past experiences drive behavior [67]. A prior negative experience with Internet advertising might lead people to avoid Internet advertising in the future [68], and adverse online privacy experiences can negatively affect patients’ information-sharing intentions in online cancer com munities [69]. Similar relationships of privacy invasion experience with disclosure intentions emerge in social networking sites [67]. In this study, we aim to replicate results from previous literature establishing a link between privacy experience and both privacy and disclosure by testing the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5. Privacy invasion experience negatively affects (a) perceived privacy and (b) disclosure intentions.

## 3.6. Contextual moderators: privacy feedback and choice

The term context encompasses “stimuli and phenomenon that sur round and thus exist in the environment external to the individual” [70 , p. 198]. It also can be classified according to four interpretations: as a technology system or platform, as a business model or business practice, as a sector or industry, and as a social domain [71]. Privacy feedback belongs to the first interpretation of context as a technology system or platform.

In this study, we use theory contextualization [71] to justify the moderating role of context. In particular, we engage in single-context theory contextualization, which involves incorporating contextual factors as antecedents of core constructs or moderators of relevant relation ships. Privacy-protective measures can be antecedents of disclosure outcomes, but we propose that the presence versus absence of privacy feedback also creates different contexts for users, which may influence their decision-making process. With this approach, we can study the interplay of several explanatory variables (perceived justice, perceived privacy) with relevant contextual factors. In addition, considering the inconsistent findings regarding the effectiveness of privacy-protective measures in past literature, we rely on this approach to enhance the explanatory power of the model [26]. Specifically, our model in corporates privacy feedback and choice as contextual factors that moderate the overall model relationships (i.e., moderating effects on H6–H10).

While privacy feedback gives users information about firms’ privacy practices, choice grants them control over methods to protect their privacy [4,47]. Notice and choice are two dimensions of the Fair In formation Principles [34], designed to enhance users’ privacy. Accord ingly, we propose that the moderating effects of privacy feedback and choice follow a similar pattern, such that they enhance the positive re lationships and reduce the negative relationships (e.g., between privacy invasion experience and perceived privacy) in the model. We present the arguments for privacy feedback next; parallel arguments for privacy choice would stem from the same logic.

Privacy feedback can satisfy users’ privacy needs, by signaling the honesty of the service provider and its respect for users’ privacy. As long as it informs users about data practices, they may regard the service provider as trustworthy. In addition, privacy feedback enables inter activity, so users can communicate their privacy preferences to service providers, which suggest the providers are paying attention to their needs and willing to make effort to support those needs. Therefore, the presence of privacy feedback should make them feel more comfortable and react to service providers by being more willing to disclose information.

Hypothesis 6. The provision of (i) privacy feedback and (ii) choice enhances the relationship between perceived justice and disclosure intention.

Hypothesis 7. The provision of (i) privacy feedback and (ii) choice enhances the relationship between perceived justice and perceived privacy.

Users also may be more likely to provide personal information when they sense control over the information collected. Privacy feedback of fers users such control, because it discloses the purposes for which the information is being collected and with whom it will be shared, which reveals limits on the access of external agents to personal information. With privacy feedback, users also can make more informed privacy decisions, so their perceptions of privacy might exert a more critical role in shaping their disclosure intentions. In the presence of privacy feed back therefore, users who have developed stronger privacy perceptions should be more comfortable about disclosing personal information.

Hypothesis 8. The provision of (i) privacy feedback and (ii) choice enhances the relationship between perceived privacy and disclosure intentions.

A belief that others are generally trustworthy should induce a similar belief that the methods used to develop and enact organizational de cisions are fair [56]. Thus, people with greater trust propensity may be inclined to assume that the provision of privacy feedback signals service providers’ efforts to create a fairer environment. The provision of pri vacy feedback should therefore prompt more information disclosure among users with greater trust propensity.

Hypothesis 9. The provision of (i) privacy feedback and (ii) choice enhances the relationships of trust propensity with (a) perceived justice and (b) disclosure intentions.

Users who have suffered from past privacy invasions may be more concerned about their information privacy and more motivated to consider privacy-related information in making informed decisions [47]. The provision of privacy feedback should satisfy their privacy needs, by giving them sufficient privacy-related information and detailed explanations about how their personal information might be used. With this information, users can reconsider their privacy decisions and adapt their privacy settings accordingly. The negative effects of prior privacy experiences even may be alleviated.

Hypothesis 10. The provision of (i) privacy feedback and (ii) choice enhances the effects of privacy invasion experience on (a) perceived privacy and (b) disclosure intentions.

## 3.7. Control variables

Various other factors can influence disclosure intentions. To reflect our primary theoretical focus, we include these factors as control vari ables to address the variance they might explain. Specifically, we control for age, gender, frequency and number of years using mobile shopping apps, as well as personal innovativeness.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Design of privacy feedback

To indicate justice, in line with justice theory, we theoretically attempt to link privacy feedback functions with procedural, informa tional, and interactional justice. To increase procedural justice [73], privacy feedback should include details about information collection and usage. To ensure informational justice [37], privacy feedback must sufficiently inform users about privacy practices. To achieve interac tional justice [74], privacy feedback should provide communication channels that enable users to express their feelings, doubts, and privacy preferences, which in turn helps service providers understand their privacy needs. More details are provided in Appendix A.

The user interface we designed for this study reflects insights from existing studies related to information feedback, with the guidance of a design space [75] that accounts for the small screens of mobile devices. The design space has been used in privacy and security research, such as in investigations of privacy-enhancing web browser technologies [76] and examinations of interfaces for anti-phishing systems [77]. We believe it provides appropriate guidance for our privacy feedback user interface design, as displayed in Appendix C.

## 4.2. Experimental design

We use an experimental vignette methodology, such that we craft three sets of vignettes that describe a mobile shopping scenario, taking place in a fictional mobile shopping app called YouH. The fictional app helps us avoid influences of extraneous factors, such as prior knowledge of the app. With these scenarios, we simulate the app’s functions, similarly to prior research [22]. Two vignettes manipulate the presence vs. absence of privacy feedback in the app (Fig. D[a] in Appendix D) while the third vignette manipulates the choice treatment, by offering participants the explicit choice to look at privacy feedback screens or not (Fig. D[b], Appendix D).

## 4.3. Measurements and pilot study

We adapted scales from prior research to fit our study context. In addition to measures of demographics and variables that were not spe cific to the scenario, we include variables to reflect each scenario. Similar to prior literature [13], we identify perceived justice as a secondorder construct, with the three first-order dimensions (procedural. informational, and interactional) as its reflective indicators. All the items and their original sources are in Appendix E. Because the original items were in English, we used back-translation to convert them into Chinese. Next, we tested the initial instrument in face-to-face interviews with 10 IS faculty members and graduate students and made some slight modifications, according to their feedback. In a pilot test with 80 graduate and undergraduate students, we confirm item clarity, verify the conciseness of the experimental procedure and instructions, and ensure the experiment can be efficiently executed.

## 4.4. Procedure and task

One week prior to the experiment, the participants provided de mographic data and responded to variables not specific to the scenario, such as their trust propensity, privacy invasion experiences, and per sonal innovativeness. Then, for the main experiment, to ensure a controlled environment, all participants were invited to physically come to the lab. To ensure each participant had the same external environ ment, the lab was empty, only containing some chairs and desks. We also ensured the participants could run the different experimental tasks without being disturbed by other people. In particular, to reduce as much as possible any bias, participants were asked to complete the experiment independently without communicating with other partici pants and without seeking additional information from their phones. To ensure a realistic experience, they used their own smartphones to install the YouH app. All the vignettes appeared directly in the app in the same order. Participants were asked to read the instructions carefully. We provided general information about YouH, including its main functions and the information it collects. Next, participants were randomly assigned to one of the three vignettes: without privacy feedback, with feedback, or with choice. At this stage, they completed a multi-step procedure, as detailed in Appendix F. Finally, a questionnaire gath ered their assessments of perceived justice, perceived privacy, disclosure intentions, and manipulation checks. As a final control, we examined the amount of time taken by each participant to complete the experiment. All participants took approximately the same amount of time (i.e. 15 min overall), ensuring everything went as expected for each participant.

## 4.5. Participants

We recruited participants by posting announcements on campuses of Chinese colleges and universities from July to November 2019. The announcements provided some background information about the study, without revealing the experimental treatments. To motivate participation, we also offered modest monetary awards and gifts. This recruitment effort yielded 486 responses with 162 assigned to each treatment. Among the respondents, 48.2% were men and 51.8% were women; Appendix G contains more detailed demographic information.

According to the Mann-Whitney tests, the gender ratio, age, and education level of the participants did not differ significantly across the three treatments. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that par ticipants assigned to each treatment also did not differ significantly in terms of their mobile shopping app usage, privacy experience, trust propensity, or personal innovativeness. The random assignment thus appears effective.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Measurement model

For the data analysis, we used a structural equation modeling partial least squares (SEM-PLS) method with the SmartPLS3.0 software pack age. We evaluated the measurement model by examining its convergent and discriminant validity. To check for item reliability, we examined the loadings of each item; the loadings exceeded 0.7 (see Table H1, Ap pendix H), with the exception of one personal innovativeness item (0.61) and one privacy experience item (0.66). We decided to keep them though, because they only fall below the threshold for one treatment and still exceed the recommended minimum value of 0.60 [78]. Overall, these results indicate acceptable reliability. Furthermore, the composite reliabilities of constructs with multiple indicators are greater than 0.70, and the average variances extracted (AVE) for the constructs exceed 0.50, in support of the convergent validity of the measurement model. With Tables H2–H4 of Appendix H, we can compare the diagonal with non-diagonal elements, revealing that all constructs share more variance with their indicators than with other constructs. Therefore, all item satisfy the requirements of discriminant validity. Finally, we used several methods (Appendix I), all of which suggest this study is relatively robust to common method biases.

## 5.2. Structural model

## 5.2.1. Test for the structural model

To test H1–H5, we merged the three treatments and conduct the model estimations using the PLS algorithm in SmartPLS3.0, with boot strapping (5000 samples, no sign changes, path-weighting scheme). Perceived justice is modeled as a second-order variable with three firstorder factors (procedural, interactional, and informational justice). The model estimation outcomes are depicted in Fig. 2. None of the control variables had a significant effect on disclosure, so we removed them from further analyses. The model explains a substantial amount of variance for disclosure intention $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 9 3 )$ ) and perceived privacy (R<sup>2</sup> $= 0 . 7 2 5 )$ . Perceived privacy has the highest influence on disclosure in tentions (compared to that of perceived justice), and it is affected by both perceived justice and privacy experience. Perceived justice is positively affected by trust propensity. Overall, the results support H1, H2, H3, H4a, and H5a.

## 5.2.2. Test for the moderating effect of privacy feedback

To test the moderating influence of privacy feedback, we used the MGA function in SmartPLS3.0 and a bootstrapping procedure. We compared the results for the two first treatments, reflecting the scenarios with or without privacy feedback (see Table 1).

Perceived justice has a significant positive effect on disclosure intention, with or without the provision of privacy feedback, but we find no significant enhancement due to feedback provision, so we reject H6 (i). Perceived justice does drive disclosure intentions, regardless of whether privacy feedback is provided. The provision of privacy feedback marginally $\left( p = . 0 5 8 \right)$ enhances the significant, strong association be tween perceived justice and perceived privacy (H7). Because the effect of justice is even more significant in the presence of feedback, we find partial support for H7(i). The relationship between perceived privacy and disclosure intentions is significant in both treatments. The correla. tion is stronger with privacy feedback, but the difference in path co efficients is not enough to be significant, so we must reject H8(i). The results confirm the important role of users’ privacy perceptions in influencing their disclosure, whether they receive privacy feedback or not. The path from trust propensity to perceived justice is insignificant for both treatments, in contrast with H9a(i). For H9b(i), we find support, because trust propensity has a significant positive effect on disclosure intentions when privacy feedback is available but an insignificant effect in the absence of feedback. The provision of privacy feedback thus ap pears to make users with greater trust propensity more willing to disclose information. In partial support of H10a(i), the path is significant in the absence of privacy feedback but insignificant in its presence. Providing privacy feedback appears to eliminate the negative impact of privacy invasion experience, but the path from privacy experience to disclosure intentions is insignificant for both treatments. Thus, privacy invasion experience has no significant, direct influence on disclosure intentions but might influence it through perceived privacy in the absence of privacy feedback.

## 5.2.3. Test for the moderating effect of choice

Moving beyond a comparison of participants who lack access to privacy feedback and those required to read it, we use the choice treatment to clarify the realistic influence and effectiveness of privacy feedback. In the YouH with choice treatment, 138 of the 162 partici pants chose to access and read the privacy feedback. We compare their input with the results for the 162 participants with privacy feedback to determine the effect of choice (see Table 1 in which the “without choice” corresponds to the “with privacy feedback" treatment. As these results show, the significance of some relationships is influenced by the addi tion of choice. Specifically, with choice, the direct effect of trust pro pensity on disclosure (H9b(ii)) becomes non-significant, but the impact of trust propensity on perceived justice becomes significant, in support of H9a(ii). When people have a choice, their trust propensity does not affect their disclosure directly but does so indirectly through perceived justice. With choice, the effect of privacy invasion experience on perceived privacy also becomes significantly negative, in support of H10a(ii).

## 6. Discussions and implications

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

With this study, we examine the impact of perceived privacy, perceived justice, trust propensity, and privacy experience on disclosure intentions while also considering the moderating effects of two contextual factors, privacy feedback and choice.

We first find that both perceived justice and perceived privacy exert important influences on disclosure intentions. Most variance in disclo sure intentions (i.e. 69.3%) is explained by these two variables, with perceived privacy being the most influential, which confirms its role as a key determinant of disclosure intentions, regardless of whether privacy feedback is provided or not. Contrary to the majority of prior studies in the area, which all used privacy concerns as a proxy for privacy, in this study we focused on users’ overall attitudes to privacy using the concept of perceived privacy instead. Compared with existing work related to the relationship between privacy concerns and disclosure intentions (e.g. [11,21,23,95,96,97,98], the effect of perceived privacy on disclosure intentions found in our study is positive and much stronger, which confirms privacy as a key factor driving disclosure intentions. As this variable has not been widely investigated in the past, this evidence of its key role as an antecedent of disclosure intentions is highly pertinent.

72.5% of the variance in perceived privacy is explained by prior privacy experiences and perceived justice, though the latter has a greater role, regardless of whether privacy feedback is provided or not. Compared with extant work related to the relationships between different dimensions of perceived justice (distributive, procedural and interactional) and privacy concerns [41,72,94], in this study we considered perceived justice as a second-order variable with three first order factors (procedural, interactional, and informational justice). While some dimensions of justice did not influence privacy concerns in previous literature (e.g. Procedural justice [72] or Distributive and Interactional justice [94]), all three dimensions of justice influence perceived privacy in our study. We also find that the effect of perceived justice on privacy is much stronger in our study, which confirms perceived justice as a key determinant of perceived privacy.

Interestingly, while past literature found a strong negative effect of privacy invasion experiences on privacy concerns [21,99], in our study, we find that the negative effect of privacy invasion experiences on perceived privacy is weak. That is, compared with other variables, pri vacy invasion experience is not a key factor in influencing (perceived) privacy. This maybe because perceived privacy does not refer to the negative aspects of privacy, but to an overall attitude to privacy including positive aspects.

In the absence of privacy feedback, both perceived privacy and perceived justice exert significant, positive impacts on disclosure in tentions, explaining only 27.9% of its variance. When privacy feedback is available, in addition to perceived justice and privacy, the effect of trust propensity on disclosure intentions becomes significantly positive, such that the variance in disclosure increases to 46.5%. The addition of privacy feedback thus enhances explanations of disclosure intention. In particular, users with greater trust propensity are more willing to pro vide information when they receive privacy feedback.

We also find that perceived justice exerts a stronger influence on perceived privacy in the presence of privacy feedback. Perceived justice is the only significant influence on perceived privacy in the model with privacy feedback, with explanatory power of 41%. Thus, perceived justice has a critical role in determining users’ privacy perceptions, and its importance increases even more with the provision of privacy feed back. Privacy feedback can thus induce users who perceive greater justice to feel enhanced level of privacy.

We also find some interesting results related to the antecedents of perceived privacy. In particular, previous privacy experience has a negative impact on users’ perceived privacy without the provision of privacy feedback, but the effect shifts with the provision of privacy feedback. Although users who have experienced privacy invasions are more worried about their privacy and perceive less privacy overall, the provision of privacy feedback may alleviate this problem.

In summary, the results affirm the effectiveness of privacy feedback. It can enhance users’ perceived justice and make them feel more comfortable, even if they have experienced privacy invasions in the past. Such privacy feedback also can encourage users to disclose information while still protecting their privacy, especially if users already exhibit a high level of trust.

In a broader perspective, if privacy protection measures sufficiently enhance users’ justice perceptions, the positive effects of some factors, such as trust propensity, on disclosure intention might be enhanced, while the negative effects of other factors, such as those related to past privacy invasion experience, may be alleviated. This emphasizes the usefulness of providing users with effective privacy protection measures, such as privacy feedback, in the m-commerce environment.

The choice treatment further clarifies whether users really want or need privacy feedback. Somewhat surprisingly, most of the participants chose to read the privacy feedback, which indicates that users are interested in such content. The comparison of the treatments with and without choice also reveals that providing privacy feedback without choice produces a direct effect of trust propensity on disclosure in tentions, but this effect becomes mediated by perceived justice when the app offers choice. This finding aligns with the central and peripheral routes in the elaboration likelihood model [20]. Privacy feedback acts as a signal and persuasion attempt. If they are not asked whether they want to access this feedback, participants likely have minor motivation to think about $\mathbf { i t } ,$ so they engage in superficial processing, focused on surface features such as the presence or attractiveness of the feedback (peripheral cues), without attending to its content. In turn, they might undertake a behavioral change (e.g., disclosure intention), but the change likely is only temporary, susceptible to fading and counterat tacks (peripheral route). But if they are asked about their willingness to access the privacy feedback, users might sense a stronger motivation to think about the message and undertake deeper processing, focused more on the content (argument quality). Their resulting cognitive response (more perceived justice) could drive behavioral change (more willing ness to disclose), which would be more resistant to fading or counter attacks (central route).

When participants are without choice, privacy experience has a nonsignificant effect on perceived privacy, which becomes significantly negative with the addition of choice. That is, the more negative expe riences people have with privacy, the more their perceptions of privacy decline. If given a choice to access privacy feedback, users’ motivation to deliberate on privacy-related information might be boosted, such that they engage deeper thinking about privacy. People who have suffered more privacy violations also might remember more of the negative ex periences, which could diminish their current perceptions of privacy.

In summary, providing choice to users might enhance their motiva tion to think more deeply about privacy-related information, including the content of the privacy feedback and their memories of previous privacy experiences, both of which leave them more conscious of their privacy-related decision. Furthermore, when asked for their opinion (choice), users with greater trust propensity tend to perceive more jus tice, so they are more willing to disclose personal data. These findings confirm some conclusions from prior literature [4]: If service providers proactively work to protect user privacy, such as by informing users of their privacy practices, users are more willing to disclose their personal information. For users, there is of course always the risk that companies will not follow the privacy rules they have claimed to follow. This risk is reduced however by the consequences it would have on the company if such an unfair practice happens to a user who starts to communicate about this: reduced trust among users, reputation damages and financial loss.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

This study makes several important contributions to existing research on privacy and justice.

First, we present privacy feedback and choice as two contextual factors and examine their moderating effects. We know of few studies that include disclosure to an organizational entity as a dependent vari able, while also noting contextual factors as moderators, and only two studies offer evidence of significant moderating effects [21,23]. Thus, deeper investigations of contextual factors are necessary. In particular, only a few contextual moderators have been studied until now, such as industry, website attributes, requester properties, type of data, amount of data, and disclosure incentives [21–25]. No prior studies test the contextual moderating effects of privacy-protective measures. With a privacy-protective approach, manifested as with privacy feedback and with privacy choice treatments, we confirm significant moderating ef fects in some of the model relationships. Moreover, by causally modeling the antecedents of disclosure intention, we provide preliminary theo retical and empirical insights into the dynamic structural relationships of these factors and the moderating influence of feedback and choice factors. Perceived justice has much more effect on perceived privacy when privacy feedback is provided, so the role of perceived justice de pends on the presence or absence of effective privacy-protective mea sures. Even if a relationship in our model appears significant with or without privacy feedback, its relative importance changes, depending on the provision of privacy feedback. The role of individual attribute such as trust propensity and privacy experience also depend on, not just the presence or absence of effective privacy-protective measures but also on the provision of choice.

Second, this study provides one of the first applications of justice theory to design privacy-protective measures, in the form of privacy feedback, and thereby promote users’ justice perceptions related to digital services. In general, IS privacy literature deals with explaining and predicting causes, rather than designing solutions. Heeding calls for more design research that can address ways to protect information privacy [16–19], we theoretically link privacy feedback with three types of justice (procedural, informational, and interactional). On the basis of these theoretical insights, we design a privacy feedback solution that simultaneously provides all three types of justice, in line with both existing research and the design space of privacy notices. The findings contribute to justice theory, by showing that it can be applied to privacy protective measures. Thus, we extend the application scope of justice theory and shrink the gap between theory and technological develop ment, while also offering a new lens on the design of privacy-protective measures.

Third, we attempt to identify the true effect of privacy feedback by giving users a choice to access it or not. Previous experimental studies mainly address the effectiveness of privacy-protective methods, by controlling their presence versus absence [14,29,44] or manipulating high versus low levels of their features [47], but they do not specify users’ actual interest in these methods. For this study, we acknowledge that users rarely read privacy content, and in line with the elaboration likelihood model, we find that users focus mainly on surface features, such as the presence or attractiveness of the feedback, if they have no choice. But if they must explicitly choose whether to read privacy feedback or not, users appear more motivated to pay attention to its contents, and then make more conscious privacy decisions. The results emphasize the need to identify the real-world effectiveness of privacyprotective methods.

Fourth, we formally replicate some relationships already tested in previous work, using a new proxy to measure privacy (i.e. perceived privacy) and testing the relationships using a new setting (m-commerce in China). Previous privacy studies present privacy concerns as a core component of information privacy and investigate their antecedents and behavioral outcomes. In this study, we explore the antecedents of users disclosure intentions using perceived privacy instead of privacy con cerns. This choice enables to measure an overall attitude toward privacy instead of focusing only on the negative aspects related to privacy (i.e. concerns). Our replication attempt provides interesting results, vali dating the interest of validation research in IS. Specifically, using perceived privacy as a proxy of privacy instead of privacy concerns, we confirmed privacy as a key determinant of disclosure intentions, and perceived justice as a key driving factor of privacy. In addition, and contrary to past literature using privacy concerns, privacy invasion experience does not occur as a main factor influencing privacy in our study. By using replication, our study not only answers the call for more replication research in IS [101], it also enhances the robustness of past privacy research (e.g. regarding the relationships between justice and privacy and between privacy and disclosure) while providing future research avenues, especially regarding the influence of past privacy experience on privacy-related decision-making.

Overall, it is important to study the privacy implications of privacyprotective approaches according to a justice lens. Insufficient research exists to explain how technological approaches might influence privacy too. With this study, researchers gain a conceptual tool for verifying the effectiveness of privacy-protective approaches.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

This study has implications for various actors in the online commerce industry, such as practitioners, service providers, privacy advocates, and government legislators. For practitioners, under growing pressure to protect consumer privacy, our study highlights the importance of increasing users’ knowledge of privacy-related practices and suggests new methods that could encourage users’ information disclosure. In this respect, our findings ask service providers to pay attention to users privacy perceptions, for example by taking measures to enhance users justice perceptions. This could be done by providing detailed informa tion about data collection and usage, or using communication channels that enable users to express their privacy preferences. More specifically, our study encourages service providers to focus on improving privacy perceptions by providing privacy feedback and choice features that could enhance the perceived fairness of the environment. Such recom mendations could also be considered as an interesting opportunity for application developers who have generally limited their efforts to pri vacy statements and permission-based approaches until now.

Privacy advocates and government legislators also might encourage service providers to offer more privacy feedback to users, increase the transparency surrounding their privacy practices, and motivate users to think more deeply about privacy-related information to make more conscious privacy decisions. Instead of burdening users, such as by posting unreadable privacy statements or popping up privacy notices to obtain user consent, service providers should take more responsibility for privacy protections. Offering users the choice to access privacy feedback could shift their role, from passive acceptors to proactive su pervisors, with the added benefit of making service providers appear more trustworthy. Such outcomes would likely benefit companies reputations in the long run, which may help them gain access to more user data, with win–win outcomes for both parties. Overall, our results encourage all stakeholders to regard privacy protection as a source of competitive advantage, instead of a burden, and invest more effort in it, which would also promote the healthy development of the online commerce industry overall.

## 6.4. Limitations and further research

Some limitations of this study create opportunities for furthe research. First, we only verify the effectiveness of our method in the context of mobile shopping applications in general, using one specific app design in particular, which may limit its generalizability. With the recognition that our approach might not be suitable in all privacyrelated contexts, continued research should design other privacy feed back features and verify their effectiveness in various other app designs and contexts. Second, our sample consists of university students, who represent a large portion of the actual population of m-commerce users in China. Still, additional research should include more diverse pop ulations, from all over the world. Third, we used a controlled lab experiment to test our approach, which has both advantages and con cerns; interactions with hypothetical scenarios might not represent people’s actual experiences in the world. A field experiment would be helpful to confirm our findings. Fourth, the moderators we study are not exhaustive, and continued research would benefit from a broader consideration of potential moderators, including government regula tions, industry norms, or other privacy-protective measures. Fifth, one variable in our model (i.e., trust propensity) was measured with a twoitem scale already used in the literature [29]. While we are confident it did not affect too much our results, using more robust scales in future studies could only improve the robustness of our results.

## 6.5. Conclusions

This study examines the influence of both privacy and justice per ceptions on users’ disclosure intentions. In accordance with justice theory, we designed a privacy feedback feature and examined the moderating effects of both privacy feedback and choice features on users’ privacy-related decision-making processes, based on theory con textualization. Perceived justice is confirmed to have a critical role in determining users’ perceived privacy, which, in turn, is a key factor for predicting disclosure intentions. The importance of perceived justice increases significantly with the provision of privacy feedback, which in turn enhances users’ information disclosure intentions. The provision of choice also can help users make more conscious privacy decisions. These study results encourage companies to invest in privacy protection, as a form of added value, which can provide a basis for competitive advan tages. We hope other researchers use these findings to advance further insights into this IS privacy domain.

## Appendix A. Detailed functions of privacy feedback

Most service providers attempt to establish procedural justice by posting privacy statements, an approach that might signal transparency by giving users privacy information before requesting disclosure of their own information [14,15]. However, few users read these statements, because of their length and lack of meaningful choices [5]. Furthermore, users do not understand how their information is treated after disclosure, such that procedura] justice cannot persist continually while the services are being used. Thus, privacy feedback should provide users with actionable information about how their personal information is handled while they use the services.

To offer informational justice, privacy feedback must provide users with sufficient information about topics important to them. The minimum information in a privacy statement usually includes the categories of personal data being collected and processed, why data processing is necessary, and whether data will be disclosed to third parties [5]. Accordingly, our privacy feedback includes these three types of feedback information.

People sense they are treated fairly if they receive responses [15]. Facilitating interactions also is essential to create communities and build ties [81]. Privacy feedback should enable two-way, dynamic information exchanges between users and service providers, such as through privacy settings and customer service. To reflect interactional justice, the privacy feedback should reveal information about the privacy practices of service providers while simultaneously facilitating users’ expressions of their privacy preferences or doubts, which in turn lead to responses from the service providers, leading to interactivity between users and service providers.

## Appendix C. Detailed design of user interface

The design space includes four dimensions: timing, channel, modality, and control. Timing refers to when the feedback is provided. Two principles should be followed. Feedback should be provided on demand, when users actively seek it. Users also should be aware of it, without being disturbed. In our design therefore, a “Feedback” button appears on the homepage of the app so that users could access the details whenever they wish.

Channel refers to how the feedback is delivered, such as by a primary, secondary, or public channel [75]. The provision of privacy feedback is intended to show that service providers respect user privacy, so we use a primary channel, as identified by the design space, and the feedback is provided during the user’s interaction with the system.

Modality refers to modes of interaction, such as visual, auditory, or machine-readable notices; the visual form, as text, images, and icons, is the most common. Privacy feedback should combine text and images. The small screens of mobile devices also prompted us to use a multilayered approach to display the feedback information [75]. Drawing on current existing app designs, we include four layers. The first reveals the classifications of collected information into four categories: equipment, personal, transactional, or other information. The first layer displays these four categories and the amount of updated feedback information for each category (Fig. C1[a]). The second layer indicates who has accessed each type of information in a category and the frequency of their visits [82,83] through a pie chart (Fig. C1[b]). Users who want to know why a particular party has accessed thei information can click on the corresponding pie chart and enter the third layer, which summarizes these purposes and the access frequency for each purpose (Fig. C1[c]). If users wish to obtain even more details, they can click on a particular purpose, which produces detailed information about the time and purpose for each visit (Fig. C1[d]).

Control refers to the provision of privacy choices or control options. Control options can make the information actionable and enable users to express their consent and privacy preferences [49,50,75]. Therefore, we ensure the privacy feedback includes privacy preference settings and customer service in the first layer of the privacy feedback interface (Fig. C1[a]), which allows for two-way, dynamic information exchanges between users and service providers. Clicking the privacy settings button reveals a user interface, for expressing privacy preferences (Fig. C2[a]). For thei convenience, users can set their privacy preferences by toggling switches for each item in this list. In addition, they can enter the customer service interface by clicking the customer service button, as shown in Fig. C2[b]. Then they can express privacy needs and suggestions; service providers in turn gain a better understanding of the privacy needs of users and can provide appropriate responses that signal their respect for users’ privacy.

## Appendix F. Experimental steps to view manipulated vignettes

All participants first saw the app homepage (Fig. D[al. Appendix D). Then a registration and login interface requested their phone number, email. and password. Although they did not need to reveal this information, they were primed by the information requested by the app. At the end of the study, we measured their willingness to disclose such information. They also received notice about the collection of their location and contact in formation through a notice and consent form, similar to those used widely in mobile apps. The participants assigned to the YouH app with privacy feedback condition were requested to click the “FEEDBACK” button on the homepage and browse each page of the privacy feedback feature; this step was skipped for participants assigned to the ‘without feedback’ treatment. For the ‘with choice’ treatment, instead of requesting that participant engage with privacy feedback, we provided a choice page (Fig. D[b], Appendix D) that let participants decide whether they wanted to browse the privacy feedback pages or not. A pop-up dialog box offered users two choices: (1) click the “FEEDBACK” button at the lower right of the page to acces the feedback pages or (2) click the “NEXT” button at the upper right of the page, which redirected them to the next step of the procedure. At this point, they saw an order confirmation for purchasing virtual goods and a list of information requests to use a “part-time job” personalized service that required personal information, such as gender, date of birth, interests, address, and educational background.

## Appendix I. Check for common method bias

In Harman's single-factor test, the first factor for YouH with and without privacy feedback and YouH with choice extracted 22.06%, 18.67%, and 30.19% of the variance, respectively, indicating no significant threat of common method variance. Moreover, no general factor accounted for more than 50% of the variance [87]. We also conducted a partial correlation test [88,89], such that we added the variable with the highest explained variance from the factor analysis as an additional independent variable. It did not significantly change the variance explained in the dependent variables. Finally, we undertook efforts to reduce acquiescence bias. For example, the order of the items was randomized in the questionnaire, to limi participants’ ability to detect underlying construct patterns that could affect their responses. We also promised the participants anonymity and asked them to answer the questionnaires honestly, to reduce the likelihood of social desirability biases [87].
