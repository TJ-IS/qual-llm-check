# Effect of sponsorship disclosure on online consumer responses to positive reviews: The moderating role of emotional intensity and tie strength

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online reviews Sponsorship disclosure Emotional intensity Tie strength Language expectancy theory Moderated moderation model

## A B S T R A C T

Sponsored reviews have become prevalent on e-commerce websites, third-party review platforms, and social media. To explore the interactive effect between sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews, emotional intensity, and tie strength on online review credibility and consumers’ purchase intention, this study proposed a moderated moderation model. In an online scenario-based experiment, we found that sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews negatively influences consumers’ purchase intention, and review credibility mediates the relationship. In addition, emotional intensity strengthens the negative effects of sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews on review credibility and purchase intention, while tie strength weakens the moderating effects of emotional intensity.

## 1. Introduction

Online reviews are one of the main forms of electronic word-ofmouth (eWOM) communication that may influence consumers’ pur chase decision making [1–5]. Given the increasingly paramount role of online reviews in the e-commerce paradigm, online marketers offer in centives to encourage existing customers to write positive reviews about products or services on e-commerce websites or third-party review platforms, a tactic known as sponsoring or compensating for reviews [6,7]. In tandem with the rapid development of new information communication technology, most e-commerce platforms have incorpo rated the service functions of social media (i.e., Facebook, Twitter, and WeChat) to increase the platforms’ capacity to connect with potential consumers [8,9]. Technology integration helps online marketers incentivize social media users to share experiences, knowledge, and recommendations regarding products or services with potential con sumers. For example, U.S. firms spent approximately \$38 billion on sponsored content on social media in 2020 [10].

Previous studies have investigated the importance of incentives in influencing consumers’ decisions in the contexts of traditional word-ofmouth (WOM) [11–14], electronic referrals [15,16], online reviews [6,7,17], and other user-generated content [18–20]. Regarding online reviews, prior studies have found that positive reviews favorably influence consumers’ attitudes and purchase intention [21–23]. How ever, recent studies have found that the persuasiveness of positive reviews might decrease when a sponsorship message is disclosed [6,7]. For example, Jensen and Yetgin [7] found that the prominent disclosure of reviewer compensation reduces reviewer credibility. Similarly, Kim et al. [6] concluded that sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews in creases suspicion about reviewers’ motives and decreases consumers attitudes and purchase intentions.

Considering the benefits of positive reviews and the drawbacks of sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews, it is important to identify conditional factors that affect consumers’ message processing when they experience sponsored positive reviews. Extant studies have suggested that affect and emotions play a significant role in readers’ judgment of online reviews [24–28], website quality [29], and question-and-answer services [30]. For example, emotional intensity is a key linguistic feature that influences potential consumers’ attitudes and purchase decisions regarding reviewed products or services [24,27]. In addition, previous work has suggested that the role of emotional intensity in influencing consumers’ purchase decisions might be related to the anonymity of reviewers [24,27]. Therefore, we posit that the tie strength between a reviewer and a consumer might interact through the language attributes of review content to influence consumers’ processing of online reviews in the context of social media. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate

two research questions:

RQ1. How does emotional intensity moderate the relationship be tween sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews and consumers’ atti tudes and purchase intentions?

RQ2. How does the tie strength between reviewers and consumers influence the moderating effect of emotional intensity?

In an effort to extend the literature and build on language expectancy theory (LET), this study is an early attempt to examine the influence of emotional intensity and tie strength on the persuasive power of spon sored positive reviews. Specifically, in this study, we sought to unveil the moderating role of emotional intensity in the paths from sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews to review credibility and purchase inten tion. We also further explored how tie strength moderates the moder ating effect of emotional intensity. To test and validate the research model, and to achieve scientific rigor and realism, we employed an online scenario experiment and collected 569 valid responses from participants through wjx.com, a leading professional data services company in China.

This study provides several significant contributions to the existing literature on online reviews. First, it extends previous research on online reviews by identifying emotional intensity as an important review feature that might influence consumers’ processing of sponsored re views. Second, based on LET, we examined the importance of the interconnection between reviewers and consumers in consumers’ online review processing by empirically testing the joint moderating effect of emotional intensity and tie strength. The findings provide important insights into online review persuasion mechanisms for online retailers and e-commerce platforms.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical background

## 2.1. Sponsored reviews

Sponsored reviews refer to consumer-generated content incentivized by merchants [6,7]. Sponsored reviews include sponsorship features and review attributes [7]. Consumers process information related to spon sorship messages and review content simultaneously [10]. In terms of the content attributes of a sponsored review. valence. which refers to the positive or negative evaluation direction of a review content, is considered an important feature of online reviews that influences an individual’s purchase decision [22,31]. Some studies have argued that positive reviews have a beneficial impact on consumers’ attitudes and purchase intentions toward a product [21,23], and influence the growth of product sales [5,32]. Regarding the features of sponsorship messages, previous research has found that sponsorship disclosure might arouse consumers’ suspicions of or doubt about sponsored content and lead to weak persuasion $[ 6 , 1 8 , 2 0 ]$ . For example, consumers who are suspicious of reviews are less likely to purchase recommended products or services [6]. Thus, due to the opposite effects of sponsorship disclosure and positive reviews, it is important to identify the conditional factors that influence consumers’ processing of sponsored positive reviews.

Emotion is an important factor in influencing online consumers judgment and choice [33–35]. In particular, in IS research, the intensity of positive or negative emotions embedded in user-generated content has been found to affect individuals’ responses to targets [24,30]. High emotional intensity in a positive review conveys positive information about products or services. However, a sponsored positive review has been found to lead to consumers’ distrust of the review [6]. Thus, we argue that high emotional intensity in a sponsored positive review might increase consumers' suspicions about sponsored reviewers' motives. Previous researchers have also examined the impact of tie strength on traditional WOM and eWOM communication $[ 1 0 - 1 3 , 1 6 , 3 6 , 3 7 ]$ and found that the extent of suspicion might be reduced if a consumer has a strong relationship with the reviewer. Nevertheless. few studies have focused on the joint effect of tie strength and the emotional intensity of a review on the efficacy of sponsored reviews. Therefore, in this study, we examined the combined effects of emotional intensity and tie strength on the relationship between sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews and consumers’ responses.

## 2.2. Language expectancy theory

Burgoon et al. [38] proposed and developed LET to explore the persuasive effects of product reviews. LET describes the effects of lin guistic message features from a group of individuals on attitude change [27,38,39] and focuses on credibility, verbal strategies, and persuasion [40,41]. LET has been widely used to examine the effect of review properties on perceptions of review credibility and behaviors toward a product or service [24,27,42,43]. The theory has been extended to other research fields, including health communication [44,45] and political communication [39,46,47].

According to LET, language behavior is rule-based, meaning that individuals develop expectations about language behaviors based on cultural and societal norms [24,40,41], and these expectations affect individuals’ acceptance or rejection of persuasive messages. Researchers suggest that receivers might perceive two types of expectancy violations: positive violations and negative violations. If message senders’ language satisfies or exceeds message receivers’ expectations—called a positive violation—receivers might accept persuasive messages. In contrast, if the communication language falls short of message receivers’ expect ations—called a negative violation—the receivers might reject the message. LET suggests that message receivers’ expectations are influ enced by the message senders’ characteristics, the relationship between the senders and receivers, or a specific context [27,40–43,48].

Researchers investigating the interaction effects of expectations and message characteristics on persuasion have noted that some senders are permitted to use language of various intensity levels in persuading re ceivers [38]. The range of persuasion techniques a sender uses without violating receivers’ expectations is called bandwidth [24]. Senders from privileged groups might have the freedom—called wide bandwidth—to use a variety of persuasion techniques and remain effective. On the other hand, senders from nonprivileged groups are restricted to employing less intense language—called narrow bandwidth—in persuading re ceivers. In an online review context, identifying information of sender groups is not displayed to the potential buyers, so senders have a narrow bandwidth to employ persuasive techniques. Using intense language that might deviate from this narrow bandwidth results in a negative expectancy violation that affects potential buyers’ perceived reviewer credibility [38,40]. For example, previous researchers have suggested that anonymous product reviews with high affective intensity might result in a negative expectancy violation that decreases reviewer cred ibility [24].

Sponsored reviews, which are incentivized by merchants, might arouse message receivers’ suspicions about the review [6,11,18] and are expected to influence message receivers’ expectations. Although LET has been applied to online reviews in the e-commerce context, previous studies have not applied LET to explain the effects of sponsored reviews. LET provides a suitable theoretical foundation for examining the inter active effects of language intensity and the relationship between mes sage senders and receivers on message persuasiveness. Specifically, this study aims to explore the effects of sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews, emotional intensity, and tie strength on review credibility and purchase intention.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

## 3.1. Research model

Based on the existing literature and theory, we propose the research model depicted in Fig. 1. The aim of this model is to understand the relationships of sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews, emotional intensity, tie strength, review credibility, and potential consumers purchase intentions toward reviewed products or services. In addition to the underlying key constructs, we postulate that review credibility and purchase intention may be influenced by gender, age, education, and online shopping experience. Furthermore, previous researchers have controlled for the impacts of trust tendency, product involvement, and brand attitude on review credibility and purchase intention [7,24]. To better understand the interactive effects of emotional intensity and tie strength on the persuasiveness of sponsored positive reviews, we included these as control variables.

## 3.2. Sponsorship disclosure, review credibility, and purchase intention

Sponsorship disclosure plays an important role in influencing con sumers’ attitudes and purchase intentions. When social media users are exposed to a sponsored message, they might recognize this situation as being targeted by marketing communication. Social media users might reject a message when they recognize it as being compensated from an external source [18]. This advertising recognition activates individuals persuasion knowledge [49], which results in skepticism and resistance to persuasive messages [18]. In the existing literature, some studies have also found that resistance to sponsored messages might trigger users negative attitudes toward products and decrease users’ adoption of the recommended behaviors [50,51]. Regarding sponsored reviews, re searchers have demonstrated that disclosing sponsorship in a review reduces the review’s credibility [7], and sponsored reviews decrease potential consumers’ purchase intention toward recommended products or services [6]. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Sponsorship disclosure of a positive review has a negative effect on review credibility (H1a) and purchase intention (H1b).

Review credibility reflects the extent to which a consumer perceives the message provided in an online review as real, trustworthy, and believable, and it is an important factor that influences consumers’ de cision making [24,52]. For example, previous researchers have found that review credibility positively impacts consumers’ product attitudes and subsequent purchase decisions [52,53]. In addition, several studies on sponsored reviews have concluded that social media users might be likely to purchase a recommended product or service when they trust the messages posted on social media [10,18,54]. Therefore, we argue that the relationship between sponsorship disclosure and potential con sumers’ purchase intentions is mediated by review credibility. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2. Review credibility mediates the relationship between sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews and consumers’ purchase intentions.

## 3.3. Moderating role of emotional intensity

Emotion, as an important language feature, has attracted the atten tion of researchers in the field of information systems. Previous researchers have found that the general or discrete emotion of a review is associated with consumers’ online purchase behavior [24,25,42,55]. For example, according to LET [40,41], language is a rule-based system in which people develop an appropriate language using expected norms in a given situation. Ordinarily, messages conforming to language usage norms have positive effects, and an inappropriate language style might decrease recipients’ attitudes toward persuasive messages [42].

The emotional intensity of a positive review is the degree to which reviewers express positive language about the products or services in the review. Previous researchers have shown that speakers who are less credible are more persuasive with low-intensity appeals [40,41]. A receiver might consider a message with high emotional intensity a violation of language usage norms when the message comes from a distrusted sender. In the context of e-commerce platforms, consumers might regard high emotional intensity in a sponsored positive review as inappropriate language usage; thus, the review would result in fewer consumer responses [24,42]. Previous researchers have suggested that a sponsorship disclosure message may arouse consumers’ suspicions about the poster’s ulterior motives [6,11,18]. According to LET, online sponsored reviewers might have a narrow bandwidth of persuasion techniques because consumers are skeptical of sponsored reviewers motives, and this narrow bandwidth might cause consumers to consider high-intensity language used in a positive review to be a negative violation of expectations. Therefore, we infer that emotional intensity might influence consumers’ attitudes and behavioral reactions to sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews. Because high emotional in tensity in a sponsored review leads to a negative expectancy violation, we argue that high emotional intensity will strengthen the negative in fluence of sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews on review credibility and purchase intention.

H3. Emotional intensity strengthens the negative effect of sponsorship disclosure of a positive review on review credibility (H3a) and purchase intention (H3b), such that the negative effect of sponsorship disclosure of a positive review is stronger when emotional intensity is high.

## 3.4. Moderating role of tie strength

Tie strength is defined as the level of the bond between network members [56]. In the context of social media, the social connection between users is categorized as a strong primary tie $( \mathrm { i . e . , a }$ close friend or family member), a weak tie (i.e., an acquaintance), or no tie (i.e., a stranger) [10,36,54,57]. Previous studies have shown that social media users are more likely to be persuaded by people with whom they have a close relationship [36,54,58]. For example, Chu and Kim [59] found that users’ eWOM, including opinion seeking, opinion giving, and opinion passing, was significantly predicted by tie strength, homophily, trust, and interpersonal influence.

According to LET, language with high emotional intensity can be applied to communication among individuals with intimate social con nections [40,41,60]. In the context of social media, online experiences and product recommendations from strong ties are more likely to be considered credible. Thus, when reviewers have strong ties with con sumers, consumers might be less suspicious about a sponsored positive review with high emotional intensity. In contrast, when reviewers have no ties or weak ties with consumers, high emotional intensity violates the expectations of persuasive messages [24], and consumers might be more resistant to the persuasive power of a sponsored positive review. Therefore, we argue that tie strength negatively moderates the moder ating effect of emotional intensity.

H4. Tie strength weakens the moderation role of emotional intensity in the relationship between sponsorship disclosure of a positive review and review credibility (H4a) and purchase intention (H4b), such that the moderating effect of emotional intensity is smaller when tie strength is strong.

## 4. Research method

To examine the proposed hypotheses, we conducted a scenario experiment with 2 (sponsorship disclosure of positive review: yes vs. no) × 2 (emotional intensity: high and low) × 3 (tie strength: strong vs. weak vs. no tie) between-subjects factors. Scenario-based experiments have been widely applied in various research topics, including infor mation security [61,62], illegal or deviant behavior [63–65], and online reviews [7,24,35,61,66,67]. By asking respondents to read a scenario and imagine themselves in the hypothesized context, previous re searchers have found that scenario-based experiments can yield valid truthful measurements of respondents’ behavioral intention [66,68]. Therefore, in this study, we used a scenario-based experiment to investigate respondents’ behavioral reactions to sponsored online reviews.

## 4.1. Stimuli materials

For this study, we designed stimuli materials based on the social media posts of a real retail product, a Sony digital camera. The materials had the same product information, including the product image, price, color, and manufacturer’s description, while each stimulus material was embedded in a positive review with different sponsorship disclosure statements, emotional intensity levels, and tie strength degrees, following previous research.

First, the manipulation for the emotional intensity of the review was adapted from Jensen et al. [24]; the valences of the two reviews were positive, and the information is the same. In the high emotional intensity condition, strong positive emotional words such as “like” and “love” were embedded in the review’s text. In the low emotional intensity condition, positive words without strong emotion were embedded in the review’s text (see Table 1). Second, we manipulated sponsorship disclosure following Jensen and Yetgin [7]. In the sponsorship disclo sure condition, the following sentence was displayed on a separate line at the end of the review: “The reviewer received payment and a free product for writing this review.” The no-disclosure condition did not contain this information. Third, following Koo [36], we manipulated three levels of tie strength. For the strong-tie condition. the review was supposed to come from a close friend of the participants; for the weak-tie condition, the respondents were told that the product review was done by a casual acquaintance. For the no-tie condition, the respondents were told that the product review came from a stranger. Table 1 displays the features and descriptions of the manipulation variables.

To ensure the reliability of review information in the stimuli mate rials, one post-test with 52 samples was conducted to check whether the two reviews were equally informative and credible when not accounting for the emotional intensity. Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which the description of the camera features (such as the appearance, focusing system, etc.) in the review was informative or credible. ## 4.2. Participants and procedures

Participants were recruited from wjx.com, a leading professional online data collection company in China. The company sent out email invitations to 3106 users and informed them of the purpose of the experiment and the anonymity of their responses. Participating in the experiment took about 15 min, and each participant was rewarded 11 RMB (almost 1.5 USD) when they accepted the invitation and completed the survey. A total of 647 participants completed the survey. Seventyeight responses were excluded because the respondents did not care fully read the stimulus material during the experiment and failed the attention check. Thus, the final sample included 569 responses.

Demographic information is outlined in Table 2.

In this experiment, we collected data in three stages. First, partici pants filled out a questionnaire for control variables, including their personal information (i.e., gender, age, and education), trust propensity for online reviews, involvement with digital cameras, and brand atti tudes toward Sony. Second, participants were randomly assigned to one of 12 conditions. In each condition, participants were asked to imagine that they were searching for information with the intention of pur chasing a new digital camera and saw a Sony digital camera review posted on a social media site. The experimental product information and online reviews were then presented. The participants read the reviews that differed in sponsorship disclosure statement, the extent of emotional intensity, and tie strength. Third, the participants were asked to read the assigned reviews carefully and then answer the questions that followed.

## 4.3. Measurement

In this study, we used previous measurement scales for the depen dent variables and the control variables. Table 3 lists the measures and sources of all latent variables. Among them are the measurement items for trust tendency, product involvement, brand attitude, and review credibility from the scale developed by Jensen and Yetgin [7]. The four measurement items for purchase intention were adapted according to the measurement methods of Verlegh et al. [11] and Jensen et al. [24]. In addition, as the source of the scale is English, this study invited PhD students in management and English majors to conduct a two-way translation to ensure the content validity of the measurement. All measurement items used a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree”, 7 = “strongly agree”).

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

## 6. Discussion

In this study, we developed a moderated moderation model to un derstand how sponsorship disclosure influences consumers’ responses to positive reviews by estimating the role of the emotional intensity embedded in the review text and the tie strength of reviewers and consumers in the context of social media. Using a scenario-based experiment, we empirically examined this research model. Consistent with previous findings $[ 6 , 7 ]$ , the results showed that the sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews was negatively related to review credi bility and potential consumers’ purchase intentions. In addition, we found a mediation effect of review credibility on the relationship be tween sponsorship disclosure and purchase intention, which was ignored in previous studies. Moreover, we identified emotional intensity in a positive review as a boundary condition impacting the paths from sponsorship disclosure to review credibility and purchase intention. The results showed that emotional intensity aggravates the negative effect of sponsorship disclosure on consumers’ perceived credibility of positive reviews and purchase intentions toward recommended products or services. Most importantly, we examined in depth how tie strength be tween reviewers and consumers affects the moderating role of emotional intensity. The results showed that strong ties can alleviate the negative moderating effect of emotional intensity on the connections between sponsorship disclosure and review credibility and purchase intention, which enriches the research focusing on the impact of social network integration on consumers’ emotional reactions to online reviews [8].

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions

This study has theoretical implications for the existing literature in several ways. First, this study extended LET to sponsored reviews. Pre vious literature has applied this theory to anonymous online reviews and found direct effects of expectancy violation-related variables, such as review contradiction, on consumers’ attitudes and behaviors [24,42]. For example, Jensen et al. [24] investigated how expectancy violations of lexical complexity, review contradiction (highlighting both positive and negative aspects of a product), and emotional intensity impact consumers’ perceptions of reviewer credibility and purchase decision responses based on LET. In addition, Wu et al. [42] estimated the in fluence of the language style (figurative vs. literal) of online reviews on consumers’ attitudes toward a hotel and their reservation intentions. However, no previous research has applied LET to examine the persuasive effects of sponsored consumer-generated content. Extending LET to sponsored reviews viewed on social media, we demonstrated the moderating roles of two other expectancy violation-related variables, emotional intensity and tie strength, on the effect of sponsorship disclosure on consumers’ responses to positive reviews.

Second, for sponsored persuasive messages, much research has paid attention to the effects of disclosing sponsorship and disclosure types on consumer responses [6,11,15,16,18,20]. Consistent with previous find ings, this study showed that sponsorship disclosure negatively impacts consumers’ perceived review credibility and purchase intentions toward reviewed products. For sponsored reviews, recent researchers have examined the negative effects of sponsorship disclosure on consumer responses to online reviews [6,7]. However, we estimated the moder ating role of emotional intensity embedded in positive reviews. We found that the negative effect of sponsorship disclosure of positive re views was strengthened by high emotional intensity.

Third, we analyzed the impact of sponsored reviews with social connections on consumers’ judgment and purchase decision making. In practice, many sponsored reviews have been displayed on consumers social media sites, such as Facebook and WeChat, so consumers may receive product reviews from a close friend, a mere acquaintance, or a complete stranger. However, while previous research has mainly proved the persuasive power of anonymous sponsored reviews [6,7], little research has focused on the effects of sponsored reviews with different levels of social connections between posters and readers. In addition, previous researchers have investigated tie strength as an important factor that impacts traditional WOM and eWOM communication [11,12,16,36]. Applying tie strength in the sponsored review context, we estimated that the moderating effects of emotional intensity between sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews and consumers’ attitudes and purchase intentions were weakened by high tie strength.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

This study also has practical implications for marketing practitioners and posters. First, the findings may help marketers develop sponsorship strategies for online reviews. In the era of social media, in which busi nesses on e-commerce platforms compete fiercely for customers’ re sources, product marketers and managers use various incentives to stimulate consumers to provide positive comments to attract more po tential customers. However, previous researchers have found that most sponsorship content does not increase companies’ product sales [20]. We found that emotional intensity is one of the boundary factors that strengthen the negative effect of sponsorship disclosure on consumer responses to positive reviews. Therefore, sponsors need to ask posters to avoid using languages with high emotional intensity in their posts. In contrast, sponsors should encourage posters to review a product in a more objective way, such as by simply describing the features of prod ucts without any emotional intensity.

Furthermore, this study provides a theoretical basis for practitioners of eWOM management to weaken the negative effect of emotional in tensity on the persuasive power of sponsored positive reviews. More and more sponsored content with the label “sponsorship” has been displayed on social media. Previous researchers have examined when and how sponsorship disclosure impacts consumer responses to sponsored posts generated by salespeople, influencers, and celebrities on social media [18,20,50]. However, we found that strong ties can relieve the moder ating effect of positive emotional intensity between sponsorship disclosure and consumers’ responses. Previous studies have shown that the level of social ties among consumers can be detected using the number of likes, shares, or comments on social networking sites [74]. Therefore, marketing practitioners are advised to identify and classify posters according to their tie strength with respect to receivers to improve the marketing performance of sponsored posts.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, to simplify complicated problems and identify key factors, we used a scenario-based experi mental method to examine the effect of sponsorship disclosure on con sumers’ attitudes and purchase intentions. However, price, product category, and consumer personality are also important factors that impact consumer responses to online reviews $[ 2 , 7 , 2 6 ]$ . Future research should explore other boundary conditions of sponsorship disclosure in a real social commerce setting. Second, previous studies have found that the distribution of online product reviews is positively skewed [75,76]. For example, Schoenmueller et al. [77] analyzed 68,700,502 reviews on Amazon and found that 78% of online reviews had a positive scale. Considering the majority of reviews at the positive end, this study analyzed the factors influencing the sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews. Future research might need to explore how customers respond to negative reviews. Finally, we applied LET and investigated the role of sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews in the context of Chinese culture. Previous research has suggested that LET is a message-based theory of persuasion, which posits that consumers develop their ex pectations of the language used in a persuasive message based on their cultural and social norms [41,78]. For example, a product review that contains both positive and negative aspects of a product is considered credible in a society where people are likely to develop behaviors of debate and confrontation. Therefore, future research could estimate the persuasive effects of sponsorship disclosure of positive reviews in different cultural contexts.

## 7. Conclusion

Although previous studies have investigated consumers’ attitudes and behavioral reactions to sponsorship disclosure of online reviews, few studies have examined the conditional factors that might affect consumers’ message processing of sponsored reviews. We applied LET to investigate emotional intensity and tie strength as two boundary con ditions that influence the relationship between sponsorship disclosure and consumers’ behavioral intentions. The results showed that high emotional intensity enhances the negative effects of sponsorship disclosure on review credibility and purchase intention, but the moderating effects of emotional intensity become insignificant when reviewers and consumers have strong ties. The present study offers important theoretical and practical implications for online review research and motivates future studies to focus on other conditional factors that might influence consumers’ message processing of spon sored reviews.
