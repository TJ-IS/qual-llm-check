## A B S T R A C T

Do people care equally about reputational gains and losses? We study this question by analyzing users’ contri bution to online knowledge repositories, with data from StackOverflow.com, a website for user-curated technical questions and answers. Based on an analysis of about 1.2 million data points (22% of all relevant activity during a six-month period on this website) focusing on consistent. regular contributors, we find that users post fewer answers after receiving upvotes as well as downvotes. This is surprising and not consistent with rational behavior, because it is in the best interest of a user to post more answers after a downvote. Interestingly, users who receive downvotes post significantly fewer answers compared to users who receive upvotes. This suggests that reputational losses (downvotes) elicit a stronger reaction in users than reputational gains (upvotes), sug gesting the presence of reputational loss aversion, after controlling for alternative explanations. Overall, users reaction is about twice as strong for reputational losses as for gains. Loss aversion as indicated by relative re action to downvotes compared to upvotes is convex in reputation – it is stronger among users in the higher reputation segments compared to the lower and middle ranges. Our paper is novel in extending the study of loss aversion to reputational settings, and studying ex post loss aversion, as compared to ex ante loss aversion in prior literature.

## 1. Introduction

In this paper we study loss aversion in the context of online repu tation. Loss aversion is indicated when “losses loom larger than gains” [9]. We examine this question empirically in the context of reputation gains and losses in online settings, using observational data from StackOverflow.com, a popular community-curated, computer pro gramming related question-and-answer website. Specifically, we compare people’s answering response after incurring gains and losses to their reputation in previous periods. We find that people respond significantly more strongly when they lose reputation, as compared to when they gain reputation. From this, we infer the presence of loss aversion with regard to ex-post incurred losses. When a contributor loses some reputation, clearly they are not happy with it. Our question is whether people react more strongly to a loss in reputation, than to an equivalent gain in reputation – and therefore whether they feel the loss more strongly than the gain. This relative salience of a loss (compared to a gain) is the essence of “loss aversion” as defined in the literature (e.g. Brenner et al. [9] – see above).

In this context, our contribution is three-fold. Firstly, we study loss aversion applied to online reputation, as compared to prior literature which studies loss aversion in the context of material gains and losses. Next, we focus on ex post loss aversion, which is conceptually more nuanced than ex ante loss aversion which has been studied extensively in prior literature. With ex ante loss aversion, the agent is asked to decide when anticipating a potential future loss. With ex post loss aversion as we study it here, the agent has already experienced a loss, and we now observe their reaction to this loss. Their reaction could in turn cause further losses or gains, and so there are elements of ex ante and ex post loss aversion in our setting. The situation modeled in this paper is different from a pure ex ante loss situation, because the user has recently incurred losses (whereas in the pure ex-ante case the user has not yet incurred losses), and that may have an impact on how the user perceives the impending actions which may result in gains or losses. Finally, we study loss aversion in the context of knowledge sharing in StackOverflow, an online community, a real world setting where users actions have real reputational consequences (in terms of job offers, consulting opportunities and so on), which complements the work done in most prior studies of loss aversion in experimental settings. We now expand on the above three themes.

## 1.1. Online reputational loss aversion

Online reputation is increasingly being recognized as an important force shaping online behavior, for both individuals and firms [11,25]. The online reputation of a user is typically an aggregation of positive and negative reputational assessments (reviews, ratings, upvotes, down votes, etc.), made by other users as a result of online or offline in teractions $[ 1 5 , 2 1 ]$ . The ever-increasing range of services for managing online reputation, for individuals and firms, suggests that people care strongly for their online reputation, and are willing to pay to protect their online reputation. While it is clear that online reputation has important consequences for individuals and firms, it is not clear whether users view reputational gains and losses symmetrically. The implications of online reputation make one wonder whether users are keener to avoid reputational losses than they are to achieve reputational gains. More insight on this is of importance to researchers and practitioners. For researchers, this work will extend the literature on loss aversion (e.g. [31]) to the context of online reputation (e.g. [11]).

## 1.2. Ex post loss aversion

Prior studies have used experimental methods to mainly study ex ante reactions of users to potential losses in risky (e.g. Kahneman and Tversky [16]) and riskless (e.g. Tversky and Kahneman [31]) settings, and have found that people are ex ante strongly averse to losses. How. ever, do people also feel strong aversion to (and consequently a desire to make up for) incurred losses ex post? In several real-life situations, people are faced with losses that are already incurred ex post (e.g. stock market losses), and have to choose between (a) enduring the losses, and taking defensive actions which may not recoup past losses, but will prevent further losses (e.g. hold cash) and (b) taking actions to mitigate such losses, which carry the potential for more losses (e.g. buy more stocks). This makes the agent’s decision more nuanced and interesting. How do people react when faced with already incurred losses, when their future actions could expose them to further gains or losses? Faced with incurred losses, people may either act to avoid future such losses, or act to recoup the losses, because trying to recoup what is lost could be the rational response under some circumstances. In the context of StackOverflow, we find that in response to reputational losses, people react by decreasing their participation levels in the short term, sug gesting the presence of avoidance behavior in the ex post case, just like in the ex ante case. This finding is consistent with prior research, and extends our understanding of ex ante loss aversion to the ex post case. In the ex post case, this result is interesting: it suggests that the drive to overcome lost reputation is not as strong as one might suppose, at least in the immediate aftermath of lost reputation, which is the focus of this study.

## 1.3. Observational data on knowledge sharing from an online community

In this paper, we address the issue of reputation gains versus losses using observational data from StackOverflow.com, a programmingrelated knowledge sharing question-and-answer website, where anyone can create an account and post questions and answers on a va riety of programming related topics. Our primary finding is that we find evidence of loss aversion in the context of online reputation. In our dataset, the impact of one downvote (which leads to reputational loss)

on a user’s contribution level (answer quantity) is overall about twice as strong (between 1.5 and 3 times as strong for various segments of the user population) as the impact of one upvote (which leads to reputational gain).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses related literature. Section 3 discusses the study context, including an overview of the StackOverflow website, and a thorough discussion of how the activities on this website are used to address the theoretical questions we raise. Section 4 deals with the hypothesis development. Section 5 discusses the dataset, empirical methodology and findings. Section 6 concludes with a discussion of main findings and implications for researchers and practitioners.

## 2. Theoretical background

Our work draws upon literature on loss aversion, online reputation management, online word of mouth, gamification, and knowledge management.

## 2.1. Loss aversion

Our work extends the literature on loss aversion [31] to the context of online reputation [11,25]. Tversky and Kahneman [31] pointed out the impact of status quo or reference level on choice (in the context of material goods with attached monetary values), and presented a reference-dependent theory of consumer choice. Their central point was that losses and disadvantages affect preferences more strongly than gains and advantages. Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler [17] experi mentally demonstrated loss aversion and the “Endowment Effect” (in the context of material goods such as wine), wherein the disadvantages of a negative movement from status quo loom larger than the advantages of a positive movement from status quo. Loss aversion has been studied extensively in experimental settings in the context of monetary or ma terial gains and losses (e.g. [11). There is however very little literature examining loss aversion in the context of reputational losses.

## 2.2. Online reputation management and online word of mouth

There is little research on how loss aversion affects agents’ response to reputational losses. Reputation is recognized as an important factor in a variety of settings [20], including most recently online reputation [11], resulting most often from aggregated online user feedback. Spe cifically with respect to online reputation, prior research has studied (a) inefficiencies in the processes by which online reputation is created – for instance, Dellarocas and Wood [12] study biases in how people report feedback, and the resulting disconnect between actual transaction out comes and reported feedback; (b) the nature of online reputation metrics and their impact on sales – for instance, Sun [28] studies the informa tional role of high variance versus high average ratings, and their im plications for product sales; (c) the effectiveness of online reputation mechanisms and factors that could hamper their effectiveness – Bolton, Katok, and Ockenfels [8] study the inefficiencies that arise when the benefits of feedback are not internalized by the providers but instead go to the whole community; (d) the impact of reputation signals on those consuming the signals – e.g. impact of hotel reviews on potential con sumers (e.g. [33]); and (e) impact of reputation mechanisms on those whose reputation is being measured – for instance, Bakos and Dellarocas [5] study litigation and online reputation as alternative quality assur ance mechanisms. In this context, our work falls within (e) – it studies the impact of reputation mechanisms on users' behavior. Our novel finding is that users react with different intensity to reputational gains and losses. To our knowledge, our paper is the first to address the question of how users’ loss aversion affects their response to reputa tional gains and losses.

We compare and contrast our work with research on Word of mouth (WOM), which is an important aspect of many online communities [3,10,11]. Word of mouth has been primarily characterized through online reviews (e.g. [10]) and its impact has been primarily studied in the context of online sales (e.g. [29]). WOM research has been applied mainly to experience goods (such as clothes or electronic goods). In our context, we study upvotes and downvotes – forms of nonverbal feedback that are different from other word-of-mouth forms such as reviews. Moreover, our context pertains to expert advice which is a credence goods. Another difference is that word-of-mouth literature mainly studies the impact of positive or negative WOM on the sales of the target product or service, but here we study the impact of positive or negative non-verbal feedback on the propensity of the supplier (contributor) to contribute content in the future.

## 2.3. Knowledge management

Our work is related to the literature on knowledge management [2] and what motivates people to contribute knowledge in online settings. The literature on knowledge management can be classified into work on the antecedents of online knowledge sharing – which focus on the fac tors responsible for increased knowledge sharing, as well as conse quences of knowledge sharing on performance and other metrics. In research on antecedents, Ma and Agarwal [23] find that perceived identity verification is strongly linked to knowledge contribution. In the present work, we find that reputational gains and losses are also strongly linked to knowledge contribution. In the above context, our work fo cuses more on the antecedents of knowledge sharing, and highlights how contributors’ aversion to reputation losses can influence their knowl edge sharing behavior. We bring to bear a behavioral economics perspective and specifically discuss loss aversion in the context of reputational gains and losses, and relate this to the literature on knowledge management that seeks to understand why users contribute to KM systems.

Our findings have implications for current research in social net works on aspects such as environmental shaping and network contagion. For instance, to what extent does environmental shaping play a role in fostering contributions by users in online communities? How does users contributing behavior spread to other users via network contagion? What are some of the motivational drivers of online content contribu. tion, and how does the importance of nonverbal community feedback such as upvotes and downvotes influence online contribution? We discuss some of these aspects under “theoretical implications” in section 6.

## 3. Study context

## 3.1. About StackOverflow.com

StackOverflow.com is a community driven question and answer website for computer programming related issues. It is free to sign up and participate. It has been in existence since 2008, and presently has more than 3.5 million users worldwide. Users can post questions and answer others’ questions, upvote good questions and answers, and downvote low-quality questions and answers. Users can also edit ques tions and moderate discussions. The website awards badges to users based on specific achievements and milestones in participation. Each user has a profile page that includes their total reputation points, badges earned, upvotes and downvotes (both received and given), questions asked and answers given, number of profile views, and other personal information that a user chooses to reveal about themselves, like their location, web page and age.

Reputation is the aggregation of all upvotes and downvotes earned by a contributor, and is a measure of how much the community values the contributor. Reputation is earned by asking good questions, and posting useful answers to others’ questions. When a user posts a question or answer, other users can vote on that post. These votes by others cause the user to gain (or lose) reputation. A question or answer is upvoted when it is found useful and downvoted when it is not found useful. For an upvoted (downvoted) question, the asker’s reputation increases by 5 points (decreases by 2 points). For an upvoted (downvoted) answer, the answerer’s reputation increases by 10 points (decreases by 2 points). When an answer is accepted, reputation increases by 15 points. Users with more reputation gain more privileges to edit the site. For example, a user with 10,000 or more reputation points can handle flags, access reports, and delete questions.

In addition to reputation, users can earn badges for five categories: asking questions (“question badges”), answering questions, participa tion, moderation, and “other”. Within each category, badge status is signified by color: Bronze badges, which are easiest to get, are awarded to encourage users to try out new features on the website; whereas silver and gold badges are increasingly more difficult to get, and are awarded for activities that earn the approval of other users.

## 3.2. Linking theory to our empirical analysis

In order to estimate the presence of loss aversion, we compare the relative impact of reputational losses (downvotes) vs gains (upvotes) on users’ answer-contribution quantity (number of answers) and quality. We now proceed to discuss how and why the theory of loss aversion can be studied by answering behavior. First, we need to observe a suitable behavior to infer loss aversion. If loss aversion is felt in the contributor’s mind, its evidence may be observed in the contributor’s subsequent behavior. We focus on the behavior of posting of answers, as that can most affect the contributors’ future reputation.<sup>2</sup> When a user posts an swers, that’s when they get upvoted (gain reputation) or downvoted (lose reputation). Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that their subse quent reaction will be manifested in their subsequent answering behavior, after getting upvoted or downvoted. When a contributor in curs a reputational loss or gain, there are three possibilities: they can (a) feel the salience of both gain and loss equally, (b) feel the gain more than the loss, or (c) feel the loss more than the gain. Next, in terms of reaction, when a contributor incurs a reputational loss, he or she can display a reaction that falls in one of three possible categories, in comparison to their reaction after enjoying reputational gain: (i) no significant differ ence in reaction after loss or gain, (ii) stronger reaction after gain than after loss, or (iii) stronger reaction after loss than after gain. Clearly, (a) corresponds to (i), (b) to (ii), and (c) to (iii).

We find evidence for (iii) above – when faced with reputation loss, contributors post significantly fewer answers than they do after a reputational gain. In other words, they register a stronger reaction to losses compared to gains. Therefore we infer (c) – that the contributors feel reputational loss aversion. This follows from the definition of loss aversion in the literature. If loss aversion was absent, contributors would have behaved in a manner consistent with (i) or (ii).

## 4. Hypothesis development

Our first hypothesis pertains to the impact of positive feedback on contribution quantity and quality. Intuitively, people may be encour aged to contribute more quantity and quality if their past contributions are valued by the community. Alternately, people may be tempted to slack off, at least temporarily, after getting community approval for their efforts. When users receive upvotes, it amounts to positive feedback from their audience. Feedback on shared knowledge has been shown to encourage knowledge sharing [7], and feedback stimulates participation in online settings [22]. Community upvotes are a form of praise, and there is some evidence that praise increases effort (in our context, effort is manifested as quantity and quality of contributions) by the recipient [6]. Upvotes result in an enhancement of contributors’ reputation. Reputation seeking behavior has also been shown to encourage infor mation sharing [24]. Upvotes are therefore desirable, and hence may elicit more and better contribution. This is consistent with B.F.Skinner’s principle of reinforcement, according to which actions whose conse quences are desirable are repeated, while actions whose consequences are undesirable are not repeated [27]. Therefore based on intuition and prior research, we may reasonably expect users who receive upvotes to post more answers as well as higher quality answers.

An alternative possibility is that users who receive positive rein forcement may slack off – be satiated and not post answers for some time. There is some evidence that satiation is more likely with constant reinforcers rather than variable reinforcers [14]. In our situation, upvotes are variable reinforcers since the number of upvotes each contribution receives is highly variable.<sup>3</sup> Therefore, since satiation is more applicable for constant reinforcers rather than variable reinforcers, user-satiation may not be a suitable alternative explanation for why users are not contributing.

It is also possible that users who get upvoted may feel they have earned some time off, and may devote less effort (manifesting as quantity and quality of answers) to answering questions in the subse quent week. Another possibility is that status quo bias [26] may induce people to prefer their current status quo in terms of their reputation, and therefore reduce their answer quantity and quality, at least temporarily. Both these possibilities suggests a negative relationship between upvotes and subsequent answers both in terms of quantity and quality of answers.

In conclusion, prior literature has found, in other (non-reputational) contexts, evidence for both increases and decreases in effort in response to positive reinforcement. People may respond strongly, either posi tively (by posting more and high quality answers) or negatively (by posting fewer and low quality answers), after getting upvoted. We therefore propose a (two-sided) hypothesis that users who get upvoted and gain reputation points will respond strongly both in their answer quantity, measured by the number of answers posted (H1a), and answer quality, measured by score per answer (H1b), against the null hypothesis that users will not respond significantly either way:

Hypothesis 1. (a): Users who receive positive reinforcement to their contributions that result in gains to their reputation will react more strongly in terms of answering quantity.

Hypothesis 1. (b): Users who receive positive reinforcement to their contributions that result in gains to their reputation will react more strongly in terms of answering quality.

Hypothesis 2 takes the view that users who incur reputational losses due to community downvotes will also react strongly in response.

Some prior literature supports the view that after getting downvoted, users may decrease their contribution quantity and quality. Taking a behavioral view of user behavior, it is possible that a user who gets downvoted may get demoralized and contribute less. As with the pre vious hypothesis, another possibility is that status quo bias [26] from the literature on loss aversion may result in people preferring to defend their diminished reputation, at least for the time being, and not risk it by posting answers requiring more effort. This may also result in fewer answers and lower-quality answers being posted after getting downvoted.

However, our sample consists of regular contributors who have demonstrated consistent commitment over long periods of time. It is difficult to imagine such users getting demoralized by a few downvotes. Intuitively, if users value reputation, reputation loss should motivate rational users to regain their lost reputation by making more and better contributions.

The above discussion again supports the possibility of both increases and decreases in effort in response to negative reinforcement. Therefore, it appears that people may respond strongly, either positively (by posting more and better answers) or negatively (by posting fewer and worse answers), after getting downvoted. We therefore propose a (twosided) hypothesis that users who lose reputation points will respond strongly in their answering behavior by putting in more effort (reflected in quantity and quality of answers) in their contributions, against the null hypothesis that users will not respond significantly either way:

Hypothesis 2. (a): Users who receive negative reinforcement of their contributions that result in losses to their reputation will react more strongly in terms of answering quantity.

Hypothesis 2. (b): Users who receive negative reinforcement of their contributions that result in losses to their reputation will react more strongly in terms of answering quality.

Next, hypothesis 3 speaks to the relative strength of the impacts of reputational losses versus gains on the propensity to contribute, and takes the view that a user will demonstrate a stronger response (in terms of quantity and quality of contributions) to a loss in reputation, than to an equivalent gain in reputation. Intuitively, our reasoning is that for a committed contributor, a downvote represents something more than just a decrease in absolute reputation value. A downvote temporarily ques tions their expertise and knowledge. A downvote is also somewhat un expected to a regular contributor who already has a high reputation. Finally, users may just have a visceral dislike of reputational losses arising from downvotes. For these reasons, downvotes may elicit a stronger response compared to upvotes in terms of answering effort (quantity and quality).

In prior literature, we find evidence of loss aversion in other contexts (not involving reputation). Kahneman and Tversky [16] discuss decision making in the context of risky choices. Their work finds evidence that the value function used to evaluate risky outcomes has three properties: (a) reference dependence – people value gains and losses defined rela. tive to a reference point; (b) loss aversion: losses loom larger than gains, and (c) diminishing sensitivity: marginal value of gains and losses di minishes with regard to size. With respect to loss aversion (b), which is the focus of this paper, consider a decision maker who has to choose between two risky outcomes x > y, each with a probability of success and failure: (x, p(x) = 0.5, − x, p(− x) = 0.5) and $( \mathbf { y } , \mathbf { p } ( \mathbf { y } ) = 0 . 5 , \mathbf { \delta - y } ,$ , p (− y) = 0.5). Kahneman and Tversky [16] find that the decision maker prefers (y, $\mathbf { p ( y ) } = 0 . 5 , \ - \mathbf { y } , \ \mathbf { p ( - y ) } = 0 . 5 ) \ \mathrm { t o ~ ( x , \mathbf { p ( x ) } = 0 . 5 , \ - x , \ p ( - x ) = }$ 0.5), due to a greater aversion to losing the higher amount (x) compared to losing y, even though the expected value of both outcomes is zero.

In another landmark study, Tversky and Kahneman [31] find evi dence of loss aversion in riskless choice settings as well. They find evi dence of Instant Endowment, wherein utility lost from giving up a good is greater than the utility gained from gaining the good. In an experi ment, they place a decorated mug worth \$5 in front of several subjects, some of whom (the “sellers”) are told that they now own the mug, and others are told that they can choose to own the mug (the “choosers”). Each seller is offered a range of prices (\$0.50 to \$9.50) and asked to indicate whether they will sell at that price and keep the money, or keep the mug. Each chooser is similarly offered the same range of prices and asked to indicate for each amount, whether they will choose the mug, or take the cash amount. The median value for sellers is \$7.12, whereas for choosers it is \$3.12. This suggests that when people feel they already own something, their utility lost from giving it up appears to be higher than the utility gained by acquiring it for someone who does not own it.

In the context of the above two papers, an important question arises in many practical situations. How do people respond when they have already just incurred losses? There are two possibilities: seek to over come losses by going for more gains, or go on defense and prevent further losses. People may, as a rational response, try to overcome their losses by aiming for more gains, but the risk is that the actions they take to achieve further gains may themselves result in losses. For instance, if an investor loses money in a stock investment, and seeks to invest more to make up for the losses, they may lose their subsequent investments as well. Alternately, to prevent further losses, the investor could liquidate all stock and hold cash for some time. Loss aversion produces a conun drum: the dismay over already incurred losses may motivate a person to make up for it, but the possibility of further losses may deter such efforts.

To our knowledge, our work is the first to study this specific situation in the context of loss aversion, reputational or otherwise. In our setting, a contributor who gets downvoted may want to regain their reputation by posting more answers. However, each new posting carries with it the risk of further downvotes. This feature of our dataset makes our situation unique compared to the situations studied by Kahneman and Tversky [16], Tversky and Kahneman [31], and others. Since we use observa tional data, our situation is more nuanced than the clean situations modeled in earlier experimental work. Prior situations have involved ex ante decisions about future choices: for instance, as we discussed, [16] study ex ante consumer decision making with regard to risky choices, while Tversky and Kahneman [31] study ex ante consumer decision making with regard to riskless choices. However, in our case, we study ex post reactions of users to experienced gains and losses. Since the losses or gains have already been experienced, there is no remaining risk there, but the users’ reactions themselves carry some risk: when a user who has been upvoted or downvoted posts more answers, each of those answers carries a risk, however small, of being downvoted. If a user posts more and better answers in response to reputational loss, despite the risk of further loss, that may offer a stronger evidence for reputational loss aversion, than if there was no risk of further loss.

Based on the above discussion, we hypothesize the presence of reputational loss aversion among contributors, i.e. that reputational losses loom larger than gains for users. We measure the impact of reputational gains (measured through upvotes) and losses (measured through downvotes) on the number and quality of answers posted by users. From the theoretical discussion in the preceding paragraphs, there are two possibilities: after getting downvoted, a user may either increase or decrease their participation quantity and quality. Our interest is somewhat neutral to whether participation quantity and quality in creases or decreases. We are more interested in the relative strength of the user’s response after a downvote, compared to their response after an upvote.

We draw upon the theory of loss aversion to hypothesize that repu tational losses induce a stronger response than reputational gains, both in terms of quantity (3(a)) and quality of contributions (3(b)):

Hypothesis 3. (a): Users will react more strongly in terms of quantity of contributions when they receive negative reinforcement of their contributions that result in losses to their reputation, than when they receive positive reinforcement of their contributions that result in gains to their reputation.

Hypothesis 3. (b): Users will react more strongly in terms of quality of contributions when they receive negative reinforcement of their contributions that result in losses to their reputation, than when they receive positive reinforcement of their contributions that result in gains to their reputation.

Hypotheses 4 and 5 below deal with the temporal aspect of loss aversion. Prior research in different fields has found evidence for a decrease in impact of emotionally salient events with temporal distance. In psychology, temporal distance has been found to affect people’s willingness to forgive (e.g. [34]). In marketing, in the area of productharm crisis management, it has been found that effects of a product recall crisis decrease with time [32].

Based on the above literature, an event that is felt more strongly must manifest its effect for a longer period. Therefore, if our conjecture regarding reputational loss aversion is true, then the impact of losses should last longer than the impact of gains on user contribution levels. This leads us to hypothesis 4:

Hypothesis 4. (a): The impact of reputation losses on users’ contribution quantity lasts longer than the impact of reputation gains.

Hypothesis 4. (b): The impact of reputation losses on users’ contribution quality lasts longer than the impact of reputation gains.

Based on the prior findings regarding the diminishing impact of events with temporal distance, we can conjecture that the impact of reputational loss aversion should decrease with time, as the negative effects of reputational loss wear off and become less salient in users memory, which leads us to hypothesis 5:

Hypothesis 5. (a): The impact of reputation losses on users’ contribution quantity diminishes with time.

Hypothesis 5. (b): The impact of reputation losses on users’ contribution quality diminishes with time.

How does a user’s reputational loss aversion vary with their repu tation? Will high reputation users be more likely to feel a stronger reputational loss aversion? That is plausible, because it could be argued that high reputation users are more visible and will “feel the sting” of a downvote more keenly. (On the StackOverflow website, users can see the downvotes as well as upvotes given to a particular answer, which also has information on the contributor of that answer). On the other hand, a certain quantum of reputation loss is a smaller fraction of someone’s reputation as that reputation is higher. By this argument, a given quantum of reputation loss means less to someone with a higher reputation. The “feel the sting” argument is more consistent with our initial stand that reputation loss is viewed as an indictment of the con tributor’s expertise, beyond just the numerical decrease in reputation points it objectively stands for. We therefore hypothesize that users with higher reputation will be more likely to feel reputational loss aversion.

Hypothesis 6. (a): Users who experience reputational loss are likely to respond strongly in quantity of effort if they have higher reputation.

Hypothesis 6. (b): Users who experience reputational loss are likely to respond strongly in quality of effort if they have higher reputation.

## 5. Methods

## 5.1. Data

For this analysis, we collected data on 2404 randomly selected StackOverflow contributors, each of whom has posted at least 30 or more answers in the year 2019 (January 1st to December 31st, 2019). We impose this activity cutoff, since we want to study how users with consistent, regular contributions react to the possibility of reputation loss.<sup>4</sup> These 2404 contributors have collectively posted 549,246 answers, and obtained 1,387,464 upvotes and 26,583 downvotes between 1st January 2019 and 31st Dec 2019. This dataset represents 22% of all relevant activity (i.e. of a total of 2,401,506 answers posted) on StackOverflow.com during this time interval. We obtain the daily number of answers posted by each of these users; the total scores for those answers; and the total upvotes and downvotes received for the answers posted by these users. We then aggregate the answers, upvotes, downvotes, scores, and badges (gold, silver and bronze) for each user on a weekly basis.

## 5.2. Variables and definitions

## 5.2.1. Dependent variable

We are interested in how the quantity and quality of effort (in a particular week) are affected by reputational loss aversion as reflected in the response to upvotes and downvotes (received in previous weeks). In the hypotheses below, part (a) of each hypothesis requires a measure of the quantity of effort by the contributing user in each week, for which we use “answers” as the dependent variable, denoting the number of answers posted in a week by a user. Part (b) of each hypothesis, on the other hand, requires a measure of answer quality for answers posted in a particular week. Answer score is a plausible measure of quality. ## 5.3. Model specification, empirical analysis and results

Our goal is to isolate the impact of upvotes and downvotes on quantity of effort (answer quantity) and quality of effort (answer qual ity, measured using score per answer). An important determinant of answer quantity and quality is the unobserved ability of individual contributors. To control for unobserved user ability, which may be reasonably assumed to be constant for a given user in the short run (1 year in our study), we adopt a panel data approach. A simple strategy might be to run a regression with the dependent variable as the number of answers posted by a focal user in the current week, and the inde pendent variables as the number of upvotes and downvotes (among other control variables) obtained by the focal user, also in the current week. This approach suffers from the problem of simultaneity – it is not clear whether the receipt of upvotes and downvotes in the current week is causing the user to post more answers in the current week, or caused by the user posting more answers. To mitigate this simultaneity issue, we could use the lagged independent variables, and run a regression with the dependent variable as before – the number of answers posted by a focal user in the current week, and the lagged independent variables as the previous week’s number of upvotes and downvotes (among other control variables) obtained by the focal user. However, this presents another issue: what if the user has been answering a lot of questions lately, so that their answers across consequent weeks are highly corre lated? This implies that users who post more answers in the previous week, end up getting more upvotes and downvotes in the previous week (our regressors), and also post more answers in the current week (our DV). We therefore need to control for the previous week’s number of answers posted. Including lagged dependent variables in the regressors is facilitated in dynamic panel data models:

dynamic panel data approach. Next, previous period upvotes are significantly negatively linked to current period answers across all col umns. We find that users who receive more upvotes in the previous week end up posting fewer answers in the current week (compared to the average number of answers they post). This supports our two-sided hypothesis H1a: the null hypothesis that users who receive upvotes don’t change their answering behavior is rejected. This suggests either the effect of status quo bias [26] resulting in a user preferring their current reputation level (which does not make sense, because contrib utors always favor a higher reputation for themselves), or the presence of some “slacking off” – users may feel they can take it easy a bit in the current period, since they have contributed well in the previous period.

Next, previous period downvotes are also significantly negatively linked to current period answers: users who get downvoted in a previous week post significantly fewer answers this week. This supports hy pothesis H2a, either because of status quo bias, or because users may feel temporarily discouraged by downvotes. Most importantly, a comparison of the coefficients of lag-upvotes and lag-downvotes suggests that the impact of downvotes is more than the impact of upvotes, which offers support for reputational loss aversion (H3a) – people appear to react more strongly to reputational losses than they do to equivalent reputa tional gains. We do not find any evidence for H4a that the impact of reputation losses outlasts the impact of reputation gains on the contri bution effort-levels of users. The coefficient of lag2-downvotes is smaller in magnitude than the coefficient of lag-downvotes, suggesting that the impact of downvotes on answers diminishes with time, offering support for H5a. Likewise, the negative impact of upvotes also diminishes with time. ## 6. Discussion and conclusion

## 6.1. Summary of findings

In conclusion, we study how users respond to reputational gains versus losses in an online context with a dataset from StackOverflow. com, an online Q&A website where users can ask questions and post answers to technical programming-related questions, and can upvote or downvote good or bad contributions. We find that users react negatively to both upvotes and downvotes. However, their reaction to downvotes is about twice as strong as their reaction to upvotes. This suggests the presence of loss aversion, as users’ responses (measured in contribution quantity) to losses in reputation are much stronger compared to their responses to gains. This effect persists after controlling for correlation of effort across adjacent periods, by using lagged dependent variables in dynamic panel data models. To our knowledge this is the first paper to study loss aversion in the context of reputational gains and losses.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

Our findings can help practitioners design better reputation mecha nisms in websites that support online communities. With regard to design of knowledge sharing websites, which could be standalone ser vices such as StackOverflow.com or software vendor-affiliated site (such as Microsoft TechNet), our research findings suggest that while downvote buttons are important as a feedback mechanism for the con tributors, it may be counter-productive to display the downvote score to the community as a whole. Recently, Youtube introduced a policy change whereby they continue to allow community members to down vote a video, but the downvote score is made visible only to the recip ient, for their private feedback. The community does not get to see the aggregate downvote score.<sup>7</sup> Product managers in charge of interaction design will be better informed in their decision to include downvote buttons but make the downvotes visible privately only to the contribu tors for their feedback, but not make the downvotes publicly visible.

## 6.3. Theoretical implications

Our work has important theoretical implications for research in so cial networks, online communities, knowledge management, and related areas. With regard to social networks, environmental shaping, contagion, structural capital, and resource access have been identified as important areas of theoretical focus [18]. In the context of environment shaping, the network environment, which exerts a significant influence on participants, could impact users’ reaction to upvotes and downvotes. Users’ upvoting and downvoting behavior, with its attendant conse quences on the users’ subsequent contributing behavior and engage ment with the online community, could have implications for the extent of contagion with regard to resources, ideas and attitudes. With regard to the literature on the motivational drivers of content contribution to online communities [30], prior work has identified several important factors such as self-oriented versus other-oriented, extrinsic versus intrinsic drivers, and more specifically factors such as opinion leader ship, self-presentation, altruism and enjoyment. In this context, our work highlights the importance of community feedback – in particular, nonverbal feedback such as upvotes and downvotes – and their impact on reputation, which in turn can be a significant influencer of user contribution.

## 6.4. Limitations

Our study has several limitations. We have only analyzed users who are already seasoned and frequent contributors, and have fairly high levels of participation. It is also important to study users at lower levels of participation. to get a more complete picture of user behavior. With regard to reputation, we were not able to study the interaction effect of reputation with feedback on user contribution, due to data limitations, as we could only obtain reputation snapshots of users at the end of our period of interest. A future study could collect daily reputation on an ongoing basis for a few months, which would enable studying such interaction effects. Moreover, our work provides insights into user behavior in certain contexts – e.g. with respect to reputation in knowl edge exchange settings or information sharing websites such as Reddit and Quora, where contributors can signal expertise through their con tributions. Our results will almost certainly not be applicable in the context of social networking sites such as Facebook, as such websites are not primarily about signaling reputation, and so negative feedback by users may have completely different implications not addressed by our paper. Our results may or may not be applicable to other important areas that involve transactional online interaction, such as AirBnB, eBay, Yelp, Amazon, and others.

## 6.5. Future research

Loss aversion in the context of reputational gains and losses opens up several interesting unexplored areas for research. One important and promising area for further exploration is the impact of feedback variability on user contribution and community dynamics. In this effort, the standard deviation of the received votes can be a key independent variable that can influence the extent of subsequent contribution and other key outcomes. It will be interesting to see how people respond to the potential for loss aversion, ex ante (rather than ex post, which thi paper studies). How do prior reputational gains or losses influence the reputation reference point that frames future reputational gains and losses? How do past cumulative reputation levels versus incremental gains and losses in reputation influence users’ perceptions with regard to future gains and losses? These and related questions are potential future avenues for exploration.
