# Clinical decision support system and hospital readmission reduction: Evidence from U.S. panel data

## ABSTRACT

Using a large-scale panel of U.S. hospitals across health referral regions (HRRs), we empirically examine how a hospital's and its neighboring hospitals' adoption of the Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) (and their meaningful use of CDSS) affects the hospital's quality of care. We find that CDSS adoption significantly reduces a hospital's readmission rate of heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia patients. We also reveal the regional spillover effects of adoption, where neighboring hospitals' CDSS adoption in the same HRR also reduces a hospital's readmission rates. Such spillover effects become more significant with electronic data interchange, facilitating seamless and errorless communications among hospitals. We also show that the spillover effects may depend on HRRs' different market structures and hospitals' meaningful-use status. Our findings offer theoretical and managerial insights for both healthcare researchers and practitioners.

## 1. Introduction

Health information technology (HIT) has been expected to help healthcare providers better manage the timeliness and accuracy of patient care by supporting effective clinical decisions and information sharing among patients and their caregivers. Owing to the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act of 2009, U.S. healthcare providers have been given financial incentives linked to HIT adoption. More than \$35 billion in incentives has promoted HIT implementation, resulting in 96% of hospitals having a certified electronic health records (EHRs) program in 2017—a ninefold increase since 2008 [44].

This dramatic increase in HIT adoption encourages healthcare researchers to justify government initiatives that drive significant private and public investments. Prior studies have focused on the hospital- or physician-level effects of HIT adoption on healthcare quality and costs (e.g., $[1,5,12]$ ), in addition to nationwide empirical evidence regarding the effects of HIT adoption and its meaningful use (e.g., $[11,27]$ ). While these studies reveal various effects of HIT adoption, they have focused only on its main effects concerning the quality of care and costs. This study aims to enhance further our understanding of the effects of HIT adoption by paying attention to its possible spillover effects and synergetic adoption effects of two distinct HITs, in addition to its main effects.

Mainly, we investigate the spillover effects of Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) adoption (and meaningful use) within an HRR and the interplay between CDSS and Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) in generating such spillover effects.

A CDSS uses knowledge bases to provide clinical information at the point of care to drive evidence-based treatment (e.g., clinical guidelines and reminders, drug allergy, drug-drug interaction alerts, and drug dosing support) [34]. It can integrate clinical workflows by supporting various managerial and medical decisions throughout the continuum of care [43]. That is, a CDSS is an active knowledge system related to avoiding errors and making appropriate clinical decisions, resulting in enhanced health outcomes.

A CDSS can improve treatment quality through two mechanisms $[13]$ . First, it helps physicians reach a correct diagnosis. For example, the diagnostic accuracy of CDSS achieves the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) curve between 0.864 and 0.937 for detecting lung nodules on the chest X-ray or CT scan $[2]$ . The concordance rate of CDSS with heart failure specialists in diagnosing heart failures was 98%, outperforming non-heart failure specialists $[17]$ . Second, it advises physicians on what and when to do for better health outcomes. A systematic review of one hundred studies shows that the CDSS increased practitioners' adherence to guidelines, recognition of functional status problems, and time to order treatment $[24]$ .

Unlike other industries, the healthcare sector often shares patient information as patients move across healthcare providers within a hospital referral region (HRR). Furthermore, hospitals collaborate and share information to deliver a better quality of care. Thus, hospitals may benefit from their HIT adoption and their peer hospitals' adoption. Timely and high-quality patient information sharing between hospitals drives such spillover effects $[4,40]$ .

Primary care physicians' referrals are often accompanied by information sharing through EDI, expediting communication and collaboration among healthcare providers [35]. As a foundation HIT that supports seamless and errorless communications among hospitals, adopting EDI may bring synergetic effects to positive spillover effects of CDSS adoption within an HRR. However, such spillover effects could depend on the market structure of an HRR, given that the referral or collaboration patterns vary within it. Big hospitals are less likely to be in multihospital systems (MHS), where member hospitals actively communicate [3]. Referral networks also depend on the market structure. When a single hospital dominates a local healthcare market, for example, other care hospitals would not have multiple choices for referrals, whereas they would have otherwise.

When we investigate the effects of HIT adoption, we also need to take into account a possible assimilation gap between the adoption and actual usage of HIT $[21]$ , considering that the recent dramatic increase in nationwide HIT adoption is not self-motivated by hospitals as necessary but is mainly driven by government initiatives. That is, mere CDSS adoption may not lead to its benefits unless adopters meaningfully use it. Thus, we further consider the meaningful use of CDSS in our empirical models.

Concerning the above discussions, our primary research questions are as follows: (1) How does CDSS adoption affect hospitals' readmission rates? (2) Do the regional spillover effects of CDSS adoption indeed exist among hospitals in an HRR? (3) How does EDI work for CDSS to generate spillover effects? (4) How do the spillover effects vary with different market structures? And (5) How is meaningful CDSS use related to effects on healthcare quality?

In order to answer the abovementioned questions, large-scale hospital panel datasets were obtained from multiple sources, including the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Analytics database and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) website. $^{1}$ Our sample includes healthcare data involving 1965 hospitals (for heart failure), 1354 hospitals (for acute myocardial infarction), and 2117 hospitals (for pneumonia) across 235 HRRs from 2010 to 2017, such as adopted HITs and detailed healthcare quality.

We find that adopting CDSS significantly reduces a hospital's readmission rate of heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia patients. Our results also indicate spillover effects of CDSS adoption within an HRR, where neighboring hospitals' CDSS adoption also reduces a hospital's readmission rates. Such spillover effects become greater with hospitals' adoption of EDI, which facilitates seamless and errorless communications among hospitals. We also show that the spillover effects may depend on HRRs' different market structures and hospitals' meaningful-use status.

Our findings provide theoretical and managerial implications by identifying the interdependence of HIT adoption among hospitals in an HRR. The contribution of this study lies in giving policy insights on effective healthcare policies across complex healthcare market structures.

## 2. Research background

The impact of HIT adoption has recently drawn a great deal of attention from the community of Information Systems $[1]$ . Several efforts have been devoted to examining its impacts on diverse dimensions, including productivity $[14,20,22,23,25,28,33,39,49]$ , medical errors $[5,15,30,37]$ , insurance premiums $[38]$ , and healthcare quality $[7,9–12,16,29,31,32,42,46,48]$ . Table 1 summarizes the previous findings.

For example, Menon et al. [39] show that two healthcare information systems—clinical information systems and administrative information systems—have different impacts on patient days and medical labor productivity. Based on the results, they argue that aggregating HIT investment may not lead to correct conclusions due to these different effects. Aron et al. [5] provide empirical evidence that automating the core error prevention functions between hospitals reduces their interpretative and procedural medical errors. Menon and Kohli [38] analyze hospital panel data in Washington state. They show that past HIT expenditures, measured by depreciation expenses, are negatively associated with malpractice insurance premiums and are positively associated with healthcare quality. Bhargava and Mishra [14] focus on physician productivity after adopting EHR systems. They find that HIT adoption may bring about immediate drops in physician productivity; however, these productivity drops can be recouped in the long run if the systems are a good fit with physicians' task requirements.

Researchers are also interested in the effects of HIT adoption on healthcare quality. For instance, Bardhan and Thouin $[11]$ examine how each category of HITs, such as clinical, scheduling, or financial management system, is related to the process quality of care and operating expenses. Lin et al. $[32]$ show that adopting EHRs had no significant quality impact, but attaining meaningful use of EHRs yielded a significant 0.19–0.43 percentage point increase in the process quality of care. Other efforts also reveal that telehealth or e-health systems may help healthcare quality in various aspects $[7,12,48]$ .

While prior literature has studied the effects of HIT from diverse perspectives, most studies have mainly paid attention to its main effects only, leaving out potential spillover effects of HIT on the quality of care in HRRs. A few studies have focused on the inter-organizational effects of HIT adoption $[6,8,31,41]$ . Most notably, Atasoy et al. $[6]$ find that adopting EHRs reduces the cost of neighboring hospitals by creating significant regional spillover effects. Ayabakan et al. $[8]$ show that inter-organizational information-sharing technologies can reduce medical test duplication. Unlike those, we investigate the regional spillover effects of CDSS adoption with different market structures in an HRR and discuss the role of meaningful CDSS use in generating these effects.

Furthermore, we also explore technology dependency among HITs that generate medical benefits, which has been neglected in prior studies. EDI facilitates seamless and errorless communication among hospitals, whereas CDSS helps healthcare professionals make appropriate clinical decisions, thus resulting in enhanced health outcomes. As such, EDI may play a critical role in generating the spillover effects of an adopted CDSS.

Our study provides evidence that CDSS adoption is positively associated with a subsequent reduction in readmission rates for heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia patients across U.S. hospitals. We also find that the average adoption rate of a hospital's neighbors is significantly associated with a subsequent reduction in the hospital's readmission rate. The effect is more significant when both a hospital and its neighbors in an HRR have adopted a CDSS. Furthermore, we show that the spillover effects are greater for unconcentrated HRRs than moderately or highly concentrated HRRs. Lastly, meaningful HIT use, captured by the Stage 2 certificate, plays a critical role in explaining HIT effects.

## 3. Empirical analysis

## 3.1. Data description

We obtained data from multiple sources. We collected Hospitals' IT infrastructure, HIT adoption, operating expenses, and other organizational characteristics from the HIMSS Analytics database. We gathered readmission rates and meaningful-use status from the CMS website. We carefully matched the observations of the same hospital from different sources based on Medicare provider codes, hospital names, addresses, and zip codes.

Our interests reside in Medicare hospitals whose information has been publicly announced. Further, to examine possible spillover effects within an HRR, we chose HRRs with at least three Medicare hospitals. The final longitudinal sample includes 235 HRRs and their 1965 hospitals (for heart failure), 1354 hospitals (for acute myocardial infarction), and 2117 hospitals (for pneumonia) that have reported the annual status from 2010 to 2017.

We focus on examining the effects of CDSS adoption on subsequent readmission rates for heart failure patients, acute myocardial infarction patients, and pneumonia patients. Hospital readmission is defined as “an unplanned admission to an acute care hospital within 30 days of discharge from the same or another acute care hospital" [19]. Readmission indicates poor quality of healthcare service during the initial hospitalization because a discharged patient's health has worsened and financial costs increase. While readmission rates have been accepted as a core quality measure, heart failure has been the leading diagnosis of hospital readmission, with a median risk-standardized readmission rate of $23\%$ , from 2009 to 2012, followed by acute myocardial infarction and pneumonia [18]. Furthermore, preventing the readmission of these diagnoses is vital for patients' life. Therefore, the U.S. government mandates hospitals to report their readmission rates for these three diagnoses and penalizes hospitals whose readmission rates are above the projection. Given that the readmission rates of heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia are on the government watch list and directly affect hospitals' profitability, we set the readmission rates of these three diagnoses as our dependent variable. Readmission rates were collected from CMS as a 3-year rolling average at the hospital level for heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia.

Table 2 provides the definition and summary statistics of the main variables used in our analysis. Fig. 1 illustrates the HRRs across states, which are used in our analysis. A larger and darker circle represents that more hospitals exist in the HRR.

## 3.2. Empirical approach

We employ a fixed-effects model that controls for time-invariant hospital-level covariates. We examine how a hospital's CDSS adoption affects its readmission rate and the spillover effects of CDSS adoption by incorporating the adoption rate of its neighbors in an HRR (Eq. (1)).

EDI systems have been adopted to support effective communication among hospitals. EDI allows hospitals to exchange electronic clinical documentation such as patient demographics, physician notes, nursing notes, medication lists, and problem lists in a fast and errorless manner. The diagnosis and treatment history and medication records supported by CDSS at the initial hospital can be safely transmitted to the referred hospital through the system. We incorporate a three-way interaction term into our main models to estimate the technology dependency of CDSS on EDI in generating spillover effects (Eqs. (2) and (3)). Our model specifications are:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} r h f _ {i, t} \left(r a m i _ {i, t}, r p n _ {i, t}\right) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} C D S S _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} C D S S _ {r - i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} E D I _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {4} E D I _ {r - i, t - 1} + \gamma X _ {i, t} + \mu_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} r h f _ {i, t} \left(r a m i _ {i, t}, r p n _ {i, t}\right) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} C D S S _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} C D S S _ {r - i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} E D I _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {4} E D I _ {r - i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} E D I _ {i, t - 1} \times E D I _ {r - i, t - 1} \times C D S S _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \gamma X _ {i, t} + \mu_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} r h f _ {i, t} \left(r a m i _ {i, t}, r p n _ {i, t}\right) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} C D S S _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} C D S S _ {r - i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} E D I _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {4} E D I _ {r - i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} E D I _ {i, t - 1} \times E D I _ {r - i, t - 1} \times C D S S _ {r - i, t - 1} \\ & + \gamma X _ {i, t} + \mu_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $rhf_{i,\ t}(rami_{i,\ t}, rpn_{i,\ t})$ is the readmission rate of heart failure (or acute myocardial infarction patients, pneumonia) cases of hospital i at time t, $CDSS_{i,\ t-1}$ ( $EDI_{i,\ t-1}$ ) is the adoption state of CDSS (or EDI) of hospital i at time t-1, $CDSS_{r-i,\ t-1}$ ( $EDI_{r-,t-1}$ ) is the average adoption rate of the other hospitals in the same HRR r, except for the focal hospital i at time t-1, $X_{i,\ t}$ is a vector of the control variables, $\mu_{i}$ is the hospital-specific fixed-effects, and $\varepsilon_{i,\ t}$ is the disturbance term.

Our control variables are time-varying hospital-level covariates, including the number of computerized document applications, the number of beds, operating expenses, and an HRR's market concentration. The number of computerized healthcare systems is included to control for the effects of the hospitals' IT infrastructure. The number of beds and operating expenses are included to control for the effect of organizational size. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) and the number of hospitals in an HRR are included to control for the effects due to the HRR's market structure. Lastly, our fixed effects term controls for effects due to all time-invariant hospital-specific factors.

## 3.3. Main results

Table 3A illustrates our results for heart failure patients. Model (1) is a null model without CDSS terms. The coefficients of $EDI_{i,t-1}$ and $EDI_{r-i,t-1}$ are negative and significant, suggesting the adoption status of EDI and the EDI adoption rate of neighboring hospitals in the same HRR are negatively associated with the subsequent readmission rate of heart failure patients.

In Model (2), the coefficient of $CDSS_{i,t-1}$ is negative and significant, demonstrating that the adoption status of CDSS is negatively associated with the subsequent readmission rate of heart failure patients. This result provides evidence regarding the positive effect of CDSS adoption on the quality of care. $CDSS_{r-i,t-1}$ has a negative and significant coefficient, implying that the average CDSS adoption rate of neighboring hospitals in an HRR is also negatively associated with the subsequent readmission rate of the focal hospital. Thus, the results demonstrate the spillover effects of CDSS within an HRR.

Models (3) and (4) contain the analysis results for the technology dependency of CDSS on EDI. The negative and significant coefficient of $EDI_{i, t-1} \times EDI_{r-i, t-1} \times CDSS_{i, t-1}$ ( $EDI_{i, t-1} \times EDI_{r-i, t-1} \times CDSS_{r-i, t-1}$ ) demonstrates that the main effect and the spillover effect of CDSS adoption are greater when both a focal hospital and its neighbors have implemented EDI. These findings imply that the effects of hospitals' CDSS and EDI adoption are interdependent in delivering high-quality healthcare services.

## 4. Additional analysis

## 4.1. Spillover effects with different market structures

A hospital's choice for referrals and collaborations requiring communication and information sharing between practitioners should be related to an HRR's market structure. We employ the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), a commonly accepted measure for market concentration in considering the spillover effects across HRRs having diverse market structures. According to the U.S. Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission, the HHI index below 0.15 indicates that the market is unconcentrated, while the HHI index between 0.15 and 0.25 indicates that the market is moderately concentrated. The HHI index above 0.25 indicates that the market is highly concentrated. We employ these cutoffs to classify the HRRs each year. Our dataset includes 855 (544, 852), 507 (319, 508), and 603 (491, 757) hospitals in the low, moderate, and high market concentration groups, respectively, for heart failure (acute myocardial infarction and pneumonia).

We replicate our panel analysis for these three subsamples with the model specifications used in the main analysis (Eqs. (1)–(3)). Table 4 shows the results from HHRs with an HHI of less than 0.15 (Panel A), between 0.15 and 0.25 (Panel B), and more than 0.25 (Panel C) for heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia.

The coefficients for $CDSS_{r-i, t-1}$ are negative and significant for heart failure patients. We estimate our models with standardized variables to compare the effects across subsamples. The standardized coefficients of $CDSS_{r-i, t-1}$ for Panels A, B, and C are -0.069, -0.041, and -0.025, respectively. These results indicate that the spillover effects of HIT adoption are negatively proportional to HRRs' HHIs. We test the equality of the coefficients across panels and find a statistical difference between the standardized coefficients from Panel A and Panel B, and Panel B and Panel C (the t-values are -3.898 and -3.258, respectively). The results imply that spillover effects are greatest for HRRs with a low concentrated market, followed by HRRs with a moderately market and a highly concentrated market. The consistent results are found in acute myocardial infarction and pneumonia. In the case of acute myocardial infarction (pneumonia), the standardized coefficients of $CDSS_{r-i, t-1}$ for Panels A, B, and C are -0.075(-0.042), -0.057(-0.032), and -0.024(-0.013), respectively, indicating that spillover effects are negatively associated with the HHI.

The standardized coefficients of $EDI_{i, t-1} \times EDI_{r-i, t-1} \times CDSS_{r-i, t-1}$ in Model (4) show that the synergetic effect of EDI in generating the spillover effects is also negatively associated with the HHI, indicating that the stronger synergetic effects with a low concentrated market.

The results illustrate that spillover effects depend on HRRs' market structures. Specifically, the effects are prevalent when the healthcare market is very competitive, such that there are many hospital choices for referrals or collaborations. These effects, however, may shrink when a few hospitals dominate within the referral region.

The above relationship between market concentration and spillover effects may be explained by hospital selection in MHS within an HRR. Hospitals in the same MHS involve high levels of communication and more substantial spillover effects $[36]$ because they have higher interoperability and fewer concerns in patient information exchanges $[6]$ . However, the selection in MHS is not random but is affected by hospital characteristics. Big hospitals with more beds or higher profitability are less likely to enter the MHS $[3]$ , suggesting that HRRs with higher HHI may benefit less from such spillover effects.

## 4.3. HRR-level analysis

Considering possible spillover effects, the overall HIT adoption at the HRR level could contribute to the healthcare quality of the region. We conduct an HRR-level analysis to examine how an HRR's average adoption rates of CDSS and EDI affect its average readmission rate. The model specification is the same as our main models, except that all variables are HRR-level. Table 6 shows the results. The average adoption rates of CDSS in an HRR are associated with a decrease in the average readmission rate of the HRR, which illustrates the positive effects. However, its interaction with EDI does not have any effect at the HRR level.

## 4.4. Meaningful use

An assimilation gap between simple HIT implementation and actual usage could exist in a hospital $[21]$ , and gap ranges are possibly diverse across hospitals. Such gaps normally result from a lack of knowledge in leveraging various HIT types and misalignment between HIT and internal clinical workflows. Thus, we further integrate a hospital's actual usage (which is measured as a hospital's meaningful-use attestation) into our models.

This analysis can identify how the effect of CDSS adoption varies with a hospital's actual usage. The actual usage was measured by whether a hospital attested to the meaningful-use Stage 2, which was initiated in 2014 and expanded the meaningful-use Stage 1 criteria (i.e., improving data capture and sharing) to advanced care processes with decision support. The purposes of the meaningful-use Stage 2 beyond Stage 1 are well-aligned with the adoption of CDSS and EDI. While Stage 1 does not pay much attention to data exchange or the use of CDSS, Stage 2 requires all medical documents to be sent electronically to other hospitals for at least $50\%$ of referral cases and involves the meaningful use of CDSS functionalities.

Thus, we further test whether meaningful use, which is measured by the receipt of the Stage 2 certificate, makes any difference in the spill-over effects of HIT adoption. We apply our panel model, Eq. (1), to hospitals that are certified as Stage 2 and hospitals that are not licensed separately. There are 1525 (1171 and 1620) hospitals in Stage 2 and 440 (183 and 497) hospitals in Stage 1 or below for heart failure (acute myocardial infarction and pneumonia). Table 7 shows the estimation results. Concerning both Stage 1 and Stage 2 hospitals, the estimates for $CDSS_{i,t-1}$ and $CDSS_{r-i,t-1}$ are negative and significant.

However, in Stage 1 hospitals, both the main and spillover effects of CDSS adoption are smaller than in Stage 2 hospitals, suggesting that hospitals with relatively low-level actual usage cannot fully utilize adopted CDSS for better quality care. These results are consistent across the three diagnoses.

## 5. Conclusion

This study investigates the effects of CDSS adoption on the hospital readmission rates with the three leading diagnoses of hospital readmission: heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia. Using large-scale hospital panel datasets from multiple sources, we find evidence that a hospital's CDSS adoption is associated with a subsequent reduction in its readmission rates. We also reveal the spillover effects of CDSS adoption within an HRR. These spillover effects become stronger for an HRR with less concentrated healthcare markets. Furthermore, our results indicate that a focal hospital's EDI adoption, along with its neighboring hospitals, is critical in fostering the spillover effects of CDSS adoption in the HRR.

Our results highlight the importance of government-driven HIT investments in the U.S. The spillover effects become greater for hospitals that have adopted CDSS, along with a higher CDSS adoption level within the HRR. The negative impact of CDSS adoption on hospital readmission rates can be greater with the CDSS adoption of the other hospitals in the HRR.

Such spillover effects vary with the market concentrations of regional healthcare markets. The spillover effects of both CDSS and EDI are strongest for HRRs with an unconcentrated healthcare market, followed by HRRs with a moderately concentrated healthcare market. The spillover effects are weakest for HRRs with a highly concentrated healthcare market. This finding suggests that policies for effective HIT adoption should consider various regional market structures. Therefore, government interventions aiming to maximize the spillover effects of CDSS adoption need to be tailored based on the types of healthcare market structures.

The spillover effect of CDSS adoption becomes greater when a focal hospital adopts EDI and when the average EDI adoption rate of neighboring hospitals in its HRR is higher. EDI adoption moderates the spillover effects of CDSS adoption. This indicates the technology interdependence among HITs in enhancing healthcare quality.

Our study contributes to the growing body of literature on the impacts of HITs. While most prior empirical studies focus on the hospital or physician-level effects of HIT adoption based on a small sample, we use large nationwide panels to examine not only the hospital-level effects of the adoption but also the possible spillover effects at an HRR level. A few recent studies have paid attention to the spillover effects of HIT adoption; however, their focus has been on the cost side $[6,8]$ . To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first to provide empirical evidence regarding the spillover effects of HIT adoption on healthcare quality, considering market concentration and actual usage beyond simple HIT implementation. The subsample analysis across various market concentrations provides the market conditions where spillover effects can prevail. The analysis of the three-way interaction of EDI adoption, the EDI adoption rate in the HRR, and CDSS adoption highlights the importance of infrastructure for effective communication among hospitals in terms of fostering the spillover effects of standalone technology. We further find that the spillover effects of CDSS adoption increase along with higher actual CDSS usage.

By the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP), hospitals are subject to financial penalties when the readmission rate of heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, and pneumonia patients exceeds the projected rate. To avoid such financial penalties and reap the spill-over effects, leading hospitals may motivate other hospitals in the same region to adopt and meaningfully use a CDSS $[26]$ . An empirical analysis of 98 hospitals in the State of Washington also reveals that while reducing readmissions is associated with decreased margin due to high operating revenue from readmission, continued readmissions are related to the gradual increase in expenses and reduced profitability $[47]$ .

As for the suggestion for future studies, while we focus on the effects of CDSS and EDI, the effects of other HITs can be explored in the next step. If an analysis of other technologies shows different results, such that no spillover effect is found, it would be interesting to investigate which technological characteristics of HITs drive such differential results. Also, we may expect prominent CDSS effects in reducing subsequent readmissions (e.g., second or third readmissions) as patient records accumulate. It is practically meaningful to confirm whether the CDSS effects are indeed more substantial on the second or third readmission. Another exciting venue is investigating HIT adoption and consumer welfare coupled with structural changes in healthcare industries. Horizontal and vertical integration of health systems may promote HIT adoption, as the massive systems would have resources for HIT implementation with its economies of scale. However, at the same time, the presence of such systems may harm industry competition and spillover effects in the region and adversely work for local hospitals' HIT adoption outside the system and payers.
