## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Charity crowdfunding Emotion expression Visual modality Verbal modality Verbal superiority Congruency effect

## A B S T R A C T

Online crowdfunding platforms offer a valuable channel to raise funding for various philanthropic causes, and fundraisers need to understand how to present campaign appeals to motivate charity giving decisions. This study examines the relationship between multi-modal emotion expression in campaign messages and charity crowd funding success. Specifically, we explore the interaction of two modalities, including the visual modality expressed through beneficiaries’ face images and the verbal modality expressed through text descriptions. Using data collected from a leading charity crowdfunding platform, we find superiority of the verbal modality to the visual modality of emotion expression in explaining crowdfunding campaign success. In addition, emotion consistency between visual and verbal modalities helps mitigate the failure of online charity crowdfunding campaigns, suggesting a congruency effect. This study offers a new theoretical understanding of the role of multimodal emotion expression in online charity crowdfunding campaigns and practical guidance on the design of effective campaign messages.

## 1. Introduction

Online platforms have been increasingly used to achieve various personal goals or altruistic causes, and have “grown into a multibilliondollar industry worldwide” over the past decade [36, pp. 587]. There are four types of crowdfunding platforms—donation-based, debt-based, rewards-based, and equity-based [50]. Among them, donation-based or charity crowdfunding platforms, such as Fundly, Mightycause, and Gofundme, focus on philanthropic or altruistic causes and help in dividuals and organizations seek donations from societies [29]. These platforms become valuable and viable channels for organizing cam paigns, raising awareness, and increasing reach to potential donors. According to a recent report, more than half of the donors prefer online contributions [65]. Despite the increasing popularity of online chari table giving, the global average success rate for crowdfunding cam paigns is only 22.4%.<sup>1</sup> The effectiveness of campaigns varies based on how potential donors are solicited [5]. Thus fundraisers need to un derstand how to present campaign appeals to achieve fundraising success.

Emotions play a crucial role in communication and decision-making processes [69,70,77]. Charity giving is “an almost automatic emotional response, producing a positive mood, alleviating feelings of guilt, reducing aversive arousal, satisfying a desire to show gratitude, or to be a morally just person.” [5, pp. 938]. Emotional contagion theory sug gests that charity messages can convey the emotions of campaign ben eficiaries to potential donors to encourage prosocial behavior [1,75]. Fundraisers can increase the awareness of the need for donation using facial emotion expressions (e.g., happy or sad faces) and emotional terms such as “heartbreaking” and “mournful” [5].

As a psychological reaction, charity giving is associated with different categories of emotions [67]. However, it remains unclear which categories of emotions can effectively motivate charity giving. Prior literature presents mixed findings regarding the two opposite emotions, happiness and sadness [1,25,39,75]. Moreover, online crowdfunding platforms enable multi-modal emotion expressions, including the visual modality such as images and the verbal modality such as text descriptions. Presentation modality has long been recog nized as influential in determining consumers’ information acquisition strategies [7,45]. Yet, there is limited understanding of how to combine multiple modalities to promote the success of online crowdfunding campaigns.

To fill those literature gaps, our research examines multi-modal emotion expressions in the context of online crowdfunding campaigns. We propose research hypotheses about charity crowdfunding success and empirically test these hypotheses using data collected from a lead ing charity crowdfunding platform. Based on the results of text and vi sual analytics, and econometric estimation, we offer new insights on how emotion expression influences online charity giving decisions. We examine two commonly used presentation modalities: the visual mo dality and the verbal modality. The visual cues are more emotive and vivid, while the verbal cues send people to the rational information processing pathway [44]. Our research findings demonstrate verbal superiority, as the verbal modality is more influential than the visual modality in online charity crowdfunding success. Specifically, the re sults show that verbal sadness has a negative association with the campaign success, and the visual emotions do not yield any direct effect. People process information carefully before donating to online charity campaigns and the verbal modality outperforms the visual modality in such a deliberative decision-making process. Moreover, our research demonstrates a congruency effect between the visual and verbal mo dalities, as emotion consistency between visual and verbal modalities helps mitigate the failure of online charity crowdfunding campaigns. Using multiple presentation modalities to deliver a cohesive message has a positive impact on individuals’ decision-making. Those findings could also help fundraisers effectively design their campairn appeals. Our research is among the first studies that investigate emotion expressions across multiple modalities in online charitable fundraising. We contribute to the literature on the interaction of emotion expression, charitable giving, and management of online crowdfunding campaigns.

We organize the remainder of the paper as follows. We first review three relevant literature streams, including emotion and charitable donation, presentation modalities, and multi-modal emotion percep tion, and identify the existing research gaps. Then, we propose hy potheses, describe our research context, and present empirical analyses and results. Finally, we conclude the paper with discussions of our major findings.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Emotion and charitable donation

Emotions are associated with behavioral reactions, such as charitable giving [30,40], information recall [70], information sharing [75], and investment decisions [64,69]. Emotive appeals have been widely used in charitable campaigns, and thus we review the literature exploring emotions expressed via campaign messages and their impacts on in dividuals’ charity donation intentions and behavior (Table 1). In gen eral, research on emotion expression in charity advertisements is still very limited. More importantly, the findings of these studies are mixed, and whether positive or negative emotions encourage people’s prosocial behavior remains inconclusive.

Some research suggests that negative emotion is effective in moti vating people to help others. According to the emotional contagion theory, facial emotion expression can be transmitted to and felt by ob servers [63,69]. The impact of beneficiaries’ facial expressions has been studied in lab experiments [1,75]. Small and Verrochi [75] find that people “are particularly sympathetic and likely to donate when they see sad expressions versus happy or neutral expressions” (pp. 777). Simi larly, Baberini et al. [1] find that people feel sad and sympathetic after viewing photos of sad children and are more likely to donate to the corresponding campaigns. Haynes et al. [35] study donation to animal welfare, showing that the negative image appeal (i.e., guilt) of dogs is more effective than the positive image appeal $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , warmth) in evoking participants’ donation intention. Lu [54] studies emotional appeals in verbal communication, suggesting that the sadness appeal is more effective than the hope appeal in promoting participants’ support for managing environmental crises.

Other studies suggest that positive emotions in advertisements are associated with prosocial behavior. Cavanaugh et al. [13] examine four different positive emotions $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ love, pride, hope, and compassion) expressed in Cannon magazine advertisements. They find that while all positive emotions induce prosocial behavior toward close entities, only love encourages prosocial behavior toward distant others [13]. Genev sky and Knutson [25] collect data from a micro-lending website. They find that images with positive affective features are associated with higher lending rates, while the percentage of positive or negative words is not associated with the lending rate. Liang et al. [51] investigate textbased donation appeal materials and demonstrate an interesting inter action effect between positive and negative emotions. Specifically, they find that “combining the positive emotion of strength and the negative emotion of sadness is more effective as a means of persuading people to donate than the sadness-dominating or the strength-dominating emotion” (pp. 283).

Given the different findings on the relationship between emotion expressions and prosocial behavior, further examination in the context of online charity crowdfunding is needed. More importantly, the inter action effect of presentation modalities and emotion expression is not well understood in the charitable giving context. Cavanaugh et al. [13] study magazine advertisements without distinguishing images and text. Genevsky and Knutson [25] separate visual and verbal modalities without examining the potential interactions between them. Their data are collected from a micro-lending platform, which is different from a donation-based platform. Small and Verrochi [75] find that images contagion effects diminish in the presence of text information. However, their study focuses on text presence without considering the emotions expressed in text and the potential interaction between visual and verbal emotions. Our research intends to fill those gaps.

## 2.2. Presentation modalities

Presentation modalities affect how viewers process information during the decision-making process and the consequent decision outcome [7,45]. Visual modality is often considered to convey heuristic cues, which are associated with peripheral or heuristic modes of pro cessing [43,81]. Meanwhile, verbal modality evokes a central and sys tematic mode of processing [47]. When both visual and verbal modalities are used to present information, they can compete with or complement each other.

Prior literature has compared the effectiveness of the visual modality and verbal modality. On the one hand, some studies suggest the visual superiority effect. Joffe [44] argues that the visual modality leads people to the emotive pathway of thought, while the verbal modality sends people to the rational and logical pathway. Compared to the verbal modality, the visual modality is more emotive, vivid, and memorable [44]. Many marketing studies examine and compare visual and verbal elements in advertisements and find that visual information is associated with better memory and greater persuasion than verbal information $[ 1 6 , 4 4 , 6 1 , 7 6 ]$ . On the other hand, other research indicates verbal su periority. Verbal cues are more explicit and specific than visual cues. In a decision situation full of uncertainties, verbal stimuli can be more effective in aiding consumers’ decision-making processes [76]. Kim and Lennon [47] investigate production presentation formats in online shopping and demonstrate a verbal superiority effect on consumers purchase intention. Verbal information might enable consumers to feel more knowledgeable about the product [47].

Visual and verbal modalities can also collaborate to create shared meaning, leading to the congruency effect. Synergies can be created based on collaborative strengths in both visual and verbal modalitie [32]. Multiple and coherent cues can enhance information processing and encoding [58,70]. Visual elements become a distractor if they are different from verbal content [21]. In contrast, similarities between images and texts are positively related to users’ engagement in social media [73]. Image-text relevance also encourages informational recall from websites [70].

The superiority of presentation modalities is context-dependent, and decision scenarios are critical in determining viewers’ engagement [18,43]. Thus it is important to carefully examine the superiority of the visual or verbal modality in the online charity crowdfunding context. The literature also suggests the congruency effect between visual and verbal modalities. However, it is unclear whether such an effect can be extended to emotion expressions in multi-modal charity messages.

## 2.3. Multi-modal emotion perception

The third stream of literature relevant to our research examines emotion recognition via multiple modalities and understands in dividuals’ abilities to recognize emotions across multiple modalities [53]. Emotions can be expressed, delivered, and integrated across different channels, such as visual, vocal, and verbal modalities [9]. Since the vocal modality is not commonly used in crowdfunding platforms, we focus on the visual and verbal modalities.

Multi-modal emotion analyses explore both superiority and con gruency effects among various modalities in emotion processing or perception [18]. One modality may dominate the other if emotion ex pressions are inconsistent across channels [2]. The congruency effect is evident through the advantages of multi-modal encoding of emotions, where automatic integration of congruent emotional information across channels enhances the accessibility of emotion-related knowledge in memory [66]. Consistent emotions from multiple channels can strengthen audiences’ perceptions [18]. Accordingly, it is beneficial to merge complementary information from different modalities.

Multimodality emotion analyses typically assume that information is transmitted simultaneously via multiple channels. For instance, a speaker delivers facial expression, vocal stimuli, and verbal content simultaneously in a speech. In our case, however, different modalities are presented sequentially, which display an image at the top followed by detailed text descriptions of a charity campaign. It is thus interesting to explore modality superiority and congruency effect between the vi sual and verbal modalities in online charity crowdfunding campaigns.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. The main effect of sadness and happiness appeals

Emotional contagion theory posits that individuals can share emo tions either explicitly or implicitly in various ways [34,69]. For instance, emotions expressed in an advertisement can trigger similar emotions in observers [75]. Accordingly, we expect that emotions expressed in on line crowdfunding campaign messages elicit similar emotions in po tential donors. A follow-up question is whether happiness or sadness would be more effective in motivating charity giving. To this end, we propose two hypotheses based on the related literature.

Prior studies have found a positive effect of sadness in encouraging prosocial behavior. When observing sadness expressed by beneficiaries asking for help, people are more likely to be saddened and become sympathetic toward those beneficiaries. Sympathetic people are more likely to be generous and prosocial, and donate more to charitable causes than unsympathetic ones [3]. After witnessing sad emotional appeals from those beneficiaries, potential donors might be concerned about the beneficiaries' well-being. A feeling of sorrow for the benefi: ciaries’ unfortunate situation might motivate an individual to offer help, as helping others contributes to attenuating their own unpleasant emotions [62]. Small and Verrochi [75] show that beneficiaries expression of sadness fosters observers’ sympathy, who in turn are more likely to donate. Baberini et al. [1] also show that sad images evoke sympathetic responses. Although both studies use controlled lab ex periments, we expect that the findings can be generalized to real-world environments. Charity crowdfunding campaigns with sad campaign messages are more likely to encourage potential donors’ contributions, leading to charity crowdfunding success. Thus we propose the following hypothesis:

## H1a. Sadness is positively associated with charity crowdfunding success.

On the other hand, happiness, which is the opposite of sadness, has also been effective in eliciting charity donations. Prior studies have shown that consumers are more likely to engage in prosocial behaviors if they experience positive emotions [39]. “If consumers feel good, they are more like to do good” [13, pp. 658]. Potential donors might feel happy if they experience a happiness appeal from a campaign. While individuals are in a positive emotional state such as happiness, they might want to do good things, such as helping the beneficiary. Fiala and Noussair [23] find that donors in a more positive overall emotional state tend to donate more. Genevksy and Knutson [25] find that microloans showing photos with positive affective features are more likely to suc ceed. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1b. Happiness is positively associated with charity crowdfunding success.

## 3.2. Verbal superiority

In online crowdfunding campaigns, emotional appeals are usually expressed through both visual and verbal modalities. Potential donors view both images and text descriptions before making their donation decisions. In general, information processing associated with the visual modality is more heuristic, while information processing associated with the verbal modality is more deliberative [43,47]. The pathways along which people often use to process visuals are more emotive, while verbal materials send people in a more rational, logical, and linear pathway of thought [44]. We propose verbal modality superiority in our research context, since making an online crowdfunding donation tends to be a deliberative effort. Donating money to others in the real world is a highinvolvement decision-making process partly because money is a type of limited or scarce resource. Given a large number of active campaigns at any given moment on an online crowdfunding website, a donor needs to carefully evaluate individual campaigns and make a rational decision regarding whether and to which campaign to donate. A highinvolvement decision-making process is likely to engage in careful and deliberate information processing rather than intuitive and heuristic processing [14]. Donations to friends tend to be intuitive and sponta neous affective reactions [74]. However, many users donate to total strangers in online crowdfunding campaigns, with whom they have no close interpersonal relationships. Donating to strangers tends to be deliberative, relying on rational processing of campaign text messages. When potential donors read text messages, they exert efforts that strengthen deliberate processing and weaken the impact of images [75].

Prior marketing research on visual superiority usually examines commercial advertising images [43,47]. However, the campaign images examined in our research are different from advertisement images. Advertisement images are rhetorical symbols, which require elaboration due to their contextualization and stylization [72]. However, online crowdfunding campaign photos depict the faces of beneficiaries, which are straightforward rather than rhetorical. Thus, campaign images require less elaborative cognitive processing than commercial adver tising images, and visual superiority is less likely to happen to campaign images. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2. The verbal modality has a stronger effect than the visual modality on charity crowdfunding success.

## 3.3. The congruency effect between visual and verbal modalities

The emotions expressed through different modalities can be either congruent or discordant with each other. Take the two polars of emo tions, sadness and happiness, as examples. Emotion is congruent when both campaign images and text express happiness or sadness.

The quality of communication can influence the perception of donors and further impact the donations [71]. Similar information delivered via both visual and verbal cues can enhance users’ shared understanding of the message [58,66,70]. In online charity crowdfunding campaigns, people are more likely to act if they can better understand the benefi ciaries’ needs. Congruent informational cues across different modalities can facilitate potential donors’ processing of campaign messages. When the visual and verbal modalities deliver a cohesive argument, they can reinforce each other to convince the audience [32]. Such an additivity effect across channels [38] makes the campaign messages more persuasive. In contrast, information discordance across channels can lead to an attenuation effect [38]. People might feel confused and dis engaged when they are exposed to opposing emotions via images and text messages. When making charitable donations, individuals want to find some assurance that their money will be used properly [80]. Congruent informational cues between the images and text messages help enhance potential donors’ trust in charity campaigns, leading to donation behavior. Therefore, we propose the last hypothesis as:

H3. Emotion consistency between the visual and verbal modalities is posi tively associated with charity crowdfunding success.

## 4. Research context and data

To test the proposed hypotheses, we collect data from one of the largest online charity crowdfunding platforms in the U.S.<sup>2</sup> The platform built a community of >120 million donations and helped people raise over \$15 billion by 2021. To help people and regions affected by COVID 19, the platform has been not only organizing a COVID-19 relief fund, but also providing much-needed aid for coronavirus fundraisers. The platform provides support for charitable fundraising in a number of categories, including medical fundraising, in which it is a global leader.

According to a Forbes report [60], nearly one-third of crowdfunding campaigns on the platform intended to raise funds for medical costs. Approximately 20% of American households reported donating to a crowdfunding campaign to pay for medical bills or treatments.<sup>3</sup> U.S. health care spending continues to skyrocket, hitting \$3.8 trillion or \$11,582 per person in 2019,<sup>4</sup> an increase of 4.6% over that in 2018. Health care costs have risen faster than the average annual income. About 41% of working-age Americans have medical bill problems or are paying off medical debt.<sup>5</sup> Medical expenses are the leading cause of bankruptcy in the U.S. [37]. A study shows that medical crowdfunding has helped prevent 3.9% of medical bankruptcies across the U.S. [10]. Thus, we choose to focus on medical fundraising in this study in view that the impact of discrete emotions on decision-making processes is context-dependent [26]. Our research is one of the first studies focusing on multimodal emotion expressions via a crowdfunding platform. Starting with a single fundraising category will help eliminate potential confounding factors associated with different fundraising categories. We collect medical campaign data from the platform for the period from February to March 2018. The raw campaign data goes through multiple preprocessing steps, including data cleansing, information extraction, emotion analysis, and information merging and filtering, as shown in Fig. 1.

## 4.1. Data cleansing

We remove metatags related to message formatting, text irrelevant to the campaign contents (e.g., “+ Read More”), and non-English cam paigns from the raw data.

## 4.2. Information extraction

We mainly extract three types of data from the cleansed campaign messages: image (the visual modality), text (the verbal modality), and metadata. The image data consist of the main images appearing at the top of campaign webpages, which are typically photos of campaign beneficiaries. The text data contain campaign descriptions, which detail personal stories for charitable causes. The metadata consist of other information about the campaigns, such as raised amount, goal amount, title, number of shares, number of donors, number of updates, disease type, date created, and fundraiser. We provide a detailed description of the extracted information in the Analyses and Results section.

## 4.3. Emotion analysis

As part of information extraction, emotion analysis is introduced separately because of its importance for this study and the complexity involved in understanding the emotion expressions in campaign images and text. The emotion analysis starts with selecting an emotion cate gorization schema. In our dataset, a campaign image usually contains one or more human faces, which justifies the use of facial expressions as the basis for emotion categorization [22]. The emotion schema consists of six basic emotions representing universal facial expressions — anger, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise, and disgust [22].

Recently, advanced machine learning techniques have been applied to analyzing sentiment [55] and emotions across multiple modalities [15,68]. However, those methods either extract a subset or a different set of the basic emotions [68] or estimate sentiment on a continuous

We employ Microsoft Azure Emotion API [19] to extract basic emotions from the human faces in images, which normalizes emotion values to the range of 0 to 1. Based on the comparisons of face detection performance [46], Microsoft Azure emotion API is the best for straight images, which successfully detects all the faces in the half-left, half-right, and straight angles. Tools are abundant for analyzing emotions in text. To enable a fair comparison between images and text, we choose the Syuzhet R package [42], which extracts the same set of basic emotions from texts, as does the Azure Emotion API from images.<sup>6</sup> The predictions of the package have demonstrated moderate to very strong positive correlations (0.52– 0.83) with human sentiment ratings [33,41]. The package has been used to understand medical-related issues such as the emotions of patients who have survived severe medical episodes, emotional health during the pandemic lockdown, and attitude toward work-from-home experience [24]. The procedure for analyzing text emotions is as follows. We first parse each campaign text into sentences, and then extract emotions from each sentence by referencing a lexicon. Next, we derive an overall measure of each of the emotions for a campaign description by computing the average score of all of its sentences. Finally, we normalize the values of all verbal emotions to the range of 0 to 1.

To ensure the accuracy of emotion analysis results, we perform manual checks on emotion extraction from text and images. We randomly sample a small set of campaign images and texts from our dataset, assigned each data point two human coders who were asked to manually tag visual and verbal emotions, and compared human coder labels with computer tool outputs. The Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.726 for the campaign text and 0.734 for the campaign images, and the percentage of agreement for the campaign text was 77% and that for the campaign images was 80.4% respectively. Thus the performances of both emotion extraction tools are acceptable.

We also extract additional attributes of the faces detected from campaign images using Microsoft Azure Face API [19]. These attributes include age, gender, smile (i.e., smile intensity), blur (i.e., whether a face is blurry or not), and noise (i.e., the noise level of face pixel). In case of more than one face, the tool measures the attributes of each face in an image separately. We derive the number of faces and aggregate the face attributes by averaging across all faces in an image. In addition, we also compute word count and the readability score using TextStat,<sup>7</sup> and subjectivity and polarity scores using TextBlob,<sup>8</sup> from the cleaned campaign texts. ## 5. Analyses and results

5.1. The dimensionality of visual and verbal emotions: an exploratory analysis

We first conduct an exploratory analysis to examine and compare the six categories of emotions communicated in visual and verbal modal ities. The descriptive statistics of visual and verbal emotions are reported in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. We can draw two main observations from the analysis results.

Observation 1. Emotions expressed in the visual modality tend to be unidimensional, and happiness dominates other emotions expressed in the visual modality.

Table 2 shows that most crowdfunding campaigns choose images showing happiness in their campaign messages. It is worth noting that the distribution of happiness visual emotion score, Happiness<sup>Image</sup>, is highly negatively skewed (see Fig. 2). Compared with happiness, negative emotions, such as anger, fear, surprise, and disgust, have a much rarer presence in images, with their mean scores being all <0.05. Among the negative emotions, sadness is the most intense, and fear is the least intense.

We further analyze the correlations among the different categories of visual emotions. The analysis yields a strong and negative correlation (coefficient = − 0.84) between Happiness<sup>Image</sup> and Sadness<sup>Image</sup>. It sug gests that the majority of campaign images show either happy or sad faces. Additionally, Happiness<sup>Image</sup> is negatively associated with other visual negative emotions. It might be difficult for someone to express multiple emotions, especially a mixture of positive and negative emo tions, in a single snapshot of his/her face. Emotions expressed through facial expressions tend to be either positive or negative [59].

Observation 2. Emotions expressed in the verbal modality are multidimensional.

Table 3 shows that emotions communicated in the text are distrib uted more evenly across the six dimensions than those expressed in images. Specifically, Happiness<sup>Text</sup> and Fear<sup>Text</sup> have the highest levels of intensity with their means being 0.220 and 0.266, respectively, while Surprise<sup>Text</sup> and Disgust<sup>Text</sup> have the lowest intensity with their means being 0.083 and 0.088, respectively. The analysis of correlations among the six emotions from the text further confirms that verbal emotions are nuanced and multi-dimensional. The correlations across all pairs of verbal emotions range from weak to moderate. The results suggest that text facilitates simultaneous conveyance of a rich set of emotions through a sequence of words, sentences, and paragraphs. Campaign descriptions typically consist of multiple sentences, which allow for the expression of multiple emotions. Medical mishaps are emotionally charged encounters. On the one hand, the medical condition itself arouses negative emotional reactions. On the other hand, the origination of a crowdfunding campaign indicates that there is still hope for improving the beneficiary’s medical condition. Thus, the campaign message is composed of a mixture of positive and negative emotions.

Despite that images are more noticeable, available, and vivid than verbal stimuli [79], the results of our exploratory analyses show the conspicuousness of the verbal modality in simultaneous communication of multiple categories of emotions. Both observations have been further verified with campaigns containing multiple face images, and all anal ysis results are consistent.

We incorporate various campaign-level control variables, including campaign features, image qualities, and additional textual features, into the model estimation. Campaigns vary in the targeted financial goals set by campaign fundraisers (Goal), the number of donors who have contributed to the campaigns (Donors), the number of updates posted by campaign fund raisers (Update), and the time elapsed since the campaign’s origination date (Month). While we have controlled for various campaign-level factors, the number of donors and campaign success may be jointly influenced by some unobservable factors. Thus, the variable, Donor, is potentially endogenous. For instance, we do not have information about the financial well-being of donation beneficiaries’ social networks or whether a campaign has been advocated in other public media outlets. These unobserved factors affect not only the number of donors but also the campaign’s success. We employ the instrument variable (IV) approach to address the endogeneity concern [12,82]. We use the number of times that a campaign has been shared across different social media platforms, such as Facebook, Share, as the instrument variable for Donor. Instrument variables should meet two conditions. First, the in strument variables are correlated with the endogenous variables. It is likely that social media sharing can help promote the campaign and reach out to a larger number of potential donors. Thus, the number of Facebook shares, Share, is likely to be correlated with the number of donors, Donor. Second, instrument variables do not directly affect dependent variables. It is sensible that Share affects campaign success indirectly via Donor. While social media sharing helps enhance cam paigns’ visibility, it contributes to campaign success via increasing the number of donors.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

Emotions motivate various human behaviors, and emotion expres sion has long been recognized as an important factor influencing char itable giving. We examine how multi-modal emotion expressions affect charity crowdfunding success using observational data collected from a leading online charity crowdfunding platform. We summarize the key findings of our study, discuss their implications for research and prac tice, and point out limitations and directions for future research.

## 6.1. Key findings

This study offers several observations and findings. In general, visual emotions expressed via facial expressions in an image tend to be unidimensional, while text has the capacity to express multidimensional emotions simultaneously. Our results demonstrate the deterrence effect of verbal sadness in charity giving, which is in stark contrast to the findings of prior literature that sadness contagion facil itates sympathy [1,35,75]. In the context of online charity crowdfund ing, we show verbal superiority—emotions expressed via verbal modality are stronger than those expressed via visual modality in affecting campaign success. In addition, this study identifies a congru ency effect between modalities — the emotion consistency between vi sual and verbal modalities helps enhance the persuasiveness of charity campaign appeals. When the facial expression in an image is happy, expressing sadness in the corresponding text description significantly inhibits campaign success. When the facial expression in an image is less happy, the negative effect of sadness words diminishes.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions and practical implications

Our research makes several notable theoretical contributions. First, our research shows verbal superiority when emotions are expressed via both visual and verbal modalities on online charity crowdfunding platforms. The result corroborates prior findings that automatic and non-inferential emotional contagion from images may diminish after deliberative thoughts [75]. We perform an additional analysis to rule out an alternative explanation of verbal superiority due to the recency effect. The recency effect of memory suggests that short-term memory tends to decay over time, and people are most likely to respond to the most recent item that they read to make subsequent decisions [31,78]. Online charity crowdfunding campaigns present photos first, followed by text messages detailing the causes and requests for assistance. If the recency effect existed, we expect that the length of the campaign description would negatively moderate the effect of visual emotion. However, the result in Appendix A shows that the interaction between visual emotion and word count is not statistically significant. The effect of visual emotion does not change with the length of text, suggesting the recency effect might not be present. Thus, careful, rational, and delib erative information processing contributes to the superiority of verbal emotion over visual emotion.

Second, we provide new evidence that emotion consistency between the visual and verbal modalities helps enhance the persuasiveness of charity campaign appeals. Harkins and Petty [33] suggest that more similar information from multiple sources is more likely to be accepted and trusted by people, and such a multiple-source effect supports emotion congruency across multiple modalities. Our results show that happy visual emotion intensifies the negative impact of sad verbal emotion.

Third, while prior literature usually focuses on the positive impacts of either happiness or sadness emotion on prosocial behaviors, this is one of the first studies that empirically demonstrate the deterrence effect of sadness verbal emotion. One plausible explanation is that sad words may create situational pressure and uncomfortable feeling, making people morally disengage from the current campaign. Such a disen gagement is more likely to occur in the real world than in a controlled lab experiment, since it is harder to exercise the exit option in the latter environment. Our research points out an important distinction between the controlled lab environment and the real-life context, which triggers social norms differently [6,49]. In future studies, researchers might add an exit option in their experimental design and ensure participants anonymity. They would allow researchers to observe how the exit option might affect the connection between emotions and charitable giving.

Our findings have practical implications for fundraisers to design and compose campaign messages effectively. When leveraging multiple modalities to present a campaign, people should ensure that information delivered through images, text, and other modalities is consistent. Multiple modalities should support each other to create a coherent campaign appeal. In contrast, expressing conflicting emotions across multiple modalities is detrimental to the success of online charity crowdfunding campaigns.

Due to verbal superiority, fundraisers need to pay special attention to emotion expressions in the text messages. People tend to be very cautious when making donation decisions to online charity campaigns. When potential donors become interested in learning about campaign details, such as beneficiaries’ specific needs and situations, they will read campaign text beyond images. Reading detailed elaboration in text demands significant cognitive efforts of donors, and such deliberative thoughts weaken the direct effect of visual emotions [57,75]. On an online crowdfunding website, people can withdraw themselves from a campaign without being noticed by others. Potential donors can easily escape from the negative state elicited by sadness [4]. Thus, fundraisers might want to avoid using too many emotionally charged words asso ciated with sadness.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

Our study has some limitations, and future research can extend this line of investigation in several promising directions. First, our findings are drawn from analyzing data collected from the medical category of charity crowdfunding campaigns on a single platform. Future research can extend them to other crowdfunding contexts to enhance our un derstanding of the intricate role of multimodal emotion expressions. For instance, potential donors’ responses to emotion expressions may change when facing other causes such as fundraising for schools and sports teams. It is important to consider subtle emotional cues, the modalities used to convey those emotions, and consistency between different modalities in planning an online crowdfunding campaign to enhance its chance of success. Second, we use observational data to examine the relationships between multimodal emotion expressions in campaign messages and the success of online crowdfunding campaigns. Future research can further explore the donors’ perceptions and emotional responses to emotion expressions in campaign messages and verify the underlying mechanisms. Third, emotion expressions and re sponses are influenced by social and cultural situations [52]. The cam paigns in our study are all created in the U.S. It is worthwhile for future research to examine the roles and interactions of different emotional modalities in impacting campaign success across different cultures. Fourth, there is space to improve performances of the emotion extrac tion tools by leveraging new developments in computer vision. In addition, image background might also play a role in recognizing visual emotions from the images in crowdfunding campaigns of other categories.
