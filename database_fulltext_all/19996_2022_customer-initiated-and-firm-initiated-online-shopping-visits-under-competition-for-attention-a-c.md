# Customer-initiated and firm-initiated online shopping visits under competition for attention: A conceptual model and empirical analysis

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Conceptual model Online shopping Customer-initiated channel Firm-initiated channel Competition for attention Goal activation

## A B S T R A C T

In the recent past, several studies have empirically compared the effectiveness of two or more online marketing channels in influencing consumer behavior. While some of these studies have found that the effectiveness tends to vary across channels, at a conceptual level, there is no clear understanding of why such consumer differences exist. There is, therefore, an urgent need to develop a conceptual understanding of how different online mar keting channels work so that they can be deployed effectively. To address this need, we present a conceptual model rooted in prior research in online visit type and competition for attention. Specifically, we examine whether the influence of the type of visit (customer-initiated or firm-initiated) engendered by the marketing channel is moderated by the competition for the consumer's attention inherent in the channel. The model helps us understand why and how different online channels influence consumers' search and purchase behaviors in different ways. We test our research hypotheses based on this model by conducting an empirical study on data obtained from a major financial services firm and also on data from an online computer retailer. The results from the empirical analysis provide support for our central thesis that competition for attention moderates the in fluence of the type of visit on online shopping behavior, and help us make important theoretical contributions and identify interesting managerial implications

## 1. Introduction

The Internet has not only brought about a massive upsurge in online promotional budgets but has also spawned an array of new marketing channels such as search engine ads, banner ads, and social media ads. Most online stores employ more than one promotional tool to draw consumers to their websites, but they rarely use the different marketing channels to the same extent. For instance, during 2018, 43.9% of the overall online advertising revenues came from search ads and 30.6% from banner ads [66].

A number of studies have empirically compared different online marketing channels (e.g., [11,16,24,27,29,51,54,63,80]). These studies have produced substantial empirical evidence that consumers respond differently to different channels; yet, at a conceptual level, there is no clear understanding of why such consumer differences exist. As the number of online channels keeps increasing at a rapid pace, it has become necessary to develop a framework for understanding the fundamental differences between channels. To address this urgent need.

we develop a conceptual model to explain how different online channels work, so that they can be deployed optimally.

During the last decade or so, researchers have recognized that the consumer visits to an online shopping site can be of different types. Specifically, they make a distinction between visits following a customer-initiated contact (CIC) and a firm-initiated contact (FIC), and investigate what impact that distinction has on subsequent consumer behavior [7,28,51,79]. While CICs (e.g., direct visit, search engine, price comparison site) are triggered by customers' actions, FICs (e.g., email, banner, Facebook) result from advertising messages being pushed to customers.

Another group of researchers [2,3,38,39,50,70] has examined the role that competition for the consumer's attention between different elements of a web page plays in consumer behavior. These studies have addressed issues such as the influence of advertiser prominence in search advertising [44], the importance of message relevance when multiple senders compete for the receiver's attention [39], design of filler interfaces to manage waiting times at websites [50], the online reviewers' strategic choice of products to review and ratings to post [70], and the design of website interfaces [38].

Interestingly, while the two concepts of “online visit $\mathrm { \ t y p e ^ { * } { } }$ and “competition for attention” have been used by researchers to study on line advertising, they have never been studied together. If both these concepts affect consumer response to online advertising, then it is nat ural to inquire whether there is an interaction between them, and whether the interaction affects advertising effectiveness. This is the primary research question that this research attempts to answer: Is the effectiveness of an online advertising channel influenced by an inter action between competition for attention (among the online ad and other elements of the web page) and visit type (customer-initiated or firminitiated)? To answer this question, we develop a conceptual model based on the type of visit and competition for attention.

We also develop an empirical model to test the conceptual model. The primary method of evaluating an ad's effectiveness is through its ability to convert a customer, i.e., performing certain actions such as purchasing, registration, and subscription [81]. However, focusing on the actual conversion leads to an overemphasis on the “last click” and ignores the influence of all previous interactions between the firm and the consumer [51,81]. A consumer may visit an online store many times to gather information about a product before accumulating information sufficient to make the purchase. Xu et al. [81] argue that the success of a conversion should be attributed to all visits that culminated in the conversion, and not just the last visit. The value of a visit can be determined by its “stickiness” [11,17], i.e., the amount of time spent at a website during a visit, because longer the time a consumer spends at a website, greater is the information that he/she can absorb. This measure also has the advantage that it can be easily extracted from the click stream data. Therefore, in this study, we assess the effectiveness of an advertising channel by its impact on the time spent at the website and conversion probability.

We extend the emerging stream of multi-channel research studies in this context $( \mathrm { e } . 9 . , \ [ 7 , 1 6 , 2 8 , 2 9 , 5 1 , 5 4 ] )$ by presenting a comprehensive empirical model that compares and contrasts the influence of four online marketing channels in jointly influencing site visit duration and purchase probability. The channels that we consider in this research are banner, social media (Facebook), email, search engine, and direct visits. While there are variants of the selected advertising channels such as retargeted ads, skyscraper banner ads, targeted banner ads, price comparison en gines, and Twitter, we limit the study to the most common version of each of these channels, so that we can maintain our focus on the main theories underlying the conceptual model. We empirically test the conceptual model on a financial data set. Our empirical analysis reveals that there is a significant interaction between the two constructs: the influence of the type of visit (CIC or FIC) on visit duration and purchase behavior is moderated by the level of competition for the consumer's attention. We believe that this is not only a major theoretical contri bution to the literature, but that this also has important managerial implications for promotion personalization, search engine advertising strategies, and optimal allocation of online advertising dollars.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the relevant literature and provide the theoretical underpinnings for our research model. We present our conceptual model and research hy potheses in Section 3. We describe the clickstream data used for this study in Section 4. In Section 5, we describe the joint search duration and purchase models that we use to test our hypotheses. The results of the study are presented in Section 6. Section 7 provides a summary of findings, theoretical contributions, managerial implications, limitations, and future directions.

## 2. Literature review

In this section, we first review past research on the efficacy of online marketing channels, and then review those studies that specifically deal with the type of visit and competition for attention.

## 2.1. Online marketing channels

Some of the earlier studies in online marketing channels focused on banner ads, probably due to their similarity with traditional print ads. The research interest initially was in developing suitable measures of the effectiveness of banner ads, e.g., clickthrough rates [18], unaided advertising recall, brand recognition, brand awareness [30], and struc ture of interaction [75]. Subsequently, researchers started examining the factors that increase the effectiveness of banner ads such as content typography [9], animation [48], behavior targeting [20], and retarget ing [12].

Another popular online marketing channel is search engine advertising [32,33,43,45,46,56]. The success of search engine advertising is dependent on a consumer clicking on the search ad that appears on the screen after the consumer keys in a keyword phrase in the search engine. Website managers bid for keyword phrases and, consequently, re searchers have been interested in learning the optimum bidding strategy [3,31,32,45,52]. There is also substantial research interest in studying the factors that influence the click-through rate (CTR) such as keyword popularity [43], link position in search results [1], organic competition [2], keyword type – branded/specific or generic $[ 3 , 5 6 , 6 9 ]$ , and keyword ambiguity [36].

Researchers have also been interested in the rise of social media advertising and its impact on purchase outcomes [19,49]. As in the case of search and banner advertising, there has been interest in developing metrics of social media advertising effectiveness and the factors that increase this effectiveness [53]. In the context of Facebook ads, Lee et al. [47] measure ad effectiveness by customer engagement – defined by them as Likes, Comments, Shares, and Click-throughs with a message. For factors that could increase ad effectiveness, researchers have focused on users' perception of control over their personal information flow [77], sponsorship disclosure in Facebook ads [13], advertising content [47], size of an artist's virtual friend network [19], and product characteristics and deal characteristics [49].

A few studies have compared different online marketing channels. Dinner et al. [29] study the cross-channel effect of banner and search advertising on brick and mortar sales. Danaher and Dagger [24] consider ten different advertising channels and develop a method to assess their relative effectiveness. Bhatnagar et al. [11] develop a multi state, multiple-spell model for identifying a window of opportunity, within which the purchase probability is greater than the exit proba bility. They find that this process is affected by the online channel (search engine or banner) and prior visit characteristics. Xie and Lee [80] study two types of social media – owned and earned – and their impact on brand purchase. Li et al. [54] show that the information stock developed as a result of exposure to six different ad channels, including that of competitors, helps drive visit and purchase behavior. De et al. [27] examine two marketing channels that retailers provide on their websites for facilitating sales: a search box and a recommendation en gine. This study finds that consumers who use the direct search function (using precise keywords) tend to purchase more products that have already been promoted/advertised but purchase less non-promoted products, whereas the use of the non-directed search function does not have a significant effect on sales. Consumers who use the firm's recommendation system tend to purchase more products, whether they are promoted or not, although the increase in purchase is larger for non promoted products.

## 2.2. Customer-initiated vs. firm-initiated visits

Marketing researchers distinguish between customer-initiated channels and firm-initiated channels [7,28,51,79]. Customer-initiated channels, e.g., organic search and paid search, allow a consumer to search for information on her own initiative. On the other hand, firminitiated channels, e.g., banner ads and Facebook ads, help a firm initiate marketing interventions to attract traffic to the firm's website. As

Li and Kannan [51] note, the type of visit engendered by different channels falls somewhere in a continuum between customer-initiated and firm-initiated:

“… a direct visit to the firm's website by entering the URL is entirely customer initiated, whereas a visit through an e-mail is at the other end of the spectrum… Display ads enable firms to target visitors at many sites online and thus are considered more firm initiated, whereas paid search requires customers to visit a search engine and type in a specific keyword(s) and thus are considered more customer initiated.” (p. 42).

Other researchers have also used this classification. Arguing for the fact that the origin of contact (firm or customer) is an important dif ferentiator for marketing channels, Anderl et al. [7] note that: “Whereas in firm-initiated channels (FICs), such as display advertising, the advertiser determines timing and exposures, in customer-initiated channels (CICs), such as SEA, potential customers trigger the commu nication with an active search” (p. 189).

A consumer moves though different stages of a purchase funnel: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, and pur chase [28,51]. While firm-initiated contacts (FICs) can reach consumers who have not yet recognized the need for a product category, customerinitiated contacts (CICs) help consumers who already have and are in the later stages of the purchase funnel [28].

In their study, Li and Kannan [51] examine the carryover and spill over effects across the following online marketing channels through which consumers visit a firm's website: directly entering the firm's website; search (organic as well as paid); referral sites; emails; and display banner ads. The first three are customer-initiated, whereas the last two are firm-initiated. One important finding of the study is that search ad click-throughs have a significantly longer impact than banner ad click-throughs, implying that even when a search happens early in the purchase funnel, it has some impact on ultimate purchase conversion.

In another study, Wiesel et al. [79] use email campaigns, catalog mailing, and flyer and fax campaigns that focus on pushing messages at consumers (FICs), and Google's AdWords as CICs, which are contacts with a firm initiated by a consumer. They find that the sales elasticity of Google AdWords is substantially higher than that of the FICs, and its estimated profit effect is 17 times that of the most effective FIC.

de Haan et al. [28] also distinguish between firm-initiated contacts (FICs) and customer-initiated contacts (CICs), which include organic search, paid search, price comparison sites, and retargeting. They argue that CICs assist consumers who have already recognized the need for a product, given that they search for more information (e.g., search en gines), evaluate alternatives (e.g., price comparison sites), or are close to purchasing the product (retargeting). They find a clear difference in effectiveness between firm-initiated and customer-initiated forms of advertising: CICs are 26.7 times more effective than FICs. The likely reason, according to the authors, “is that prospective customers are more attentive to information that is directly relevant to what they seek; however, firm-initiated advertising often reaches them at the wrong time and with a sub-optimal message” (p. 502).

## 2.3. Competition for attention

Another factor that could influence online search behavior is competition for attention. “Attention is focused mental engagement on a particular item of information” [26,p. 20]. The theory of competition for attention, also known as attention economics, states that human attention is a scarce commodity that needs to be allocated properly. Because the online world offers an abundance of information, visitors suffer from information overload, which creates a scarcity of attention and, there fore, there is a need to allocate that attention efficiently among the in formation sources that compete for attention [72]. The layout of visual information on a web page has a bearing on the level of competition that any one item faces from other items on the web page.

Consumer behavior scholars have found the concept of attention economics to be useful for understanding the design of catalog page layout and visual merchandising [40], and the attention paid to a print ad in a newspaper [71]. Janiszewski [40] presents evidence that objects are viewed for longer durations when competition from non-attended items is limited; the main finding is that for a set of items, there are certain subsets of items that are studied more intensely.

Researchers studying print advertising have used insights from the attention economics literature to learn how to shift attention away from the editorial content (focal stimuli) to the print ad (non-focal stimuli). There have been two approaches. The first approach is to make the print ad disruptive with physical properties: brightness, color, size, contrast [73]. The second is to increase the salience of the print ad by adjusting certain semantic characteristics of the ad [61]. It is the second approach that is relevant for this study. Suppose there are two consumers focused on perusing the content on a web page that has an embedded ad. If one of the consumers reorients her attention from the content to the ad, then that would imply that the ad is more salient to her than to the other consumer, probably because of pre-existing interest in the product category. This is the strategy behind Google's Adsense [34].

In the context of e-commerce, researchers have found the competition for attention theory to play an important role in the design of web pages since different elements of a page attract different levels of attention [2,3,31]. For example, it has been empirically found that organic listings attract more attention than sponsored listings [2,41], and that the po sition of a competing ad determines the attention consumers pay to it, thereby influencing the performance of the focal ad [3]. In the domain of mobile geofencing, local competition significantly reduces the CTR of the focal ad [37].

## 3. Research hypotheses

In this section, we formulate a set of hypotheses based on the type of online visit and competition for attention.

## 3.1. Influence of visit type and competition

In a customer-initiated visit, the consumer seeks information on her own initiative, either by visiting a website directly or using a search engine. There are other types of CICs, such as price comparison sites and retargeting [28], but in this paper our focus is only on direct visits and search engine ads. While search engine retargeting is also considered to be a CIC, it is less customer-initiated than search engine ad because of the involvement of the firm in reaching new audiences by placing retargeting ads with personalized content. That is why we limit our examination of the focal firm's CICs to direct visits and search engine ads.

When a consumer visits the target store directly by entering the URL of its site, no other store competes for the consumer's attention. How ever, if she engages in keyword search, the links that appear at the top of the search results would depend on the specificity of the keywords used by her in the search [31]. Customers who are early in their purchase funnel use more general or “upper funnel” keywords to identify addi tional information about products, whereas customers who have a spe cific item/retailer in mind, use more specific or“lower funnel" keywords [52].

There will be several links (search ads) from competing stores vying for the consumer's attention. her stage in the purchase funnel notwithstanding. A consumer using upper funnel keywords will attract more attention from competing brands [31] and, therefore, lower conversion rates than a consumer using lower funnel keywords, who is likely to be more involved in the product [43], The overall search time would be distributed across the different stores that she decides to visit. The competition for attention that the focal ad faces from other ads – which will depend on keyword specificity – would likely influence its click and conversion performance. Consequently, her search duration at the focal store would vary inversely with the competition that it faces from other stores for her attention.

In contrast to customer-initiated channels, which are stored in the consideration sets in advance, “firm-initiated channels enter into cus tomers' consideration sets only when customers encounter them as a result of a firm's targeting” [51,p. 42]. Such a visit, therefore, is driven by a stimulus planted by the advertising firm in a visual environment that includes multiple stimuli. The different stimuli on a web page are the different links embedded in the page. While pursuing regular ac tivities on the Web (i.e., activities not related to purchase), a consumer is constantly exposed to different external stimuli (online ads) that try to shift her attention away from her current activity and toward the in formation provided by those stimuli. It is important to note that while for all marketing channels, the competition for attention plays out be tween different elements of a web page, the elements themselves are different for different channels. In the case of FICs, there are all kinds of stimuli on the web page (e.g., news story, emails, weather, banner ad, Facebook ad, etc.), but in the case of keyword search, the elements are only the search ads from competing firms.

For each stimulus (link), a consumer compares the benefit of continuing with her current activity to the value of information to be obtained by clicking on that link. If a consumer clicks on the link, it indicates that she values that more than continuing with her current activity or clicking on any of the other links. The value of a link to the consumer is the outcome of two factors: the strength of the signal generated by the link and the relevance or salience of the signal to the visitor. The consumer is more likely to click on links that have strong signals because those links have a greater probability of grabbing her attention. A link would be salient to the consumer if it promotes a product in which she inherently has a high ongoing interest.

Anstis [8] has shown that the strength of a signal from a non attended item deteriorates the farther it moves from the attended area in a visual layout. Therefore, the larger the distance of a link (online ad) from the focal area, the harder it has to compete for a consumer's attention. The focal area is a small area of the screen where her attention is focused on. This area includes the perceptual or semantic elements that could be attended to concurrently [62]. If, despite a weak signal, a visitor shifts her attention to a link which is far away from her focal area, then it implies that the link is more salient than the ones nearer to the focal area. What makes a link salient is the consumer's interest in the product being promoted by the stimulus. If the consumer is actively engaged in shopping for a product, she would be highly attentive to any advertising tool related to that product. Therefore, when a consumer clicks on a non-focal link that is farther from the focal area and, consequently, has weaker signal strength than other links, it indicates a very high level of interest in the promoted product.

In the case of FICs, therefore, if the consumer switches to a stimulus that is facing a high level of competition from other items, it indicates that she is highly interested in the stimulus because of its salience. Hence, when a consumer is lured by a stimulus that is facing strong competition for her attention, she could end up spending more time visiting the online store promoted by the stimulus than by another stimulus that is facing low competition. This is in sharp contrast to what happens in a customer-initiated visit. In other words, the competition for the consumer's attention introduced by the online marketing channel could moderate the influence of the type of visit (customer-initiated or firm-initiated) on search duration. We, therefore, state the following hypothesis:

H1a: The influence of the type of visit (CIC or FIC) on a consumer's search duration at an online store is moderated by the competition for the consumer's attention.

There is considerable evidence in the traditional search literature that consumers who search more are also more likely to purchase [68]. This is also what one would expect in the online domain, and, in fact, a positive link between the extent of online search effort and probability of purchasing has been documented [59]. Venkatesh and Agarwal [78] find that website use, or stickiness [4], along with content and made-forthe-medium, positively influences purchase behavior. Based on hy pothesis H1a, which states that competition for attention moderates the influence of visit type on search duration, and the evidence in the literature of the link between search duration and purchase probability, we postulate the following hypothesis:

H1b: The influence of the type of visit (CIC or FIC) on a consumer's purchase likelihood at an online store is moderated by the compe tition for the consumer's attention.

Fig. 1 shows our conceptual model, which includes type of online visit as an independent variable, search duration and purchase likelihood as dependent variables, and competition for attention as a moderating vari able. The model incorporates two types of visit (customer-initiated and firm-initiated) and two levels of competition for examining online search and purchase behavior. As the figure shows, firm-initiated channels, such as banner and Facebook, allow the firm to push the message to the consumer [28,51]. In contrast, a customer-initiated visit is triggered by the consumer's actions [28,51,79], which pull the infor mation from the firm's website.

## 3.2. Influence of competition on CICs

We now specifically consider two customer-initiated online chan nels: direct and search engine. As Li and Kannan [51] note, a direct visit by entering the URL of the firm's website is entirely customer initiated. The consumer knows exactly which site to visit to purchase a product. The direct channel is characterized by a total lack of competition for the consumer's attention during the visit from other sources. In contrast, when a consumer initiates a search by entering keywords in a search engine such as Google, the search engine responds by generating a list of hyperlinks related to those keywords. Compared to the direct channel, therefore, the competition for the consumer's attention is much higher. The search returns a list of online retailers that carry the product or product category the consumer is interested in, along with information on prices, specifications, reviews, shipping fees, etc.

Website managers devote substantial resources to optimize thei online store for search engines. The purpose of this optimization is that if a consumer does a keyword search for the company's products using the search engine, the hyperlinks to the web pages about the store's products should show up prominently in the search results. The consumer's attention is usually focused on the top three or four links that show up on her screen. She clicks on one of those links to enter an online store, which faces competition for the consumer's attention from other competing brands that show up at the top. In the domain of mobile geofencing, local competition – defined as the number of alternatives in a consumer's vicinity – has a significant negative effect on click-through rate [37].

The clickstream data that we employ in this study is only specific to the store that provided the data. So, when a consumer uses a search engine to visit this store, there are other stores that would compete with this target goal. We use the goal-activation model [5] to understand how goals are activated in environments with different levels of competition for attention. The “interference level depends on the number and strength of distractors – the more there are, or the stronger they are, the more they interfere with the target” [5,p. 70]. If the activation strength of the target is higher than the interference level, then the probability of it being sampled is higher than that of any distractor, and the reverse is true if the target is below that level.

The consumer's intention in a direct visit is to accomplish a specific goal or target. Because there is no competition for attention from any other items, there is no interference. The target goal, which is to visit a specific store, is activated and keeps gaining in strength till the peak is reached. It is the sole active goal in memory and is the one that will direct behavior, such as visiting the online store, navigating through the web pages on the site, and purchasing a product.

In contrast to a direct visit, a consumer who enters a store through a search engine is exposed to several links (search ads) from competing stores. Those stores compete for her attention, thereby acting as dis tractors. The distractors interfere with the target goal, namely, shopping at the focal store, and increase the likelihood that the consumer will switch to a new goal, namely, visiting a competing store. H2a: The search durations of consumers who enter an online store directly are longer than the search durations of those who enter the store via a search engine.

When a consumer visits a site multiple times through different channels, she accumulates contextual information, such as information about alternatives, price information, promotional details, etc. This cumulative information stock could increase or decrease the overall utility of purchasing a product at the site [51]. Prior research has found that carryover effects of search engines and direct visits are positive [15,21], implying a higher likelihood of purchase. Also, organic search and banner ads have a significant positive spillover effect on direct visits, while the reverse in not true [51]. Based on these results and the fact that direct visits would tend to occur further down the purchase funnel, we state the following hypothesis:

H2b: The purchase likelihoods of consumers who enter an online store directly are higher than the purchase likelihoods of those who enter the store via a search engine.

## 3.3. Influence of competition on FICs

We now consider two other online marketing channels – Facebook ads and banner ads – which are different types of stimuli planted by the advertising firm to lure consumers, who are in the earlier stages of the purchase channel, to its site. Both are firm initiated [51], but they operate in environments where demands for the consumer's attention are quite different. In the Facebook ad format, an online retail firm posts ads in the space where the consumer sees other posts, akin to how email ads push a promotional message for the firm's products. These ads are typically placed inside the list of posts that the consumer receives; she goes through the list by reading them one by one. Consider the Facebook ad shown in Fig. 2a. Since the Facebook ad is embedded inside the list of posts, it is positioned well within the consumer's focal area of attention and gets easily noticed. The consumer may click on a Facebook ad depending on her interest in the promoted product, but the ad does not have to compete much with other stimuli to catch the consumer's attention.

A banner ad usually provides a graphic display along with text/ content and is clickable to a landing page at the online store. Banner ads are located at third-party online content sites, such as newspapers, blogging sites, etc., that prospects are expected to visit (see Fig. 2b for an example). A consumer encounters a banner ad while consuming the content at those sites. The consumer may ignore the banner ad or click on it. If she clicks on the banner ad, it is because the offer may, in general, be interesting enough to tempt her to click on the link and learn more about the offer. Her online search behavior is not induced by a specific product or service she has in mind, but by a stimulus planted by the promoting firm to lure her to the site. Clearly, the banner has to be salient and attractive enough for the consumer to suspend her current activity on the web and get engaged in exploring the promoted product.

Unlike in the case of a direct visit or a search engine, a consumer never actively seeks out banner ads for a specific product as there is no way to do that, but can only respond to the stimulus of banner ad if she comes across it while surfing. As Pravettoni et al. [65] note, “The design of banner advertisements is based mainly on theories of attention as stimulus-driven, in which visual selection attention is activated in bottom-up manner, than to user-driven models that imply top-down interferences." When the consumer switches her attention to a banner ad, her visual attention gets controlled by bottom-up factors that reflect sensory stimulation provided by the banner, especially when it is salient

[22].

The advertising firm places banner ads on sites that are likely to expose its products to a large audience of prospective customers. Clearly, the banner has to be attractive enough for the consumer to suspend her current activity on the web and get engaged in exploratory search for information on the advertised product. It is the stimulus (banner) that lures the shopper to the site. Such consumers are likely to browse the site for some time and return to what they were doing before they entered the site, for example, reading news, buying supplies, checking stock prices, etc.

Contrast the Facebook ad in Fig. 2a with the banner ad in Fig. 2b. The latter is located far from the news story or blog that the consumer is focused on and, therefore, its signal strength is much weaker than that of the Facebook ad. The banner ad is in the peripheral vision, implying that it has to compete with several other ads and editorial material on the same page, and the consumer needs to quickly decide whether she should attend to the banner ad or not. The banner, therefore, faces a much stronger competition for the consumer's attention than the Face book ad. As Pieters and Wedel [64,p. 59] note, banner ads.

“... placed in top and right locations were looked at barely longer than the duration of a single fixation of the eye… Many banner ads did not even get this briefest of looks… Not only are exposures to the majority of ads very short, but the impressions that are obtained from them are often coarse because exposures occur in the visual pe riphery… ”.

Both Facebook ads and banner ads interfere with the primary goal or task that the consumer is engaged in; responding to such ads is pe ripheral to the primary task at hand. As discussed above, the value of a promotional link to a consumer depends on two factors: the strength of the signal in the link and the salience of the signal. The signal strength of banner ads, which are located far away from the consumer's focal point, will, in general, be much lower than that of Facebook ads. So, when a consumer clicks on a non-focal banner ad, after suspending her current activity, it indicates that the ad provides a highly salient stimulus. The stimulus is salient or relevant because the consumer's interest in the product being promoted is high; otherwise, she would not have clicked on a link that is far from her focal area of interest and that is much more peripheral than a Facebook ad. To test whether consumers who click on a banner ad are less motivated to return to the interrupted primary task, as compared to those who click on a Facebook ad, we, therefore, test the following hypotheses in this study:

H3a: The search durations of consumers who enter an online store via a banner ad are longer than the search durations of those who enter the store via a Facebook ad.

H3b: The purchase likelihoods of consumers who enter an online store via a banner ad are higher than the purchase likelihoods of those who enter the store via a Facebook ad.

## 4. Data

To study the effectiveness of the different types of online advertising channels, we collected proprietary clickstream data from a leading financial services firm, which provides information about all thing financial such as credit cards, savings account, mortgage loans, and home equity loans. The reason we study financial services is because we want a category in which a customer conducts extensive search prior to purchase, because that is one of the outcomes of interest. Visitors to the site of the firm can seek out various kinds of financial information from different banks such as mortgage rates, savings account rate, CDs, and best credit cards in different categories (travel, business, etc.). From the site itself, a visitor can apply for a financial product such as credit card, mortgage loan, or savings account. Whenever a visitor applies for a financial product, the site gets compensated by the bank sponsoring the financial product. From this data set, we know the time spent by each consumer visiting the online store and whether she applied to purchase a financial product or not.

The company employs a mix of online marketing channels to reach and acquire customers. The data set provided by this company captures the visitor's visits from a third-party website to the company website. This data set has information about which online channel (direct visit, search engine, banner, or Facebook) was used by a visitor to arrive at the online store, as well as the date and time of landing at its site. We conduct our analysis on a random sample collected from the data pro vided by the company. The sample includes 212,759 observations from January 2016 to June 2018, out of which 29.6% were direct visits, 41.4% were search engine visits, 20.7% were banner ad visits, and 8.2% were Facebook ad visits (see Table 2).

## 5. Models

In this section, we describe our modeling approach. Online search effort can be measured based on the total time an individual spends searching on the Web, i.e., search duration. We develop a model for jointly measuring the two variables of interest: search duration and purchase probability. We model search duration by a Tobit regression submodel and purchase likelihood by a Probit regression sub-model. The two sub-models are estimated jointly. For the sake of exposition, we initially describe the two sub-models separately, and then explain how the two models are estimated jointly. Because our models measure individuallevel data on consumers' visits in the purchase funnel, they are able to account for the heterogeneity across consumers' consideration set of channels for visiting the focal site [51,54].

## 5.1. Search duration sub-model (Tobit regression model)

Let the search duration be given by $y _ { i } { ^ { * } } { } _ { ; }$ where

$$
y _ {i} ^ {*} = \beta^ {\prime} x _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i 1}\tag{1}
$$

Here, x 's are the covariates that influence the search duration of individual i. The covariates in our study are the four online channels: direct, keyword search, banner, and Facebook. We use the log of the number of pages as a control variable, because this could influence search behavior [58]. The four covariates are indicated by four dummy variables. For instance, if a consumer came to the website after con ducting a keyword search, the dummy variable for keyword search is 1, otherwise it is zero. The covariates shift the duration propensity up or down, and β is a parameter vector that measures the sensitivity of the search duration propensity to the covariates and needs to be estimated from the model.

In the data, the recorded time duration for a large number of visitors is 0. These visitors visit just one page on the site and then exit without requesting any more pages on the Google Analytics server. Google An alytics calls these visits “bounce,” and Moe [58] calls them “shallow visits.” This data characteristic destroys the linearity assumption of visit duration data, and, hence, the standard OLS methods are no longer correct [6]. In practice, we do not observe $y _ { i } { ^ { * } } { } _ { ; }$ , which is a latent variable, but observe the censored time $T _ { i }$ that the consumer i spent searching for information at the website.

Due to censoring, if we regress $T _ { i }$ on its different determinant factors, then we will get downward-biased parameter estimates. Such censored data are analyzed by Tobit regression, which has been used to study the effect of market competition on trust and trustworthiness [14], and the relationship between firm boundaries and investment in IT Capabilities [35]. In order to express the likelihood in Tobit regression, we define an indicator variable I(T ) as follows:

$$
I (T _ {i}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & y _ {i} ^ {*} \leq 0 \\ 1 & y _ {i} ^ {*} > 0 \end{array} \right.\tag{3}
$$

If Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, φ the standard normal probability density function, and $\sigma _ { 1 } ^ { 2 }$ the variance of the error term, then the likelihood is given by [57]:

$$
L _ {1} (\beta , \sigma_ {1}) = \prod_ {I = 1} \frac {1}{\sigma_ {1}} \varphi \left(\frac {T _ {i} - \beta^ {\prime} x _ {i}}{\sigma_ {1}}\right) \prod_ {I = 0} \left(1 - \Phi \left(\frac {\beta^ {\prime} x _ {i}}{\sigma_ {1}}\right)\right)\tag{4}
$$

If we were interested in studying only search duration, then the log of likelihood function of $\operatorname { E q } .$ (4) could be maximized to obtain an estimate of the $\beta$ vector and $\sigma _ { 1 }$ .

## 5.2. Purchase sub-model (Probit regression model)

Let the purchase propensity be given by $z _ { i } { ^ * } { } _ { \mathrm { : } }$ , where

$$
z _ {i} ^ {*} = \gamma^ {'} x _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i 2}\tag{5}
$$

In practice, we do not observe $z _ { i } ^ { * }$ which is a latent variable, but observe the actual purchase variable $P _ { i }$ which indicates whether a consumer made a purchase or not. It takes the value 1 if individual i made a purchase, and 0 otherwise.

$$
P _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 0 & z _ {i} ^ {*} <   0 \\ 1 & z _ {i} ^ {*} \geq 0 \end{array} \right.\tag{6}
$$

Here, x 's are the covariates that influence the purchase propensity of individual i. The covariates in the purchase model are the same as in search duration propensity: direct, keyword search, banner, and Face book, plus the log of the number of pages. γ is a vector of parameters that measure the sensitivity of purchase probability to the covariate vector and needs to be estimated.

The error term ε is assumed to have a standard normal distribution. This leads to a standard probit regression model, where the likelihood is given by [57]:

$$
L _ {2} (\gamma) = \prod_ {P _ {i} = 0} \Phi (- \gamma^ {\prime} x _ {i}) \prod_ {P _ {i} = 1} (1 - \Phi (- \gamma^ {\prime} x _ {i}))\tag{7}
$$

If we were interested in studying purchase propensity only, then the log of likelihood function of Eq. (7) could be maximized to obtain an estimate of the γ vector.

## 5.3. Simultaneous equation model (joint Tobit - Probit regression model)

We are interested in studying the search duration and purchase sub models jointly as they are related, i.e., the error terms $\varepsilon _ { i 1 }$ of Eq. (1) and $\varepsilon _ { i 2 }$ of Eq. (5) are correlated. In this case, the two models should be estimated as a system of equations. We assume that $\varepsilon _ { i 1 }$ and $\varepsilon _ { i 2 }$ have a bivariate normal distribution such that:

$$
f \big (\varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2} \big) \sim \mathrm{N} \Bigg [ \binom{0}{0}    ,    \left( \begin{array}{c c} \sigma_ {1} ^ {2} & \rho_ {\varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}} \\ \rho_ {\varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}} & 1 \end{array} \right) \Bigg ],\tag{8}
$$

where $\rho _ { \varepsilon _ { i 1 } , \varepsilon _ { i 2 } }$ is the correlation between the error terms of the search

duration and purchase models.

The joint likelihood of Eq. (4) and Eq. (7) for individual i is then given by:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} L _ {i} (\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \rho_ {\varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}}) & = \int_ {\frac {\beta^ {\prime} x _ {i}}{\sigma_ {1}}} ^ {\infty} \int_ {- \infty} ^ {- \gamma^ {\prime} x _ {i}} f (\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}) d \varepsilon_ {i 1} d \varepsilon_ {i 2} \text {   if   } I (T _ {i}) = 0, P _ {i} = 0 \\ & = \int_ {\frac {\beta^ {\prime} x _ {i}}{\sigma_ {1}}} ^ {\infty} \int_ {- \gamma^ {\prime} x _ {i}} ^ {\infty} f (\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}) d \varepsilon_ {i 1} d \varepsilon_ {i 2} \text {   if   } I (T _ {i}) = 0, P _ {i} = 1. \\ & = \int_ {- \infty} ^ {- \gamma^ {\prime} x _ {i}} f (\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}) d \varepsilon_ {i 2} \text {   if   } I (T _ {i}) = 1, P _ {i} = 0 \\ & = \int_ {- \gamma^ {\prime} x _ {i}} ^ {\infty} f (\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}) d \varepsilon_ {i 2} \text {   if   } I (T _ {i}) = 1, P _ {i} = 1 \end{array}
$$

The joint likelihood of the sample is given by:

$$
L \left(\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \rho_ {\varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}}\right) = \prod_ {\mathrm{i}} \mathrm{L} _ {\mathrm{i}} \left(\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \rho_ {\varepsilon_ {i 1}, \varepsilon_ {i 2}}\right)\tag{9}
$$

The joint likelihood of Eq. (9) can be maximized to obtain parame ters $\beta , \sigma , \gamma , \rho _ { \varepsilon _ { i 1 , } \varepsilon _ { i 2 } } .$

A major question we address in this research is whether the influence of the type of visit on search duration and purchase likelihood is moderated by the level of competition for the consumer's attention engendered by the online channel used.

We argue that in the context of customer-initiated visits, when a consumer uses a search engine to seek information, her goal could be satisfied by online retailers other than the focal store. This level of competition could shift the consumer's attention, making her exit the site quicker than when she makes a direct visit to the site for which there is no competition from other stores. The empirical results of our study support this argument. We also argue that for firm-initiated visits, when a consumer selects a non-focal item that is competing for attention with a large number of items, it is because of its greater salience. The empirical results of our study confirm this: visits of non-focal item through banner ads indeed last longer and yield better purchase con versions than visits of focal items through Facebook ads or email ads.

## 7.1. Theoretical contributions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that develops a conceptual model rooted in both visit type and competition for atten tion, and conducts an empirical analysis using real clickstream data to test the research hypotheses. The finding that there is a significant interaction between visit type and competition for attention is a novel contribution to the field.

As discussed in the Literature Review section, none of the prior multichannel studies have addressed the twin issues of visit type and competition for attention together. The focus of these studies has been on using the purchase funnel concept to explain differences in click and conversion performance between CICs and FICs. But the purchase funnel on its own does not explain why direct visits have longer durations than search engine visits, or why banner visits have longer durations than Facebook or email visits. Given that direct visits are entirely customer initiated [51], they are much deeper into the funnel than visits through other channels and, therefore, have very high cumulative information stock on a product or store. We should, therefore, expect direct visits to be shorter than search engine visits. The fact that the results of our study reject that argument and support hypothesis H2a instead, lends further credence to the role of competition and supports our use of the goalactivation theory to explain why distractors in the search engine re sults could help switch attention away from the focal site.

Our conceptual model also accounts for the moderating influence of competition for attention on search durations and purchase probabili ties, by arguing that for FICs, when a consumer switches to a banner ad that is far away from her focal area, the stimulus has to be salient or relevant. Note that unlike CICs, which are already stored in the con sumer's consideration set, FICs enter the consideration set only when she encounters them [51]. The fact that, despite the high level of competi tion from other stimuli, she decides to click on a banner ad that is in her peripheral vision indicates that she is highly interested in the promoted product because of its salience.

The conceptual model we present in this paper advances our un derstanding of the effectiveness of online visits through the dual lens of visit type and attention economics. It provides a theoretical under standing of why the advertising performance of certain channels is better than others. It also helps explain why more specific keywords tend to result in better performance for search engines [3,31,36,52].

## 7.2. Managerial implications

Retailers often try to convert site visitors by providing incentives such as coupons or rebates. Typically, all site visitors are offered iden tical coupons at a given time. The extensive price-discrimination literature would, however, suggest that a retailer can increase its profits by offering bigger discounts to visitors less likely to purchase [60]. While the Internet makes it possible to personalize the coupon offer for each visitor [67], the empirical challenge lies in identifying consumers who are more likely to purchase. Our model could provide support to retailers in making such personalization decisions.

Among CICs, we show that customers have a lower purchase prob ability when using search engines, due to high competition for attention. Retailers using search engine advertising can adopt different techniques to reduce the competition for attention for consumers seeking infor mation about the firm's products and increase it for consumers seeking information about the competing firm. To increase competition for consumers seeking information about a competitor, a firm could pur chase competitors' branded keywords, a legal strategy at Google [25]. This increases the competition for a competitor when somebody searches for its brand keywords.

Among FICs, we show that purchase probability is higher for con sumers who visit via banner ads. This would suggest that online firms should make greater use of banner ads. Unfortunately, though, the clickthrough rates on banner ads are notoriously low. To increase signal strength, the banner ads could be positioned closer to the text; for example, a skyscraper ad would be preferable to one at the top or bottom of a page. An ad button, even though smaller than a banner ad, can be more effective, because of being positioned closer to the text. The firm could also use affiliate marketing, where affiliate partners try to moti vate site visitors to click on the banner ads through content that is more salient. FICs should be used for reaching consumers who have not yet recognized the need for a product category [28]. This is true of unsought goods, such as funeral services, encyclopedias, fire extinguishers and reference books [42]. FICs should also be used for impulse purchases [74].

## 7.3. Limitations and future directions

Researchers have studied variants of the marketing channels exam ined in this study, such as targeted banner ads [82], and other online marketing channels, such as online product reviews [83] and adsupported streaming media [55]. We focus on only four advertising channels and their most common versions in this study. In the future, the scope of the study could be extended by including additional variants, as well as other marketing channels. Also, even though the marketing literature has classified online store visits as customer-initiated or firminitiated, in the future we could see channels that are more hybrid with respect to the visit type they support, i.e., they cannot be neatly classi fied as CICs or FICs. Our study does not cover hybrid channels; future research could examine the factors that influence the search and pur chase behavior for such channels.

Tan et al. [76] find that incorporating distracting website features lead to better purchase decisions for more complex shopping tasks. They specifically investigate the benefits of using pop-ups, which are a major website distraction, on online purchase decisions. In our study, we use standard inline banner ads, which are not as obtrusive as pop-up banner ads. Future research could examine how different types of banner ads – inline, pop-up, animated, floating, targeted, etc. – attract consumer attention in different ways, and how the varying levels of competition for attention, triggered by the different banner types, influence online search duration and purchase likelihood

In this paper, we define competition as the demand for attention from other items or links that appear within a visual layout. Some of the banner ads could carry product pricing information, which might also influence competition; our study does not address that issue. In the future, therefore, other aspects of competition, such as those arising from pricing information, could be examined. For example, price com parison sites explicitly incorporate product prices and it would be interesting to investigate what kind of influence that form of competi tion has on online search and purchase behavior compared to

competition for visual attention.
