# Advancing our understanding and assessment of cognitive effort in the cognitive fit theory and data visualization context: Eye tracking-based approach

Dinko Baˇci´c <sup>a,\*</sup>, Raymond Henry b

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems and Supply Chain Management, Quinlan School of Business, Loyola University Chicago, Chicago, IL, USA <sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems, Monte Ahuja College of Business, Cleveland State University, Cleveland, OH, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cognitive fit theory Cognitive fit Cognitive effort Eye tracking Data visualization

## A B S T R A C T

In Cognitive Fit Theory (CFT) based research, there is a consensus about cognitive effort as the underlying mechanism impacting performance. Although critical to the theory, cognitive effort and its direct empirical assessment remain a challenge. In this repeated measures experimental study, we introduce a research model and develop hypotheses based on the fundamental relationships underlying CFT while integrating eye tracking as an approach for assessing cognitive effort. Our study finds that eye tracking technology, specifically fixation-based metrics, can be used in the understanding of cognitive processes initiated by our data representation choices. Specifically, we find that in all tasks except the complex-symbolic task, users experience meaningful change in the physiological assessment of cognitive effort based on the condition of cognitive fit. We contrast our findings to existing research and find that physiological indicators of cognitive effort can provide critical insights often missed in traditional CFT research

## 1. Introduction

Cognitive Fit Theory (CFT) [1,2], even after over 30 years, continues to significantly impact our understanding of how visual data represen tations, through their matching with problem tasks and users' mental models, impact decision performance. In the extant CFT research, there is a consensus on the critical role of cognitive effort (sometimes called cognitive workload, burden, strain, or load), yet the research largely fails to integrate cognitive effort measurement [3]. Instead, empirical studies typically only assume cognitive effort's theoretical existence as a function of cognitive fit and as the mechanism that drives decision speed and accuracy. Without a greater understanding of the theorized mech anism, the research could be exposed to criticism that challenges the fundamental underpinnings of substantial portions of CFT literature [4]. One possible reason for the existing state of the research may be found in the difficulty of measuring cognitive effort. Therefore, there is a clear need to enhance current research methods and theory by offering and testing ways cognitive effort could be measured and integrated into CFT and the broader data visualization literature.

To address this opportunity, in this research effort, we turn our attention to a physiological indicator of cognitive effort by deploying eye tracking technology through its ability to collect biometric data that describes users' visual attention. The scarcity of physiological response research in CFT is surprising, given that research findings suggest that evaluating visual interface performance without measuring cognitive processes, such as workload, may lead to incorrect conclusions about the efficiency of an interface. Driven partly by the ‘eye-mind hypothesis [5], eye tracking is considered effective in assessing cognitive processes as it reveals how the user reads and scans the displayed information by capturing users' eye fixation-derived metrics [6,7]. These metrics have been successfully linked to cognitive processes indicating various forms of attention, interest, mental effort, and cognitive load in psychology [8], neuroscience [9], art, human factors, marketing, and computer science [10]. In recent years we have also seen the adoption of eye tracking technology in IS research [11–15]. Given the mature and validated use of eye-tracking-based metrics across fields, the application of eye tracking to evaluate cognitive effort in the CFT context is timely and appropriate.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first highlight the relevant background associated with CFT and the role of cognitive effort and introduce eye tracking as an approach for measuring cognitive effort. We then present a research model and develop hypotheses based on the fundamental relationships underlying CFT. The following sec tions present our experimental design and data analysis. We then discuss our findings and contextualize these results by discussing implications, limitations, and potential next steps.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Cognitive fit theory

A series of studies, starting with the Minnesota experiments [16], compared decision performance in various tasks by offering subjects information in tabular and graphical formats. These early studies yiel ded inconclusive results, motivating the introduction of CFT [1,2] to interpret those results and provide a theoretical basis to predict decision performance. CFT introduced the idea of cognitive fit or a match be tween the problem representation and the problem-solving task that occurs when the problem representations support the task strategies required to perform that task [1]. The theory advocated that, when faced with a symbolic task, the user's mental solution model requires a tabular representation of the problem to achieve cognitive fit. Alternatively, a graphical representation of the problem was the most effective in sup porting decision processes when solving spatial tasks. CFT, over time, gained popularity and was used across numerous domains, such as modeling [17], software tools [18], social network analysis [19], geographic representation [20], knowledge management systems [21], virtual reality [22], decision aids [23], to name a few. In the process, CFT moved beyond symbolic and spatial tasks and was deployed across varied and more complex task types, some of which had inconclusive [24,25] or contradicting results [26]. The theory was also expanded a number of times by integrating problem-solving skills [2], external and internal representation congruence [27], and problem domains [28].

Regardless of the context or empirical findings, virtually all CFTbased work recognizes the role of cognitive effort as the underlying mechanism in theory- or hypotheses-building. For example, in the original theory-building paper, Vessey and Galletta state: “One of the ways to reduce processing effort [emphasis added] is to facilitate the problem-solving processes that human problem solvers use in completing the task. This can be achieved by matching the problem representation to the task, an approach that is known as cognitive fit” (p. 65 [2]). However, despite literature-wide consensus on the critical role of cognitive effort, the research rarely integrates cognitive effort mea surement. Baˇci´c and Henry addressed this gap and attempted to measure cognitive effort as a perceptual construct [3]. However, the findings using perceptual measure (cognitive strain) were mixed, and a sugges tion was made that perceptual and subjective measures of cognitive effort may have some drawbacks. More specifically, users may not consciously recognize their cognitive decision-making process when using visualizations [29], or they may be uncomfortable, unwilling, and unable to accurately self-report cognitive processes [30]. To address this opportunity, we turn our attention to a physiological indicator of cognitive effort by deploying eye tracking technology.

## 2.2. Eye tracking and cognitive effort

Cognitive effort has been defined as cognitive resources needed to complete a task [31]. Cognitive effort research originated as a theoret ical construct in cognitive psychology [32–34] whose impact on human performance is widely recognized. With advances in biometric tech nology, sciences such as neuroscience, vision science, and psychology have made significant gains in our ability to collect and track human neurophysiological signals and link those signals to underlying percep tual and cognitive processes, such as cognitive effort.

Based on the notion that visual attention and eye movements are cognitively controlled [6], we focus on assessing cognitive effort through eye tracking technologies' ability to capture vision and eye gaze behavior in this paper. Vision is our dominant sense [35], as the human brain is engaged in processing visual information more than processing information from any other sense [36]. Due to the cognitive control of eye movement, the importance of the vision sense, and technology ca pabilities to capture eye gaze behavior accurately, eye tracking has emerged as a method to assess one's cognitive processes when observing/evaluating a stimulus. Eye movement is often described through three critical events: fixations - eye movements that stabilize the retina over a stationary object of interest; saccades - rapid movement between fixations; and smooth pursuit - visually tracking a moving target. We process visual information between saccades, fixating when we keep our gaze relatively steady for short periods to reposition a new image. Fixations take up most of our viewing time and correspond to the desire to maintain one's gaze on an object of interest. Eye tracking technology allows us to capture these eye movement events by using an image of the eye based on pupil and corneal reflection to estimate the point of gaze [10].

Eye tracking-based metrics have been used across many disciplines measuring attention in reading, psycholinguistics, website usage, online gaming, writing, and language acquisition [7]. Largely driven by the ‘eye-mind hypothesis’ [5], eye tracking is considered effective in assessing a user's attention and in reflecting cognitive effort [12] as it reveals how the user reads and scans the displayed information by capturing users' eye fixation, saccadic movement, blinks, and pupillary responses [9]. This study focuses on fixation-derived metrics of fixation duration (fixation length in milliseconds) and fixation count (the num ber of fixations). These are the most used eye tracking metrics to mea sure cognitive processing [5], allowing one to investigate whether individuals mainly scan or attend to information as they reason or make judgments about it [37].

A number of studies have assessed cognitive effort through fixation based metrics [12]. Eye-fixations have been used to detect cognitive effort differences in web-browsing viewing behavior and preferences, showing that baby boomers had significantly more fixations than those of Generation Y [38]. Similarly, eye-fixation data was used to assess cognitive effort in evaluating the impact of visual complexity on how users view a webpage [39]. Fixation duration and count were used to assess the effect of executive working memory load on a search task and found that the influence of working memory load on search times arose from changes in fixation durations [40]. Fixation frequency was used to assess cognitive effort when interacting with decision constructs in the e: business transaction context [41], while other studies suggested that in the task of free viewing, fixation duration is sensitive to memory and processing load [42] or the role of fixation durations and count in assessing the effect of decision framing [43]. In the context of various forms of visualizations (salient to this research), fixation-based metrics were used to understand cognitive effort in the context of design layout of UML diagrams [44], business process visual layouts [14], the impact of the misuse of color in dashboard design [45]. This eye-tracking research is rooted in the concept of assessing cognitive load based on the cognitive load theory [46]. where the term mental load often reflects the task-centered dimension of the cognitive capacity needed to process task complexity, while the term effort is often reflecting humancentered, the internal dimension of an individual's invested cognitive capacity [47]. In this paradigm, the intensity of effort is considered the index of cognitive load [48]. In CFT. we see three elements (task external representation, and the mental model) elevated in cognitive load theory through the concepts of extraneous (task), intrinsic and germane cognitive loads (representation and mental model). Therefore, it is not surprising that CFT literature uses cognitive load. workload burden, effort, and cognitive effort interchangeably to describe cogni tive fit's user impact. For that reason, our research is informed and builds on cognitive load literature (see detailed review in [49]) and extends its assessment via eve-tracking to the concept of cognitive fit.

In summary, given the importance of cognitive fit, cognitive effort's central role in understanding data representations' performance impli cations, and the lack of measures to assess cognitive effort, we utilize eye tracking to address this gap in the CFT literature. Specifically, we pro pose using eye fixation count and average duration to assess cognitive effort.

## 3. Hypothesis development

According to CFT, if an external problem representation does not match that emphasized in the task, there is nothing to guide the decision-maker in working toward a task solution. As a result, a greater cognitive effort is required to transform the information into a form suitable for solving that particular type of problem [1,2]. The lens of describing cognitive effort as the emergent property of cognitive fit and as the underlying mechanism that influences decision performance dominates the CFT empirical literature [4]. For example, in the exten sion of CFT to natural language, the assumption was made that “cognitive fit has a characteristic such that consistent mental represen tations reduce the mental effort required to solve a problem [50].” A similar mechanism was assumed in the context of spatial decision sup port systems and paper maps: “…this should create a decision-making environment that more consistently fits the cognitive requirements of the decision-maker and thereby reduces cognitive load [20].” The same mechanism is declared in the context of web search and mobile devices: “If a mismatch between task and information presentation occurs, users must make extra cognitive effort to transform information into a format that is suitable for accomplishing the task [51].” These, and many other examples various contexts [18,21,27], suggest that cognitive fit will lead to lower cognitive effort when compared to an alternative scenario without such fit [3]. Therefore, we reintroduce cognitive effort more explicitly as an outcome stemming from cognitive (mis)fit, and we measure eye fixation-based metrics to assess the level of cognitive effort in that context (Fig. 1).

This model is contextualized in understanding the critical role of the task. The importance of task was recognized early on and was part of the initial introduction of CFT as a response to inconclusive results when representing data in tabular or graphical format [1]. Initial CFT work acknowledged that the link between data representation and task characteristics would not be easy due to a large number of task char acteristics [1]. As a response, a new task taxonomy was developed based on cognitive style and task requirements characteristics resulting in the introduction of spatial and symbolic tasks. Spatial tasks consider the problem area as a whole rather than as discrete data values and require making associations or perceiving relationships in the data. A typical spatial task, for example, may require an understanding and recognition of trend patterns in data. On the other hand, symbolic tasks involve extracting discrete and precise data values [2]. Following the intro duction of these two categories, the original theory and associated research stream suggest an emergence of cognitive fit and resulting decrease in cognitive effort when a spatial task is matched with a graphical data representation (spatial format) and when a symbolic task is matched with a tabular representation (symbolic format). If, on the other hand, there is a mismatch, the user is forced to exert additional cognitive effort to mentally transform data representation to be able to solve the task. In this study, we adopt this context of spatial vs. symbolic task and tabular vs. graphical representation as it represents the original context that gave rise to CFT. Furthermore, the table vs. graph debate continues to be relevant in the practical arena of data visualization. Lastly, it is important to understand and measure the impact of fit on cognitive effort in the original context before evaluating in other con texts where the link to the original theory may be altered [3].

Early empirical work applied CFT in the context of simple mental tasks [2,24]. Therefore, in line with previous work [3,24,25], we hy pothesize the impact of cognitive fit on cognitive effort for simple tasks as a baseline (H1,H2). On the other hand, research applied CFT and cognitive effort mechanism to more complex tasks in the context of financial statement analysis [26], geographic information systems [52], interruptions [25], operations management [24], quality assurance [53], and financial accounting [3]. Because of somewhat mixed findings when it comes to decision accuracy and decision time in more complex tasks, it is important to validate the impact of cognitive fit on cognitive effort for a task with higher complexity; hence we hypothesize the same nature of the relationship for complex tasks as well (H3, H4).

In H1a – H4a, we use the average fixation duration to assess cogni tive effort:

H1a. For Simple-symbolic tasks, information representation in table format results in lower average eye fixation duration than in graph formats.

H2a. For Simple-spatial tasks, information representation in graph format results in lower average eye fixation duration than in table formats.

H3a. For Complex-symbolic tasks, information representation in table format results in lower average eye fixation duration than in graph formats.

H4a. For Complex-spatial tasks, information representation in graph format results in lower average eye fixation duration than in table formats.

Similarly, in H1b – H4b, we stated the same relationship using eye fixation count to assess cognitive effort:

H1b. For Simple-symbolic tasks, information representation in table format results in lower eye fixation count than in graph formats.

H2b. For Simple-spatial tasks, information representation in graph format results in lower eye fixation count than in table formats.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Experimental design

The experiment was a fully randomized within-subject three-factor design: Task Complexity (simple and complex), Task Type (spatial and symbolic), and Representation (table and graph). This resulted in 8 cell, 2 by 2 by 2 factorial design. All participants performed all experimental tasks associated with eight cells in random order. To avoid the potential bias of using the same answer from the same task and different repre sentation influencing the answer in another representation, a slightly modified version of tasks was created while preserving task level of complexity and task type. Eye fixation count and average fixation duration were used to assess participants' cognitive effort. As most fix ations are in the 200-300 ms duration range [6], we adopted 200 ms as a minimum duration threshold to be considered a fixation.

Four tasks (simple-spatial, simple-symbolic, complex-spatial, and complex-symbolic) and two data representation types (spatial (graph) and tabular (tables)) were used to conduct the study. Both tasks and data representations were adopted from Baˇci´c and Henry [3] (prior research focused on a perceptual measure of cognitive effort), who themselves modified it and adapted it based on earlier CFT task/representation work [24.25]. The tasks followed Wood's (1986) definition of task classification for simple (a low number of variables/information cues and calculations) and complex (a high number of variables/information cues and calculations) [54]. In the simple-spatial task, subjects were presented with performance indicators for three company locations across months and were asked to select the month where the combined performance indicator was the highest. Following Wood's [54] meth odology to classify tasks, this simple-spatial task required participants to use three information cues (performance indicator, location, and month), add the indicator metric for each month and location, and then evaluate those across six months to discover the correct answer (month). This task required assessing the relationship between data points while identifying the month associated with the highest indicator for com bined locations [3,24,25] (See Appendix, Figs. A1 and A2).

The simple-symbolic task required subjects to obtain specific data by directly extracting information. More specifically, subjects were asked to decide how much was the metric for a particular firm location and specific month above the target rate. Once the value for a specific location and indicator was located, it needed to be subtracted from the target rate (also displayed), which resulted in the correct answer. Following Wood's methodology to assess tasks [54], this simple sym bolic task involved four information cues (metric, target metric, month, and location), one behavior (calculate), and subtraction between the selected metric and its target [3,24,25] (See Appendix, Figs. A3 and A4).

In the complex-spatial task, subjects were asked to use existing in formation for six firms to assess which companies meet two financial scenarios. The scenarios included target requirement conditions for financial metrics such as Sales, Gross Profit, ROI, ROA, ROE, and ROE. Using the same task assessment methodology [3,54], this complex spatial task required participants to evaluate 17 information cues and use them in nine different acts of comparison. The task called for eval uating the relationship between data points and did not necessitate precision, making it a spatial task [3](See Appendix, Figs. A5 and A6).

In the complex-symbolic task, also from prior research [3,24,25], subjects were asked to determine which firms to invest in based on the given scenario's precise conditions. More specifically, we provided the participants with five different balance sheet (liabilities) line items/ categories associated with six firms. They had to determine which firms to invest in. The complex symbolic task required participants to assess 11 information cues (dollar amount, firm, accounts payable, accrued expenses, notes payable, bonds payable, total liabilities, fixed amount of total liabilities, fixed percent limit for notes payable, and fixed percent limit for accounts payable) and perform acts of comparison and ordering. Given the number of cues and behavioral actions [54], this task involved substantially more complexity for the user when compared to two simple tasks. Further, the task required participants to obtain specific data by directly extracting information, which made it a sym bolic task [3,24,25]. (See Appendix, Figs. A7 and A8).

Participants were exposed to each experimental task using two data representation types: graph(s) and table(s). Tabular representations were tables with firms or locations placed vertically and attributes such as a month, year, and various metrics horizontally. Two-dimensional bar charts and line charts were operationalized as the spatial format. All representations and task problem statements fit on one computer screen (See Appendix A).

## 4.2. Experimental procedure

We recruited 34 participants from various business classes at a large public university in the Midwestern United States. Students received partial course credit for their participation, a \$10 gift card, and could win one of three US\$50 rewards for performance in terms of accuracy per unit of time. All subjects used the same computer with a 21<sup>′′</sup> monitor. GP3 Eye Tracker (accuracy of 0.5–1 degree of visual angle, 60hz update rate) was used to collect eye tracking data. To ensure highquality data collection, each subject went through a 9-point calibration. Once the calibration process was successfully completed, each partici pant performed all four tasks using both tabular and graphical data representation. The order of tasks and data representation types for each subject was randomized in advance using a randomization algorithm. There was no explicit time limit to this study. After all sessions were completed, recordings were reviewed, and data for two subjects was deemed unusable. Therefore, the subsequent analysis was conducted using data from 32 <sup>1</sup> subjects (37.5% male and 62.5% female). The median age of the participants was 28, and the average age was 30 (SD = 8.75). Seventy-seven percent of the participants were undergraduate students. Forty percent of the participants had at least some work experience in a professional or technical job, while 18% had some work experience as a manager or proprietor. Participants came from a wide number of business majors.

A manipulation check for Task Complexity was completed by asking subjects their perceptions of task complexity on a 7-point Likert scale. A manipulation check for Task Type was completed by asking subjects their perception of the level of need for data re lationships and the need for precise values. The combined score for question one and reverse coded score for question two was used to assess the subjects' ability to detect the difference in Task Type. ## 6.1. Simple tasks

In the case of simple tasks, users' attempt to solve the tasks does appear to be in line with expectations, as fixation-based metrics captured the attention and the focus of the decision maker's pupil on data representation. If representation made it hard for a user to assess the meaning of a particular representation area, they needed to focu more intently and frequently to understand information. In our study, this led to a longer average fixation duration and higher fixation counts. In the instance of simple–spatial task, users fixated on average 19 ms (378 ms vs. 359 ms) longer (5.3% difference) when assessing informa tion with tables over when assessing information with graphs. At the same time, those same users were forced to make, on average, over 40 (119.53 vs. 159.72) more fixations (33% difference) to arrive at a

solution (Fig. 2).

To enrich our understanding of the performance associated with the impact of fit and cognitive effort, we conducted a post-hoc analysis and found that the advantage of using graphs for the simple-spatial task does result in significantly quicker decision time. The speed improvement did not impact decision accuracy, nor did it impact users' confidence in the accuracy of their answer (Table 9). The simplicity of the task used may explain relatively high accuracy (0.94 vs. 0.84) regardless of the representation.

In the context of the simple-symbolic task, users exhibited 31 ms (384 ms vs. 415 ms) longer fixation durations (8% difference) when using graphs compared to fixation durations using tables. At the same time, those same users were forced to make, on average, about 28 (133.66 vs. 105.59) more fixations (27% difference) to reach a solution (Fig. 3).

In relative contrast to our study, another study [24] found no support for impact on decision performance (time) for simple-symbolic tasks, indirectly contradicting the CFT-assumed role of cognitive effort. The author suggested that subjects might have believed that graphical data representation gave them no chance to arrive at an optimal solution, thus resorting to guesstimating and solving the task quicker and with less effort but with less accuracy. While it is possible that for some tasks. users may decide to optimize and therefore exert levels of effort not in line with CFT, we caution when making this assumption for simple tasks without considering the non perceptual assessment of cognitive effort or exploring factors other than effort. However, it becomes difficult to correctly assess any other factor's impact if one fails to measure cognitive effort.

The results for simple tasks are well aligned with the existing CFT theoretical assumptions. Our results indicate strong support for the notion that, in simple tasks, when users are provided with graphical representations performing symbolic tasks or tabular representation performing spatial tasks (i.e., lack of fit), they experience an increase in cognitive effort relative to when representations match the mental model to solve the problem. However, what is novel and revealing in this study is the ability to detect the underlying cognitive effort mechanism in the context of simple tasks. One previous study [3] that attempted to detect cognitive effort as a perceptual measure using survey questions was unable to identify a meaningful change in subjects' cognitive effort. That study suggested that cognitive fit's weak impact on cognitive effort perception may have arisen because of the simplicity of tasks. Hence, when tasks are too simple, users may not consciously recognize their cognitive decision-making process [29], or they may be unable to accurately self-report on cognitive processes such as cognitive effort [30]. Our study, however, suggests that even in these simple tasks, the CFT assumption of changes in cognitive effort is validated but should be assessed using physiological rather than perceptual measures.

However, our results for the complex-symbolic task align with an explanation that users, when facing complex-symbolic problems with graphical representations, had no direct ability to calculate precise values and, at some point, potentially recognized no value in further evaluation. This notion has been noted in consumer research where consumers may avoid a particular choice selection process because it requires a significant effort and opt to use an easier process instead (Cooper-Martin, 1994). Speier (2006) offered related interpretation by introducing a complexity framework suggesting that tasks could be evaluated across a continuum, from low to high experienced complexity. This experienced complexity results from objective complexity [54], user characteristics, and the decision aid [55]. In the experienced complexity framework, tasks are sequentially categorized into simple (trivial, low demand), feasibly-solvable, trade-off, and limiting tasks based on information cues and information processing strategies (perceptual, analytical) most likely to be deployed by the user. Ac cording to this framework, the complex-symbolic task used in our research (along with our subjects' characteristics and provided repre sentation) resulted in experienced complexity most accurately described as a ‘trade-off’ task. In trade-off tasks, users feel that additional effort will not result in adequate improvement in performance; hence they may decide to trade off lower decision performance for lower effort. Our post-hoc analysis confirms this potential explanation as subjects, when using graphical representation, appear to have traded lower accuracy for not spending additional effort. When using tabular representation, subjects decided to continue extending cognitive effort that would be rewarded with higher accuracy (Table 12 – see Accuracy). Furthermore, our subjects appear to be willing to engage in this trade-off evaluation and choice, as the difference in accuracy is accompanied with the dif ference in the reported confidence in decision accuracy (Table 12 – see Accuracy Confidence).

## 7. Implications, limitations and next steps

There are important implications, questions, and next steps emerging from this research. We identified a consensus around the role of cogni tive effort in the context of the visual representation of data. Cognitive effort is the underlying mechanism and one of the key ingredients that makes user response prediction and visual representation recommen dation choices possible. Yet IS literature that depends on a deeper un derstanding of that cognitive effort is scarce, mostly theoretical, and lacking in its direct assessment. We responded to this challenge by conducting the first study to assess, in a controlled environment and through the CFT lens, how task type and representation match impact physiological assessment of effort. We provided evidence that eye tracking technology, specifically fixation-based metrics, can be used in the understanding of cognitive processes initiated by our data repre sentation choices. Our findings provide the research community with a challenge and opportunity to consider physiological sensors to assess the cognitive effort mechanism that is central to CFT. We focused on fixation-based eye-tracking measures as they reveal an important visual attention component of user interaction that remains the predominant approach to assessing cognitive processes and effort. In addition to continuing to measure both perceptual/experienced and eye-trackingbased cognitive effort (including pupillometry [56,57], we encourage others in CFT research to consider additional physiological and neuro logical sensors and related data that can be useful in expanding our understanding and assessment of cognitive effort (GSR devices, facial expression detection, EEG, fMRI).

Our findings raise an important question regarding possible differ ences between cognitive effort perception, physiological indicators of cognitive effort, and theoretical inferences researchers make about the cognitive effort mechanism in the absence of measurement. For example, studies in a similar context suggested that users are unable to perceive cognitive effort in simple tasks [3] or that even in simple yet non-trivial/feasibly-solvable tasks, the performance benefit of infor mation representation and task fit may disappear [24] as users switch from analytical to perceptual processing strategy [26,58]. Our finding suggest that theoretical interpretations are possible but potentially premature until we understand the cognitive effort phenomenon better. One way to achieve that better understanding is to measure cognitive effort more directly instead of only focusing on the interpretation of task performance as the indicator of effort. The ability to detect the change in cognitive effort physiologically, even in simple tasks, and perceptually in more complex tasks suggests that focusing on the theoretical inter pretation of effort based on decision performance may not adequately describe the effort placed on users. Hence, we suggest a need to revisit interpretations others have made in CFT empirical work about the cognitive effort mechanism, especially when interpreting unsupported hypotheses. Replication studies that include a direct assessment of cognitive effort may shine a new light on a number of CFT-based studies, offering potential new insights and alternate explanations.

Next, while the condition of cognitive fit induced a similar impact on both fixation duration and count in our experimental setting, this may not be the case in other contexts. For example, in certain scenarios, a manifestation of cognitive effort may primarily stem from the user's confusion or distraction and not from a need to explore visualization deeply [10]. In those instances, such as more novel or bespoke data displays, the user may primarily focus on assessing how to ‘read’ the display. In such scenarios, the user's effort may be mainly captured through a higher fixation count. Alternatively, cognitive effort may be driven by a necessity for deeper or more purposeful attention. In those instances, the user's cognitive effort may manifest through longer fixa tion duration and less so through fixation count. While the user is required to extend more cognitive effort to solve the task in both in stances, our ability to assess cognitive fits' mechanism (effort) is enabled by tracking both fixation duration and fixation count.

Additionally, we provide further evidence that understanding and predicting cognitive effort using CFT under more complex tasks is difficult and may challenge the bounds of CFT. Our study appears to confirm the notion of a potential cross-over point being induced by increased task complexity and data representation's ability to mitigate it [24]. Although more research and evidence are needed to understand user impact when solving more complex tasks under CFT, our study suggests that a cognitive fit's hypothesized and perceived benefit may disappear once users reach that cross-over point. In the case of the complex-spatial task, our subjects did not reach that point; thus, they continued to benefit from lower effort inducement when representing data in a spatial format (graphs), i.e., cognitive fit. In the case of the complex-symbolic task, however, the task may be no longer considered feasibly-solvable (where decision-makers can fully deploy their analyt ical and perceptual processes [59]) even when information is presented in a symbolic format. Instead, our complex-symbolic task becomes a trade-off task where users decide to satisfice and limit the effort even if that may lead to less accuracy [32]. In the context of this possible explanation, our study offers an important contribution as it is the first study in the CFT context to empirically capture effort-limiting ap proaches by focusing on the effort itself rather than indirectly implying it by evaluating decision performance. Although we did not hypothesize this optimizing relationship for the complex-symbolic task, existing theoretical work on effort optimization [32,34], task complexity [59], CFT research focused on complex tasks [3,24–26], and our research findings should provide sufficient support to test this explanation more formally in future studies.

Our results and implications should be evaluated in the context of its limitations. We used a controlled experiment with student subjects that often do not represent the data visualization user population and may not be motivated to perform the experimental tasks. We attempted to mitigate those issues by including students (i) from a diverse group of academic and professional interests, (ii) from both undergraduate and graduate-level education, and (iii) offering a monetary award for participation. Although most of our subjects are data visualization users or represent those users well, there is a great value in further studies that would target additional population segments with more professional experience, design experience, domain knowledge, and decision-making responsibility. Our choice of task and data representation was pur poseful to incorporate tasks and data representations that are founda tional in existing CFT research and typical in current practice. In our definition of task complexity, we adopted Wood's task framework and the resulting CFT task complexity operationalization literature to conceptualize two task levels. Defining and classifying task complexity is a difficult endeavor, and other operationalizations of task complexity are possible with different results. Therefore, we encourage future research incorporating cognitive assessment across other tasks and representations. Although our research detected most hypothesized

We attempted to mitigate the issue by designing a fully randomized, withinsubject experiment that includes roughly double the number of subjects used in a typical eye-tracking study [60]. Future research may benefit from including more subjects to allow for more complex models and relationships. While we adopted a 200 ms threshold in our eye-fixation operationalization based on existing literature, results may vary if using alternative thresholds. Lastly, the results should be evaluated in the context of lower-than-expected decision accuracy for spatial tasks. This underscores the importance of measuring cognitive fit, cognitive effort, and performance to understand the underlying relationship.

## 8. Conclusion

In this study, we identify and elevate the need for CFT research to focus on advancing our understanding and assessment of cognitive effort. Recognizing the difficulty in measuring cognitive effort, we offer the potential of eye-tracking technology to assess cognitive effort physiologically in the CFT context. Several important implications for phenomenon measurement, theory and empirical research, and practice emerged from our study: (i) fixation-based metrics can be useful in advancing our understanding of cognitive fit and its cognitive effort mechanism, (ii) physiological indicators of cognitive effort can provide insights that may be missed if using only perceptual measures of cognitive effort or theoretical-only discussion of cognitive effort, and (iii) while additional evidence is still needed, more complex tasks may push the boundary of CFT's predictive power using traditional perfor mance measures (time and accuracy), requiring the ability to incorpo rate multiple measures of cognitive effort, along with other variables and theoretical lenses.
