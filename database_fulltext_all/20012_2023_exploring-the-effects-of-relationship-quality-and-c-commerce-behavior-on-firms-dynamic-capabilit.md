# Exploring the effects of relationship quality and c-commerce behavior on firms’ dynamic capability and c-commerce performance in the supply chain management context

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Relationship commitment Institution-based trust Collaborative commerce behavior Dynamic capability C-commerce performance Collaborative learning

## A B S T R A C T

Although previous studies indicate the critical role of collaborative commerce (c-commerce) adoption and dy namic capability in the supply chain process, they have not addressed the relationship between c-commerce behavior and dynamic capability. By adopting the commitment-trust theory and the dynamic capability view, this study empirically examines the effects of relationship quality, c-commerce behavior, and dynamic capability on c-commerce performance. Survey data collected from 257 professionals in various manufacturing industries were analyzed using the partial least squares technique. The findings suggest that c-commerce behavior and dynamic capability are positively associated with c-commerce performance. However, negative calculative commitment does not positively affect c-commerce behavior. Moreover, institution-based trust does not nega tively affect negative calculative commitment. This study is one of few studies investigating the relationships among the variables that contribute to c-commerce performance. The findings can serve as references for practitioners in supply chain practices.

## 1. Introduction

In the digital age, firms are devoting efforts to obtaining competitive advantages and achieving sustainable profitability growth in highly competitive markets. Building close business relationships may be crit ical. This includes connecting with external suppliers, customers/cli ents, or other channel members and closely integrating internal and external resources or organizational functions [1–3]. These relationships have been considered effective supply chain management (SCM) assets for firms to maintain commerce prosperity, stability, and growth. Additionally, firms may need to sustain their competitive advantage based on the collaborative commerce (c-commerce) behavior and dy namic capability view [4–7]. However, few studies have examined the role that relationship quality plays in generating competitive advantage by examining its influences on firms’ c-commerce behavior, dynamic capabilities, and c-commerce performance.

Top managers have been pay attention to enhancing the relationship quality between their firms and supply chain partners, and how these relationships influence a firm’s revenue, costs, and profitability [8–10]. Therefore, this study aims to explore the influences of relationship quality, which can be represented by institutional-based trust and various dimensions of commitment, on c-commerce behavior in SCM contexts. Commitment-trust theory [11] reveals that trust and rela tionship commitment determine the long-term relationship among partners, facilitating cooperation and information/knowledge sharing to reduce conflicts and uncertainty within a cooperative relationship [12,13]. Relationship commitment refers to the willingness of supply chain partners to invest in asset-specific resources, namely time and money [14,15]. Supply chain partners in alliances are more likely to exchange resources with other parties. Additionally, trust refers to the degree to which a party is willing to be vulnerable to the actions of the other party based on the expectation that the other party would perform particular actions important to the trustor, regardless of the capability of monitoring or controlling that other party [16].

It is important to understand the attributes of interorganizational relationships promotes c-commerce adoption in a dynamic environ ment. While prior studies have focused primarily on investigating the effects of organizational, technological, and environmental factors on ccommerce adoption [21,22], this study examines the effects of interor ganizational relationships on c-commerce behavior and dynamic capability, which is an underresearched issue in the literature. Improving the dynamic capabilities can better leverage the competitive advantages of all the parties in a supply chain and enhance their per formance by, for example, keeping up with rapid changes in the product life cycle, customized products, and services [23–25].

Accordingly, this study develops a research model of relationship quality and c-commerce of firms. This model includes the core con structs of relationship quality and dynamic capability, which may be more practical than a resource-based view (RBV) in a rapidly changing environment. To be specific, this study adopts multiple dimensions of interorganizational commitments to empirically examine the effects of various kinds of interorganizational commitments on firms’ c-commerce behavior, dynamic capability, and c-commerce performance. Therefore, the primary research question (RQ) of the current study is as follows.

RQ. How do firms leverage relationship quality and c-commerce be haviors to enhance their dynamic capabilities and c-commerce performance?

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. C-commerce behavior in SCM

In c-commerce, effective information/knowledge transmission, pro cess integration, collaborative learning, and collaboration with business partners allow firms to gain competitive advantages. However, the literature fails to provide an overall and consistent view of the benefits of firms’ c-commerce behaviors. A handful of studies indicate that two ccommerce behaviors are crucial in SCM: partner sharing and technology use [6,26]. In addition, several researchers have argued that relation ship commitment and trust in network resources are critical de terminants of sharing behaviors [27]. Information technologies (IT) enable collaborative supply chain participants to develop long-term relationships, share information/knowledge, and handling external risks resulting from alliance relationships more effectively [28,29]. Ccommerce behaviors that involve suppliers, partners, distributors, pro viders, and customers can be categorized into four types (i.e., vertical collaboration, horizontal collaboration, diagonal collaboration, and intersectorial collaboration) [30]. For example, vertical collaboration combines raw-material suppliers, product design, manufacturers, lo gistics, and marketing, enabling SCM to perform more efficiently. Generally, supply chain collaboration includes information or resource sharing, joint knowledge creation, goal congruence, collaborative learning, decision synchronization, and incentive alignment [13.28.31]

Therefore, c-commerce is defined as information-technologyenabled and facilitated collaborative relationships manifested by multidirectional knowledge flows among distributed participants involved in various supply chain activities to increase value for all supply chain participants [32]. C-commerce behavior is related to business and technological practices that make the partners’ relation ship closer by making contacts or returning favors, allowing them to exchange knowledge/information or acquire resources [1,19]. Research indicates that in business-to-business manufacturing contexts, collabo ration between sellers and buyers is critical when both parties perceive that the collaborative efforts are capable of achieving mutual goals for creating value for both parties and maintaining long-term relationships with long-term benefits for both parties [8]. Additionally, when sellers continue to develop buyer-centered innovative solutions that enable buyers to remain flexible and proactive and thus to acquire sustainable competitive advantages, long-term collaborative relationships between sellers and buyers are likely to be sustained [33].

Research has indicated that web services play a crucial role in ccommerce by enabling better business process management for inte grating internal and external resources effectively, namely materials, expertise, information, and new opportunities [7,23]. Such c-commerce facilitates the relationships among supply chain partners and overcomes traditional operational patterns for cocreating value. By sharing infor mation through IT, monitoring processes, reducing costs (i.e., moni toring and coordination), and shortening the reaction time, firms can solve problems and increase product availability, thus enhancing oper ational efficiencies [9,10]. In summary, c-commerce behavior can be considered a mode by which business partners collaborate via IT to effectively share information/knowledge and reduce production time, thus raising cocreation values.

## 2.2. The core competitive advantage of dynamic capability

RBV suggests that the attributes of firms’ resources (valuable, rare, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable) can sustain their competitive advan tages in a global economic environment [34]. Thus, a firm should ac quire, integrate, and create unique resources to maintain its competitive advantage. However, the RBV is a static analysis that does not consider how internal resources are used to create competitive advantages in a dynamic environment. With advancements in technology and changing environments, competition has changed over time [23,35]. Thus, some researchers have extended the RBV perspective to elaborate on how dynamic capabilities influence firms’ abilities or performance [36].

Additionally, because of the rapid advancements in IT, firms must have an essential innovative ability to cope with challenges. Strength ening dynamic capabilities requires integrating internal and external resources, such as innovative new products or the improvement of processes [35]. A firm’s dynamic capabilities can be promoted by building, extending, and modifying complementary resources to enable firms to meet the demands of markets and gain sustainable competitive advantage. Some researchers argue that dynamic capabilities directly or indirectly affect firm performance [4,7,34,37]. It can be inferred that a firm has to adjust its internal resources or operational routines and leverage external resources or identify opportunities, which will effec tively enhance positive outcomes. For example, firms should manage threats and reconfigure (combine, learn, integrate, and cooperate) re sources, renew environmental sensitiveness, and seize opportunities in the market to improve their performance [29]. Finally, joint asset management, knowledge management, and patent protection can effectively improve a firm’s dynamic capabilities [37]. Renewing envi ronmental sensitiveness means that when firm faces a challenging environment, it should be aware of or develop knowledge pools regarding global competition, distinctive customer needs, advanced technologies, and the strategies and behaviors of leading customers, competitors, and suppliers. Seizing opportunities means that when a firm discovers new market opportunities, it must choose a specific business model.

This study defines dynamic capabilities as collections of processes, sensing capabilities, seizing opportunities, and working well with absorptive, innovative, and reconfiguring capabilities [38]. Once a firm can seize new opportunities, resources, and technologies from collabo rative partners, they have to plan, learn, apply and transform techno logical knowledge in their organizational structures [36]. Meanwhile, they must assess the existing and new capabilities to design or recon struct their technologies to align with the requirements of the market [39]. Moreover, knowledge or resource reconfiguration (i.e., building, codevelopment, and modification) enables firms to utilize tangible and intangible assets to support their supply chain practices [29].

## 2.3. Relationship quality in supply chain practices

In the digital age, managers need to strengthen the relationship quality (i.e., relationship commitment and trust) between buyers and suppliers to meet the requirements and expectations of the other [10,12]. Therefore, Morgan and Hunt [11] proposed that relationship quality includes two key mediating variables (KMV): relationship commitment and trust. They argued that partners would be more likely to cooperate when the levels of relationship commitment and trust are high because they reduce conflicts and uncertainties. Generally, when both parties perceive each other’s capabilities as meeting their expec tations, trust will occur. In addition, trust is positively related to rela tionship commitment, both of which improve and motivate interparty cooperation. As mentioned above, this study defined relationship commitment as the dedication of trading partners to a valuable rela tionship who are willing to maintain continuous collaboration.

Several studies have suggested that commitment is one of the keys to maintaining a long-term relationship in SCM or relationship marketing $[ 2 4 , 2 7 , 4 0 , 4 1 ]$ ]. Relationship commitment is key to encouraging partners to continually make efforts to maintain valuable relationships [11]. Additionally, relationship commitment is a psychological contract that provides resources and supportive cooperation under a high level of trust in exchanging relationships [14,41]. Consequently, research in dicates that relationship commitment is positively associated with product quality, knowledge transfers, adaptation, cooperation, and customer loyalties [8,42]. When the marginal revenue or benefit is higher than the marginal cost, firms are more willing to cooperate with trading partners. In such a case, it can be inferred that firms need to maintain good relationships with partners to achieve and/or maintain sustainable competitive advantages.

Relationship commitment enables partners to overlook short-term benefits sustain a long-term relationship [11]. In the context of SCM, partners can easily understand others’ values or beliefs about achieving common goals, such as finding solutions to problems via knowledge/ information sharing. Such cases make them more likely to cooperate. However, relationship commitment among trading partners is difficult to build. Fawcett et al. [24] stated that relationship commitment de termines collaboration, leading to a highly competitive advantage in a dynamic environment. This study conceptualize relationship commit ment into four types: affective, positive calculative, negative calculative, and normative [8,43]. Affective commitment refers to the intense af fective commitment of members who are willing to stay in an association because they want to. Positive calculative commitment is the intense value-based commitment to remain in the relationship because of the benefits it produces. Negative calculative commitment refers to a locked-in commitment when one partner perceives a lack of alternatives and stays in a relationship [8]. Normative commitment refers to obligation-based commitment, which results from formal or informal regulations that cover social norms or customs. In addition, this kind of commitment cannot be changed by personal preferences or emotions [24,44].

Researchers have also indicated that trust is crucial to reducing un certainty and assuring the profits of both parties when partners are reliable and honest with each other [45]. Here, trust is defined as the actions performed by one party to satisfy another party’s beliefs about the future [11]. Subsequently, the partners have a high level of trust and confidence in the relationship, which promotes successful supply chain relationships [26]. In this study, trust is conceptualized as institutionbased trust, which includes situational normality and structural assur ances. The former refers to the regulations or habits in the context; the latter refers to safety policies, such as legal assistance, regulations, and guarantees [19]. Prior research has revealed that institution-based trust is the best way to facilitate the exchange of information, reducing un certainty in supply chain practices [46]. While supply chain partners come from different backgrounds and are motivated to cooperate and pursue long-term relationships when institution-based trust exists. Institution-based trust can thus improve performance when the inter firm collaboration processes are effective [3,47]. Therefore, it can be inferred that institution-based trust improves collaborative willingness, dynamic capabilities, and performance.

## 2.4. Summary of the research gaps and opportunities

The research gaps in the areas of SCM and c-commerce that this study intends to address are as follows. First, from a relationship quality viewpoint, this study investigates how institution-based trust and mul tiple dimensions of commitment can be helpful in enhancing the overall strength of firms in the context of c-commerce. Second, this study empirically investigates the antecedents and consequences of c-com merce behavior to identify the critical success factors of collaboration among member firms of a supply chain, which is an overlooked issue in prior c-commerce studies. Finally, most studies have focused on the static view of RBV to gain a competitive advantage. In contrast, this study emphasizes the critical role of dynamic capabilities in obtaining the resource conception of positioning in global marketing.

## 2.5. Research hypotheses

This study examines the relationships of factors associated with supply chain relationship quality (i.e., relationship commitment and trust) based on the KMV model [11]. Additionally, supply chain ccommerce behavior is regarded as the core of the construct. This study tries to reveal how the enhancement of c-commerce behavior impacts dynamic capabilities and firm performance. The proposed research model and the associated research hypotheses are shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.5.1. Institution-based trust

Morgan and Hunt [11] indicated that the more vital the trust be tween two parties is, the greater their mutual commitment to each other is. Trust can improve the level of commitment, by reducing opportu nistic behaviors motivated by the other party, utilizing fairness or reciprocity to maintain the long-run relationship, and reducing trans action costs [9]. Institution-based trust is valuable in promoting the effect of long-term relationships with buyers and sellers, thus enhancing partner cooperation [46]. Additionally, when the trust in the relation ship is high, both parties are more likely to cooperate [3,48]. Conse quently, the following hypothesis is developed:

H1a. Institution-based trust is positively related to affective commitment.

A high level of trust reduces the risks that members take, and the behaviors of both parties are thus predictable to the other [3,49]. In such a case, a supply chain partner will have lower monitoring or correcting costs, improving operational efficiency [5]. In other words, a high level of trust enables firms to achieve profits, making partners believe their relationship is beneficial to each other. This sense of belongingness is impossible to replace [42]. Moreover, trust is positively associated with calculative commitment [44]. In contrast, trust is negatively related to negative calculative commitment [8]. Therefore, the following hy potheses are developed:

H1b. Institution-based trust is positively related to positive calculative commitment.

H1c. Institution-based trust is negatively related to negative calcu lative commitment.

Trust is a core element for building a collaborative relationship with supply chain partners [2,27,31,49]. Trusting partners tolerate each other when confronting conflicts in interests and values [12,43]. Addi tionally, trust and active bonds are closely constrained and enable be haviors in the relationships of supply chain partners, which is called normative commitment [50]. Thus, it can be inferred that as trust in creases, one party feels that they should continue the relationship with the other party. In other words, those with higher trust feel obligated to maintain their relationship with supply chain partners. Therefore, the following hypothesis is developed:

H1d. Institution-based trust is positively related to normative commitment.

Morgan and Hunt [11] proposed trust to be a key mediating variable of relationship marketing for facilitating collaborative behaviors.

Previous studies have confirmed that trust positively affects collabora tion [17,18]. We can infer that trust plays a critical role in supply chain partners’ relationships in terms of determining potential risks regarding maintaining those relationships. Therefore, the following hypothesis is developed:

H1e. Institution-based trust is positively related to c-commerce behavior.

## 2.5.2. C-commerce behavior

In the dynamic operative environment, each partner might face un certain challenges or risks. Thus, a closer relationship can reduce trading and opportunity costs, and promote commitment and collaborative behavior between two parties $[ 5 , 2 6 , 4 7 ]$ . Affective commitment enables the partners to be loyal to the supplier/buyer of the supply chain [48]. High affective commitment allows both parties to continue the rela tionship or attachment, improving the collaboration and partnership of firms [51]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is developed:

H2a. Affective commitment is positively related to c-commerce behavior.

Positive calculative commitment is a value-based commitment that is formed dependent on the rational calculation of benefits [8]. Similarly, negative calculative commitment originates from individuals’ perceived lack of alternatives. In other words, this commitment leads to a continuation of the relationship when the benefits outweigh the costs [52]. Other studies argue that calculative commitment increases users loyalty to maintain the current relationship with suppliers/buyers and strongly lock-in commitment [53]. Accordingly, efficient supply chain practices usually integrate the costs and benefits of calculative commitment [48]. In such a case, it is challenging to change the current state of both parties, thus driving the calculative commitment of both parties to perpetuating the relationship. Therefore, the following hy pothesis is developed:

H2b. Positive calculative commitment is positively related to c-com merce behavior.

H2c. Negative calculative commitment is positively related to c-com merce behavior.

Normative commitment encourages both parties to stay in the rela tionship and accept collective norms [43]. Subject norms can impact normative commitment among supply chain partners [41,53]. The more substantial the normative commitment is, the weaker the service pro vider’s alternatives. Therefore, it can be inferred that normative commitment reflects a partner’s loyalty or cocreative value in enabling suppliers/buyers to continue their relationship with supply chain prac tices [24]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is developed:

H2d. : Normative commitment is positively related to c-commerce behavior.

## 2.5.3. C-commerce behavior, dynamic capability and collaborative performance

IT-supported C-commerce activities facilitate information/knowl edge sharing in the supply chain [20]. Thus, high levels of information/ knowledge sharing and collaboration enable cocreation of value among supply chain partners [47]. Additionally, c-commerce behaviors can affect the effectiveness of various SCM processes, including knowledge acquisition and innovation [31].

Dynamic capability involves organizational agility that allows firms to reconfigure their regulations or processes [5,6,54]. Firms are more likely to gain external resources and capabilities from other parties when their relationships are enhanced by SCM practices. These complimen tary resources increase a firm’s abilities to integrate, develop, and transform competencies related to internal and external resources and cope with rapidly changing business environments [37]. The close partnership of supply chain partners can mitigate the impact of uncer tainty and bring to light potential opportunities, including new product designs, emerging markets, and new customers [39]. Research also in dicates that collaboration among supply chain partners can create competitive advantage by improving firms’ agility [51,54,55] and their operational, financial, and environmental performance [26,36,47]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is developed:

H3a. C-commerce behavior is positively related to dynamic capabilities.

H3b. C-commerce behavior is positively related to firm performance.

## 2.5.4. Dynamic capability and collaborative performance

High dynamic capabilities enable firms to enter new markets and improve their level of competitiveness [1,22,25,34,37]. Therefore, firms with more vital dynamic capabilities can develop effective strategies to respond to the uncertainty in their business contexts, including pricing, distribution, and communication in a highly competitive environment [56]. Several studies have indicated that dynamic capabilities enable firms to continue to learn from their supply chain partners, achieving better performance [4–6,34]. Thus, the following hypothesis is developed:

H4. Dynamic capabilities are positively related to c-commerce performance.

## 3. Research method

This study used a survey to collect cross-sectional data. A five-point Likert scale was adopted to measure the survey items (5-point being strongly agree and 1-point being strongly disagree).

## 3.1. Data collection

This study adopted a convenient sampling procedure and used both offline and online approaches to distribute our questionnaires. The participants were mainly supervisors who work in different industries related to supply chain collaboration and were familiar with the firms suppliers and c-commerce activities. The active members of the Institute of Internal Auditors-Chinese Taiwan (IIA-CT) were targeted as potential survey respondents. Survey invitations were distributed through two channels. The first was by physically handing out the questionnaires to the potential participants at the site of the annual conference of the IIA-CT that most of the IIA-CT members attend, being the most convenient and effective way to approach those potential survey participants. Email invitations were then sent to those IIA-CT members who were not pre sent at the site of the indicated conference. A total of 396 survey in vitations were distributed, and eventually we received 366 responses, yielding a response rate of 92.42%. Among all the responses received, 257 responses were considered valid, yielding a valid response rate of 70.22%.

We chose these professionals for the following reasons. First, these participants work for private or state-owned over-the-counter (OTC) companies in different manufacturing or service industries. Those companies tend to adopt state-of-the-art IT-based SCM applications and often have strict requirements when selecting their business partners and suppliers at all layers of their supply chains. Second, the participants constantly conducted internal auditing tasks which required them to have a thorough understanding of the manner in which their companies conduct collaborative activities with their suppliers and other busines partners. To perform those auditing tasks well, all those participants were required to have sufficient work experience in other functional areas of business operations, and were motivated to constantly update and enhance their understanding of the corporate strategic orientations, key performance indicators, and operational protocols and processes that were associated with their companies’ collaboration with the sup pliers and business partners. Third, the internal auditing departments of OTC companies in Taiwan are supervised directly by the chief executive officers (CEOs). Therefore, internal auditors usually serve as important advisors of the CEOs to help them oversee the overall operations of the companies. Thus, the internal auditors have the opportunity to acquire key knowledge/information about the companies’ strategic planning and critical operational protocols from the CEOs or other members of senior management of the companies. Finally, to be eligible to acquire the positions of internal auditors of the OTC companies, a majority of those participants had to pass an examination organized by the IIA-CT to acquire an advanced Certificate of Internal Auditors (CIA). The prereq uisite for taking the advanced CIA examination was a minimum of two to three vears of full-time work experience in various functional business administration areas, including. The advanced CIA exam questions are particularly associated with the key knowledge of the areas of finance, accounting, and production and operations management that are highly relevant to SCM. We thus believed that our participants were knowl edgeable to appropriately answering our survey questions.

Our respondents’ profile is as follows: 156 (60.7%) were female; 237 (92.22%) participants were aged over 31; 236 (91.83%) participants had bachelor’s degrees or higher; 197 (76.65%) participants had job tenure above ten years; 148 (57.59%) participants were in management positions or above; and, 186 (72.37%) of the 257 participants worked in manufacturing industries.

## 3.2. Measurement

This study measured 11 first-order constructs for representing the eight latent constructs in the research model using 44 items adopted from the literature (See Appendix A). Institution-based trust (TR) [19], four types of relationship commitment (affective (AC), positive calculative (PC), negative calculative (NC), and normative (NoC)) [8,44], and ccommerce performance (Pe) [32] were measured as first-order reflective latent constructs. Additionally, c-commerce behavior is defined as the actions that a firm takes to collaborate with its partners in a supply chain through IT use, joint product development, or offering services to one another. C-commerce behavior is operationalized as a second-order formative construct which is measured by two first-order reflective constructs of operational (CO) and strategic (CS) collaboration [57]. The former refers to a firm and its supply chain partners’ collaborative ef forts to share resources and solve problems. The latter refers to creating an interorganizational team that invests resources, shares benefits, and handle risks related to strategic activities.

Dynamic capability is defined as companies’ ability to develop, inte grate, or reconfigure their corporate competences to effectively respond to the rapid changes in the business environment [58]. Therefore, dy namic capability incorporates firms’ capabilities which are difficult to replicate by others and can enable the firms to adapt to changes in customer and/or technological opportunities and even to change the ecosystem that they occupy [59]. For analytical purposes, dynamic capability, as a second-order formative construct, is conceptualized as firms’ capacities to sense opportunities, seize opportunities, and reconfigure/redeploy intangible and tangible resources [39,59]. Sensing (DS) refers to when employees participate in professional association activities to find target markets, refine operational methods to satisfy customer needs, and collect financial information. Seizing (DSS) refers to investing resources to find solutions to problems for customers, to change operational processes to respond to their clients, and to obtain feedback from employees about the dynamics of the focal problems. Reconfiguring (DR) refers to when a firm has implemented new man agement methods, such as new marketing methods or strategies, renewed operational processes, and new methods to achieve the firm’s goals.

We evaluated and modified the questionnaires based on the results of a pilot test in which 30 individuals participated. The Cronbach’s alpha statistics of the 11 first-order constructs ranged from 0.73 to 0.89, sur passing 0.7 [60], suggesting that all items should remain in the formal questionnaire.

## 3.3. Data analysis approach

Some statistical approaches were adopted in this study, including descriptive statistics, homogeneity tests, and confirmatory factor anal ysis (CFA). Additionally, by using the software package of Smart PLS 3, this study measured the model fit and examined the proposed hypoth eses via the structural-equation-modeling technique of partial least squares. For performing the CFA, we first examined Cronbach’s alpha to ensure the internal consistency and reliability of the measures. Addi tionally, Convergent validity, which refers to the degree to which the measures of a specific latent construct converge or share a high pro portion of variance in common [60], was also checked. Finally, discriminant validity was assured by examining whether the correlation coefficients among constructs were lower than the square roots of their average variance extracted (AVE) values. Discriminant validity refers to the degree to which different constructs should be unrelated. Finally, the proposed hypotheses were validated via the bootstrapping approach.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Measurement model

This study complied with the principle of keeping at least three items of each first-order construct for CFA [61]. We then excluded five items (AC3, CO1, CO2, CS3, and DSS2) with factor loadings lower than 0.7, and the remaining 39 items were used for the subsequent path analysis procedure (Appendix A).

First, we conducted ANOVA tests of the participants’ various types of work departments to test the homogeneity. According to the findings, the p values were 0.29 and 0.6 for c-commerce behavior and c-com merce performance, respectively; similarly, the results were 0.47 and 0.55 for these types of industries, respectively. The p values exceeded 0.05, revealing an insignificant difference among the sample of con structs. Second, regarding convergent validity, Hair et al. [60] indicated that convergent validity should consider the factor loadings, AVE, and composite reliability (CR) values. Appendix A shows that all factor loadings ranged from 0.7 to 0.93, more significant than 0.7. Table 1 shows that the standardized Cronbach alphas ranged from 0.7 to 0.88, which are equal to or higher than the recommended threshold value of 0.7 [60]. Additionally, all AVE values ranged from 0.53 to 0.74, which are higher than the recommended threshold value of 0.5 [60]. Finally, all CR values ranged from 0.82 to 0.92, which are higher than the rec ommended threshold value of 0.7 [60]. Therefore, the results suggest that the measurement model exhibits adequate convergent validity. Finally, Table 1 shows that each square root of AVE was higher than the correlation coefficients. Additionally, all the indicators’ outer loading on their associated constructs were greater than all of their cross loadings [62]. Therefore, discriminant validity was ensured. Overall, the CFA results demonstrated that the measurement model has good quality.

## 5. Discussion

The research model of this study aims to investigate how institutionbased trust and relationship commitments influence the c-commerce behavior of supply chain practices. Subsequently, the findings (H2) show that affective, positive calcu lative, and normative commitment are positively associated with ccommerce behaviors, suggesting that a high level of commitment fa cilitates c-commerce behaviors. However, negative calculative commitment (H2c) is negatively related to c-commerce behavior. A plausible explanation for this unexpected finding is that when a firm has a relatively significant number of suppliers to turn to as alternative sources of supply, the lock-in effects derived from the firm’s negative calculative commitment to a particular supplier may not be sufficient to motivate it to remain committed to the relationship with the supplier, particularly when the alternative suppliers are capable of offering the product that the company requires with the same level of costeffectiveness and quality. Additionally, the asymmetry of market powers of the member firms of a supply chain may have contributed to this finding. When a firm has relatively larger market power than most of its business partners in a supply chain, it tends to dictate the collab oration terms within the supply chain to the degree that its business partners can no longer bear. Therefore, the effects of those business partners’ negative calculative commitment to the firm may be consid ered to be a potential hazard, thus motivating those business partners to seek opportunities to reduce their collaboration with the firm by collaborating with the able rivals of the firm.

Moreover, c-commerce behavior is positively and significantly related to enhancing dynamic capability (H3a) [55] and c-commerce performance (H3b) [26,36]. The findings suggest that c-commerce behavior is one of the critical elements of technological innovation in the area of supply chains. It is also vital to help firms take advantage of supply chain partners to meet clients’ requirements or customers’ de mands. Furthermore, such collaborative behavior reduces uncertainties related to investing in potential opportunities when firms and suppliers share costs and risks via information sharing, thus enhancing firms productivity and contributing to better performance. Finally, dynamic capability is positively related to c-commerce performance (H4) [4,6,25,34]. This finding implies that these firms can enhance perfor mance and sustain competitiveness by creating collaboration or alli ances, identifying the right partners, investing in innovative activities, and offering specialized products/services to gain new markets.

## 5.1. Implications for theory

The findings of this study contribute to the current understanding of how interorganizational relationship quality impacts c-commerce behavior, dynamic capability, and c-commerce performance by adopt ing commitment-trust theory as the theoretical lens, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been specifically investigated in the c-commerce or SCM literature. The findings imply that for supply chain partners to find suppliers who meet requirements quickly through advancements in technology, value-oriented trust is crucial.

Additionally, examining the effects of multiple dimensions of commitment on c-commerce behavior has provided us with a more comprehensive understanding of why the c-commerce behaviors of firms are motivated. The results reveal that affective, positive calcu lative, and normative commitment improve the process of c-commerce among these supply chain partners. The findings suggest that facilitating partners’ relationship commitment might enhance their propensity to ward c-commerce efforts. The findings contribute to the literature by extending commitment-trust theory to the investigation of the SCM of manufacturing industries.

Finally, the research findings also highlight the importance of firms efforts, including leveraging the resources of suppliers, manufacturers, and clients/customers, for enhancing their dynamic capabilities/agility to acquire competitive advantages and achieve desirable c-commerce performance. This study validates the significance of the associations among institution-based trust, various types of relationship commit ment, dynamic capability, c-commerce behaviors, and c-commerce performance. Therefore, this study contributes to the development of ccommerce and SCM theories by emphasizing the significance of adopt ing an integrated theoretical view of relationship quality and dynamic capabilities when investigating c-commerce related phenomena.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

The results of this study confirm the positive effects of firms’ ccommerce behaviors on their dynamic capability and c-commerce per formance. This indicates that it is critical for firms to, from both the operational and strategic points of view, to carefully make decisions regarding with which of their business partners in the same supply chain they should collaborate to acquire access to critical tangible and intan gible resources that allow the firms to respond to emerging hazards and/ or opportunities in their business environments in a more agile manner. This may be achieved by identifying business partners who share some common objectives regarding enhancing the effectiveness of operational activities and/or strategic planning of the firms and can offer the firms complementary resources that can lead to increased profits and/or reduced risks.

Additionally, identifying suitable business partners to collaborate with is just the first step for firms. Determining how to motivate those business partners to devote efforts to collaborate with the firms is the next critical task. While institution-based trust plays an important role in enhancing various types of commitment among firms, firms should work together to develop methods for facilitating information sharing be haviors among them. This can increase the level of information transparency among the firms, help them jointly resolve problems, ensuring that all the firms behave for the best interest of the partnership, and enhance mutual trust among them [28].

Moreover, the confirmation of the significant positive effects of af fective, positive calculative, and normative commitments on c-com merce behaviors offers a couple of implications. First, managers of the firms should identify and constantly emphasize the shared value to highlight the similarity and facilitate interfirm communications via in formation sharing to increase the levels of attachment and familiarity among the firms involved in the same partnership. The increased levels of perceived similarity, attachment, and familiarity can make both firms to have positive emotional perceptions of the other (i.e., affective commitment) and feelings of being obligated to continue to collaborate with one another for the greater good of all the collaborating parties (i.e, normative commitment), resulting in a high likelihood of those firms to continue to stay in this partnership. Additionally, it is more important for managers of firms to ensure that the business partners can recognize the foreseeable tangible benefits of the partnerships among them, such as increased benefits, reduced costs, or availability of critical resources (i.e., positive calculative commitment), than to emphasize the irre placeability of the partnership (i.e., negative calculative commitment). Therefore, firms should devote themselves to regularly assessing the operation of the existing partnerships to ensure the existence of mutual benefits in partnerships.

## 5.3. Limitations for future research

First, all the participants were supervisors in manufacturing in dustries. Thus, future studies can expand our findings by including diverse firms from, for example, the food, transportation, retail, or healthcare sectors. Second, the research finding of the insignificant relationship between negative calculative commitment and c-commerce behavior implies the importance of taking into consideration the dy namics of the competition among firms of a supply chain. To address this issue, future studies may include factors related to interfirm competi tion, such as power asymmetry among supply chain members and the availability of alternative collaborating parties in the supply chain [33,66]. Finally, we investigated the effects of c-commerce behaviors on organizations from the viewpoint of interorganizational relationships by referring to commitment-trust theory. Nevertheless, it is universally agreed that whether companies adopt and effectively leverage appro priate IT applications to conduct their c-commerce activities plays an important role in determining firms’ c-commerce performance. There fore, future studies may consider adopting theories that are associated with IT adoption to enrich the knowledge base of this research area.
