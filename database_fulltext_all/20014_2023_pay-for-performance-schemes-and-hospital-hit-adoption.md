## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Health information technology Spillover effect Pay-for-performance programs Hospital value-based purchasing program Hospital readmission reduction program

## A B S T R A C T

Pav-for-performance (P4P) schemes are implemented to incentivize or penalize hospitals for their safe care: giving. Given that health information technology (HIT) results in better healthcare outcomes, P4P schemes are expected to promote hospital HIT adoption. However, P4P schemes could also discourage hospitals from adopting HIT because they may take away resources initially allocated for HIT adoption. This paper is one of the first to empirically investigate the double-edged role of P4P schemes in HIT adoption. We leverage a natural experiment in the US healthcare system, which introduced P4P schemes in 2013. Our empirical analysis reveals an unintended side effect of P4P schemes that could potentially impede HIT adoption. We also find that P4P schemes have a negative spillover effect on nonparticipating hospitals in the same multihospital system (MHS) as participating hospitals, particularly for small nonparticipating hospitals. Our findings provide important impli cations for health policy design and MHS management.

## 1. Introduction

In past decades, pay-for-performance (P4P) programs have become an increasingly popular healthcare policy adopted by regulators around the globe [1]. Such programs aim to achieve long-term quality and ef ficiency improvement in participating hospitals. P4P programs provide financial incentives or penalties for hospitals to improve value-based healthcare provision by directly linking a proportion of the payments to the quality metrics of healthcare providers [2]. The financial conse quences for hospitals are substantial; for instance, the Hospital Read mission Reduction Program (HRRP) in the United States imposed \$566 million in penalties on 2583 hospitals in 2019 alone.

Extensive academic attention has been devoted to examining the effectiveness of P4P programs in the past decade [1–3]. Despite the importance of learning P4P program outcomes, it is equally (if not more) crucial to take a closer look at hospitals’ specific responses to P4P pro grams. Knowing the particular adaptations made by hospitals helps policymakers deepen their understanding of why P4P programs are effective (or ineffective) in improving policy implementation and potentially avoiding unintended side effects. For example, HRRP is generally acknowledged as effective in reducing readmission rates. However, recent evidence shows that this is due to hospitals’ altered admission decisions. Such alterations could harm affected patients and contradict the policy’s original intention [4].

We investigate hospitals’ reactions from the health information technology (HIT) adoption perspective due to its potential implications for participating hospitals’ long-term healthcare quality improvement. Governments around the world have rolled out incentive programs to encourage the active adoption and meaningful use of HITs, as HITs are expected to improve the overall quality, safety, and efficiency of the healthcare system. IS researchers have identified many factors influ encing HIT adoption, one of which is government regulation such as P4P programs.

The impact that P4P programs have on hospital HIT adoption is an important empirical question in that it urges policymakers to evaluate these programs at a longer time horizon. If P4P programs encourage HIT adoption, it may help explain why some P4P programs seem ineffective right after their launch [5]: HIT usually takes years to improve perfor mance, oftentimes with setbacks in the beginning [6]. If P4P programs discourage HIT adoption, they might attenuate the effectiveness of other incentive programs that encourage HIT adoption, e.g., the Health In formation Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act (HITECH). These large-scale regulatory programs often require tremendous administrative resources, which may not be optimal if some of these programs offset each other.

It is also essential to investigate the spillover effect of P4P programs on nonparticipating hospitals related to participating ones, as doing so helps policymakers comprehensively evaluate the full influence of these programs. Suppose P4P programs boost HIT adoption in participating hospitals, and yet impede such adoption in the nonparticipating hospi tals related to the participating ones. This undesirable impact could be neglected if only the treatment effect is considered.

It is a reasonable conjecture that quality incentives alone can potentially spur HIT adoption because numerous studies have identified the positive impacts of HIT on the diverse dimensions of healthcare delivery [7,8]; moreover, many industrial reports have stated that HIT drives success in value-based care [9]. However, we suspect that the actual effect of P4P programs on hospital HIT adoption could go otherwise. Participating hospitals in P4P programs face financial un certainty from punishment pressures and financial constraints if they are penalized. As a result, participating hospitals might prioritize other quality improvement initiatives over HIT implementation, given that HIT generally requires high acquisition costs, and the benefits are un likely to be fully realized in the short run [10,11]. Nevertheless, empirical evidence is scarce on this issue, and no research has scruti nized the causal link between P4P programs and HIT adoption in hos pitals. Also, no previous study has examined the potential spillover effect of government regulation on HIT adoption in nonparticipating hospitals.

To investigate hospitals’ reactions from the perspective of HIT adoption, we leverage a natural experiment in the US healthcare system: only some types of hospitals are eligible for two of the P4P programs initiated by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). We study how implementing the Hospital Value-Based Purchasing Program (HVBP) and the Hospital Readmission Reduction Program (HRRP) impact hospital HIT capabilities. We address the following research questions:

1) Does implementing P4P programs affect the overall HIT adoption levels in participating hospitals?

2) Does implementing P4P programs have any spillover effect on nonpar ticipating hospitals in the same multihospital system (MHS) as partici pating hospitals?

Our research context is the US healthcare system. Two P4P pro grams, HVBP and HRRP, began in 2013 for a subset of hospitals. Both programs link the largest share of Medicare spending (i.e., inpatient stays) to the quality of care provided by hospitals. The introduction of these two programs allows us to investigate the effect of P4P programs on HIT adoption levels in participating and nonparticipating hospitals in a difference-in-differences (DID) setting. We divide these hospitals into three groups, namely the treatment, control, and spillover groups. The treatment group includes all eligible hospitals for the pair of P4P pro grams from the fiscal year 2013, whereas the spillover group includes ineligible hospitals in the same MHSs as some participating hospitals. Hospitals affiliated with the same MHS engage in intensive communi cations, facilitating organizational learning and alleviating the risk associated with HIT adoption [12,13]. As a result, implementing these programs could also affect ineligible hospitals in the same MHSs as the eligible ones, leading to a potential spillover effect. The control group includes ineligible hospitals not affiliated with any MHS or in MHSs that do not have any participating hospitals.

Our DID analysis of the treatment and spillover effects generates two main findings. First, concerning our first research question, we find that P4P programs impede hospitals’ overall HIT adoption. Second, regarding our second research question, we find that P4P programs have a negative spillover effect on HIT adoption levels in nonparticipating hospitals in the MHS of participating ones.

Our findings provide theoretical implications by emphasizing the role of government regulation on HIT adoption; furthermore, they echo a previous empirical study where hospitals that have not met government-initiated HIT requirements are more likely to improve their operational outcomes in HVBP [14]. The managerial implications of this study lie in uncovering an unintended side effect of P4P schemes that could potentially impede HIT adoption. We thus urge policymakers to evaluate P4P programs with a longer time horizon and a more comprehensive range of hospitals, including both participating hospitals and related nonparticipating hospitals.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Organizational response to regulation

Our study is closely related to the stream of literature on organiza tions’ responses to government regulations. One particularly interesting context in this stream is the healthcare industry due to its heavily regulated nature. Cook et al. [15] proposed a general theory of organi zational response to regulation (TORR) and fleshed out this theory by examining regulations in the healthcare industry. In addition to theo retical studies, how hospitals behave in the face of regulation has also attracted extensive empirical attention. For example, Alexander and Morrisey [16] developed and tested hypotheses on how a regulatory program (certificate of need) affects hospital entry into contractmanagement arrangements. Researchers have also investigated the ef fects of the Prospective Payment System on hospitals’ shift in services, change in the size of the administrative component, and use of new technology [17].

There is a burgeoning literature on how hospitals react to P4P pro grams. Such programs introduce financial incentives for healthcare providers with a considerable amount of money at stake; thus, their effectiveness is of primary concern to policymakers and healthcare providers. Early studies mainly focus on the overall impact of P4P pro grams on their respective target metrics, given that they are direct measures of program effectiveness. For instance, many studies have found HVBP ineffective in improving patient outcomes [5,18,19], while several other studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of HRRP in reducing readmission rates [20,21]. Later, increased academic attention has been devoted to understanding healthcare providers’ specific re sponses and the connections between responses and outcomes, as such knowledge could shed light on policy improvements. For instance, Gupta [4] suggests that hospitals alter their admission decisions under HRRP by reducing the admission rate for returning patients that contribute to the readmission metric, which could harm affected pa tients. Most notably, Menachemi et al. [22] surveyed Florida physicians treating children to examine the impact of pay-for-performance pro grams on physicians’ HIT use. They found that P4P programs that fail to directly emphasize HIT use do not influence the adoption of electronic health records (EHR). Compared to their study, our analysis is con ducted at the hospital level, with a more comprehensive sample, and our methodology allows for more rigorous causal inferences. Nevertheless, empirical evidence is scarce regarding the relationship between P4P programs and HIT adoption. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to causally investigate the effect of P4P programs on hospital HIT adoption, suggesting potential mechanisms for the effect as well.

## 2.2. HIT adoption in hospitals

Another stream of literature to which our study is closely connected involves organizations’ information technology adoption, particularly HIT adoption by healthcare providers. IS researchers have identified four major factors influencing HIT adoption: finance, functionality, users, and the environment [23].

One of the environmental factors concerns government regulations. Healthcare is a heavily regulated industry; as a result, government regulations significantly impact hospital management decisions, including HIT adoption. Academic attention has been devoted to examining government regulations’ impacts on HIT adoption, such as electronic medical records (EMR) [24–29] and health information exchanges (HIEs) [30,31]. Table 1 summarizes previous findings involving this environmental factor. While prior literature has studied the influence of various regulations, none has investigated the impact of pay-for-performance on HIT adoption, despite it being an increasingly popular policy trend. Also, no prior study has considered the potential spillover effects of a policy on HIT adoption in hospitals that are not directly targeted by the policy. By studying the spillover effect, we emphasize the importance regarding the collateral effects of government regulation that might otherwise be overlooked.

## 3. Theoretical background and research questions

## 3.1. Treatment effect on participating hospitals

We ground our first research question in the general theory of organizational response to the regulation proposed by Cook et al. [15], a framework used to examine the specific ways in which organizations behave in the face of regulation. According to TORR, responses could be divided into adaptation and selection [15,33]. Adaptation involves the efforts made to adapt to regulatory constraints; if such efforts turn out to be inadequate, organizations are vulnerable to selection pressure and may cease to exist. Adaptive responses occur at three organizational levels, namely institutional, managerial, and technical [34]. The insti tutional level refers to the relationship between the organization and other entities in society. The managerial level regards the part of the organization attempting to procure and allocate resources, while the technical level (or the technical core) involves the part of the organi zation directly providing services and products. This theory specifies an ordering of adaptive responses to evolving regulatory environments. In the face of regulations that constrain financial resources, organizations will first respond at the institutional level, followed by managerial-level changes. Technical-level changes are made only when responses at the first two levels fail, given that modifications in service/production ac tivities are complicated to make, and organizations always attempt to protect their technical core from uncertainties [35].

In our context of the two P4P programs, hospitals may respond at the institutional level to reduce uncertainty in the pre-implementation stage by attempting to become involved in the regulatory process or chal lenging the programs’ legality. However, as the HVBP and HRRP were officially implemented starting in the 2013 fiscal year, with participa tion mandated by CMS, hospitals were forced to make managerial-level changes afterward. P4P programs impose financial uncertainty in hos pitals and even constrain financial resources in terms of penalization, leading to internal conflicts over scarce resources [15]. Hospitals are likely to reallocate resources internally, which could lead to variations in HIT investment.

It is a common conjecture that P4P programs can spur HIT adoption because the positive impacts of HIT established in the literature are closely connected to the target metrics of many P4P programs [36,37]. However, HIT adoption and meaningful use lead to considerable changes at the technical level, which might not be desirable for hospitals because new HIT implementation is likely to disrupt existing work practices, routines, and information flows for clinicians and staff. As a result, hospitals may suffer immediate productivity loss and may take a considerably long period (often many years) to reap the benefits [6]. Also, HIT adoption generally requires considerable financial resources, from acquisition to maintenance costs [10]. From this theoretical point of-view, we conjecture the following: to avoid immediate penalization, at the managerial level, hospitals might reallocate their resources to initiatives that are instantly effective in improving target metrics [38] and might decrease their HIT investment to minimize changes at the technical level.

Empirical evidence is mixed. Menachemi et al. [22] found that P4P programs that do not directly emphasize HIT use do not influence EHR adoption among physicians. On the other hand, Lee et al. [14] investi gated hospitals participating in the HVBP program and found that hos pitals that have not yet adopted a government-mandated IT system are more likely to improve their operational performance. The authors attribute this phenomenon to the fact that HIT takes years to improve hospital performance and suggest that participating hospitals in P4P programs could potentially refrain from HIT adoption.

It is unclear from the literature whether P4P programs lead to higher or lower overall HIT adoption in hospitals. The above discussion moti vates our first empirical question regarding how P4P programs impact the overall HIT adoption levels in participating hospitals.

## 3.2. Spillover effect on nonparticipating hospitals

Hospitals within the same MHS might affect each other’s HIT adoption through social influence driven by organizational learning.

Organizations hesitate over the in-house adoption of complex tech nology until they obtain sufficient technical know-how to implement and operate it successfully; this is where organizational learning could play an important role [39,40]. Organizational learning happens when experience and routines are disseminated by contacts (e.g., consultant and personnel movement) among organizations, one with and one without prior knowledge [41]. Managers in organizations that have not adopted an innovation learn about tacit knowledge and best practices related to technology implementation from adopters, which signifi cantly enhances the chance that the technology innovation will be successfully implemented [12,39].

In our context, health information technologies are often complex systems, and hospitals face significant knowledge barriers to successful deployment [42]. Hospitals in an MHS are “owned, leased, sponsored, or contract managed by a central organization” [43]. They are very close in social proximity and characterized by high levels of informal and formal communication, which eases the information flow between them [12,13]. Trust and common interests afforded by their system mem bership can also enhance adopters’ willingness to provide invaluable knowledge to non-adopters in the system [40,41]. As a result, adopter hospitals within the same MHS help reduce the risk of and facilitate nonadopter hospitals’ HIT adoption through information transmission and organizational learning.

Specifically, if hospitals participating in the two P4P programs in crease (reduce) their HIT adoption level, more (less) information about their newly adopted HIT applications will be disseminated through network ties within the MHS. Consequently, other nonparticipating hospitals inside the same MHS as the participating ones have more (less) access to information critical to their adoption success, thus facilitating (inhibiting) their HIT adoption.

Empirically, a focal hospital’s likelihood of HIT adoption is closely associated with adoption by a hospital within its MHS [12]. Based on the above theoretical arguments and the empirical finding, HIT adoption levels seem to be closely correlated among hospitals in the same MHS. Our second research question thus examines whether P4P programs have any spillover effect on nonparticipating hospitals in the same MHS as participating ones.

## 4. Research context and data

## 4.1. Research context and data source

To understand how P4P programs affect HIT adoption in hospitals, we leverage a natural experiment in the US healthcare delivery system.

HVBP and HRRP obey the statutory requirements set forth by Section 1886(o) of the Social Security Act and Section 3025 of the Affordable Care Act, respectively. They both began in the fiscal year 2013 for subsection (d) hospitals as defined in the Social Security Act Section 1886(d)(1)(B) to improve healthcare quality and reduce costs [44,45]. The two programs financially incentivize hospitals to improve their performance in all care domains to achieve such goals. They comple ment each other, with HRRP focusing on readmission rates and HVBP focusing on metrics, including clinical outcomes, patient and commu nity engagement, safety, and cost-efficiency.

The two programs affect Medicare payment for inpatient stays in approximately 3000 acute care hospitals across the country [44]. With HVBP and HRRP penalization, CMS withholds underperformers’ Medi care reimbursements up to 2% and 3%, respectively.<sup>2</sup> The magnitude of these financial incentives introduced by the two programs is consider able, given that a one-percent reduction in inpatient reimbursement is estimated to be approximately 0.2% of a penalized hospital’s total revenue. For a typical midsized hospital, such a penalty translates to a loss of roughly \$654,000 for the fiscal year [46].

Note that both HVBP and HRRP began in the fiscal year 2013 and mandated that the same group of hospitals participate, meaning that hospitals must participate in both programs or neither of them. It is therefore impossible to tease out the standalone effect of one program on HIT adoption. They are complementary P4P programs sharing the same design, i.e., reducing Medicare payments for underperforming hospitals. Our primary goal is to investigate P4P programs’ impact on hospital HIT, so it is reasonable to consider them as a single treatment in the empirical design.

Our data come from three different sources—the AHA Annual Survey Database, the HIMSS Analytics Database, and the official website of CMS. The AHA Annual Survey Database is a comprehensive hospital database covering extensive aspects of hospital operations. HIMSS An alytics™ conducts nationwide annual surveys on hospitals’ HIT use and builds its database with survey responses from chief information officers and other IT executives in hospitals. We matched hospital information in different sources using Medicare ID numbers.

We excluded hospitals that are not participating in the EHR incentive program under the HITECH Act, given that they are likely to have a very different pretreatment trend (i.e., before the fiscal year 2013) from hospitals participating in the EHR incentive program. The reason for doing so is that the EHR incentive program, starting from the fiscal year 2011, pays hospitals for the adoption and meaningful use of EHR, and has been demonstrated to have significantly increased EHR adoption among eligible hospitals [28]. We also excluded participating hospitals exempted from the pair of P4P programs for at least one specific fiscal year due to some exceptional circumstances.<sup>3</sup> They account for approximately 2% of all participating hospitals from our sample.

We constructed panel data with hospital information four years before and after implementing the two P4P programs in the fiscal year 2013, that is, over an eight-year observation window from 2009 to 2016. We further excluded hospitals established after the start of our obser vation window, 2009. Given that the dependent variable might be un available for some hospitals in a specific year, the resulting panel data are unbalanced, with 31,861 observations and 4190 hospitals.

## 4.2. Variables

To answer our research questions, we construct the following vari ables for our analysis.

## 4.2.1. AppsNum

We use the number of operating HIT applications to measure each hospital’s overall level of HIT adoption. The HIMSS database provides information on the adoption status of various HIT applications in the surveyed hospitals. We obtain this dependent variable by counting the number of HIT applications that are live and operational in each hospital yearly.

## 4.2.2. After

We create a dummy variable for each year, coded as 1 for 2013 and the years afterward because the penalty of the two P4P programs was first imposed for the fiscal year 2013. The dummy variable is 0 otherwise.

## 4.2.3. Treatment and spillover dummies

The CMS Acute Inpatient PPS Impact File provides information on hospitals’ participation in the pair of P4P programs throughout the years. We divide hospitals into three groups: treatment, control, and spillover groups. The treatment group includes all hospitals partici pating in both programs after their launch, whereas the spillover group consists of nonparticipating hospitals in the same MHS with at least one participating hospital. The control group includes nonparticipating hospitals with no participating hospitals in the same MHS and nonpar ticipating hospitals not affiliated with any MHS.

## 4.2.4. HHI

We measure the level of market competition in the hospital referral region using the widely adopted Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) [47]. The index ranges from 0 to 1, with a higher number indicating a more concentrated market. We annually calculate this index for each hospital referral region based on the total number of inpatient days in the AHA database.

## 4.2.5. Expenses

We operationalize hospital size as the total facility expenses in the hospital. These data come from the AHA database.

## 4.2.6. Other control variables

Given that the pair of P4P programs concerns hospitals’ Medicare inpatient reimbursement, we need to control for the different percent ages of revenue from Medicare inpatient payments in different hospitals. We approximate this percentage with the hospital’s Medicare inpatient days divided by the adjusted patient days. The adjusted patient days are the sum of the inpatient and equivalent patient days that generate the same revenue from outpatient services. The relevant data are from the AHA database. In addition, we collect the hospital’s number of beds and include it to further control for the effect of hospital size on HIT adop tion. Last but not least, the literature has shown that hospital age (the number of years since a hospital’s establishment) significantly affects HIT adoption [12]; it is also included in the model as a control variable.

## 5. Empirical analysis

## 5.1. Empirical models

We employ a DID model with two-way fixed effects on hospitals and years to estimate the impact of P4P program implementation on hos pitals’ HIT adoption levels. The hospitals were not randomized in these two P4P programs; instead, a certain category of hospitals (subsection (d) hospitals as defined under the Social Security Act) is statutorily required to participate. Note that the nonrandom treatment selection does not necessarily bias the DID estimation because the DID model allows dependence between the treatment assignment and individualspecific time-invariant variables [48]. In our context, being (or not being) a subsection (d) hospital is a characteristic of a hospital that does not change over time; therefore, the treatment assignment determined by this hospital characteristic will not bias our estimation. To answer our research questions, we estimate the following model (hereinafter referred to as the Equation) to examine whether implementing the two P4P programs has any impact on the hospitals in the treatment and spillover groups:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \log (\text { AppsNum }) _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \text { Treatment } _ {i} \times \text { After } _ {t} + \alpha_ {2} \text { Spillover } _ {i} \times \text { After } _ {t} + \boldsymbol {\gamma} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {x} _ {i t} \\ + \mu_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \epsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

Here, log(AppsNum) represents the log-transformed number of HIT applications in hospital i for year t. Treatment and Spillover denote whether hospital i belongs to the treatment and spillover groups, respectively. $A f t e r _ { t }$ indicates whether year t occurs after implementing the two P4P programs. The term x represents a vector of all timevarying control variables, including the approximation of the percent age of revenue from Medicare inpatient payments $( M c r P c t _ { i t } ) ,$ , the number of beds (BedsNum ), total facility expenses (Expenses ), the HHI of a hospital’s referral region $( H H I _ { i t } ) ,$ and hospital age (HospitalAge ). The effects of both observed and unobserved time-invariant characteristics of hospital i are captured by the first fixed effect term $\mu _ { i \cdot }$ . The second fixed effect term $\delta _ { t }$ accounts for any potential temporal shocks at the yearly level. The term $\epsilon _ { i t }$ is the random error.

The main effect of Treatment (Spillover ) captures the difference be tween the treatment (spillover) and control groups before implementa tion. It is absorbed into the hospital-specific fixed effects. and therefore is not explicitly estimated. The coefficient of the interaction term Treatment $\times \ A f t e r _ { t }$ (Spillover × After ) represents the additional differ ence between the treatment (spillover) and control groups after imple mentation. Therefore, $\alpha _ { 1 } ( \alpha _ { 2 } )$ can be interpreted as the treatment (spillover) effect of the two P4P programs on HIT adoption in partici pating hospitals (nonparticipating MHS members).

CEM allows researchers to bound the imbalance between the matched treated and nontreatment groups a priori and choose how to coarsen each variable manually. Because of its power and simplicity, CEM has been commonly used in recent DID analyses [50].

## 5.3. Model-free plot

To explore how the introduction of P4P programs affects HIT expansion in hospitals belonging to the treatment and spillover groups (as opposed to how it affects hospitals belonging to the control group), we draw a model-free plot to show how the average number of HIT applications (log-transformed) in the three groups changes year by year in Fig. 1.

We see that the numbers of HIT applications in the three groups are essentially parallel before introducing the P4P programs, i.e., their HIT expansion speed used to be similar. However, after the introduction, the number of HIT applications in hospitals in both the treatment and spillover groups increases slower than in hospitals in the control group.

## 5.5. Robustness tests

We conduct a series of robustness checks for our main results. We start with (1) testing the parallel trends assumption and find that all treatment (spillover) dummy interactions with the time dummy are insignificant before implementing the two P4P programs [52]. (2) To demonstrate the robustness of our findings to the operationalization of the dependent variable, we redo our DID analysis using the change in the number of HIT applications in the hospital as the dependent variable. (3) To alleviate the concern of reverse causality, we re-estimate the DID model in our main analysis with no control or lagged control variables, following previous studies [53]. (4) To show that the results from the main analysis are robust to other time window choices, we shorten our time window to only include years after implementing the EHR incen tive program (i.e., from 2011 to 2016) [54]. (5) To account for the dependent variable being a count variable, we also consider a Poisson model with two-way fixed effects. (6) To ensure that our findings are not observed by chance, we estimate the random implementation models to determine how likely a random implementation of P4P programs would yield treatment and spillover effects with sizes comparable to our main analysis, following Greenwood and Agarwal [55]. The results of the above robustness checks all support our main findings. The detailed result tables can be provided upon request.

## 6.2.1. Clinical IT

In our main analysis, we obtain the dependent variable by counting the number of all HIT applications that are live and operational in the hospital, including both clinical and administrative IT. Because clinical IT is more crucial to hospital operations than admin IT, we are particularly interested in the P4P programs’ impacts on clinical IT ap plications. We define the dependent variable, ClinAppsNum, as the number of operating clinical IT applications. Many categories of appli cations are recorded in the HIMSS database, and we divide them into clinical and administrative IT based on the categorization in previous studies [57,58]. ## 6.3. Different types of hospitals

To investigate the heterogeneity of the treatment and spillover ef fects, we conduct two additional analyses on different types of hospitals.

## 6.3.1. Asymmetric treatment effects for different market structures

Adopting new healthcare information technology often helps diver sify a hospital’s range of services, creating a competitive advantage for the adopter and attracting more patients. Such an advantage is much more important for hospitals in competitive markets than their coun terparts in less competitive markets [62–64]. As a result, hospitals in different market structures are likely to respond differently (in terms of HIT investment) to the resource constraints under P4P programs. To investigate how market structures moderate the effect of P4P programs on the overall HIT adoption levels in participating hospitals, we estimate the DID model that includes two three-way interactions with HHI. HHI is a time-invariant variable generated by taking the average of the timevarying one, and we demean it before estimating the model.<sup>4</sup> Column 1 in Table 7 summarizes the results of the model. The coefficient of the interaction term Treatment × After × HHI indicates that the negative treatment effect is more pronounced for hospitals in a less competitive market. The result is consistent with our theoretical arguments for the treatment effect; in less competitive healthcare markets, the returns from HITs are smaller, and HIT adoption is even more easily outweighed by other quality improvement initiatives.

## 6.3.2. Asymmetric spillover effects for different hospital sizes

P4P programs negatively impact HIT adoption levels in participating hospitals in the MHS: as a result. nonparticipating hospitals in the same MHS will have less access to information critical to adoption success, and thus will face increasing adoption risk. However, large nonpartici pating hospitals have more opportunities for organizational learning from outside the system [12], thereby mitigating such risks better than the smaller ones. Consequently, small hospitals are less likely to adopt HIT than large hospitals under such circumstances. To examine how hospital size moderates the spillover effect of P4P programs, we estimate the DID model that includes two three-way interactions with the facility expenses. We generate the time-invariant variable, log(Expenses), by taking the average of the time-varying one, and we demean it before estimating the model.<sup>5</sup> Column 2 in Table 7 summarizes the results of the model. The coefficient of the interaction term Spillover × After × log (Expenses) indicates that when the hospital size is above average, the negative spillover effect is smaller or even nonexistent, implying that smaller nonparticipating hospitals slow down their HIT adoption more than their larger counterparts. This finding is consistent with our theo retical arguments for the spillover effect; smaller hospitals, with fewer organizational learning opportunities from outside the MHS, are more reliant on the tacit knowledge about HIT implementation disseminated from other participating hospitals within the MHS.

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, by leveraging a natural experiment in the US health care system, we causally infer whether (and if so, how) implementing two P4P programs, HVBP and HRRP, influences HIT capability in both participating and nonparticipating hospitals.

Specifically, we uncover an unexpected negative treatment effect: participation in P4P programs discourages hospitals from expanding their HIT capability. Because participating hospitals face immediate punishment pressure from P4P programs, they respond to P4P programs by adapting and reallocating resources. Developing HIT capability might not be a priority for hospitals in the face of P4P programs, considering that the upfront cost of HIT adoption is usually high, and returns from HIT adoption are often realized in the long term. Additional analyses on HIT-related expenses support this argument, showing that P4P programs indeed impede HIT investment. Furthermore, additional analyses reveal that the adoption of resource-consuming HITs is negatively affected by these programs to a greater extent, also corroborating this argument. The moderating role of market structures is also consistent with this explanation; we find that hospitals in less competitive referral regions slow down their HIT adoption more than their counterparts in competitive regions. In less competitive healthcare markets, the returns from HITs are smaller, and HIT adoption is even more easily outweighed by other quality improvement initiatives.

In addition to the negative treatment effect, the participation of eligible hospitals in the two P4P programs has a negative spillover effect on their nonparticipating peer hospitals inside the same MHS. We reason that such a spillover effect is due to the organizational learning between hospitals inside the same MHS. As participating hospitals in the MHS slow their HIT adoption, they communicate less information or tacit knowledge about HIT implementation in the organizational network within the MHS. This lack of communication leads to fewer organiza tional learning opportunities for nonparticipating peers and an increased risk of HIT adoption, eventually discouraging their adoption. This notion is supported by the finding that hospital size moderates the spillover effect. Smaller hospitals are more reliant on tacit knowledge communicated within the MHS and are faced with a higher risk of HIT implementation than their larger counterparts. Thus, they are indeed more vulnerable to such adverse spillover effects.

## 7.1. Theoretical and empirical contributions

We discuss theoretical and empirical implications for researchers in the field of IS and beyond.

We contribute to the stream of literature on organizations’ reactions to government regulations. We ground one of our research questions in the theory of organizational response to a regulation (TORR), testing the theory in the context of P4P programs [15]. We find that P4P programs hinder hospitals’ overall HIT adoption, which is more pronounced for hospitals in less competitive hospital referral regions. We provide empirical evidence to support the validity of the contextual variables proposed by the theory, namely the degree of market competition, as the theory claims that it could influence the effects of regulation on response strategies.

Our findings add to the literature on the role of regulations in HIT adoption, reinforcing the importance of government policy. Past liter ature has emphasized the influence of many government regulations on HIT adoption. Despite being an increasingly influential policy trend, the impact of P4P programs on hospital HIT adoption has never been investigated before. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to causally investigate hospitals’ responses to P4P programs concerning HIT capability. In addition to the treatment effect, our study is also one of the first to investigate the spillover effect of government regulation on HIT adoption. We highlight the often-overlooked collateral effects of government regulation and urge policymakers to evaluate programs on a comprehensive scale. Similar spillover effects might exist for other healthcare policies beyond P4P programs.

Our result echoes another empirical study focusing on the effec tiveness of HVBP penalization. Lee et al. [14] find that HVBP is more effective in improving operational outcomes in penalized hospitals that have not yet adopted government-initiated HIT requirements. The explanation given by the authors is that these hospitals that have adopted the government-mandated IT system experience no perfor mance improvement or even performance decline, given that HIT adoption often takes years to improve performance, which has been widely recognized in the IT adoption literature. They also suspect that the phenomenon could cause participating hospitals in P4P programs to refrain from HIT adoption, which is confirmed by our study.

## 7.2. Implications for practitioners

In addition to theoretical and empirical implications for scholars, we also provide meaningful insights for policymakers and managers.

We find that participating hospitals in the two P4P programs restrain their expansion of HIT capability, which might hurt these hospitals quality improvement in a longer time horizon. Policymakers should be fully aware of responses that might go against the P4P programs’ orig inal goal and design P4P programs taking these unexpected reactions into account. One possible direction for policymakers is to optimize the longitudinal order of various programs such as HVBP and HITECH [14].

We find that the participation of eligible hospitals in the pair of P4P programs has a negative spillover effect on their nonparticipating peer hospitals inside the same MHS, especially for small hospitals. Even without P4P programs, hospital size is positively associated with the adoption likelihood of various HIT applications. Thus, this asymmetric negative spillover effect could further disadvantage small hospitals $[ 1 0 , 2 4 ]$ . Policymakers or MHS headquarters might need to consider providing more support for small hospitals.

## 7.3. Limitations and future research directions

Last but not least, we discuss some limitations and potential avenues for future research. Due to the limited length of the observation window in our data, we cannot examine the impact of the two P4P programs in a longer time horizon. It is possible that as long as P4P programs are in effect, HIT expansion is inhibited for participating hospitals due to limited resources. It is also possible that as time goes by, participating hospitals may realize that HITs offer a unique edge that other quality initiatives fail to provide and may speed up their HIT adoption again. Therefore, future research could investigate how hospitals’ responses to P4P programs vary over time regarding HIT investment.
