# Understanding user misrepresentation behavior on social apps: The perspective of privacy calculus theory

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Social apps Privacy concerns Perceived benefits Privacy calculus theory Misrepresentation behavio

## A B S T R A C T

Due to the rapid growth of social media and mobile devices, social apps have become deeply integrated into people's lives. Extensive adoption of social apps entails the collection of massive amounts of users' private in formation, causing serious privacy issues. To protect their privacy, in practice, social app users are quite likely to disclose false information (i.e., to engage in misrepresentation behavior). Although misrepresentation behavior both influences the regular involvement of fellow users and reduces the efficiency of the marketing strategies designed by the social app operator, this phenomenon has received limited attention from academics. Accord ingly, we use privacy calculus theory to investigate user misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. Our empirical analysis shows that both privacy concerns and social rewards motivate users to engage in misrepresentation behavior, while personalized benefits discourage users from doing so. We verify the inter dependence of privacy concerns and perceived benefits by confirming that users with a high degree of privacy concerns tend to discount the perceived benefits of information disclosure. Moreover, we find that, as ante cedents, perceived privacy control and app permission sensitivity influence privacy concerns significantly, while disposition to value privacy and perceived effectiveness of privacy policies have nonsignificant effects on privacy concerns. We also conduct various mediation analyses to extend our basic model. This study enriches the literature on privacy calculus theory and information disclosure behavior. Additionally, our findings shed light on the strategies that the social app operator can adopt to incentivize users to refrain from disclosing false information.

## 1. Introduction

The past twenty years have featured the tremendous growth of social media; 2.95 billion active users were reported in 2019, and this number is expected to increase to nearly 3.43 billion by 2023 [26]. Currently, most social media take the form of social apps (e.g., Facebook, WeChat, LinkedIn, Sina Weibo, Twitter, Tik Tok) due to the rapid development of mobile internet and the widespread adoption of mobile devices. Social apps enable users to share information easily and thus connect with each other more efficiently. Despite these prominent benefits, social app users are highly concerned about the privacy of their information [10,22,37,43]. On the one hand, privacy issues are caused by the fact that the operators of social apps usually collect massive amounts of personal information about their users. Moreover, this issue is exacerbated by serious information asymmetry between the operators and users of social apps regarding the protection and utilization of personal information [5]. On the other hand, after being posted on social apps, users' personal information might be intentionally misused by their fellow users [18,40]. Given the serious privacy issues associated with social apps, it has become important for managers to understand users' privacy-related behavior, a topic which has been extensively examined in other settings (e.g., [2,11,55,60]).

A literature investigation shows that privacy calculus theory has been widely adopted to study users' privacy-related behavior, and it has been assumed that users' privacy-related behavior depends on both their privacy concerns and perceived benefits [11,60,71].<sup>1</sup> In particular, many previous papers examining users' privacy-related behavior have focused on the disclosure of accurate information. However, users are likely to engage in the privacy-related behavior of disclosing false in formation (abbreviated in our paper as misrepresentation behavior), which has received limited attention from academics [11,39,52,60,61,74].<sup>2</sup> Note that disclosing false information differs from the simple failure to disclose information, although both behaviors are privacy-related.<sup>3</sup> Moreover, it is evident that typical benefits in the context of social apps, including access to certain sorts of information and interaction with other fellows are conditional on users' provision of personal information, such as users' personal demographic information. Thus, instead of disclosing accurate information, disclosing false infor mation can help users alleviate their privacy concerns while enabling them to enjoy the benefits of using social apps, which is less possible if they were simple not to disclose any information. Our survey of 166 users of the Sina Weibo app shows that 65 of these users had provided false information to the app operator or other fellow users, while 150 of them believed that other users had provided false information.<sup>4</sup>

It has been well documented that users tend to disclose false infor mation in contemporary social interactions [6–9,19]. This claim is especially true in the context of certain social apps, sich as Sina Weibo, Twitter, and Tik Tok, on which users are not familiar with their fellow users. Moreover, these social apps rely on the rapid and generally un restricted formation of relationships among users, enabling users to succinctly share their thoughts with potentially vast audiences. Accordingly, users of these apps are highly likely to disclose false in formation to their audiences without incurring harsh penalties. How ever, such misrepresentation behavior has negative effects on both social app operators and social app users. On the one hand, it can in fluence the efficiency of the marketing strategies designed by social app operators. For example, most social app operators utilize their users' information to provide targeted advertising, and the collection of false information notably reduces the quality of targeted advertisements and thus that of personalized services [61]. In addition, the widespread dissemination of false information among users can reduce their eval uation of social apps and consequently their willingness to use them. In this sense, it is necessary for social app operators to discourage users from disclosing false information.

For these reasons, our paper investigates user misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. In particular, we construct a theoretical model based on the perspective of privacy calculus to examine the ways in which privacy concerns and perceived benefit impact user misrepresentation behavior. Our empirical analysis obtains the following results. First, we confirm that, in the context of certain social apps, privacy concerns motivate users to engage in misrepresen tation behavior. Second, we consider users' perceived benefits in terms of two dimensions, i.e., social rewards and personalized benefits.<sup>5</sup> Interestingly, our analysis shows that personalized benefits discourage users from disclosing false information; however, social rewards incentivize users to disclose false information. Third, we validate the interaction between privacy concerns and perceived benefits and find that privacy concerns negatively influence perceived benefits. That is, users with strong privacy concerns tend to discount the perceived ben efits of information disclosure. Fourth, we investigate four antecedents of privacy concerns and find that perceived privacy control and app permission sensitivity are significant antecedents of privacy concerns, while disposition to value privacy and perceived effectiveness of privacy policies are nonsignificant in this context. Finally, we conduct various mediation analyses to extend our basic model. These analyses show that social rewards and personalized benefits partially meditate the effect of privacy concerns on misrepresentation behavior and that the indirect effects are much smaller than the direct effects.

Our main theoretical contributions are threefold. First, we utilize privacy calculus theory to examine user misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. More importantly, we select certain social apps on which users are not intimately familiar with their fellow users as our research context, which enables us to study the prevalence of misrepresentation behavior among users. Second, considering the particular context of social apps, we explore the construct of perceived benefits in terms of two dimensions, i.e., social rewards and personalized benefits. Subsequently, we investigate and validate the contradictory impacts of these dimensions on user misrepresentation behavior. Third, we examine the interaction between privacy concerns and perceived benefits, which has received limited attention from privacy calculus theory. We find a negative correlation between the two constructs, indicating that users with strong privacy concerns discount the benefits of information disclosure.

Moreover, our research provides useful implications for practitioners with respect to decision-making. First, our findings suggest the preva lence of user misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. Thus, practitioners who request personal information from social app users to develop marketing strategies should be aware of the phenom enon of data falsification. Second, we discuss typical negative effects of user misrepresentation behavior and show that such behavior is aggra vated by users' privacy concerns and mitigated by users' personalized benefits. That is, it is imperative for social app operators to address misrepresentation behavior either by reducing users' privacy concerns or by increasing their personalized benefits. Third, we find that privacy concerns are largely the result of users' perceived app permission sensitivity and perceived privacy control. Therefore, social app opera tors should be more careful when requesting app permission and more active in providing users with a variety of user-controllable privacy customization options.

In the upcoming section, we review the relevant literature. Subse quently, we construct our theoretical model and develop corresponding hypotheses based on privacy calculus theory. The following sections document the design of our survey and the collection and analysis of the data, in which context we also present our main results. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical contributions of this research and conclude this paper.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Privacy calculus theory

Privacy calculus theory claims that users' behavior of disclosing private information depends on the trade-offs between privacy concerns and perceived benefits [1,18,21,37,42,64]. This theory has been widely examined in a variety of online contexts, such as e-commerce [60,70], mobile apps [60,70], social network services [18,65], and the sharing economy [66].

Although the characterization of privacy concerns is generally consistent throughout the literature, perceived benefits can vary significantly across different research settings [37,66]. In the context of social media, Sun et al. [64,65] categorized perceived benefits in terms of three dimensions: social rewards, utilitarian benefits and hedonic benefits.<sup>6</sup> To account for our specific research contexts, this paper uses social rewards and personalized benefits (representing utilitarian ben efits) to measure perceived benefits. We employ personalized benefits to represent utilitarian benefits since providing personalized services is the most effective way of motivating users to disclose their information [37,45]. Moreover, although previous research has identified various aspects of utilitarian benefits, personalization is often viewed as the most prominent [46,68]. Moreover, in line with both Zhao et al. [75] and Sun et al. [64], our theoretical model does not incorporate hedonic benefits for two reasons. First, hedonic benefits are based on users' in terest in the behavior itself; however, there is no evidence in the liter ature to support a clear relationship between hedonic benefits and misrepresentation behavior. Second, as suggested by Sun et al. [64], the consideration of hedonic benefits might lead to conceptual overlap among different dimensions of perceived benefits since hedonic benefits are likely to be the results of obtaining other perceived benefits (e.g., social rewards).

Social rewards, which are derived from companionship, respect and approval, can motivate users to disclose their private information [39,40,64]. These social rewards are becoming highly important as an increasing number of users share information and establish connections with each other via social media. Moreover, the use of social apps en ables users to interact with each other in unprecedented ways, such as through live streaming and location sharing, which in turn amplifies the role of social rewards. The operators of social apps are able to provide personalized services by utilizing information collected from their users [68]. Accordingly, people might be willing to disclose their private in formation in exchange for personalized services that match their indi vidual interests and preferences [37,71]. Thus, although personalization may raise users' privacy concerns, highly personalized services can motivate people to disclose their private information [41,75].

Our paper differs from those previously cited in the following ways. First, in accordance with the specific contexts of certain social apps, we construct the perceived benefits in terms of two dimensions, i.e., social rewards and personalized benefits: social rewards have received inadequate study in the literature. Second, we include privacy concerns and perceived benefits as interdependent variables and investigate their systematic impacts on user disclosure behavior; these factors have been typically modeled as independent variables in research based on privacy calculus theory. Third, we take into account the unique features of social apps and examine several antecedents of privacy concerns, which have been absent in the cited papers.

## 2.2. Privacy-protective behaviors on social media

In a landmark paper, Son & Kim [61] identified several typical privacy-protective behaviors, most notably including misrepresentation behavior.<sup>7</sup> Unlike conventional privacy calculus theory, which focuses on users' disclosure of accurate information (e.g., [23,40]), our paper focuses on users' disclosure of false information. Thus, our paper builds upon emerging studies that examine users' privacy-protective behaviors.

Recent works have examined privacy-protective behaviors in different settings. For example, Miltgen and Smith [52] found that consumers' trust in a firm is the most critical factor determining their privacy-protective behavior in terms of misrepresentation or refusal; Crossler & Belanger [20] investigated mobile users' privacy-protective behaviors based on self-efficacy theory. In the context of social media, previous studies have explored how users' privacy preferences influence their privacy-protective settings [17], how they control and manage their information boundaries [48] and how they protect their privacy by utilizing specific features of social apps [49].

Although misrepresentation behavior is prevalent on social media, especially in the context of social apps, on which users have great freedom to share, post and edit their personal information (2008; [40]), the impacts of such behavior have never been adequately investigated. Thus, we take social apps as our research context and utilize privacy calculus theory to examine user misrepresentation behavior. Our paper is mostly related to Jiang et al. [39], who also examined misrepresen tation behavior in online social contexts. However, our paper differs from this research in several ways. We explore users' perceived benefits in terms of two dimensions, i.e., social rewards and personalized bene fits. This approach allows us to explore the different impacts of various dimensions of perceived benefits on misrepresentation behavior. Moreover, we select Sina Weibo as the social app to be studied, which is an app on which users are not intimately familiar with their fellow users, a distinction from most other social apps. Such special characteristics enable us to explore unconventional relationships between social re wards and misrepresentation behavior. Additionally, we examine the interdependence between privacy concerns and perceived benefits and identify typical antecedents of privacy concerns in the context of social apps; neither of these tasks were accomplished in the paper by Jiang et al. [39].

## 3. Research model and hypothesis

Our research model is shown in Fig. 1 below. We utilize privacy calculus theory to examine how privacy concerns and perceived benefits (i.e., social rewards and personalized benefits) influence user misrep resentation behavior on social apps. We also study the interaction be tween privacy concerns and perceived benefits in an attempt to examine how privacy concerns impact perceived benefits. Additionally, we explore four typical antecedents of users' privacy concerns in the context of social apps. In the following, we develop the corresponding hypoth eses proposed by this paper.

## 3.1. Privacy calculus and misrepresentation

Privacy calculus theory claims that people's behavior of disclosing information depends on the trade-offs between privacy concerns and perceived benefits [21,37]. We utilize this theory to analyze user misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. In particular, in our paper, privacy concerns are taken to indicate the extent to which a social app user is concerned about the flow of his or her personal in formation, including the transfer and exchange of that information [53,58].

Miltgen & Smith [52] highlight two primary choices when people are required to provide personal information: disclosing accurate informa tion or disclosing false information $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the misrepresentation behavior discussed in our paper). As suggested by privacy calculus theory, peo ple's disclosure behavior should be driven by privacy concerns. A large body of literature has investigated the ways in which privacy concerns impact people's choice to disclose accurate information. However, little research has examined the ways in which privacy concerns impact people's choice to disclose false information [39,60,61]. In conventional social interactions with colleagues, friends, and families, significant penalties might be incurred if false information is disclosed [9]. Namely, on the one hand, continuous efforts must be made to safeguard previous lies [19] and, on the other hand, unacceptable outcomes can occur when previous lies are revealed [39]. Therefore, it is not easy for people to adopt misrepresentation behavior in such social contexts [39]. In contrast, on certain social apps, such as Sina Weibo and Twitter, on which users are not intimately familiar with their fellow users, these penalties for misrepresentation behavior are fairly low or even negli gible. As such, people are more likely to disclose false information in the context of these social apps.

Moreover, it is highly likely for users to engage in misrepresentation behavior as an important form of privacy-protective behavior when addressing privacy concerns [52,61]. This claim is especially true in the context of social apps, which usually allow the operator of the social apps to collect massive amounts of users' personal information and potentially to distribute this information for certain purposes. For example, most operators of social apps share their users' information with other companies to provide targeted advertising. In addition, when posted on social apps, users' personal information might be intentionally misused by their fellow users [18,40]. Accordingly, given strong privacy concerns, social app users are likely to disclose false information. Thus, we hypothesize that the stronger a user's privacy concerns when using social apps are, the more likely this user is to disclose false information, as proposed by H1 below<sup>8</sup>:

H1. Privacy concerns are positively associated with misrepresentation behavior.

In addition to privacy concerns, perceived benefits affect people's information disclosure behaviors according to privacy calculus theory. It has been widely acknowledged that perceived benefits impact people's choice to disclose accurate information positively, while limited infor mation has been found concerning the ways in which perceived benefits impact people's choice to disclose false information. Considering the specific features of social apps, we explore users' perceived benefits in terms of two dimensions, i.e., social rewards and personalized benefits. Although both dimensions present typical incentives for users to disclose information, their roles might differ substantially [64], as discussed below.

Personalized benefits refer to the gains that individuals derive from content on social apps that is tailored to themselves based on their preferences and behaviors [75]. One of the most attractive functions of social apps is the provision of personalized services, for example, in dications of people nearby or people you might know and news updates that match your interests. The development of personalized services is based on users' personal profiles, such as their identity information, instant location and search history. Thus, it is expected that the perceived benefits of such personalized services motivate users not to disclose false information [65]. Namely, misrepresentation behavior can reduce users' obtained benefits by leading to less-relevant or lower quality personalized services [61].

Accordingly, we hypothesize that the better the personalized benefits of using social apps are, the lower the possibility of a user disclosing false information, as proposed in H2a below:

H2a. Personalized benefits are negatively associated with misrepre sentation behavior.

In our paper, social rewards refer to the gains that individuals derive from companionship, respect, and approval in the context of interper sonal interactions via social apps [39]. Previous research has confirmed that people might disclose false information to obtain social rewards; for example, social app users may disclose false information regarding their identity, appearance, and experiences to gain approval and respect from their fellows, i.e., to improve the impressions they make [7]. Note that this conclusion likely pertains to certain social apps, e.g., Sina Weibo, Twitter, and Tik Tok, rather than others, such as Facebook, WeChat and LinkedIn. Namely, users of these social apps engage in short-term in teractions; thus, the disclosure of false information is less likely to be revealed by fellow users. Equivalently, it is much less risky to lie to strangers than to acquaintances, given that it is extremely challenging for the operators of social apps to regulate users' disclosure of false in formation [9,39].

Therefore, in our specific context of social apps, we argue that people are likely to disclose false information to obtain social rewards. The more that users value the social rewards gained through the use of social apps, the higher the possibility of a user disclosing false information, as in proposed in H2b below:

H2b. Social rewards are positively associated with misrepresentation behavior.

Conventionally, privacy calculus theory investigates information disclosure behaviors by assuming the independence of users' privacy concerns and perceived benefits $[ 2 4 , 4 4 , 7 1 ]$ ]. Recently, it has been shown that privacy concerns and perceived benefits should be considered interdependently [25,42,64]. For example, Kehr et al. [42] hypothesized that the perceived risks of information disclosure are negatively asso ciated with the perceived benefits of information disclosure. Thus, we presume that social app users tend to discount perceived benefits as their privacy concerns increase, i.e., an increase in privacy concerns decreases users' perceived social rewards and personalized benefits. The underly ing rationale for this effect, on the one hand, is that individuals are typically risk-averse and thus discount benefits as losses increase [42,67]. On the other hand, the literature on consumer behavior research has reported that individuals tend to think that losses and benefits are negatively correlated even though they are often positively correlated in reality [59].

Accordingly, we argue that the more social app users are concerned about their privacy, the fewer benefits they perceive from information disclosure. Thus, there should be a negative correlation between privacy concerns and perceived benefits in the context of social apps, as pro posed in H3a and H3b below:

H3a. Privacy concerns are negatively associated with social rewards.

H3b. Privacy concerns are negatively associated with personalized benefits.

## 3.2. Antecedents of privacy concerns

Given the important role played by privacy concerns in the context of social apps, we propose four typical antecedents of privacy concerns after considering the unique features of social apps, as discussed below.

Perceived privacy control refers to individuals' beliefs regarding their ability to manage the disclosure and use of their private informa tion [70]. It has been well documented that perceived privacy control is one of the most important factors influencing users' privacy concerns [14,37,68]. Perceived privacy control can help alleviate users' privacy concerns, especially in online contexts that feature serious information asymmetry between users and businesses regarding the collection, protection and use of private information. Namely, users' control over their private information can significantly restrain businesses' opportu nistic behavior [50].

Users of social apps are offered various options for their privacy control [12,40]. For example, they are allowed to designate the content and time span of posted information for publicization [49]. In addition, they can determine whether their information is collected and how their information is used through the provision of certain options, such as optout. Given these mechanisms underlying the dissemination of private information, social app users should perceive a certain degree of privacy control. Moreover, the more privacy control social app users perceive, the weaker their privacy concerns. Thus, there should be a negative correlation between perceived privacy control and privacy concerns in the context of social apps, as proposed in H4a below:

H4a. Perceived privacy control is negatively associated with privacy concerns.

It is evident that privacy concerns vary significantly not only in specific contexts but also across different individuals $[ 1 , 2 0 , 4 1 , 4 2 , 7 0 ]$ Xu et al. [70] proposed using an individual's disposition to value privacy to measure that individual's privacy concerns in specific contexts. Particularly, disposition to value privacy reflects the degree to which an individual values his or her private information and indicates an in dividual's general tendency to protect his or her private information or to restrain the disclosure of such private information [30,41,70].

We argue that users of social apps might have different dispositions to value privacy, as in other social media contexts [30,42,70]. Some such users might value their private information highly and thus be more likely to protect their privacy or less willing to disclose their pri vate information. That is, they are more concerned about their privacy. In contrast, other users might value their private information less and thus be less concerned about privacy. Accordingly, there should be a positive correlation between disposition to value privacy and privacy concerns in the context of social apps, as proposed in H4b:

H4b. Disposition to value privacy is positively associated with privacy concerns.

Given the different types of information that can be disclosed, people have different levels of sensitivity regarding privacy concerns, a phe nomenon which is known as information sensitivity [25,30,50]. People have greater privacy concerns when they are required to disclose more sensitive information. In general, people are more sensitive to financial, medical and personal identity-related information, and less sensitive to lifestyle, demographic and purchasing information [50].

With users' permission, social app operators normally collect various types of sensitive information, for example, device parameters, geographical location, photos, and videos. However, it is challenging for users to monitor the ways in which such information is processed [22,68]. Thus, similar to the effect of information sensitivity on privacy concerns, social app users with high sensitivity to app permission should have higher levels of privacy concerns [22,30]. Thus, there should be a positive correlation between perceived app permission sensitivity and privacy concerns in the context of social apps, as proposed in H4c:

H4c. Perceived app permission sensitivity is positively associated with privacy concerns.

Previous research has demonstrated that users' privacy concerns are directly affected by businesses' privacy policies [37,51,72]. In practice, social app operators commonly provide privacy policies to describe the ways in which they collect, utilize and protect users' private information [70]. Privacy policies can help increase users' trust in businesses [29,69], which consequently reduces users' privacy concerns [63].

The impact of privacy policies depends on users' perceived effec tiveness of such policies, which refers to the extent to which users believe that the privacy policies in question provide accurate and reli able information regarding businesses' information protection practices [70]. Thus, the more effective users perceive the privacy policies to be, the lower their privacy concerns. Accordingly, there should be a nega tive correlation between the perceived effectiveness of privacy policies and privacy concerns in the context of social apps, as proposed in H4d:

H4d. Perceived effectiveness of privacy policies is negatively associ ated with privacy concerns.

## 3.3. Control variables

This research controls for several typical variables that may influ ence user misrepresentation behavior. In line with previous studies, our control variables include gender, age, level of education and internet use experience. In addition, we include individuals' moral beliefs regarding misrepresentation behavior as a control variable. Namely, if people believe that it is unethical to provide false information, they are less inclined to do so in the context of social apps [39].

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Research context

We select a particular social app, Sina Weibo, to empirically test our theoretical model. Unlike typical social apps, such as Facebook, WeChat, and LinkedIn, Sina Weibo relies on the rapid and generally unrestricted formation of relationships among users who are not intimately familiar with each other, thus enabling users to provide false information to vast audiences. This unusual relationship among Sina Weibo users allows us to investigate both misrepresentation behavior and the unique effects of certain constructs, e.g., social rewards, on misrepresentation behavior.

Moreover, we select the context of the Sina Weibo app for three other reasons. First, the Sina Weibo app is a leading social app in China, with 224 million daily active users as of September 2020. Its users can freely create, obtain and spread information in real time based on their social connections [36,73]. Second, it is evident that user misrepresentation behavior is prevalent on the Sina Weibo app. The wide dissemination of false information has become a serious issue on the Sina Weibo app.<sup>9</sup> Third, for the Sina Weibo app, the provision of personalized services, such as personalized advertisements and news, has become a core marketing value. Meanwhile, this business model also entails seriou privacy concerns due to its wide collection of personal information [36].

## 4.2. Measurement

All variables are measured as latent, reflective constructs that are captured indirectly with direct measurement items. All items measuring the constructs used in this study are adopted from existing measures, but they are adapted in minor ways to suit the purposes of the current study.<sup>10</sup> Moreover, the items are assessed on a seven-point Likert scale anchored by 1 = strongly disagree, 4 = neutral, and 7 = strongly agree.

We provide the details for all measurement items as well as their sources in the supplementary appendix. Privacy concerns use four typical measures adapted from Gu et al. [30] and Xu et al. [70]. The three measures used for personalized benefits are adapted from Xu et al. [71] and Wang et al. [68]. Social rewards and misrepresentation behavior use four and three measures adapted from Sun et al. [64] and Jiang et al. [39], respectively. Regarding antecedents of privacy con cerns, perceived privacy control is associated with four measures adapted from Xu et al. [70] and Dinev et al. [25]. Both disposition to value privacy and perceived effectiveness of privacy policies use three measures adapted from Xu et al. [70]. Perceived app permission sensi tivity uses four measures adapted from Gu et al. [30] and Harborth and Pape [34]. The model controls for typical variables associated with so cial media, including gender, age, level of education, internet use experience and moral beliefs regarding misrepresentation behavior.

The preliminary instrument was first pilot tested to ensure its comprehensiveness and clarity. Subsequently, the instruments' validity was assessed by an expert panel included three experienced information system researchers and six master's students. Third, after a small-scale pretest with 22 respondents was performed, the survey instrument was pretested in a large-scale study with 100 respondents to check the psychometric properties of the measurement scales. Following these pretests, the measurement instrument was shortened, refined, and validated for its statistical properties.

## 4.3. Survey administration

The research model was empirically tested using data collected from active users of the Sina Weibo app. For this purpose, we developed an online survey and distributed it via Credamo.com, one of the most famous online platforms in China to provide online survey services. A brief introduction was presented at the beginning of our survey to inform the respondents of the study's context. Moreover, to reduce po tential bias regarding self-selection among respondents, the aim of the survey was not revealed to respondents. To ensure that respondents answered the questions honestly, anonymity and confidentiality were emphasized.

Credamo has been widely used by scholars to distribute surveys due to its effectiveness with respect to data collection $[ 6 2 , 7 6 ] . ^ { 1 1 }$ First, Cre damo offers appropriate mechanisms to improve participant quality. Credamo sends survey links only to registered members who have pre viously been qualified. Moreover, Credamo grades its registered mem bers according to their history of participation<sup>,</sup> including their rates of adoption and test times. These instruments allow researchers to screen ideal participants for their purposes. Second, Credamo provides various ways of controlling the surveying process. For example, Credamo en sures the quality of surveying by embedding check questions, and enlisted participants are rewarded only after they pass the check. In addition, Credamo prevents participants from exhibiting repeat re sponses by monitoring their IP addresses and detects intelligent behavior by requiring a captcha.

A total of 465 subjects participated in the survey, and 336 responses remained usable following the screening process. We first deleted questionnaires from participants who had no experience using the Sina Weibo app. Subsequently, we deleted questionnaires answered in under 4 min (this time baseline was validated through a pretest). Third, we deleted suspected malicious questionnaires (e.g., those that contained conflicting answers). The detailed demographic characteristics of these 336 participants are shown in Table 1. Nonresponse bias was assessed by verifying that the respondents' demographics were similar to those of typical Sina Weibo app users as reported by online commercial sites.<sup>12</sup>

## 5. Data analysis and results

We used the partial least squares (PLS) method for measurement validation and to test the structural model. PLS employs a componentbased approach rather than a covariance-based approach, for estima tion. It makes minimal assumptions regarding measurement scales, sample size, and residual distributions [15]. Thus, PLS is best suited to test complex relationships by avoiding inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy [16]. Hence, we chose PLS to accommodate the presence of a large number of variables and relationships in this paper.

Discriminant and convergent validity were tested using the following five rules. First, the correlations among all constructs were well below the 0.90 threshold, as shown in Table 3, suggesting that all constructs were distinct from one another. Second, the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct was larger than other cross correlations [27], as shown in Table 3. This result demonstrates the adequate discriminant validity of our measurement model. Third, principal component factor analysis was conducted, in which context all items loaded on their respective constructs, which were much higher than all cross loadings, as shown in Table 4. Fourth, the heterotrait monotrait (HTMT) ratio values, i.e., the values above the diagonal as shown in Table 3, were much <0.85 [35]. Fifth, the AVEs of all con structs were well above 0.5, ranging from 0.627 to 0.91, thus indicating adequate convergent validity [27]. Taken together, these five tests suggest adequate discriminant and convergent validity.

Common method bias (CMB) was a potential problem for this survey, since the data were self-reported. In this paper, the extent of CMB was assessed using the following three typical tests.

First, according to the suggestions of Podsakoff et al. [56], we con ducted Harman's single factor test to test for common method variance. If common method variance were a serious problem for the study, we would expect a single factor to emerge or one general factor to account for most of the covariance among measures [56]. We conducted an exploratory factor analysis by loading all items on a single factor. The first factor accounted for only 32.6% of the variance, indicating a lack of substantial CMB.

Second, we compared the variances explained by both the substan tive factors and a method factor. Specifically, following the guidelines by Liang et al. [47], we found that the substantive factors explained nearly 74.5% of the total variance, while the method factor explained only 1.7% of the variance. In addition, most method factor loadings were not significant. Given the small magnitude and non-significance of method variance (see Table 5), we contend that CMB is unlikely to be a serious concern for this study.

Third, the correlation matrix (as shown in Table 3) did not indicate any highly correlated factors (the highest correlation was $r = 0 . 7 2 ) $ whereas evidence of CMB should have resulted in extremely high cor relations $( r > 0 . 9 0 )$

## 5.2. Structural model

We conducted bootstrapping analysis with 5000 subsamples at the 5% significance level to test the path. The standardized PLS path co efficients used to test the structural model are shown in Fig. 2. Addi tionally, for ease of exposition, only significant control variables are shown in Fig. 2. The quality of our structural model was evaluated based on R-squares. We also tested for potential multicollinearity among constructs by conducting variance inflation factor (VIF) tests. The result showed that the VIFs were all acceptable (with values ranging from 1.30 to 2.20), suggesting that multicollinearity is not a notable issue for this study [32].

First, as hypothesized, privacy concerns and perceived benefits strongly influence misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. In particular, privacy concerns have a significant positive impact on misrepresentation behavior $( \beta = 0 . 3 2 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus supporting H1. Personalized benefits have a significant negative impact on misrepresentation behavior $( \beta = - 0 . 1 4 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 5 )$ , thus supporting H2a. Meanwhile, social rewards have a significant positive impact on misrepresentation behavior $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 9 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus supporting H2b. It is important to note the high degree of explained variance in the misrepresentation behavior highlighted by our model, i.e., $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 5 6 . 3 \%$

Second, as hypothesized, privacy concerns and perceived benefits interact with each other, i.e., users tend to discount perceived benefits more strongly when their privacy concerns increase. In particular, pri vacy concerns have a significant negative impact on both personalized benefits $( \beta = - 0 . 3 1 4 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and social rewards $( \beta = - 0 . 4 1 4 , \rho <$ 0.001), thus supporting H3a and H3b.

In terms of the four antecedents, perceived privacy control and perceived app permission sensitivity strongly influence privacy con cerns. In particular, perceived privacy control has a significant negative impact on privacy concerns $( \beta = - 0 . 4 2 , \rho < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus validating H4a.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key findings

This paper has several key findings that are validated by our various empirical analyses. First, we explore misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps, which is influenced by privacy concerns and perceived benefits. Moreover, users' privacy concerns motivate them to engage in misrepresentation behavior, while their perceived personal ized benefits discourage them from engaging in misrepresentation behavior. More interestingly, our results reveal the positive relationship between social rewards and misrepresentation behavior. These findings suggest that personalized benefits and social rewards, as two dimensions of perceived benefits, affect users' disclosure behavior in different manners, i.e., disclosing false information can attenuate personalized benefits while enhancing social rewards.

Second, we confirm the interdependence between users' perceived benefits and privacy concerns in the context of social apps; that is, privacy concerns are negatively associated with perceived benefits and consumers tend to discount perceived benefits as privacy concerns in crease. This finding highlights the increasing importance of privacy concerns in the context of social apps, contradicting previous papers that have reported that users' perceived benefits outweigh their privacy concerns [28,37,38]. These results also indicate that users' privacy concerns not only influence users' misrepresentation behavior directly but also cause them to discount the perceived benefits, thus influencing their misrepresentation behavior indirectly.

Third, we show that users' privacy concerns in the context of social apps are largely determined by perceived privacy control and app permission sensitivity. However, effectiveness of privacy policies perceived by users does not influence their privacy concerns signifi cantly. This finding is in line with the conclusions of the previous literature; for example, Aïmeur et al. [3] argued that privacy policy is not effective because it generally favors businesses rather than users. It is also noteworthy that although users' disposition to value privacy in fluences their privacy concerns, this relationship is not significant (p = 0.054). This finding is consistent with Xu et al. [72], who noted that, instead of general privacy attitudes, context-specific privacy attitudes are better suited to determining people's behavior.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

First, this study enriches the literature on user information disclosure behavior. Although academics have focused on the behavior of disclosing accurate information [52,60], disclosing false information is prevalent in practice, especially the context of certain social apps on which users are not intimately familiar with each other. To address this research gap, we investigate users' behavior of disclosing false infor mation in the context of social apps. Particularly, we use privacy cal culus theory to explore the ways in which social app users' privacy concerns and perceived benefits impact their behavior of disclosing false information.

Second, our discussion of privacy calculus theory contributes to the related literature. Most studies in this field have considered the effects of privacy concerns and perceived benefits independently. Meanwhile, few studies have examined their interaction effects, and those that have done so have reported that users' perceived benefits can alleviate their privacy concerns [28,37,38]. In contrast with previous studies, we find that users' privacy concerns affect their perceived benefits negatively. Thus, when faced with information disclosure decisions, users with higher privacy concerns are more likely to discount the perceived benefits of this disclosure behavior. This result is in line with endowment theory, suggesting that people tend to be more appreciative of what they already possess $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ information privacy); thus, higher levels of privacy con cerns can lead to lower levels of perceived benefits.

Third, our paper highlights the necessity of investigating the theo retical role of social rewards while taking into account distinct research contexts. Our results show that social rewards in the context of certain social apps motivate users to disclose false information to obtain respect, approval, and so on, differing from the extant literature, which has re ported that social rewards motivate users to disclose accurate informa tion [40,65]. Such a remarkable difference is caused by the different costs of misrepresentation behavior between the traditional social in teractions considered by previous papers and the context of social apps discussed in our paper. Particularly, in the context of traditional social interactions, it is not easy for people to engage in misrepresentation behavior due to the unacceptable costs of doing so [39]. However, in the context of certain social apps, the costs of misrepresentation behavior are fairly low or even negligible since such behavior is much less likely to be revealed.

Finally, our study contributes to protection motivation theory, which is regularly employed to examine users' protective behaviors related to privacy and security [4]. In particular, this theory has been utilized to investigate users' information disclosure behavior in social media con texts [54,57]. Unlike the papers in this stream of research, our paper conceptualizes and examines user misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. While this behavior has been widely observed in the context of social apps, it has received limited attention from the perspective of protection motivation theory. Moreover, although pri vacy calculus theory is used in this paper, our findings support certain important assumptions drawn from protection motivation theory. For example, we find that user misrepresentation behavior is determined by privacy concerns, which is consistent with the claim of protection motivation theory that negative threat appraisals (e.g., the privacy concerns discussed in our paper) determine users' protective behaviors [23].

## 6.3. Implications for practice

Our research validates the widespread phenomenon of users disclosing false information in the context of social apps. This behavior is especially prevalent for certain social apps, such as Sina Weibo, Twitter, and Tik Tok, on which users are not familiar with their fellow users. Such undesirable behavior largely influences the effectiveness of the marketing strategies designed by the social app operator. For example, the collection of false information definitely reduces the effectiveness of targeted advertisements and customized services [61]. In addition, the widespread dissemination of false information among users affects their evaluation of social apps and consequently their willingness to use them. In this sense, it is critical for social app opera tors to discourage users from disclosing false information.

Moreover, our research implies that social app operators can moti vate users not to disclose false information by alleviating their privacy concerns. Thus, social app operators should be more careful when requesting app permission and more active in providing users with a variety of user-controllable options for privacy customization. Mean while, our study indicates the ineffectiveness of current privacy policies with respect to alleviating users' privacy concerns. A reason for this ineffectiveness might be that existing privacy policies are often too long and difficult to understand and are thus easily ignored by users. Thus, it is urgent for social app operators to improve the effectiveness of their privacy policies. For example, privacy policies can be distributed using more vivid and attractive media rather than via lengthy and boring texts.

## 7. Conclusion

The phenomenon of user misrepresentation behavior has been widely observed in social media contexts, especially in the context of certain social apps on which users are not intimately familiar with their fellow users; however, it has received limited attention from academics. Accordingly, our paper investigates user misrepresentation behavior in the context of social apps. In particular, we construct a theoretical model based on the perspective of privacy calculus theory to examine how users' privacy concerns and perceived benefits impact their misrepre sentation behavior. Our empirical analysis shows that both privacy concerns and perceived social rewards motivate users to engage in misrepresentation behavior, while users' perceived personalized benefits discourage them from exhibiting such behavior. Moreover, we confirm the interaction of privacy concerns and perceived benefits; that is, users' privacy concerns affect their perceived benefits negatively. We also find that perceived privacy control and app permission sensitivity are two substantial factors that influence users' privacy concerns, while dispo sition to value privacy and perceived effectiveness of privacy policies do not significantly influence users' privacy concerns.

This paper has several limitations. First, our research contexts focus on certain social apps on which users are not intimately familiar with their fellow users, which causes the user misrepresentation behavior under study to be prevalent. Thus, it is worthwhile to examine user misrepresentation behavior on other types of social apps. Second, we highlight two dimensions of perceived benefits, i.e., personalized ben efits and social rewards, and validate their adverse effects on user misrepresentation behavior. Future research can develop other di mensions of perceived benefits in the context of social apps to explore additional interesting results. Third, in this paper, we consider four antecedents of privacy concerns, and additional antecedents can be evaluated in the future, especially those regarding personality, social norms and macro culture. These questions are challenging but worth exploration in the future.
