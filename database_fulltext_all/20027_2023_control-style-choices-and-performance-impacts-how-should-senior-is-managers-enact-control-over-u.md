# Control-style choices and performance impacts: How should senior IS managers enact control over uncertain IS projects?

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IS project uncertainty Control enactment Control-style choices Senior IS managers Process performance Product performance

## A B S T R A C T

Information systems (IS) projects are notoriously difficult to control, especially under conditions of uncertainty. This difficulty is particularly pronounced for senior IS managers, such as CIOs and IT Vice Presidents, who tend to have scarce time and limited project-related knowledge but are ultimately held accountable for IS project performance. Focusing on this under-researched controller category, the study at hand contributes new insights into the enactment of controls by exploring how IS project uncertainty affects senior IS managers’ control-style choices, as well as how it moderates the impact of such choices on process and product performance. Based on a survey of 150 senior IS managers, we find that IS project uncertainty increases managers’ use of an authoritative control style but is unrelated to their use of an enabling control style. Further, in IS projects characterized by uncertainty, an authoritative control style is found to be effective for process performance, whereas an enabling style is found to be effective for product performance. Moreover, the results of a post-hoc analysis show that using the two control styles simultaneously under uncertainty delivers no discernible benefits, suggesting a decision-related control dilemma. Theoretical and practical implications are discussed

## 1. Introduction

The control of information systems (IS) projects is a key activity for the effective deployment of information technology (IT) resources and ultimately for IT-enabled value creation [8,71,76]. However, even in the best of times, IS projects are notoriously difficult to control [e.g., 48], and especially so in situations of high uncertainty [e.g., 14,29], such as when the overall project task is novel and non-routine, and project team experience with similar tasks or technologies is absent [68,69]. In the IS and related literature, control is broadly defined as any attempt to align individual behaviors with organizational objectives [6,41,60]. Much of the focus within IS project control research has traditionally been ori ented toward control modes [e.g., 22,41]. More recently, this research focus has been extended to also consider how controls are enacted in terms of control styles, as articulated in the Integrated IS Project Control Theory [76], which “offer[s] a new way forward for studies on the control of IS projects” [65, p. 134]. Despite these theoretical advances, it remains unclear whether the existence of uncertainty in an IS project corresponds with managers increasingly adopting an authoritative control style (i.e., a top-down style with limited controller-controllee interaction) or an enabling control style (i.e., a collaborative style with frequent controller-controllee interaction) [50,65,76]. This is of particular relevance because managers’ control-style choices have been found to be more important than their control-mode choices in explaining the performance effects of control activities [65]; and because IS project uncertainty presents increased chances of project failure and these ‘higher stakes’ may induce managers to make poor decisions, contributing to detrimental performance impacts [76].

In this study, we set out to test key elements of the Integrated IS Project Control Theory in the specific context of senior IS managers enacting control over IS projects, and to extend this theory to account for the role of IS project uncertainty. In particular, we are interested in how project uncertainty influences senior IS managers’ control-style choices and how such choices correspond with IS project performance. Although uncertainty places increased demands on the (core) project team as well, the role of senior IS managers is critical in navigating the challenge by overseeing IS project activities in order to ensure value creation and alignment with business needs [25,35,49,52]. In this regard, prior research has found that senior IS managers are likely to approach the control of an IS project differently than project managers [35], for example, by being more prone to using an authoritative control style [2,76]. Senior managers’ preference for this control style is at least partly due to power asymmetries (vis-a-vis \` IS project managers) and severe time constraints for control activities due to competing demands [51]. Still, earlier studies also tell us that an enabling control style has clear advantages over an authoritative control style in terms of project outcomes [2,65], which suggests a decision-related control dilemma for senior IS managers. To address this dilemma, our study examines two research questions: (1) How does IS project uncertainty influence senior IS managers’ control-style choices? (2) How does it moderate the impact of control styles on the performance of IS projects?

To answer these questions, we collected survey data from 150 senior IS managers (including CIOs and IT Vice Presidents) regarding their experiences related to a recently completed IS project, where they were actively involved in control activities in the role of project sponsor and/ or as a member of the project steering committee. The results of our study extend existing research by offering empirical evidence that high levels of IS project uncertainty prompt senior IS managers to use an authoritative control style, while not significantly affecting their use of an enabling control style. Further, we find that, under high uncertainty, an authoritative control style is positively associated with process (but not product) performance, whereas an enabling control style is posi tively associated with product (but not process) performance. In addi tion, our results indicate that using the two control styles simultaneously in uncertain IS projects delivers no discernible benefits. This implie that, under conditions of uncertainty, senior IS managers must make ‘tough’ decisions and choose between the two styles and their con trasting performance impacts.

## 2. Research background

## 2.1. IS project control, performance, and uncertainty

The core motivation of employing IS project controls is to achieve performance benefits [10]. In this regard, extant research suggests that, based on relevant context factors, controllers choose to implement a portfolio of controls, which influences controllee behavior and con tributes to project performance [10,76]. Studies looking into the per formance effects of IS project control activities commonly distinguish between two major dimensions of project performance: process and product performance [e.g., 22,39,57]. The former refers to efficiencyrelated aspects of the project process (i.e., the extent to which an IS project was delivered on time and within budget), whereas the latter refers to quality-related aspects of the project outcome [69,75].

To analyze the performance effects of IS project controls, existing studies typically rely on the control-mode framework [e.g., 41,60], which groups controls into formal modes (input, behavior, and outcome control), oriented around resource allocation and performance evalua tion, and informal modes (clan and self-control), focusing on social norms and intrinsic motivation. Although corresponding studies do report positive control effects on both process performance $[ \mathbf { e } . \mathbf { g } . , 2 2 , 3 9 ]$ and product performance [e.g., 22,57], as well as on IS project perfor mance in general $[ \mathbf { e } . g . , 7 2 , 7 8 ]$ , some of these studies have also found certain control modes to have no performance effect, or even a negative effect [22,72]. In attempting to reconcile why some controls are more effective than others, Wiener et al. [76] draw on a comprehensive re view of the IS project control literature to conclude that whereas control modes help clarify what control activities are employed, they stop short of explaining how controls are enacted. On this basis, they propose an expanded theoretical framework, later renamed the Integrated IS Project Control Theory [65], which includes both control modes (what) and control styles (how) [23,24,35].

Wiener et al. [76] define control style as “the manner in which the interaction between the controller and the controllee is conducted” (p.

755) and distinguish between two basic and contrasting control styles: authoritative and enabling. While an enabling control style relies on close and frequent controller-controllee interaction, an authoritative control style draws on a bureaucratic, top-down management approach [23,24]. More specifically, two key features distinguish an enabling from an authoritative control style: repair and transparency [1,76]. Repair refers to the anticipation of control-activity breakdowns and the capability of fixing them. In an enabling control style, repair is viewed positively by welcoming controllee feedback, whereas in an authoritative control style, deviations from controller prescriptions are generally considered as a negative. The second feature, transparency, refers to the visibility of control activities and the overall project context. For instance, using an enabling control style, controllers seek to provide controllees with an understanding of the rationale behind enacted controls. In contrast, using an authoritative control style, controllers focus on formulating controllee instructions and duties and communicating them in a unilateral way.<sup>1</sup> While the importance and explanatory power of control styles have been recognized in prior IS project control studies [23,65], less is known about their antecedents [76]. Here, Wiener et al. [76] suggest that the con troller’s use of a particular control style (i.e., authoritative or enabling) depends on power asymmetries between controller and controllee, stemming from differences in formal authority and project-related knowledge. This conjecture is consistent with the observation that se nior IS managers (high formal authority, but limited project knowledge) and IS project managers (high project knowledge) enact IS project con trols differently (i.e., use different control styles) [35].

Moreover, Wiener et al. [76] identify the level of uncertainty sur rounding the IS project task (in a broad sense) as a key context factor influencing controllers’ control choices. This is in line with extant IS project control research, which points to uncertainty as a key antecedent of control choices [68,74], and a key factor that moderates the perfor mance effects of such choices [e.g., 29,47,69]. For instance, studying the control tactics used by IS project managers, Rustagi et al. [68] find that the amount of formal control increases with the level of uncertainty associated with the IS project task; and Sakka et al. [69] show that managers’ interactive control use—which shares key characteristics with the use of an enabling control style—enhances IS project perfor mance when uncertainty is high, but deteriorates performance when uncertainty is low.

The uncertainty concept was originally defined in the information processing literature as “the difference between the information an or ganization has and the information it needs” [69, p. 511]. Consistent with this broad definition, prior IS project control studies refer to various types of uncertainty, which can be classified along two di mensions: uncertainty areas and sources [cf. 3,11]. The former concerns the specific area or ‘object’ that is uncertain, such as the project task [e. $\mathbf { g } _ { \cdot } , 6 8 , 6 9 ]$ , the project’s internal or external environment $[ \mathbf { e } . \mathbf { g } . , 3 2 , 7 4 ]$ or the market served by a project [e.g., 29]; whereas the latter pertains to factors driving uncertainty, such as environmental/market change [e. g., 29,74], complexity [e.g., 15,32], or novelty [e.g., 3,69].

In the study at hand, we focus specifically on the uncertainty sur rounding the overall project task (uncertainty area) and resulting from project novelty (uncertainty source). We do so because novelty-induced project (task) uncertainty arguably ‘forces’ senior IS managers to take on a more active and/or directive role in IS projects. Among other things, this can be explained by project novelty increasing the risk of decision paralysis at the project-team level [54] and exacerbating accountability issues at the senior-manager level [51,61]. Against this backdrop, we draw on Rustagi et al. [68] and Sakka et al. [69] to define (novelty-induced) IS project uncertainty as the degree to which the specific requirements and process of the overall IS project task are novel to project participants and thus cannot be anticipated or forecast. In line with this definition, high levels of IS project uncertainty may, for example, result from a lack of prior experience with the technologie used in a given IS project (e.g., use of a new software development platform) or a lack of established procedures and practices that can be followed to perform the project.

## 2.2. The critical role of senior IS managers

Most existing IS project control studies focus on the control rela tionship between IS project managers and team members [e.g., $^ { 2 4 , 4 1 , 6 5 , 7 2 ] }$ . Although these studies focus on an important controllercontrollee dyad that exists within a project, past commentators have noted the relevance of other controller groups, including senior IS managers, who play a critical role in exercising control over IS project [e.g., 35,55]. Corresponding studies highlight that senior IS managers fulfill an important directive role, which includes overseeing and con trolling IS project activities in order to ensure IT-enabled value creation in alignment with business needs [25,35,49,52]. For instance, serving on the steering committee of an IS project, senior IS managers are tasked with articulating the project vision, defining the project scope, cost, and schedule, as well as with monitoring progress and exercising control over the IS project manager [e.g., 49,51,61].

Extant research has also established that the control of IS projects poses unique challenges to senior IS managers, especially when compared to IS project managers. One such challenge pertains to a lack of relevant knowledge [35]; that is, while senior IS managers are less likely to be experts in the project domains and tasks they oversee, they must exercise project ownership [51] and are ultimately accountable for overall project performance [61]. A related challenge concerns senior IS managers broad range of organizational responsibilities, making it difficult for them to invest the time necessary to become sufficiently well informed to guide complex projects [52]. Moreover, because of their position in the orga nizational hierarchy, senior IS managers are presented with unique control challenges, such as the challenge to ensure that IS projects deliver on their strategic objectives, while balancing efficiency and innovation $[ 2 5 , 6 1 ]$ . In light of these challenges, Heumann et al. [35] find that senior IS managers and IS project managers use similar control modes but differ in terms of their control styles. Relatedly, Wiener et al. [76] highlight that IS projects are often characterized by considerable (novelty-induced) uncertainty, placing higher demands on senior IS managers to be involved, commit time, and provide direction [52].

In the following, we build on the arguments put forward in Wiener et al. [76] to develop our research hypotheses on how IS project un certainty influences senior IS managers’ control-style choices, as well as how such choices relate to the process and product performance of IS projects [75]. Our research model follows the general structure of the Integrated IS Project Control Theory, which broadly distinguishes among control antecedents (IS project uncertainty in our study), control activ ities (control styles), and control consequences (IS project performance).

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

In the specific context of our study, we argue that there are three key factors that prompt senior IS managers to adopt an authoritative control style in relation to uncertain IS projects. First, under conditions of un certainty, senior IS managers will seek to take advantage of valuable and scarce time by focusing on specifying and communicating controls in a unilateral way [cf. 23,76]. Second, by virtue of their position in the organizational hierarchy, senior IS managers have superior formal au thority and are thus in general more likely to use an authoritative con trol style [1,37], even if they do not have the appropriate knowledge to use such a control style effectively [76]. Third, senior IS managers may employ an authoritative control style because controllees may actually expect them to provide clear direction and leadership [31], which might be particularly true for uncertain IS projects. For example, Goleman [21] describes an authoritative leadership style as a ‘come with me’ style that works best “when a clear direction is needed” (p. 82).

In addition, albeit not explicitly focusing on senior IS managers, existing studies provide initial evidence for the positive relationship between high levels of IS project uncertainty and the use of a more rigid control approach. For example, Rustagi et al. [68] find that project (task) uncertainty is positively associated with the amount of formal control used in IS outsourcing projects. This suggests that outsourcing clients try to alleviate IS project uncertainty “by using a greater number of control mechanisms or by using some more intensely” (p. 130). Relatedly, Shenhar [70] finds that projects characterized by high tech nological uncertainty increasingly rely on the formal approval of major decisions by (senior) managers who, at least at a certain point in time, “have to resort to a more rigid form, freezing specifications and avoiding any additional changes” (p. 38).

Separately, we hypothesize that senior IS managers will be less apt to choose an enabling control style in situations of IS project uncertainty. As indicated above, senior IS managers tend to have limited time [51,52], which is at odds with the significant time investment associated with such a control style. In particular, the repair and transparency features that characterize an enabling control style require the controller to closely interact with the controllee [76], which requires a noticeable time commitment [24,69]. Also, under conditions of high uncertainty, such as when the IS project manager and team members lack prior experience with the technologies used in the project, senior IS managers may feel that there is little to gain from closely interacting with the controllees [cf. 76]. As a consequence, they may prefer to not engage in close interactions, but rather focus on enacting controls in a unilateral way [23]. We therefore hypothesize:

H1. While IS project uncertainty (a) increases senior IS managers’ use of an authoritative control style, it (b) decreases their use of an enabling control style.

When using an authoritative control style, senior IS managers focus on enacting controls in a unilateral way rather than engaging in ‘costly interactions with controllees [23.69]. Especially under conditions of high IS project uncertainty (i.e., when the project task is non-routine and participants lack relevant experiences), this top-down approach can save valuable time and resources on the part of both controllers and con trollees and can thus help increase the process performance of IS projects [24,76]. The process performance-enhancing effect of an authoritative control style is also empirically supported by prior studies. For example, Hekkala and Urquhart [31] find that “project members often resorted to formal authority as a means of getting things done” (p. 92).

However, in terms of product performance, senior IS managers’ use of an authoritative control style is likely to stifle controllee creativity, motivation, and learning due to a lack of involvement in, and under standing of, control activities [1]. Relatedly, the use of an authoritative control style can lead to a lack of shared understanding between controller and controllees, which in turn may have a negative effect on the quality of project outcomes [23,24]. For instance, Choudhury and Sabherwal [6] present the cases of two outsourcing clients that ‘forced their vendors to design the contracted software system with minimal client involvement, even though the vendors lacked a solid under standing of the business domain. In both cases, the client’s use of an authoritative control style resulted in an insufficient quality of the vendor’s software design. As well, by using an authoritative control style, senior IS managers run the risk of misguiding controllees. This seems to be particularly true under conditions of high IS project un certainty and when the controller lacks relevant project knowledge [76]. Furthermore, while reducing the level of interaction with controllees can be expected to benefit immediate task execution and thus process performance (see above), it can also be expected that such lack of con trollee engagement and involvement in the control relationship would not benefit the quality of project outcomes; that is, product performance [2]. Therefore, the combination of high IS project uncertainty and a control style without repair and transparency is likely to reduce the chances for high product performance in projects. As such, we propose:

H2. IS project uncertainty strengthens the extent to which senior IS managers’ use of an authoritative control style (a) increases the process performance and (b) decreases the product performance of IS projects.

Prior research suggests that while the repair and transparency fea tures characterizing an enabling control style require considerable time and resource investments [24], these investments are likely to pay off —at least in terms of product performance [76]. The performanceenhancing effect of an enabling, or interactive, control style also finds support in earlier studies in a variety of control contexts [e.g., 1,2,65]. In uncertain IS projects, the product performance-related benefits of using an enabling control style are further elevated [69]. On the one hand, this is because enacting controls in an enabling style provides senior IS managers with feedback from controllees about real-work contingencies (repair), which can trigger learning processes and enable managers to compensate for potential knowledge gaps [31]; on the other hand, using an enabling control style, senior IS managers give controllees regular feedback on their performance and provide them with updates on relevant developments in the broader project context (transparency), thereby also facilitating learning processes on the part of the controllees [1]. Further, the distinctive features of an enabling control style (repair and transparency) help create a project environment conducive to the development of trust, encouraging subordinates to openly discuss quality-related problems with the senior IS manager overseeing the project [22]. Relatedly, managers’ use of an enabling control style can help prevent, or at least mitigate, negative socio-emotional side effects of control activities [76], which appears to be particularly important in IS projects with high task uncertainty. For example, extant research indicates that control-related side effects such as controllee dissatisfac tion and stress diminish the quality of work outcomes at the individual level, and ultimately at the project level [cf. 65,73].

In contrast, given the significant time and resource investments associated with an enabling control style, senior IS managers’ use of this style can be expected to slow down the progress of an IS project and thus impede its process performance [24,76]. For example, Gregory and Keil [24] highlight that the use of a collaborative (enabling) control style requires controllers to engage in close interactions with the controllees and “invest in creating consensus, something that takes time [and] needs a lot of coordination” (p. 351). Also, especially under conditions of high IS project uncertainty (i.e., when the project task is novel), it is ques tionable whether senior IS managers can further project efficiency by closely interacting with project participants who lack relevant experi ences. Here, we argue that, under such conditions, close and frequent controller-controllee interactions are likely to lead to a further decrease in process performance, as there is arguably little to gain from those interactions from an efficiency perspective. We thus suggest:

H3. IS project uncertainty strengthens the extent to which senior IS managers’ use of an enabling control style (a) decreases the process performance and (b) increases the product performance of IS projects.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection and sample

For the purpose of data collection, we developed a survey instru ment. The initial instrument was pretested with three experienced IS researchers (not part of the author team) and then used for a pilot study with 92 senior IS managers. Pilot study respondents were not included in the main sample. The pretest and pilot study led to minor adaptations in survey flow and wording.

To identify senior IS managers willing to participate in our survey, we used the support of a market research firm with access to a large pool of professionals. A similar approach has been used in prior IS research [e.g., 53,64]. The use of online panels is advantageous due to the ano nymity that it grants participants, as well as the broad spectrum of in dividuals that can be contacted [53]. Respondents were required to currently serve as a CIO, Vice President IT, Head of the IT Department, or in another senior IS manager role in order to participate in the survey. We excluded respondents who had not served in a controller role (as project sponsors or project steering committee members) in relation to an IS project manager during the last 12 months. We also excluded se nior IS managers who worked for companies with fewer than 250 em ployees, as their role is likely to be considerably different from senior IS manager roles at larger firms (e.g., in smaller firms, a senior IS manager may actually act as project manager).

The survey was administered online using Qualtrics and data was collected in spring 2017. Respondents were asked to refer to one recently completed, major IS project in their organization (e.g., application development, ERP implementation) for which they served as the project sponsor and/or sat on the project steering committee. The participants provided information on the IS project context (including project un certainty), their interactions with the IS project manager, and the project performance. All survey questions were mandatory. Attention filters were included in the survey (e.g., “Please select strongly disagree”) and re sponses submitted in less than the minimum expected time were excluded to avoid ‘mindless’ submissions that were too quickly completed by re spondents. In total, 307 senior IS managers accessed the online survey, of which 150 managers from 150 distinct IS projects completed the ques tionnaire (completion rate of 48.86%). All respondents were based in the United States, and they held job titles such as CIO (21%), Vice President IT (19%), and Head of IT Department (51%). 61% and 33% of them reported to have worked at their current employer 3–10 years and >10 years, respectively. Two-thirds of the respondents were between 30 and 49 years old, and roughly one-fifth was 50 years or older. Also, our data sample includes companies from various industries (e.g., financial ser vices, manufacturing, retail/wholesale, and telecommunications) and covers a broad range of IS projects in terms of their focus, methodology, and size. For instance, while 49% of the projects focused on the imple mentation of standard software, 43% concerned the development of custom software applications. 137 of the projects were run internally and 13 were outsourced to external vendors.

## 4.2. Construct measures

To measure IS project uncertainty, authoritative control style, and enabling control style, we used existing item sets from earlier research [59,65,68] and adapted them to the context of our study. To assess process and product performance, we adopted items from Wiener et al. [78] (based on [75]). The two latent control variables (behavior observability and outcome measurability) were based on items from Kirsch et al. [42]. An overview of the items used is shown in Appendix A.

To transform our research model into a structural equation model (and test our hypotheses). we used SmartPLS 3 [66]. We assessed the measurement model with a maximum of 1000 iterations and the struc tural model with a bootstrap size of 5000 subsamples [28]. PLS path modeling was chosen due to its capability to perform predictive analyses [16]. Also, PLS is remarkably stable even at relatively low sample sizes [20]. We evaluated the reliability and validity of the measurement model as follows: To ensure item reliability, we analyzed the outer loadings of each item. Consistent with Hair et al.’s [27] recommenda tion, we only deleted items with outer loadings between 0.4 and 0.7 when deleting these items led to an increase in average variance extracted (AVE) or composite reliability (CR) above the recommended thresholds of 0.5 and 0.7, respectively. This led to the exclusion of two enabling control style items, one behavior observability item, and one outcome measurability item (see Appendix A). After this, all constructs exceeded the critical CR threshold of 0.7 [17], indicating construct reliability; and the AVE for each construct was >0.5, establishing convergent validity for all scales [17]. Also, each construct shared more variance with its assigned items than with any other construct (see Appendix B), and loadings of all construct items were greater than their cross loadings with other constructs (see Appendix C), establishing discriminant validity [17]. Further, we examined the heterotrait monotrait (HTMT) ratio of correlations, which is an alternative approach to assessing discriminant validity [34]. All HTMT values were below the threshold of 0.9 (see Appendix D).

Finally, to check for common method bias, we conducted Harman’s single-factor test [62] and performed a full-collinearity test by calcu lating the variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all independent and dependent variables included in our model [44]. Considering all model variables, a full-collinearity test is an effective test for the identification of common method bias [43]. All VIF values were clearly below the most conservative threshold of 3.3 [44], with product performance showing the highest VIF value (2.181). Taken together, the test results indicate that neither common method bias nor multicollinearity are likely to be a serious concern in our study.

We included a total of six distinct control variables in our research model to test for alternative explanations. Specifically, we controlled for the effects of behavior observability, outcome measurability, the con troller’s rank in the organizational hierarchy (i.e., whether the controller is a senior IS executive),<sup>2</sup> the IS project’s strategic importance, and the project manager’s tenure on senior IS managers’ control-style choices [76]. Moreover, we controlled for the influence of strategic importance, project manager tenure, and project team size on the pro cess and product performance of IS projects. In prior studies, these three variables have been found to affect IS project performance/success [e. g., 13,36]. Relatedly, we conducted several robustness checks. For example, we checked for the impact of senior IS managers’ personal characteristics (in terms of past experience/tenure) on their controlstyle choices, which did not reveal any significant effects, and for the influence of different IS project types (internal vs. outsourced). Excluding the outsourced projects (n = 13) from the sample did not affect the hypothesis test results.<sup>3</sup> Also, test results were consistent in an alternative reflective-formative specification of enabling control style.

## 5. Data analyses and results

In this section, we first analyze our research model and test our hy potheses (see Sub-section 5.1). We then conduct a post-hoc analysis regarding the performance effects of the combined use of an authori tative and an enabling control style under conditions of IS project un certainty, followed by a test for potential endogeneity issues with our model (see Sub-section 5.2).

## 5.2. Post-hoc analyses

The main analysis results can be summarized as follows: under conditions of high IS project uncertainty, senior IS managers increase their use of an authoritative control style, and the use of this control style has a positive impact on process, but not on product performance. On the other hand, IS project uncertainty does not significantly affect senior IS managers’ use of an enabling control style, while the use of this control style under uncertainty has a positive impact on product, but not on process performance. Given these results, it also becomes relevant to examine whether the simultaneous use of the two control styles repre sents an effective strategy in increasing both process and product per formance under conditions of high IS project uncertainty. We therefore followed the process outlined by Henseler and Fassott [33] to calculate the three-way interaction effects of authoritative control style, enabling control style, and IS project uncertainty on both process and product performance. Since the calculation of three-way interaction terms is not yet supported in SmartPLS, we used the PROCESS macro developed by Hayes [30] to calculate them in SPSS. The results show that neither of the two interaction effects is significant (process performance: β = $0 . 0 9 8 , t = 0 . 9 1 6$ , ns; product performance: $\beta = - 0 . 0 5 0 , t = - 0 . 5 6 2 , \mathrm { n s } )$ which suggests that combining an authoritative with an enabling control style in uncertain IS projects is not an effective strategy. This also implies that, under high levels of IS project uncertainty, senior IS managers are required to make tough decisions as to whether they aim for process performance (by choosing an authoritative control style), or for product performance (by choosing an enabling control style).

## 6. Discussion

The study at hand contributes to the IS project control literature by offering novel insights on how senior IS managers enact control over IS projects under conditions of uncertainty. In particular, we consider how IS project uncertainty influences managers’ control-style choices and moderates the effects of such choices on the process and product per formance of IS projects. Based on a survey with 150 senior IS managers, we find empirical support for three of our six hypotheses. Next, we discuss our study’s main contributions and practical implications (see Table 3 below for a summary), followed by a discussion of its limitations and associated directions for future research.

## 6.1. Research contributions

An overarching contribution of our study lies in empirically testing key aspects of the Integrated IS Project Control Theory [65,76] in the specific context of senior IS managers enacting control over IS projects, and to extend this theory to account for the role of IS project uncertainty. In doing so, the results of our study contribute novel insights that advance our understanding of the important role played by control style—a central concept of the aforementioned theory—in the control of IS projects. Through recent studies [e.g., 50,65], we have begun to gain an understanding of the practices related to, and significance of, the enactment of IS project controls. These studies have shown that control enactment—the ‘how’ of control—is crucial for understanding IS project control and its outcomes. However, although we know that uncertainty is a frequent ‘unwelcome guest’ in IS projects, we have had a limited understanding of how uncertainty influences control-style decisions and their consequences for project outcomes. By addressing this gap, our study supports prior studies in establishing the central role of control style in understanding IS project control, while moving beyond them to address how novelty-induced IS project uncertainty influences both control-style decisions and their impact on project performance.

On this basis, our study contributes to the existing body of research on IS project control and decision support systems in three ways: First, it provides empirical support for senior IS managers’ preference for an authoritative control style under conditions of high IS project uncer tainty. This finding contextualizes and extends the findings of previous research, which suggest that the use of an authoritative control style is primarily a function of power asymmetries between controller and controllee [1,76], or triggered by performance problems in IS projects [23,35]. That IS project uncertainty is positively related to senior IS managers’ use of an authoritative control style has parallels to studies in related fields, where it has been suggested that, in such situations, senior managers need to provide subordinates with clear guidance on the project direction without allowing “too many voices” [51], p. 57; cf. [70], and that coping with uncertainty can require clear instructions and directives to help reduce uncertainty and potential confusion [21,31]. Navigating such uncertainty is a key factor in attaining project resil ience, which refers to the capabilities necessary to overcome a project disturbance [79]. Interestingly, a study by Heumann et al. [35] on the enactment of formal control in a large-scale IS project finds that senior management relied heavily on the use of an enabling control style and that their use of this style “was driven to a great extent by task complexity” (p. 345). A potential explanation for this seemingly con tradictory finding relates to the important distinction between different sources of IS project (task) uncertainty [e.g., 3,11]. Specifically, in light of our results, one explanation of the Heumann et al. [35] finding is that the studied IS project was characterized by high complexity-induced uncertainty (as opposed to novelty-induced uncertainty), promoting senior IS managers’ decision to use an enabling control style (instead of an authoritative one). In addition, our study results indicate that novelty-induced uncertainty does not influence the use of an enabling control style, which suggests that the repair and transparency features of this control style are considered less beneficial by senior IS managers in corresponding high-uncertainty situations. Mirroring the above argu ment for clarity and simplicity in control, the lack of preference for an enabling control style can be understood as a way to avoid decision paralysis under uncertainty [54], and to allow for actions to be taken expediently, such as resorting to formal authority to get ‘things done [31].

Second, another important contribution of our study lies in providing empirical support for how novelty-induced IS project uncertainty in fluences the relationship between senior IS managers’ control-style de cisions and IS project performance. Here, under ‘normal’ circumstances, an enabling control style has been found to further task performance [65]. This finding is in keeping with our results, which suggest that, in general (i.e., when neglecting the influence of IS project uncertainty), an enabling control style is positively and significantly related to both process and product performance (see Table 1). However, considering the moderating role of IS project uncertainty, we find that opting for this control style contributes to product performance (but not process per formance), whereas an authoritative control style improves process performance (but not product performance). The efficiency-related benefits of an authoritative control style are commensurate with its absence of support for creativity, empowerment, and learning [1,2]. Instead, this control style is attuned toward streamlining project activ ities and executing existing plans, while eschewing the capturing of additional opportunities to increase the value created through project outcomes [77]. Still, as noted above, our study also offers evidence for the performance benefits of an enabling control style. Although less frequently used by senior IS managers under conditions of uncertainty, if used, an enabling control style offers benefits in terms of adaptability, flexibility, and responsiveness, which also under high uncertainty can pay off in terms of higher quality in outcomes. Specifically, this means that in situations where senior IS managers seek to ensure that an IS project delivers high-quality outcomes (e.g., a software application well attuned to user needs), they would be better off opting for an enabling control style, despite the additional effort required and despite the associated process-performance risks.

Third, while an authoritative and an enabling control style can potentially be combined in certain contexts, the results of our study —capturing a larger and more diverse set of IS projects compared to earlier case-based research addressing ambidexterity in control enact ment [24] and the role of senior managers in IS projects [63]—suggest that such a combination of control styles is not a widespread practice among senior IS managers enacting control over IS projects. Impor tantly, our results also suggest that it is not a reliable and repeatable way to achieve performance enhancements in IS project settings where project participants lack relevant experience with similar tasks or technologies. Nonetheless, we recognize that a manager’s deliberate decision to balance or mix different control styles might be effective under special circumstances, such as when an organization has ambi dextrous capabilities related to IT and control enactment [25] or when a project features high levels of both formal and informal control [71].

## 6.2. Practical implications

The study results have important practical implications for senior level IS managers overseeing uncertain IS projects (i.e., controllers), as well as for other project stakeholders (i.e., controllees). First, project managers finding themselves reporting to senior IS managers in an un certain IS project setting might be well advised to prepare for control lers’ use of an authoritative control style. Such preparedness might shield controllees from misinterpreting controller actions as signs of mistrust. Second, as for the controller role, our study frames an impor tant decision-related control dilemma for senior IS managers overseeing uncertain IS projects: They must choose between enacting control authoritatively and prioritizing process performance, and enacting control in an enabling style and thus prioritizing product performance. This, in turn, implies that novelty-induced IS project uncertainty re quires clear-cut control-style decisions, along with some tough prior ities. Third, the findings of our study raise questions concerning the effectiveness of ambidextrous control approaches [24,71], in spite of the face-value attractiveness of combining contrasting control styles in order to achieve both process and product performance. In particular, our findings suggest that such an approach, when adopted by senior IS managers, would render control enactment ineffective in regard to both performance dimensions. While this does not necessarily imply that managers should categorically decide against blending an authoritative control style with an enabling one, we can at the very least state that practitioners should carefully assess their ambidextrous capabilities related to control enactment. Fourth, the above recommendations also help shine a light on an important managerial capability related to securing IS project performance under adverse conditions, such as a high degree of novelty-induced project uncertainty; namely, to develop a mindful and nuanced control approach, where particular aspects of the situation feed into managers’ control-enactment decisions. The more we learn about IS project control, the less plausible a ‘one size fits all control approach becomes. ## 6.3. Limitations and future research

The results of our study should be interpreted with several limita tions in mind, which point to promising avenues for future research First, while our data sample covers a broad range of IS projects (e.g., in terms of project focus, methodology, size), the great majority of these projects were run internally (91%). Therefore, more research is needed to understand the extent to which our findings generalize to outsourced IS project settings. Relatedly, it would be interesting to study projec control in relation to the development of artificial intelligence (AI)- based systems (including next-generation decision support systems), where novelty-induced uncertainty might be particularly high. Second, in line with earlier IS project control research [55,68,69], our study views uncertainty as a potential threat and focuses on one specific type of IS project uncertainty; namely, the level of uncertainty surrounding the overall project task and resulting from project novelty. As such, future research would benefit from acknowledging the ‘constructive potential’ embedded in uncertainty [26,46], as well as from considering how managers adapt their project control activities, and in particular their control style, in response to other uncertainty types, such as complexity-induced project uncertainty [15] or change-induced market uncertainty [29]. Third, senior IS managers may be inclined to describe their control style as more enabling (and less authoritative) or choose to report on a successful IS project. To reduce the risk of response bias. future studies should try to collect data from both sides of the contro dyad. Such studies may provide new insights into issues around communicational and evaluational control incongruence [76] and how such issues influence the effectiveness of IS project control tactics [56,58]. Fourth, on average, the senior IS managers who participated in our study reported having relatively high levels of IS knowledge, which may have affected their uncertainty perception [cf. 12] and contributed to their preference for an authoritative control style, as well as the positive link between this control style and process performance unde conditions of uncertainty. Future research should extend to senio managers with less, or more varied levels of, IS knowledge to advance our understanding of how different levels of expertise influence control style decisions and their effectiveness [51]. Fifth, our study focused or the control relationship between senior IS managers and IS project managers but did not consider the project-team level. Doing so could reveal interesting control-related differences between agile and water fall projects [9,57]. For instance, for senior IS managers, it might be particularly challenging to exercise control over agile project teams, which operate in a self-organizing manner and thus may be less welcome to control from outside the team. Sixth, we note that our data were collected in 2017 from respondents located in the United States, raising some potential concerns about the external validity and generalizability of our results. This suggests an opportunity to collect new data from other geographic regions and/or other IS project types (see also above) in an attempt to replicate our study’s findings in other project contexts, as well as to explore the role of cultural differences in senior IS man agers’ uncertainty perceptions and their control-enactment decisions [cf. 38,40]. Finally, past work on the control of IS projects suggests that control choices can change over time [23,35]. Future research may thus uncover valuable insights into the factors that trigger such changes by conducting longitudinal studies that explore how and why senior IS managers alter their control style over the course of a project.

## 7. Conclusion

Focusing on the important context of senior IS managers, this study contributes novel insights to existing literature by empirically exam ining how IS project uncertainty relates to control-style choices and their project-performance impacts. Drawing on a survey with 150 senior IS managers, our study reveals an interesting pattern that may help explain the continuing high failure rate of IS projects. Specifically, while we find that senior IS managers are likely to opt for an authoritative control style under conditions of high uncertainty, we also find that, under such conditions, an authoritative style only corresponds with improved pro cess performance (whereas an enabling control style only correspond with improved product performance). In conclusion, the study at hand represents an important step toward developing richer theories that provide a deeper understanding of, and actionable advice on, how senior IS managers can exercise effective control over IS projects, especially under adverse conditions.
