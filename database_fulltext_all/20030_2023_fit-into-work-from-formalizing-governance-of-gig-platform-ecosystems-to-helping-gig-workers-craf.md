# Fit into work! From formalizing governance of gig platform ecosystems to helping gig workers craft their platform work

Jianyu Chen <sup>a</sup>, Jinlian Luo <sup>a</sup>, Wenan Hu <sup>b,\*</sup>, Jun Ma <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Economics & Management, Tongji University, Shanghai, China

<sup>b</sup> Institute of Talent Development Strategy, Shandong University, Jinan, China

<sup>c</sup> School of Business, Jiangnan University, Wuxi, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Gig economy Gig platform ecosystem Platform formalization governance Performance appraisal Job crafting Platform work insecurity climate

## A B S T R A C T

The current governance model of gig platform ecosystems benefits gig platforms and customers, but puts gig workers in the weakest position of the triangular relationship. Thus, gig workers experience difficulty in addressing hardships related to gig work. To this end, we must determine how gig platforms build a governance environment that matches gig workers to help them craft gig work. However, extant studies have remained in the qualitative research surface, lacked a certain empirical insight, and have not clarified the direction to creating an effective platform governance model. Thus, based on such literature, we first conceptualize platform formal ization governance. Drawing on person-environment fit theory, the PLS-SEM analysis of 290 data from gig workers finds that platform formalization governance directly promotes gig workers' job crafting and indirectly propels it by improving performance appraisal systems' transparency. However, platform work insecurity climate weakens such an direct/indirect positive relationship. These findings contribute to strategic values and effec tiveness of the governance model of gig platform ecosystems, thus balancing interests between gig platforms, gig workers, and customers. Gig platforms should consider appropriately formalizing the current governance model and improving work insecurity climate, which helps gig workers more easily address hardships associated with platform work through job crafting.

## 1. Introduction

Distinct from conventional employment, the emerging gig economy is a market-oriented economic system in which independent service providers (gig workers) and organizations engage in short-term on-de mand work arrangements mediated by digital platforms (gig platforms) [1]. Thus, one main characteristic of the gig economy is online gig platform [2], which operationalizes the governance mechanism of mutual exchanges, thus creating value for all parties involved [3]. Digital intermediary platforms for the exchange of goods and services between parties have enabled the gig economy whose average growth rate is 10% per year before the 2020 pandemic and roughly 40% at the beginning of the pandemic in March 2020 [4]. It is experiencing rapid growth with a forecast global economic value of USD335 billion by 2025 [5].

However, there are not a set of universal work classifications or rules that have thus far been adapted to the short-term work mediated by gig platform systems [6]. Gig platform ecosystems and business models bypass many regular employment responsibilities and costs, therefore bringing widespread legal ambiguity [7]. They can easily shift their business risks to gig workers and then transform them into personal risks, thus creating the precarious and fragile nature of gig work [8,9]. Thus, some scholars have earlier suggested that digital platform workers cope with hardships associated with gig work via job crafting [10,11], which denotes an effort to adjust their work to better align the latter with their objectives, values, passions, as well as likes [12].

However, promoting gig workers' job crafting may be a challenge and need help from the other sides (e.g., gig platforms, customers, and government) [10,11]. In the gig economic ecosystem, gig platforms informally govern many things through various information manage ment systems (e.g., algorithmic control, monitoring, and ratings) without excessive burden [6,13,14]. They constitute a new governance environment with which they and their customers can nearly place power in their own hands [15]. However, the present platform gover nance environment can be detrimental to gig workers' proactivity. Given the precarious nature of gig work, gig workers might be negatively affected by this type of work [16]. Moreover, given the isolated nature of gig work and the lack of informal social learning and HR-related formal training and career development, supportive resources are less acces sible to them [10]. Thus, under the current platform governance envi ronment, gig workers might be unwilling to proactively change their platform work to fit into what they value (i.e., job crafting) to overcome hardships related to gig work.

The current studies consider gig platforms' governance as the so called, “modern-day, digitalized Taylorism” [6,17]. Given algorithmic surveillance, gig workers face emerging forms of control and coercion and thus a more democratic digital governance form should be pre sented [17]. Despite the call for the digital governance of gig platform ecosystems, gig economy studies have remained on the qualitative sur face and have not clarified the direction to create an effective platform governance model (see Table 1) [18]. Although qualitative research methods can more comprehensively obtain a series of valuable infor mation regarding gig platforms' governance model, they lack empirical evidence and deepening insight into whether, how, and when the pre sent governance model of gig platform ecosystems influence gig workers' psychologies and behaviors [19]. Moreover, we have not found any direct research on how and when to improve the current platform governance model to help gig workers craft their platform work. Nonetheless, extant gig work research has emphasized the importance of a good governance model because it improves the value and efficiency created by gig work for three key stakeholders including gig workers, platforms, and customers (e.g., reducing workers' high dropout rates, lowering labor costs, and promoting worker performance) [20,21]. To fill this void while increasing the strategic values and effectiveness of gig platform governance, we use person-environment fit theory to address the key research question—whether, how, and when do gig platforms build a good governance environment that matches gig workers to help them craft their platform work?

In Table 1, scholars have more or less looked into how to formalize the present governance of gig platform ecosystems but without explicitly defining and focusing on it. Combined with the conventional definitions of both formalization [22] and governance [23], based on the gig economy context, we define the formalization governance of gig plat form ecosystems as the extent to which gig platforms coordinate, con trol, and regulate all involved parties through explicit operating rules, procedures, instructions, and communications in various managerial information systems (e.g., algorithmic control, monitoring, and ratings). We then use person-environment fit theory to introduce the specific mechanism and the boundary condition to examine whether, how, and when platform formalization governance promotes gig workers' job crafting, thus solving our research question.

The current platform governance is tightly associated with algo rithmic management systems [20]. Given the general use of algorithmic management systems, the lack of transparency in performance evalua tion generates information asymmetries between the platform and gig workers [24] and limits their agency and autonomy [25]. Gig workers develop a weak perception of the platform and unpredictable work performance appraisals [26]. The lack of transparency in performance appraisals may further make them little know about how to handle performance-related results [27]. Person-environment fit theory high lights that the consistent characteristics between individuals and the environment yield positive results for both organization and individual [28]. As demonstrated by one study with rich qualitative data and inductive analysis, digital platform workers might customize and craft their jobs according to their needs, preferences, and perceived favorable work features [11]. Accordingly, compared with the current platform governance, platform formalization governance might help gig workers perceive the consistent platform work characteristics (e.g., transparent performance appraisals), thus encouraging them to craft their platform work. We thus address how platform formalization governance in fluences gig workers' job crafting by considering performance appraisal transparency as the mediator.

Gig platforms provide neither a fixed work schedule, an employment contract, a promised hourly pay, nor social insurance [8]. Low remu neration, precarious income streams, and lack of social security benefits are typical features of gig work [29]. Scholars have thus argued that this work insecurity climate notably exists in the gig platform ecosystems [8]. Given the platform work insecurity climate, many gig workers have shared common perceptions of their environment as being unpredict able and leading to unemployment threat [26]. One qualitative study has further found that the online insecure environment constrains the actions of gig workers who use the platform's technological advantages to succeed [30]. Thus, according to person-environment fit theory, platform work insecurity climate may represent a non-fit environment where gig workers who lack stable employment opportunities might be constrained to have a consistent perception of platform formalization governance and its advantages, thus weakening their job crafting. Accordingly, we explore when platform formalization governance af fects gig workers' job crafting by regarding platform work insecurity climate as the boundary condition.

We make the following contributions. First, extant studies have remained on the qualitative surface and have not clarified the direction to establish an effective governance model of gig platform ecosystems (see Table 1). Thus, we lead scholars to focus on the proper formaliza tion of gig platform ecosystems' governance model and further explore its key consequences. Second, while some studies have found the problem regarding opaque performance appraisal systems, they are primarily qualitative and have not further explored its antecedents and consequences. However, these systems' transparency might tightly involve gig workers' benefits (e.g., reward, remuneration, or even deactivation) and impact their work performance. Thus, this gap in spires us to explore how to make gig platforms' performance appraisal systems more transparent, explicit, and clear. Third, while scholars focus on the role of work insecurity in influencing the precarious nature of gig work, we first emphasize its key moderating role in platform governance and guide scholars to improve strategic values and effectiveness of platform governance by reducing such climate. Fourth, the present study extends the theoretical implications and applicability of person environment fit theory in the gig economy. Ultimately, having consid ered our useful suggestions, gig platforms can help gig workers craft their platform work by properly formalizing the governance of their ecosystems and improving platform work insecurity climate.

## 2. Literature review

We focus on whether, how, and when gig platforms establish a governance environment that matches gig workers to help them craft gig work. However, previous studies have not yet established a strong knowledge system to resolve our core research question. To this end, we first illustrate why and how to apply person-environment fit theory to the unique governance environment of digital gig platforms. We further analyze the present governance environment in the gig economy and determine its disadvantages. Finally, we illustrate why formalizing the current platform governance might be an underlying direction to establish an effective governance model of the gig platform and help gig workers craft their platform work.

## 2.1. Person-environment fit theory

The concept of person-environment fit—refers to the match, congruence, similarities, or correspondence between an individual and an environment [33]. Person-environment fit theory has developed many different lenses including person-group, person-job, and personorganization, among others [34]. Its basic logic assumes that a per son's behavior is generated from interactions between a person and an environment [34]. A “person” is defined as an individual's abilities, skills, intelligence, skills, and other traits (e.g., goals, interests, values, and personality); an “environment” typically refers to those characteristics outside the person such as job characteristics (e.g. chal lenge) and the organization (e.g. values, systems, and strategy) [33]. The fit between individuals and the work environment is built upon their consistent characteristics, which in turn promote positive results for both organization and individual [28].

Our research questions include whether, how, and when gig plat forms establish a governance environment that matches gig workers to help them craft gig work. Job crafting, which is our research focus, denotes an individual's effort to adjust work to better align with her/his personal characteristics such as objectives, values, passions, and likes [12]. Not hard to find, while adjusting their platform work, if the governance environment's characteristics in the gig economy are more consistent with gig workers' personal characteristics, gig workers might craft their platform work more easily. It is in line with the assumption of person-environment fit theory. However, research has thus far scarcely focused on the key role of such theory in the gig economy. Additionally, task-technology fit and IT-strategy fit theories focus on the conceptu alization of fit in the context of an information system [34]. Although the gig economy's governance model is enabled by digital technologies and information systems, the concept of fit should be expanded from the person-task/strategy perspective to the broader person-environment perspective for a comprehensive appreciation of the interactions be tween gig workers and platform governance environment. To fill this void, we use person-environment fit theory to analyze our research question, which drives us to review the current governance environment of gig platform ecosystems.

## 2.2. Current governance environment in the gig economy

The digital economy develops different forms of economy such as the e-commerce economy and the gig/sharing economy. In the e-commerce economy, an e-commerce platform (e.g., Amzon, Jingdong, and Taobao) is usually designed to serve as the critical transaction medium in the digital economy [35], within which customers are not only able to finish online shopping operations but also access experiences and knowledge from the other to help them make better purchase decisions in terms of customer-produced online reviews [36]. By contrast, in the gig econ omy, gig platforms (e.g., Uber, Meituan, Upwork, and Amzon MTurk) are commonly designed to act as matchmakers between gig workers and customers [37], within which individuals directly sell their personal labor service to the task providers (i.e., customers) and obtain task as signments through the mediation of the online platform [38]. Consid ered together, the e-commerce platform focuses on customers' online shopping, while the gig platform aims at online labor services. Thus, these two types of online platforms have different governance envi ronments. Here, we focus on the governance environment of the gig platform ecosystem.

Previous studies have explored the governance environment of gig platform ecosystems as shown in Table 1. By summarizing major themes in the literature on gig platforms, Vallas and Schor (2020) [15] found that gig platforms represent an emerging type of governance mecha nism, within which as permissive potentates, they externalize re sponsibility, control economic transactions, and continue to exercise concentrated power. By summarizing case data from several extant works of literature, Athreya (2020) [17] found that gig workers are manipulated by algorithmic management enabled via information asymmetries, a lack of labor protection, and predatory governance models. Thus, stakeholders should handle the issue of gig worker data ownership and control and facilitate other forms of democratic data governance [17]. Based on a comprehensive review of the literature on gig work and governance, Gol et al. (2019) [20] built an overarching conceptual model integrating control and coordination systems as two complementary mechanisms that facilitate gig platform governance effectiveness and job provider benefits. Building on a comprehensive literature search, Wagner et al. (2021) [31] derived one of five boundary conditions that form the definition of a digital knowledge platform (e.g.,

Upwork and Amzon MTurk), which requests the platform to centralize governance by regulating the exchange and directing resources to pro mote the matching, contracting, and executing processes. Drawing on a systematic content analysis of 12,294 scraped comments, Bucher et al. (2021) [32] found how gig workers abide by the governance mecha nisms of algorithmic management on gig platforms that are not trans parent, clear, or explicit. However, these few extant studies have remained qualitative. They have explored the current situation of the governance environment of gig platform ecosystems but have not pointed out a clear direction to establish an effective governance envi ronment in the gig economy. To this end, we further review the current governance practices in the gig economy to clarify such direction.

The current governance model of gig platforms involves three core stakeholders, namely, gig workers, customers, and intermediary plat forms [20,39], thus constituting an inherent platform governance structure. On the one hand, the present platform governance creates insightful accounts of unequal situations and social isolation through algorithmic management systems [26]. These algorithm-based man agement systems are innovative and can create hyper-flexibility but leave gig workers isolated in their roles without secure employment and development opportunities [27]. Without any participation of gig workers, algorithm monitoring systems can automatically collect heu ristic data from their smartphone app [13,40]. Many gig platforms assign tasks to gig workers by using algorithms and determine their pay rates [6]. However, owing to the opaque algorithmic management [14], gig workers perceive injustice in work planning insecurity, a lack of transparency in performance appraisal, insufficient clarity in task briefings, and low payment [29]. Given the lack of social negotiations between them, these platforms are capable of exploiting gig workers when a large, competitive pool of new entrants appear [39]. Considered together, the current platform governance creates an ongoing informa tion and power asymmetries between gig platforms and gig workers, which are extremely weighted in favor of the former [6].

On the other hand, gig platforms outsource performance manage ment to customers through platform rating systems for gig workers, a process which demonstrates how intertwined customers are in perfor mance management in gig platform ecosystems [41]. On many gig platforms (e.g., Upwork, Uber, and Amazon Mechanical Turk), various forms of customer-based rating systems exist (e.g., a dynamic Job Suc cess Score, Master Qualifications, Five Star, and a Top-Rated Badge) [42,43]. Many gig platforms heavily rely on customer reviews and rat ings as one of the governance methods to develop trust and credible relationships with customers as well as to discipline unwanted behaviors [6]. By using platform rating systems, gig platforms place the power in the hands of customers who are directly engaging with gig workers [44]. To avoid terrible ratings and obtain good ones, gig workers must deliver high-quality services to customers on time and according to specifica tion [11] and even perform emotional labor for customers [40]. Consequently, the current platform governance leads to extremely un equal relationships between gig workers and customers, posing risks to gig workers' physical and psychological well-being [9].

Conventionally, informal governance highlights an inherent social structure that aims to achieve the purposes of control, regulation, and coordination by influencing all involved parties' behaviors by estab lishing trust and social identification relationships [45]. Apparently, the current governance of gig platform ecosystems can be regarded as one type of informal governance, as it indeed constitutes an inherent social structure by using unequal relational structures of trust and power for the interrelation between gig workers, platforms, and clients. Specif ically, as permissive potentates, gig platforms externalize responsibility, control economic transactions, and retain concentrated power; by outsourcing performance management to customers, gig platforms build trust and credible relationships with customers and discipline unwanted behavior of gig workers [15,41]. In the present platform governance structure involving three parties, gig workers are in the weakest position of the triangular relationship and information resource [9,17].

However, gig workers cannot share the risks associated with gig work with the other two parties and experience difficulty in changing their gig work to protect their own labor rights and fit into their values, likes, and so on [9–11,39]; this situation damages their job crafting ability to handle these risks and difficulties.

Juillerat (2010) [46] underlined the necessity of formalization, especially in the current gig platforms' business environment that is complex, uncertain, and interdependent [47]. Given that the present platform governance cannot benefit gig workers' job crafting despite its benefits for gig platforms and customers, we consider whether platform governance can become more effective when informal platform gover nance changes into a formal one because it might benefit the three involved parties (i.e., gig platforms, gig workers, and customers). Accordingly, we further analyze what may happen when formalizing the current platform governance.

## 2.3. Platform formalization governance

Formal governance means that the organization supports and sus tains cooperation and collaboration through contracts, standards, pro cesses, as well as structures to achieve common objectives [45]. Formalizing governance includes contracts, standards, processes, and structures [48]. First, as a core of formalizing governance, the contract explicitly describes work arrangements, thus influencing the coordina tion efficiency of platform governance by depicting the transactional engagement between gig platforms, customers, and gig workers [20]. Second, gig platforms can provide general directive control through standards, policies, and rules that guide behavior on digital gig plat forms [21], thus efficiently allowing the monitoring of performance and environment of gig workers [49]. Third, both unclear task descriptions as well as complex interfaces can damage work quality, because in dividuals are uncertain of correct processes and expectations [20]. Gig platforms can govern work processes including instructions, configura tions, and task assignments to propel the work engagement of gig workers and promote their productivity [21]. However, Gol et al. (2019) [20] considered that with the increase in platform work complexity, platform governance related to work processes has become more chal lenging. Fourth. the governance structure can be divided into both decentralization and centralization [50]. For gig platforms, they provide a governance structure to address difficulties in a distributed and scal able online platform work setting, which decentralizes certain powers to customers (e.g., rating control) and gig workers (e.g., work autonomy and flexibility) [21]. However, centralization by formalizing platform governance is sometimes necessary to effectively operationalize a plat form governance mechanism that forces all involved parties to follow relevant rules and curtail power misuse [17,20].

However, no literature has thus far defined the formalization governance of gig platform ecosystems, let alone its consequent effects. Hence, we first need to conceptualize the formalization governance of gig platform ecosystems by combining it with conventional literature and the gig economy background. Conventionally, formalization depicts the extent to which the organization writes down clear operating rules, procedures, instructions, and communications [22]; whereas gover nance refers to the coordination, control, and regulation of relationships [23]. In the emerging gig economy, gig platforms govern by employing a variety of information technologies, such as rating systems, algorithmic monitoring, algorithmic control, and so on $[ 1 3 , 1 4 , 2 0 ]$ . Thus, given the gig platform ecosystems, we define platform formalization governance as the degree to which gig platforms coordinate, control, and regulate all involved parties through clear operating rules, procedures, instructions, and communications in various algorithmic management systems.

Through a series of literature reviews, we indeed find that having formalized the current platform governance, gig platforms might so lidify collective interests, thus decreasing both opportunism and con flicts between gig platforms, customers, and gig workers and increasing gig workers' positive psychologies and behaviors $[ 3 , 1 5 , 1 7 , 2 0 , 4 9 ]$

However, no study has thus far directly conceptualized the formaliza tion governance of gig platform ecosystems. Whether platform formal ization governance can be truly helpful toward establishing an effective governance environment in the gig economy still requires empirical investigation. This gap offers a prospective opportunity for us to examine the advantages of platform formalization governance and how to improve its effectiveness by introducing some appropriate impact mechanisms (e.g., performance appraisal transparency, platform work insecurity climate, and job crafting).

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. Platform formalization governance and job crafting

The current informal form of platform governance has raised scholars' concerns [17]. Given an extremely virtual online environment with nearly no participation of human managers and scarce interaction with customers, many studies have found that under the current governance model of gig platform ecosystems, many operation rules, procedures, and other aspects are unclear and opaque in various algo rithmic management systems [25,29,39,40]. Thus, platform governance based on binary-code algorithms also produces information and power asymmetries between the platform/customer and gig workers, which is more in favor of the former [6,24]. According to person-environment fit theory, workers prefer to work in organizations in which they can perceive a consistent work environment [28]. However, the current platform governance model might hinder gig workers from perceiving a consistent platform environment which is detrimental and unclear for them, thus perhaps less actively crafting their platform work to handle these aforementioned hardships.

Thus, when formalizing the present platform governance model, gig platforms can clarify gig workers' knowledge concerning operating rules, procedures, instructions, as well as communications in various algorithmic management systems. Originally, job crafting refers to how employees respond to opportunities to actively change tasks and the relational boundaries of their work [10]. From the person-environment fit perspective, the content of platform governance that becomes clearer will help gig workers fit into the platform work environment, thus actively responding to opportunities to change tasks and relational boundaries of their work (i.e., an increase in job crafting). Thus, we formally propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. (H1). Platform formalization governance is positively related to job crafting.

## 3.2. Platform formalization governance and performance appraisal transparency

In the gig economy, various algorithmic information systems including control, monitoring, tracking, and rating/reputation, among others, together constitute the current platform governance model [13,14,20]. These algorithm-based management systems determine gig workers' performance evaluation/appraisal results [27,40]. Thus, whether gig platforms' performance appraisal systems are transparent relies largely on whether these algorithmic management systems' con tent is clear and explicit [14,40]. Whereas formal governance facilitates an organizational governance environment, which can trigger the virtue of broad information sharing (i.e., the extent to which information on unit vision, unit performance, and work quality, among others, is communicated) [51]. Thus, when formalizing the current platform governance model, operating rules, procedures, instructions, and com munications that are inserted in these algorithmic management systems will become clearer and create broad information sharing among gig workers. Thus, gig platforms' performance appraisal criteria and pro cesses will further become more explicit and clearer. Thus, we formally propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. (H2). Platform formalization governance is positively related to performance appraisal transparency.

## 3.3. Performance appraisal transparency and job crafting

Performance appraisal is highly crucial for human resource man agement. In the traditional workplace, supervisors and managers must be strictly and clearly appraising their subordinates' job performance to decide a series of benefits that subordinates have [52]. Thus, whether performance appraisal criteria and processes are clear, explicit, and transparent can seriously influence whether behaviors at the personal, group, and corporate levels are positive [53]. The significance of per formance appraisal transparency similarly applies to gig workers who are working hard on gig platforms because it will decide their remu neration, reward, and even deactivation [15,40], which therefore in fluences their behaviors in handling hardships related to gig work through job crafting.

The present platform governance model is unique in that contrary to the conventional work type, it does not take place within commercial hierarchical organizations [15,29,38]. Thus, different from conven tional organizations, gig workers' work performance contents are captured in real-time via their smartphone app throughout the algo rithmic management process [54]. With little input from gig workers, gig platforms' algorithmic monitoring can automatically collect the heuristic data related to gig workers' work performance [13], which has become the dominant modality of gig workers' performance appraisal [40].

However, the algorithmic management processes mediated by gig platforms allocate tasks, calculate remunerations, as well as appraise performance using opaque and frequently changing criteria [40]. Pre vious studies have found that transparency is lacking in performance appraisal criteria and processes in gig platform ecosystems, but gig workers are incapable of influencing or raising objections to the per formance appraisal process itself [17,27,29]. By interviewing gig workers, some scholars have found that given the general use of algo rithmic management, a lack of transparency centered on platform de cision making might constrain their agency and autonomy to perform gig work [25]. Thus, from the person-environment fit perspective, these opaque performance appraisal criteria and processes heavily prevent gig workers from handling the results related to performance appraisals, thus not matching the platform work environment with the promotion of their job crafting. By contrast, based on person-environment fit theory, we consider that performance appraisal criteria and processes that are transparent, explicit, and clear enable them to easily understand how to handle the outcomes associated with the platform performance appraisal setting. The latter consequently helps them easily craft their platform work. Thus, we formally propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. (H3). Performance appraisal transparency is positively related to job crafting.

## 3.4. Mediating effect of performance appraisal transparency

As aforementioned, when formalizing the present platform gover nance model, gig platforms will enable gig workers to widely share and explicitly understand operating rules, procedures, instructions, and communications of these algorithmic management systems. Thus, more transparent and clear performance appraisal systems are facilitated. Previous studies on the conventional workplace have suggested that a firm's performance appraisal system can become a practical tool for subordinates' motivation and development when subordinates consider their performance appraisals as accurate, transparent, as well as fair, thus influencing their active reactions toward their work [55]. Person environment fit theory also points out that workers' perceptions to ward the consistent work environment in turn facilitate organizational and individual outcomes [28]. Thus, owing to the facilitation of plat form formalization governance, gig platforms' performance appraisal criteria and processes that become more explicit and clearer will pro mote gig workers to perceive a consistent platform work environment, thus helping them cope with many hardships on gig platforms by actively changing the task and relational boundaries of their work $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ an increase in job crafting), which also means an improvement in gig platforms' governance effectiveness. ## 3.5. Moderating effect of platform work insecurity climate

On the one hand, the business model in the gig economy erodes labor protections and facilitates gig workers' economic insecurity [8]. On the other hand, gig workers are undergoing planning insecurity, a lack of transparency in performance appraisals, as well as inadequate clarity in task briefings, thereby inducing perceived performance insecurity in gig work [29]. Considered together, in the emerging gig economy, gig workers are generally suffering from a platform work insecurity climate.

In its essence, person-environment fit theory emphasizes that in dividuals are likely to fit into the organization when the organization and its workers share similar fundamental characteristics, or individuals needs or desires are achieved by the organization and/or both, which in turn affects their work behaviors, attitudes, and performances [33,34]. Similarly, platform work insecurity climate means that gig workers needs for maintaining platform work will be threatened by gig platforms that do not offer social insurance and protections, a risk which also initiates the precarious nature of gig work [8,29]. Given such insecure climate, gig workers cannot perceive similar characteristics and are unlikely to fit into crafting their platform work, which thus negatively influences the perceptive strength of the platform governance environ ment. Thus, we formally propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5. (H5). Platform work insecurity climate weakens the positive relationship between platform formalization governance and job crafting.

Based on person-environment fit theory, we continue to extend the aforementioned logic to the formalization governance of gig platform ecosystems. Formalizing the present governance model of gig platforms can indeed make algorithmic management systems' rules, instructions, and procedures, among others, become explicit and clear, thus improving their performance appraisal systems' transparency. However, prior research has also suggested that the online insecure environment constrains gig workers to use the platform's technological advantages and features to succeed [30]. From the person-environment fit perspective, thus, gig workers who perceive a strong level of platform work insecurity climate might weaken the perceptive strength of ad vantages that platform formalization governance brings (e.g., more transparent performance appraisal systems) because they do not prefer to fit into the platform governance model due to the dissatisfaction of their demands for work security. In other words, with a higher degree of platform work insecurity climate, gig workers who are unwilling to fit into the platform formalization governance will be less likely to perceive transparent performance appraisal systems that the formalization in the current platform governance mechanism brings. Then, a decline in the perceptive strength of advantages that platform formalization gover nance creates will reduce the likelihood of overcoming gig work hard ships by those who suffer from platform work insecurity climate; this scenario happens when workers scarcely adjust their platform work to better align with their objectives, values, passions (i.e., job crafting) [12]. Thus, we formally propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 6. (H6). Platform work insecurity climate weakens the positive relationship between platform formalization governance and performance appraisal transparency.

Hypothesis 7. (H7). Platform work insecurity climate weakens the indirect positive relationship between platform formalization governance and job crafting through the mediation of performance appraisal transparency.

## 4.1. Data collection procedures

Some researchers believe that digital gig platforms bear different managerial characteristics and different levels of work insecurity [6,29,47]. Hence, they resolve their research questions based on a spe cific gig platform context, including Amazon Mechanical Turk (M-Turk) [10], Uber [14], and DidiTax [26], among others. However, some scholars also investigate any type of gig workers and any type of gig platforms rather than focusing on a specific gig platform background to obtain the variances of all measured variables [13,37]. Ultimately, we decided to adopt the latter's research design to survey how different levels of platform formalization governance affect gig workers' job crafting through the mediating effect of different levels of performance appraisal transparency and the moderating effect of different levels of platform work insecurity climate. Accordingly, our findings can be generalized in the gig economy context.

With 2.8 million's users from different industries, Credamo (http s://www.credamo.com) is a professional platform in China, which is similar to Amazon Mechanical Turk (M-Turk). ## 5. Data analysis

Our data analysis includes verification of common method bias, assessment of global model fit, and assessments of measurement and structural models. The partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) method enables us to handle both the measurement (outer) and structural (inner) models simultaneously [59]. It is also an appro priate method for analyzing path models with a small sample size as well as testing the causal relationships between variables [13]. Thus, we use the SmartPLS 3.2.9 version based on the PLS-SEM method to first assess the measurement model and then the structural model.

## 5.1. Common method bias

With an aim to reduce the threat of common method bias (CMB), we used several procedural remedies during the research design, such as simplifying our questions, protecting against double-barreled questions and the dependence between independent and dependent constructs, and inserting a screening question with the expected answer in the middle of the questionnaire [60]. However, our data were admittedly self-reported by respondents, thus necessitating verification whether CMB can heavily threaten our results through several statistical, posthoc analyses.

First, by using the IBM SPSS 20 version, Harman's single facto analysis demonstrated that the accumulative variance of the first factor was 29.932%, which was obviously less than the recommended cutoff of 40%; thus, CMB was not severe in the current study [60]. Second, extremely high construct correlations can develop the threat of CMB. Nevertheless, the maximum correlation between our constructs was 0.606 (see Table 6), which was largely below the suggested cutoff of 0.9 [61]. Hence, this finding proved that CMB cannot threaten the current study. Third, we carried out a multicollinearity test which was recog nized as an effective method to identify CMB [62]. As shown in Table 3, all VIF values were significantly below the recommended cutoff of 3.3 [62]. Considered together, through several methods, we ensured that CMB and multicollinearity cannot become threats to our findings.

## 6. General discussion

Our core research question focuses on how gig platforms build a good governance environment that matches gig workers to help them craft gig work. According to our empirical findings from the person-environment fit perspective, formalization governance of gig platform ecosystems can encourage gig workers to craft their platform work (H1). Meanwhile, it can also improve the transparency of gig platforms' performance appraisal systems (H2). Performance appraisal transparency of gig platforms can promote gig workers' job crafting (H3). Thus, platform formalization governance can also help gig workers craft their platform work by improving performance appraisal transparency (H4). However, we also find that platform work insecurity climate not only can weaken the positive relationship between platform formalization governance and job crafting (H5) but also gig workers' perceptions toward advan tages that platform formalization governance brings (i.e., performance appraisal transparency) (H6), thus further weakening their reaction to craft their platform work (i.e., job crafting) (H7). These findings will help the gig platform clearly know how to establish a good governance environment to promote gig workers' job crafting, thus finally solving our research question.

The current platform governance might benefit gig platforms and customers but may be detrimental to gig workers. By contrast, appro priately formalizing current platform governance can benefit three main parties (gig platforms, gig workers, and customers) in the gig economy, which is supported by our findings. These findings also support previous studies' consideration, that is, relevant stakeholders must resolve the problem of gig workers' data ownership and control and then facilitate other forms of democratic platform governance [15,17]. This finding helps us further point out both theoretical and practical implications which serve as our research contributions. Given the advances in the applications of algorithmic management in gig platform ecosystems [41], we believe that our theoretical and practical implications can improve the current governance of gig platform ecosystems and prevent some negative effects of the platform governance environment on gig workers. Ultimately, given the increasing formalization of platform governance, gig workers have a strong ability to cope with relevant hardships by making an effort to better align their platform work with their objectives, values, passions, and likes.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

We contribute to the gig economy literature in four distinct aspects. First, this study contributes to the literature on the governance of gig platform ecosystems by conceptualizing platform formalization gover nance and exploring its advantages. Given the profound changes in conventional employment brought by gig platforms' emerging business model that is dependent on the ability to hoard and monetize data, some scholars have started to study the uniqueness of gig platforms' gover nance model [3.17,18,20,211. In Table 1, these studies have more or less obtained some interesting conclusions regarding the present platform governance model, but they have thus far remained on the qualitative surface without making a clear direction to build an effective gover nance of gig platform ecosystems. Most of them have posited that the governance of gig platform ecosystems remains a challenge [17,20]. Therefore, the current research status severely hinders the progress in improving the platform governance model and damages its strategic values and effectiveness [20]. In Table 1, we find that extant studies conclusions regarding gig platforms' governance model have been more or less geared toward formalizing the current governance model of gig platform ecosystems. To this end, we base on these studies' theoretical foundations to conceptualize an explicit variable—platform formaliza tion governance. We further empirically determine its advantages, such as the increasing transparency of gig platforms' performance appraisal systems, and the promotion of gig workers' job crafting, among others. Accordingly, we compensate for the current research status by empiri cally exploring how to improve the current platform governance model and exert its strategic values and effectiveness. More importantly, this study emphasizes that scholars can consider how to obtain good syn ergies between informal and formal governance of gig platforms by balancing the interests between the involved three parties (i.e., gig platforms, gig workers, and customers).

Second, this study contributes to the literature on algorithmic man agement systems' transparency by resolving how to improve the problem of the opaque performance appraisal criteria and process. The transparency of algorithm management systems has raised wide concern among relevant stakeholders given that this problem is related to governance [68]. Thus, scholars who extend this concern to the gig economy further find that algorithmic management systems in gig platforms lack transparency, thus limiting many aspects of gig workers (e.g., autonomy, flexibility, agency, and reward) [25,40]. However, the study that actually focuses on the transparency of gig platforms' per formance appraisal systems related to algorithms is scarcely known or even nonexistent. This finding might seriously damage our under standing of algorithmic management systems' transparency in the emerging gig economy because the performance appraisal system is an important link of algorithmic management systems [40]. To this end, we empirically find that formalization governance of gig platform ecosys tems can improve performance appraisal transparency, but platform work insecurity climate can weaken gig workers' perceptions toward such an advantage that platform formalization governance leads to. Accordingly, we not only deepen the understanding of algorithmic management systems' transparency but also encourage scholars to pay attention to the optimizing strategies of gig platforms' performance appraisal systems.

Third, this study contributes to the literature on gig work insecurity by extending the influence of platform work insecurity climate to the problem of optimizing and improving the governance of gig platform ecosystems. Gig work's precariousness is derived from high platform work insecurity because of its on-demand nature as well as the numerous microtasks mediated by digital platforms [10,69]. Thus, the relevant gig economy literature concentrates on the relationship be tween job insecurity and the precarious nature of gig work [29,38]. Little is known regarding platform work insecurity that creates an insecure climate. Thus, we extend platform work insecurity to become a general climate where gig workers perceive fewer advantages of formalization governance of gig platform ecosystems and then craft gig work with difficulty. Accordingly, we not only extend the role of plat form work insecurity in influencing other aspects but also guide scholars to focus on how to reduce its negative effects on the platform governance model's effectiveness.

Fourth, we extend the theoretical implications and adaptability of person-environment fit theory to the gig economy. The gig economy without the hierarchy of the so-called boss-employee relationship is eroding the traditional employment environment, thus creating a digital work environment [15,38]. Thus, gig workers far away from conven tional organizations take part in one style of work different from that assumed via organizational theories [26]. Therefore, the adaptability of person-environment theory as a type of organizational theory in the gig economy needs rigorous verification. By exploring the extent to which gig workers fit into the platform governance environment and platform work insecurity climate, all hypotheses in the present study are sup ported. Thus, the application of person-environment fit theory in the gig economy can be extended.

## 6.2. Practical implications

The current study offers crucial governance practices for digital gig platforms as well as scholars who are interested in the governance mode of gig platform ecosystems. Specifically, the current platform gover nance model bypasses the conventional governance mechanisms, which allows gig platforms to benefit from shifting many business risks to gig workers (e.g., lower labor cost) [8]. Meanwhile, to reduce management costs and maintain customer relationship management, the present platform governance model outsources performance management to customers [41], thus forcing gig workers to exhaust their resources to deliver high-quality services for customers to maintain good ratings [11]. Consequently, gig workers have been seriously exposed to the weakest position in the triangular power chain [9]. Given the present platform governance structure, the ongoing power struggle and information asymmetries between gig platforms/customers and gig workers have become increasingly fierce, which benefits the former [6]. However, this phenomenon heavily damages gig workers' ability to craft their gig work, thus creating an extreme challenge of effectively coping with gig work difficulties [10,11]. Thus, from the person-environment fit perspective, our results emphasize that relevant stakeholders (gig platforms, gig workers, the government, and customers) should recon sider the present governance model of gig platform ecosystems, thus balancing interests between all involved parties.

We suggest that appropriately formalizing platform governance is necessary. The current governance model of gig platform ecosystems lacks a certain transparency of performance appraisal [29,32]. However, results based on performance evaluation/appraisal are very important for gig workers because it tightly involves their reward, remuneration, and even deactivation [40]. Given that gig workers' performance appraisal is tightly related to algorithmic management systems, gig platforms should formalize their governance systems such as algo rithmic control and monitoring, platform rating systems, and so on. Within an acceptable range, they can clarify some rules, instructions, communications, and procedures in these algorithmic management systems to the public. Meanwhile, they also need to consider the pro vision of supplementary mechanisms of upward information transfer, whether in the form of gig worker representation, customer feedback, or platform operators. Thus, when gig workers or customers face some inquiries on platform functioning processes, they can promptly obtain a satisfactory answer through these information transfer mechanisms. These measures formalize the governance environment of gig platform ecosystems, thus promoting the transparency of gig platforms' perfor mance appraisal systems. Ultimately, when explicitly understanding the platform governance model and performance appraisal systems' criteria and processes, gig workers can easily overcome hardships associated with platform work by properly changing platform work procedures. This scenario is also an embodiment of increasing platform governance value and effectiveness.

The present study also emphasizes that digital gig platforms should actively reduce the negative effects of the platform work insecurity climate perceived by gig workers. Platform work insecurity climate is mainly derived from two aspects: planning insecurity and economic income insecurity [29,44], thus leading to the precarious nature of gig work [69]. Thus, when wanting to decrease the dark sides of platform work insecurity climate, gig platforms must consider these two key as pects. On the one hand, planning insecurity means that gig workers can never be sure how many platform tasks can be received and whether they can ultimately obtain a certain remuneration. In fact, planning insecurity is associated with performance appraisal systems' trans parency [29]. From this perspective, gig platforms should make their performance appraisal criteria and processes more explicit, thereby turning back to the importance of platform formalization governance again. On the other hand, economic income insecurity indicates that gig workers must handle relatively low remuneration, no social security benefits, as well as income fluctuations. Hence, within an acceptable range, gig platforms can offer gig workers the advantage of securing certain degrees of subsidy and insurance with the progress of time, which thus improves the insecurity climate even if only a little. These considerations in the two main aspects can mitigate the negative impacts of the platform work insecurity climate perceived by gig workers, therefore enabling them to more significantly realize the strategic values of platform formalization governance. Ultimately, this scenario will enhance their ability to craft their gig work and increase the expected effectiveness of platform formalization governance.

Furthermore, this study points out that customers as participants and the government as regulators should also engage together in formalizing the present platform governance model and in addressing the platform work insecurity climate. First, gig platforms' governance model out sources performance management to customers through platform rating systems for gig workers [41]. Thus, customers can casually decide on gig workers' job performance or even deactivation on the platform, which damages the fair principle that should ideally rely on both sides and makes gig workers feel insecure [44]. Meanwhile, this situation is also adverse for gig platforms that hope to use reasonable and accurate customer ratings to improve workers' performance and their core ad vantages. Thus, as the intermediary party, gig platforms should cultivate customers' civilization accomplishment. By formalizing the current governance model of gig platform ecosystems, gig platforms enable customers to more clearly understand and be likely to comply with the operating rules, procedures, instructions, and communications in various managerial information systems. Second, numerous gig workers have been far away from extant labor protection policies, which causes feelings of insecurity among gig workers [9]. However, the government has not served as an effective regulator that protects them, even though gig workers are in such a weak position because of emerging regulatory loopholes in the digital platforms' governance model [70]. Thus, as the policy-maker, the government should drive the policy-making trend toward appropriately formalizing the present platform governance model while reconsidering the applicability of extant labor protection policies in the gig economy.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

The present research is not without any limitation. First, although the SmartPLS analysis based on PLS-SEM method is somewhat capable of improving the conjecture of causal relationship [71] and our study has no threat of CMB, our data are cross-sectional and influenced by respondents' self-reporting information. Thus, collecting the multi-time longitudinal data through field experiments or other approaches is necessary, which enables our findings to attain better verification. Sec ond, we conceptualize platform formalization governance and further explore its important consequences (i.e., performance appraisal trans parency, and job crafting) and key boundary condition (i.e., platform work insecurity climate). Nonetheless, future research should deeply explore other consequences and boundary conditions of platform formalization governance. Third, despite having analyzed informal and formal governance practices of gig platform ecosystems in the theoret ical background section, we did not further explore whether, how, and when the dynamic change between them can achieve good synergies. Thus, future research can consider analyzing the dynamic change be tween informal and formal governance of gig platform ecosystems and its consequences. Fourth, our survey is situated in China; thus, a straightforward generalization of our conclusions to other regional and cultural backgrounds is possibly inappropriate. Researchers can thus collect data among gig workers from other countries to generalize our results to other cultures.
