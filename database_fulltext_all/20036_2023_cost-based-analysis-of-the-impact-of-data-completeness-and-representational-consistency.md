## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cost-based analysis Data quality Completeness Consistency

## A B S T R A C T

Data quality is an important topic for businesses and therefore requires appropriate analysis tools. Although several rule-based systems exist today for quality measurement, their results do not always reflect the real impact of quality issues on practical data usability and are therefore not well-suited to base economic decisions on. This work practically implements and evaluates an alternative, cost-based approach for data quality analysis starting from a ‘fitness for use’-perspective. The practical impact of completeness and representational consistency of data stored in an integrated relational database is investigated in an experiment with 218 volunteers. Two alternative versions of this database are then prepared by manually improving their data quality. Participants are randomly assigned to one of three databases and are given a set of questions to resolve by means of SQL. As questions are resolved, we measure several cost-based indicators such as abilit to solve, time to solve and number of attempts. Results indicate that the impact of data quality issues can differ significantly from what would be expected when using rule-based measurement. Effects range from almost no impact to a 65% reduction in time needed to solve tasks. Effect sizes up to 0.43 using one-way ANCOVA tests are observed.

## 1. Introduction

In the last couple of decades, enormous amounts of data have been created, hereby impacting business processes. Data enable companies to gain competitive advantage and make data-driven decisions [1,2]. As a consequence, decision quality and productivity are directly impacted by the quality of the underlying data [3]. Data quality is thus extremely important for corporate decision support systems [3,4]. However, in practice data quality is often low, leading to recurring data quality related problems [2,5,6]. According to Gartner, the average annual cost of poor data quality per organization is \$15 million [7]. Consequently, research concerning the assessment and improvement of data quality has received a lot of attention [8].

From the very beginning, data quality has been approached as a multi-dimensional concept, leading to a variety of measures for differ ent quality dimensions [9]. Most of them handle data quality from a common perspective: qualitative data are data that adhere to a number of predefined rules [10]. This rule conformity is then expressed using an ordinal quality level. Rule-based measurement certainly contributes to the understanding of data quality that we have today, but also has disadvantages. First, it describes data quality using an ordinal scale. While ordinal numbers enable quality comparison (which dataset is more qualitative?), they do not quantify quality differences [11,12]. Moreover, assigned quality levels can only be interpreted within the context of the measurement system. Because of this, it can be difficult to understand the practical meaning of rule-based results. Hence, managers often do not know how to adequately handle data quality [13]. As an example, rule-based results typically do not allow to decide whether a given investment in data quality improvement should be made or not. Indeed, it is very difficult to estimate the impact of actions to improve data quality on the perceived usefulness of the data. In 2004 already, Eppler and Helfert mentioned that ‘‘if the data quality field would be to make significant progress in terms of its acceptance in the business world, the costs associated with low data quality must be made more explicit, prominent, and measurable’’ [14]. Since then, research has however mainly focused on (1) technical quality measures that do not always have a clear connection with its practical impact, and on (2) usage of utility theory, whereby it is often unclear how it should be practically applied [15]. It is thus clear that a better understanding of the connection between data quality and economic impact is needed for enhanced decision-making [16]. Second, rule-based measures typically consider data quality to be a purely intrinsic property. Nonetheless, it has been shown that quality also depends on the context in and purpose for which data are used [17]. Despite the importance of context, Illari and Floridi argue that until now, the research field of computer science has not fully embraced the fact that data quality requires a purposedependent approach [17,18]. As such, it has not yet become clear how to apply purpose-dependent approaches to analyze data quality. Third, evaluation of rule-based measurement typically involves human judge ment (e.g., data quality checklists). Consequently, rule-based systems produce inherently subjective results. This can lead to low reliability of the obtained results. Stegenga found that in the biomedical context rule-based tools have low inter-rating operability and low inter-tool operability [17,19].

In order to circumvent these drawbacks, other approaches have been proposed. Notable here is data quality as fitness for use [20]. This originates from the ideas coined by Juran, who stated that data quality can be defined as the fitness of the data to serve a purpose in a given context [21,22]. Because of this broad definition, until now it has been difficult to put this idea into practice [22]. Recently however, a framework was proposed that formalizes the ‘fitness for use’-principle, surpasses the ordinal scale and does not require subjective assessment [11]. Quality is hereby analyzed by measuring the cost that is associated with solving actual tasks using the data. Despite its potential, to our knowledge, no research has been performed that investigates the practical usability of such a cost-based framework for data quality measurement. This work aims to bring change to that by applying costbased analysis in the context of the interesting use case of analyzing scientific data completeness and representational consistency. Most importantly, this work describes a practical implementation of cost-based data quality analysis. Second, it is illustrated how cost-based results are complementary to results obtained by traditional quality measures and how they can help businesses in gaining insights. It should thereby be noted that, similarly to many measurement techniques, it is not the aim of cost-based analysis to indicate how data quality should be improved. Instead, it allows for the quantification of how large the actual impact of quality improvements is.

Cost-based analysis is a context-aware technique, implying that the results that are obtained during such an analysis, are relative to the scenario that is being investigated. In order to demonstrate the merits of cost-based quality analysis, it is therefore important to consider a relevant context to apply it in. An important and frequently occurring quality problem in practice stems from the difficulty that arises when trying to combine multiple, co-referring datasets in an integrated way in order to obtain new insights. Indeed, in practice, numerous well known and freely available datasets exist that refer to other datasets, but combining their data is often far from trivial because of qual ity issues (such as identifier incompleteness or inconsistencies). An important example of this can be found in the context of combining in formation from the co-referring online scientific datasets PubMed [23], ORCID [24] and GRID [25]. A quick look makes clear that, although the datasets contain mutual information, practically combining the data is difficult due to deteriorated data quality. Several values are missing, while other values are differently represented across the three datasets. A survey from 2011 indicates that 63% of the financial institutions say that ‘‘inconsistency (value and format) and diversity of data sources are main recurring challenges’’ [8]. Therefore, we believe that the use case of improving data completeness and consistency for smooth data integration forms an excellent context to investigate the practical applicability of cost-based analysis. Cost-based analysis is applied by setting up an experiment involving human subjects who have to solve tasks Each task can only be solved by combining data from at least two of the datasets. Tasks were constructed such that the task set in its entirety is diverse and representative for those operations that a typical scientist that regularly works with datasets such as ORCID, GRID and/or PubMed would want to perform in order to obtain combined insights from the data. One group of subjects has to solve these tasks using an integrated relational database containing data originating from the original datasets. Two other subject groups need to solve the same tasks by using two other integrated databases that are created by starting from the original database and subsequently applying actions to improve completeness and representational consistency. In the remainder of this work, the original PubMed, ORCID and GRID data files are referred to as datasets, while the integrated databases are referred to as databases. The impact of data quality is then evaluated by looking at the usability of the data to solve the tasks (i.e., task complexity). We can summarize the main results and contributions of this work as follows:

1. To our knowledge, this is the first work to practically apply a cost-based procedure for analysis of data quality impact. An experiment is set up in which 218 subjects have to solve six tasks by combining data that originally stem from different datasets. We work with a database containing data from three existing datasets, and two enhanced database versions. As such, our work provides businesses with an alternative approach for data quality analysis and the understanding of how, amongst others, decision making may be affected by it.

2. The results of the cost-based approach are used to get insights into the extent to which improving data quality (e.g., by filling up missing values) impacts usability of the data and, hence, decision-making.

3. Contextual factors are observed that can possibly influence the impact that data quality has (e.g., learning and fatigue effects).

The remainder of this work is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses related work, after which the methodology is elaborated on in Section 3. Section 4 gives an overview of the results, whereas in Section 5 these results are discussed. Finally, conclusions, implications and future work are discussed in Section 6.

## 2. Related work

## 2.1. Data quality dimensions

Data quality is a multi-dimensional concept [9,20,26]. The most prominent dimensions in literature are completeness, consistency, cor rectness and timeliness [10,27]. In this work, focus is given to the latter two. First, completeness is here defined as ‘‘the extent to which data are not missing, cover the needs of the tasks and are of sufficient breadth and depth to the task at hand’’ [20]. We analyze completeness assuming a closed world [28]. Stated differently, we use the term completeness to refer to the absence of missing values, and not to the fact that entities could be missing in the data. An overview of existing completeness measures is given in [29]. Most of these measures are based on comparison of the number of missing values to the total number of values [30–33]. Second, representational consistency relates to ‘‘the extent to which data are always presented in the same format and are compatible with previous data’’ [20]. Stated differently, the representation of a data value should be the same in all cases. To our knowledge, almost no research concerning representational consistency measurement has been performed. A straightforward measure calculates the percentage of values that should be replaced by a consistent value in order to obtain perfect consistency [34]. Moreover, Blake and Mangiameli mentioned that they were developing another representational consistency measure [35]. However, their utility-based approach is only applicable within the context of data mining and the authors note that their work is preliminary. In conclusion, the available mea sures for both completeness and representational consistency, although useful, suffer from the drawbacks mentioned in Section 1.

## 2.2. Data quality and its impact

Apart from the dimension-specific approaches, literature focuses on the relationship between data quality, usability and economic implications. First of all, several studies investigated the types of costs associated with poor quality. Eppler and Helfert [14] propose a framework in which costs can be categorized in two classes: (1) costs caused by low quality and (2) costs associated with improving or assuring quality. Costs that belong to the first group can be further divided into direct and indirect costs, while costs of improving quality are further divided into prevention, detection and repair costs. Moreover, the authors estimate the share of the different cost types in the total quality cost in function of the exact data quality level. They conclude that a theoretical data quality optimum exists that has the lowest associated cost. Haug et al. build further on this model to define the optimal data maintenance effort, while also proposing an elaborated cost classification framework [13]. However, both studies are not based on empirical evidence, but on observations made in other quality domains. Therefore, they stress the importance of further empirical work.

Second, several authors tried to gather empirical evidence for the impact of data quality. As an example, several studies investigated the direct effect of data quality on machine learning model performance (see for example [34]). However, impact of data quality can also be indirect. As an example, Borek et al. propose a data quality model that assesses risks associated with low quality and that was built and refined using six industrial use cases [15]. Although the results obtained by this model are definitely considered useful by surveyed managers, a fundamental problem remains that it heavily relies on subjective assessment, for example for parameter estimation. Hazen et al. conducted a longitudinal study in a military context to investigate the impact of data quality improvement actions by surveying and observing employees before and after quality was improved [36]. However, results are again solely based on subjective and qualitative assessment. A final study was performed by Samitsch [37]. This work studies the impact of nine quality dimensions in the context of a company that should decide on how many bottles to produce. Some participants were given low quality data to base their decisions on, others received perfect data. The impact of data quality on decision-making time and quality was evaluated. This first attempt to estimate data quality impact for decision making is certainly valuable, although the study comes with several limitations. Data quality is only subjectively assessed, such that questions can be raised concerning reliability. As an example, the mean data completeness was deemed almost identical for all treatment groups, although for some groups only half of the data were available. Moreover, only 87 participants participated in the experiment. Finally, the competence of the participants was not taken into account.

Thirdly, some authors have adopted the definition of data quality in terms of fitness for use [20,38,39]. Practical implementations are however scarce. Relevant work adopting the ‘fitness for use’-perspective was done by Yeganeh et al. [40]. They propose a data quality-aware query system. Here, the actual data that are returned by collaborative information systems depend on the quality preferences of the user querying the system. The underlying data source having the highest quality with respect to the user preferences is queried and returns the data. However, selection of the most qualitative data source is performed using traditional quality measures. Although the overall system adopts the idea of fitness for use, underlying quality measures may thus fail to reflect the practical considerations of the end user. Other authors have brought the concept of cost directly into quality measures. Konijn et al. adopt the idea of annotating data with an associated cost in the problem of subgroup discovery [41]. In the context of data quality, the adoption of costs as a potential quality measure has been theoretically proposed in [11]. They argue that quality can be expressed as an inverse of the costs that are associated with solving tasks using these data. As such, they adopt both the ‘fitness for use’-perspective on data quality and surpass the ordinal quality scale. However until now, the proposed cost-based procedure has not been used for estimating the impact of actual quality issues.

## 3. Data and methods

This section describes the data and methodology used to practically set up a cost-based experiment. The following subsections contain all information that is necessary to understand the approach that was taken. However, datasets and other information that is related to the study but not crucial to the reader’s understanding (e.g., informed consents . . . ) are available in an OSF-repository.<sup>1</sup>

## 3.1. Use case

The use case considered in this work consists of three public scientific datasets. Firstly, PubMed is a repository that contains biomedical articles and that grants each article a unique PubMed ID. Secondly, ORCID is an initiative to uniquely identify scientists with an ORCID ID, and to annotate researcher’s profiles with, for example, publications and affiliations. Finally, GRID is a dataset that contains data about research-related organizations, each being uniquely identified using a GRID ID. An alternative unique identifier that is available for some (grant-giving) organizations in the GRID dataset is the FundRef ID [42]. The PubMed, ORCID and GRID data partially overlap, making the combination of the data potentially synergistic. In an ideal scenario, the three datasets would be highly interoperable. However, in reality, combining the datasets is difficult. For example, many articles present in ORCID do not have an associated PubMed ID, and reverse. Moreover, the same author is sometimes present multiple times in ORCID, accompanied by different ORCID ID representations. It is clear that this absence and/or unstructured presence of identifiers lowers data quality, of which the impact can be analyzed using a cost-based approach.

Cost-based analysis takes into account the purposes of the data when evaluating the impact of their quality. It is thus necessary to define an appropriate context in which quality is analyzed. Here, this is done by creating a set consisting of six tasks (see Table 1). Each task requires data from at least two datasets to be solved. The task set covers different aspects of PubMed, ORCID and GRID. As such, cost-based analysis is conducted in a credible context.

## 3.2. Data

Starting from the downloaded datasets, an integrated PostgreSQL database (called $D _ { A } )$ was constructed that contained PubMed, ORCID and GRID data. Moreover, two alternative versions of this database, $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C } ,$ , were created by performing several data quality improving actions. As such, three versions of the integrated database were obtained (see Fig. 1).

## 3.2.1. Initial database $( D _ { A } )$

$D _ { A }$ was constructed by downloading the data, transforming them and storing them in a PostgreSQL database. Hereby, relational tables were created for (1) each entity type and (2) each binary many-tomany relationship type between entity types in the original data files. Only a subset of attributes of the original datasets (i.e., those that are relevant to the task set) was stored in the database to prevent unnecessary complexity (see Fig. 1). Moreover, only a subset of the original data records was stored because we wanted to prevent to overload the machine on which the databases were running. However, limiting the number of attributes and records did not have any impact on the presented observations and conclusions. The number of records was reduced by only considering PubMed articles containing the term ‘‘microbiome’’ and being published in 2018 or later. This lead to a final number of 31,128 publications. Publications containing the term ‘‘microbiome’’ were considered interesting since we know, based on earlier experience, that these are typically heavily interconnected.

Once the PubMed data were retrieved, all associated ORCID authors were downloaded using the ORCID API. Finally, the full GRID dataset was downloaded.

3.2.2. Additional databases $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$

Two additional versions of $D _ { A }$ were created by improving data quality: an intermediate $( D _ { B } )$ and a final version $( D _ { C } )$ . While creating $D _ { B }$ (starting from $D _ { A } )$ and $D _ { C }$ (starting from $D _ { B } ) ,$ a few structural changes were made:

1. In creating $D _ { B }$ from $D _ { A } ,$ , duplicate rows were removed from PubMed by merging authors that have the same firstname and lastname and that have at least one institute in common at which they were employed. As such, table size was reduced from 256,033 to 162,615 rows. The ORCID ID of the merged author was chosen to be its best available representation (i.e., the representation that is used in ORCID).

2. In creating $D _ { C }$ from $D _ { B } ,$ again rows in PubMed depicting the same author were merged, this time by merging rows having the same ORCID ID. As such, the number of author rows was reduced even further to 154,802.

3. In $D _ { A } ,$ PubMed contains unstructured textual information about the institute at which an author was working when publishing a paper. However, in creating $D _ { B }$ from $D _ { A } { \mathrm { : } }$ , GRID identifiers were added in PubMed where possible. This was done by per forming (sub)string matching of the affiliation string in PubMed and the names of the scientific institutes in GRID. In total, 93,359 associations could be identified.

4. The database normalization level was improved by creating separate tables in $D _ { B }$ for concepts within the data domain that can be clearly distinguished as separate entity types but for which no separate table exists in $D _ { A } .$ Earlier research has already indicated that good database design impacts the extent to which databases are easy to use [12].

In the case of step 1, the affiliation strings and author names that are used to merge authors contain detailed information (firstname, initials, lastname, institute, department, and sometimes an address or email address). For step 2, rows were only merged if they had the same ORCID ID. In the case of step 3, GRID IDs are assigned by finding, for every GRID institute, the affiliations in PubMed that contain the institute name as a substring. All institute names were hereby written out fully in the GRID dataset. Finally, for step 4, no new data were inserted. Combining all information, it can be concluded that, although it is possible that steps 1, 2 and 3 introduce a very small number of errors in $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C } ,$ the validity of the steps is guaranteed. This is further underpinned by the fact that, for each step, the correctness of 100 randomly chosen added data values was examined and not a single mistake was found. Next to the structural changes that were made to improve data quality, small scale actions were also taken to improve the quality of the data that are relevant for the tasks at hand. For each reference type (e.g., ORCID IDs) a standardized way of representing the identifiers (e.g., ‘‘0000-0001-8861-1305’’) was chosen. These standardized representations were then used to replace missing references with to create $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ . Moreover, these standardized representations were also used to replace alternative identifiers (e.g., ‘‘0000000188611305’’).

## 3.3. Methodology

A theoretical framework to analyze data quality is needed to practically apply cost-based measurement. We start from the framework proposed in [11]. They formalize the connection between data quality and cost by calculating the total effort needed to solve a set of tasks $\mathcal { T } = \{ t _ { 1 } , \dots , t _ { m } \}$ with the help of a dataset ??. Here, whether or not a task can be completed is a Boolean matter.

When a task cannot be completed, data must be modified by using a sequence of transformations that each have an associated cost. For each task, the goal is to find the sequence of transformations that (1) allows to complete that task and (2) comes with a minimal sum of costs. Applying this formal model, data quality for task ?? is inversely proportionate to the minimal cost associated with completing ??. Data thus has perfect quality if all tasks can be solved without any transformation being required and has the lowest possible quality if it is impossible to solve some tasks, regardless of changes we make.

Cost-based principles were practically applied by setting up an experiment, approved by an ethical committee, in which 218 subjects participated. A schematic overview of the experiment design is given by Fig. 2. All participants were undergraduate students that just finished an introductory database course. Initially, 336 subjects were invited to participate. All of them were asked to (voluntarily) sign an informed consent. Moreover, all subjects were randomly assigned to either $D _ { A } , D _ { B }$ or $D _ { C }$ (112 participants for each database). Due to practical reasons and in order to guarantee a sufficiently high number of participants, the deadline for returning informed consents was extended and group assignment was already performed before receiving all informed consents.

Eventually, we ended up with 218 participants (response rate 69%). This finally lead to 66 participants working on $D _ { A } ,$ 72 on $D _ { B }$ and 80 on $D _ { C }$ . Being assigned to one of the three database versions, each participant had to solve as many tasks as possible within a given time frame. Participants were able to interact with the database by using an online, in-house developed, query platform. On this platform, SQL queries could be submitted that are passed through to the database. By comparing the returned data with the data returned by the model solution, the platform decides on the query correctness. If wrong, subjects could correct their query and submit it again. Participants were able to submit as many queries as needed to find a correct solution. All submitted queries were registered in a database, together with a participant ID, a timestamp and the task number. All participants had followed an SQL course in the weeks preceding the experiment and task solving only required concepts that were taught during this course. Moreover, participants were also accommodated with the online query platform in the weeks preceding the experiment. Therefore, they were sufficiently trained to successfully participate. The tasks were presented in the same order as in Table 1, but could be solved in the order of preference.

Participants were granted 70 min for solving, individually, as many tasks as possible. This choice was based on the fact that the SQL exercise sessions that were organized before the experiment also took 70 min. Moreover, this time span forms a good compromise between making it doable for participants to solve several tasks on the one hand, and preventing the experiment from taking too long and demotivate participants on the other hand. Finally, cost measures were chosen that reflect task solving complexity and, hence, fitness for use. Three wellsuited measures were considered: the ability to solve the tasks, the amount of time and the number of attempts needed to solve the tasks

## 4. Results

In this section, cost-based results are presented. For each database an overview is given of the fraction of participants that is capable of solving the tasks and the amount of time that those subjects needed to construct a correct SQL query. Statistical significance of the observed differences is verified. Finally, a qualitative analysis of the (number of) submitted queries is performed.

## 4.1. Percentage of participants finding correct solutions $( P _ { c o r r e c t } )$

A first important aspect of the impact of data quality that was examined is the ability of participants to solve the tasks at hand using the databases.

Table 3 gives, for each task and database, the percentage of partici pants that is capable of constructing a correct query $( P _ { c o r r e c t } )$ . For each task, three tests were performed to determine statistical significance of the observed differences in $P _ { c o r r e c t }$ (the dependent variable) between the independent variable (i.e., Database, taking on the values $D _ { A } ,$ $D _ { B }$ or $D _ { C } )$ . This was done by building binomial logistic regression models in SPSS. The binomial logistic regression model tries to predict a dichotomous outcome (in this case, whether the participant is capable of solving the task or not) using one or more independent variables. Apart from the independent variable in which we are mainly inter ested $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ , Database), we also included two covariates to control for, since they possibly impact the outcome. These covariates are (1) the SQL competence of the participants and (2) the exact time at which participants started solving a given task (the later, the more difficult it was to find a solution). Individual SQL competence was expressed as a score between 0 and 1. This score was the result of six assignments (consisting of four SQL tasks each) that were organized in the context of the database course that all participants followed. These assignments were all handed out in a time period of (at most) 25 days before the start of the experiment, and participants had one week to complete each individual assignment.

An important difference between the assignments and the actual experiment is that the experiment lasted for only 70 min, while partici pants were granted one week to finish the assignments. This difference could impact the accuracy of the assignment scores as a measure of SQL competence in our experiment. However, we believe that our choice is justifiable. Most importantly, there are several reasons to believe that the impact of the time difference on the representativeness of the assignment scores is limited. First, the remaining time was included as a covariate in all analyses. Second, apart from the amount of time, all other organizational aspects were identical: tasks descriptions and database information were formulated similarly, the same query tool was used . . . . Third, the participants were informed extensively that their performance would not have any impact on the grades that they would eventually obtain for the course. As such, we tried to diminish (time) pressure for the students. Finally, the number of tasks offered during the experiment was limited in comparison to the time frame granted to the participants. Moreover, alternative ways to measure SQL competence come with disadvantages as well. The alternative of organizing the second session right before the actual experiment was not considered to be a good option as well, since that would imply that participants would already be tired and demotivated when starting the actual experiment.

## 4.3. Solution path analysis

Tables 4 and 5 indicate that significant differences exist between the databases in terms of ability and time needed to solve tasks. Although it is clear that task complexity differs, it would be interesting to see how these differences are reflected in the content of the submitted queries. Therefore, we investigated the solution path, i.e., the sequence of all incorrect queries that participants submitted before solving the task. This qualitative content analysis is referred to as solution path analysis. Such an analysis could deliver insights regarding the extent to which problems encountered by participants during task solving are related to data quality. We introduce solution path analysis as an essential part of cost-based analysis to obtain insights concerning the impact of data quality.

The analysis was performed by manually investigating all SQL queries that were submitted by participants that correctly solved the tasks. For each incorrect query, the reason(s) why this query was not correct was/were identified. Mistakes could range from syntax errors to not taking into account that the same identifier is stored inconsistently in the database. After analyzing all erroneous queries, for each task and error type, the number of corresponding queries were counted. If multiple error types were present in a query, each error type was attributed an equal fraction. As such, for each task an overview was obtained of the total number of queries for each error type. Finally, error types were divided in two categories: those that were (1) caused or (2) not caused by data quality problems. As an example, not taking into account the fact that an ORCID ID can have multiple representations is an error that arises from representational inconsistency, while a syntax error is made independently of quality problems. The presented results allow for several interesting insights, both regarding the impact of data quality and the usefulness of cost-based analysis itself.

## 5.3. Structured identifiers have a considerable impact on data usability

To solve tasks 3 and 5, it is necessary to combine information from PubMed and GRID. The PubMed tables in $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ contain a correctly represented GRID ID for every row that is needed for this task, while no GRID IDs are present in PubMed in $D _ { A } .$ Instead, the PubMed table in $D _ { A }$ only contains unstructured text referring to the affiliation. It was therefore expected that fitness for use of the data for these tasks would be much higher for $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ in comparison to $D _ { A } .$ Indeed, Tables 3 and 6 indicate for task 3 a large reduction of 60% in $T _ { s o l v e d } ,$ 80% in the number of queries needed when comparing $D _ { A }$ with the other databases, and a clear increase in the fraction of participants that is able to solve the task (from 8% to 73%). These differences are significant (see Table 5). Moreover, the average number of data quality related queries drops from 19 for $D _ { A }$ to 2 for $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ (Table 6). Since the number of queries that a participant submits is related to the total time that a participant needs to solve a task, it is clear that the observed differences are impacted by the extent to which participants encountered data quality issues. Solution path analysis for $D _ { A }$ learns that quality issues are exclusively related to difficulties with extracting information from the affiliation string. On the contrary, using $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ , participants are almost directly capable of combining the information via the GRID ID. As such, it is clear that for task 3 dataset interoperability is heavily improved by the introduction of consistent identifiers. For task 5 as well, significant differences for $P _ { s o l v e d }$ are obtained between $D _ { A } , D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ (see Table 4). Differences in ?? were not significant (see Table 5). ## 5.4. One perfect identifier is better than two complementary ones

For task $^ { 6 , }$ information from ORCID and GRID should be combined by using FundRef IDs and/or GRID IDs. However, for $D _ { A } ,$ rows exist that do not have a consistent GRID ID, nor a consistent FundRef ID. In $D _ { B } ,$ all rows have either a consistent GRID ID or FundRef ID (but never both). Finally, in $D _ { C } ,$ , all rows have at least a consistent GRID ID. We would expect the fitness for use of $D _ { A }$ to be lower compared to $D _ { B } ,$ and similarly for $D _ { B }$ versus $D _ { C }$ . Indeed, statistically significant differences were found for $P _ { s o l v e d }$ and $T _ { s o l v e d }$ between $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C } .$ . Although it is perfectly possible in $D _ { B }$ to relate each row in ORCID to a corresponding row in GRID (by alternating between GRID IDs and FundRef IDs), results clearly indicate that task complexity is heavily reduced when only one type of identifier is needed. No tests could be performed to compare the results for $D _ { A }$ with the other databases, as not a single participant was able to construct a correct query for $D _ { A } .$ . However, this adds evidence to the statement that solving the task with the help of $D _ { A }$ was indeed complex. The numbers thus indicate that a strong increase in the fitness for use of the data can be obtained by the presence of a single identifier type for all rows instead of alternating between different identifier types. However, further research based on a higher number of participants is needed to obtain more precise estimates.

## 5.5. Learning effects possibly impact perceived data quality

Cost-based analysis takes into account data purposes and user characteristics. Their importance in analyzing data quality impact is illustrated by the results for task 2. Task 2 is very similar to task 1, since both require the combination of ORCID with PubMed data. Moreover, for $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C } { \mathrm { : } }$ , completeness and consistency percentages are exactly the same for both tasks. Therefore, we would expect that the results obtained for these tasks for $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ would be similar. Indeed, measured task complexity is lower for $D _ { C }$ than for $D _ { B }$ for task 2 (see Table 3). However, the impact of replacing alternative ORCID representations by one single ORCID ID is less in the context of task 2 than for task 1. This is also reflected in the decreased effect size (see Table 5). A possible explanation for this is the existence of a learning effect. Most of the participants on $D _ { B }$ that started task 2 had already solved task 1. As such, they knew that their database contained imperfect references. Possibly, when starting task 2, participants had already adjusted their mindset (not assuming that all data were perfectly persisted), enabling them to find the solution more quickly. Evidence for this is also provided by solution path analysis. For both $D _ { A }$ and $D _ { B }$ the average number of data quality-related queries drops strongly from around 8 to only 2 (see Table 6), while the number of queries on $D _ { C }$ remains stable. The existence of a learning effect would mean that perceived task complexity does not only depend on task difficulty, but also on earlier user experiences. When organizations want to analyze the data quality impact, it is thus important that they take experience and knowledge into account as well. These findings contradict the intuitive idea that to calculate the return of an investment to make data more fit for use, one could simply measure the gain in the cost that is associated with solving a task, and multiply it by the number of times that this task has to be fulfilled. On the contrary, results illustrate that users can adapt to the difficulties provoked by inferior quality and the impact of quality enhancements may diminish over time.

## 5.6. Data quality issues can propagate

Apart from the possible existence of a learning effect, other factors as well possibly influence the impact of data quality. For tasks 3 and $^ { 5 , }$ databases $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ are identical. Still, differences are observed between them in terms of the ability to solve the tasks (in favor of $D _ { C } )$ Indeed, fractions are higher on $D _ { C }$ in comparison to $D _ { B } ,$ , the difference even being statistically significant for task 5. A possible explanation for this might be that the difficulty of earlier encountered tasks has an impact on the perceived difficulty of new tasks. Indeed, tasks 1, 2 and 4 were perceived as more difficult on $D _ { B } .$ Consequently, some participants solving tasks 3 and 5 on $D _ { B }$ could expect that this task would be difficult as well. Possible reasons for this are that (1) participants on $D _ { B }$ have observed that data are stored incorrectly/incompletely, leading to more difficult solutions than actually needed; (2) effects of fatigue are involved, as participants working on $D _ { B }$ had already encountered difficult tasks, contrary to participants working on $D _ { C } .$ Another unexpected observation that can possibly be explained by the impact of previous tasks is that for task 3, participants on $D _ { A }$ submit far more queries that are wrong due to data quality issues (on average around 11) compared to $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ participants (around $^ { 3 , }$ see Table 6). We would expect query problems that are not related to data quality to be more or less equally present across all three databases. Again, a possible explanation for this is that the difficulty of previously encountered tasks on $D _ { A }$ (in comparison to $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C } )$ impacts solving ability for new tasks. In conclusion, it could be that earlier experienced data quality-related difficulties have an impact on the ability to solve new tasks, even if these do not include any quality-related difficulties. Again, results indicate that the practical impact of data quality is a very complex topic that requires empirical, contextual evidence.

## 5.7. Cost-based analysis should be carefully applied

## 5.7.1. Average time at which task solving is started impact time estimates

One important source of research bias is the fact that the estimated time values in Table 3 are impacted by the remaining time that partic ipants had when they started solving a task. We observe both a direct and indirect impact of the remaining time on $T _ { s o l v e d } .$ . As a direct effect, the results in Table 5 indicate that the remaining time has a significant impact on $T _ { s o l v e d }$ in two cases. The indirect effect of the remaining time on $T _ { s o l v e d }$ is illustrated by Table 4. Indeed, remaining time has a significant impact on the ability of users to finish a task in 12 of the 16 investigated cases. Consequently, the remaining time also has a significant impact on the subgroup that is used in Table 3 to measure $T _ { s o l v e d } ,$ and therefore indirectly impacts $T _ { s o l v e d } .$ . In conclusion, although the differences in remaining time for a task do not affect the statistical significance of our results, we should be aware that it impacts precision of the measured time values.

## 5.7.2. Differences in SQL competence impact time estimates

Cost estimates are also, directly and indirectly, impacted by SQL competence. Evidence for the direct impact is found in the one-way ANCOVA results. In three out of 16 cases, SQL competence has a significant impact on the measured time values (see Table 5). There is also an indirect impact, since in four out of 16 cases investigated by binomial logistic regression, SQL competence has a significant impact on task solving ability (see Table 4). Hence, SQL competence impacts the subgroup of participants being able to solve the tasks and, thus, measured values for $T _ { s o l v e d } .$ . This can be clearly observed for task 4. PubMed and ORCID data had to be combined to solve this task by using an ORCID ID or the author name. In $D _ { A } ,$ some rows only have an inconsistent author name, while other rows have either a consistent ORCID ID or author name. In $D _ { B } ,$ all rows have at least one consistent reference (name or ID) to ORCID. Finally, $D _ { C }$ contains a consistent ORCID ID for all rows. Counter-intuitively, no significant time differences were observed in Table 5. A possible explanation for this is that the number of participants that started tasks 4, 5 and 6 clearly drops in comparison with the first three tasks, especially for $D _ { A }$ (see Table 3). The subset of participants that start with task 4 (and further) typically consists of participants that were able to solve the previous tasks, while those that do not start task 4 are still busy solving previous tasks. Therefore, starting from task 4, we are only looking at the ‘‘most competent’’ group of participants (SQL competence is a predictor of ability to solve task $^ { 4 , }$ see Table 4) working on $D _ { A ^ { \prime } }$ . This is confirmed by the fact that, for example, the subset of 37 participants starting task 4 on $D _ { A }$ on average needed 35% less queries to solve task 1 in comparison to the entire group that started task 1 on $D _ { A }$ . As such it can be expected that the values for task 4 for $D _ { A }$ are better than they would be when all participants had been included. In conclusion, the absence of clear differences for task 4 can possibly be explained by the fact that the participants working on $D _ { A }$ are more competent than those working on $D _ { B } ,$ and similar for $D _ { B }$ versus $D _ { C } .$ . Additional evidence for this hypothesis can be found in the fact that the share of data quality-related queries decreases going from $D _ { A }$ to $D _ { B }$ and $D _ { C }$ (see Table 6). On the contrary, the number of queries belonging to the other category increases, while one would expect these numbers to be similar. This is another indication that the participants working on $D _ { A }$ for task 4 are better at constructing correct queries, independently of data quality issues. As such, one could expect that in reality, observed differences for this task would be larger. However, additional research is needed to confirm this.

## 5.7.3. Number of participants impacts conclusiveness

The number of participants that actually started solving task 6 is very low. This has an impact on the conclusiveness of the analyses. Especially for $D _ { A }$ the number of participants was limited, such that no binomial logistic regression and ANCOVA tests could be performed. However, even if one would have been able to solve the task and, as such, we would have been able to conduct ANCOVA tests, it is still probable that no statistically significant result between $D _ { A }$ and $D _ { B } / D _ { C }$ would have been observed. While it could be true that such a significant difference indeed does not exist, we should be aware that small sample sizes tend to decrease statistical power and increase type II error probability [43]. Therefore, it should be clear that it is important to design cost-based experiments such that the number of participants is sufficient for all tasks.

## 6. Conclusion

In this work, we propose to analyze data quality from a ‘fitness for use’-perspective by looking at the complexity to solve a set of tasks. This complexity is expressed in terms of the cost associated with solving tasks using the data. Cost-based analysis is practically implemented via an experiment for the analysis of a use case consisting of three data sets that have completeness and representational consistency problems. It mostly confirmed (and quantified) the increase in data usefulness that was expected due to improvements in data quality. However, it also points out that possibly a number of contextual factors exist that influence the impact of data quality. This work has a number of important implications. Firstly, this paper illustrates, for the first time, how cost based principles allow to make the connection between data quality and its practical impact. The cost-based approach as it is applied in this work (or variants in design) can be of value for businesses that require a detailed, economic analysis of the (impact of the) quality of their data that cannot be fully obtained using traditional approaches. Conducting their own cost-based analysis can provide businesses with useful and contextual information on whether it is appropriate to invest in specific data quality improvements or not. Secondly, the results presented in this work should make businesses aware of the fact that they should be careful when interpreting traditional measurement results. Our experiment illustrates that observations in practice may significantly differ from what would be expected using rule-based measurements. Indeed, it is clear that external factors possibly influence the extent to which data quality impacts the usability of the data. Thirdly, this work is, to our knowledge, the first to make a detailed cost–benefit analysis of how several well-known actions to improve data quality impact data usefulness in a practical setting. The results of this work should give data practitioners an idea of the order of magnitude of the impact that completeness and representational consistency can have on usability.

This study only offers a first impression of how cost-based analysis can help to develop insights concerning data quality impact. Severa options for further work exist. First, our insights should be validated for other use cases. Specifically, additional research concerning the psychological effects is necessary. Secondly, we discovered potential sources of research bias. These can however possibly be prevented by using alternative experimental setups. Alternative designs should be investigated to reproduce results, to make cost-based analysis more practically applicable and to obtain more precise estimates. Thirdly, a limitation of this work is that it only focuses on completeness and repre sentational consistency, while many other dimensions exist. Depending on the specific quality dimension, changes will probably have to be made to the experimental setup. Consequently, it could be that cost based analysis is more useful for certain dimensions than for others. This as well should be investigated further.
