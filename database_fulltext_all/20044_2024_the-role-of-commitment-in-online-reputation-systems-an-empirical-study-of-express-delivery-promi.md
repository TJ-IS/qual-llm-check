# The role of commitment in online reputation systems: An empirical study of express delivery promise in an E-commerce platform

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Electronic commerce Online reputation Express delivery promise Logistics performance Expectation disconfirmation

## A B S T R A C T

Express delivery, enabled by automated warehouse systems, offers a competitive advantage to e-commerce re tailers by shortening order shipping time. It is a common strategy used to attract customers to maintain customer loyalty and improve customer satisfaction on an electronic commerce platform. Yet promising express delivery may heighten customer expectations, subsequently affecting their post-purchase satisfaction and potentially affecting their rating behavior for the logistics service performance. Using a dataset from an e-commerce plat form, we show that orders with an express delivery promise are less likely to be reviewed and are reviewed with a lower rating score compared to orders without an express delivery promise but with the same delivery time. Interestingly, the negative effect persists even if the express order arrives earlier than the promised delivery date. We propose that customers have a preference referencing a higher expectation driven by the promise effect of the express delivery, resulting in a change in rating behavior. We recommend that firms, especially online retailers, should carefully consider anticipated customer expectations and post-purchase reactions when developing an express delivery commitment strategy.

## 1. Introduction

Decision support systems (DSS) play a crucial role in automating warehouse operations and predicting demand, thereby facilitating an express delivery [1] and delivering the order within a short time win dow. E-commerce platforms like Amazon or Alibaba leverage their automated warehouse systems and operational scale to empower seller by providing faster deliveries, ultimately enhancing the customer experience. Once the platform has established a robust supply chain infrastructure, sellers can offer express delivery services, often accom panied by an express delivery promise that assures buyers of receiving their items within a specified short time window, typically communi cated during the checkout process.

The introduction of express delivery services impacts customer satisfaction in two distinct ways: through the delivery speed and the delivery promise. On the one hand, the improved delivery speed en hances customer satisfaction, as evidenced by management literature highlighting increased consumer surplus, improved customer loyalty, and higher customer satisfaction associated with express delivery [2–5], making the offer of express delivery an attractive strategy for sellers.

On the other hand, it is important to consider the potential impact of the express delivery promise on customers' perception of logistic ser vices. Existing literature suggests advantages of the express delivery promise, such as its ability to attract customers for more purchases [6]. However, offering an express delivery promise may also raise buyers' expectations, potentially resulting in a higher likelihood of disappoint ment. In particular, the promise sets higher expectations among buyers and may influence their perception of the platform's service quality. This phenomenon, known as expectation-disconfirmation [7,8], suggests that customer satisfaction stems from the comparison between their perceived product performance, the actual delivery speed in our case, and their initial expectations, which is the promised delivery speed. Specifically, customers who receive express delivery orders may be less impressed with the speedy delivery compared to those who receive regular orders with the same delivery time. This could be due to their heightened expectations driven by the express delivery promise. Consequently, the heightened expectations could potentially impact the formation of ratings in terms of both valence (rating score received) and volume (number of ratings received) [9–11].

While the speed impact of express delivery, such as increasing speed, has been well-established (e.g., [2,3,5,6]), the commitment aspect of offering an express delivery, namely the express delivery promise, has received less attention. However, understanding how the express de livery promise influences online rating formation is critical, particularly since e-commerce sellers heavily rely on online reviews as a signal for their service quality. Therefore, our study aims to address two key questions: 1) Does the express delivery promise affect the volume of logistics service ratings, measured by the number of ratings received? 2) Does the express delivery promise affect the valence of logistic service ratings reflected in the rating score received?

By exploring these questions, we offer important insights to the sellers and platforms offering express delivery service by analyzing a less-discussed aspect of the service—the express delivery promise—and its impact on the seller's reputation as the form of customers' ratings of the sellers, enabling us to evaluate the effectiveness and implications of offering express delivery services.

In this study, we obtained sales and logistics performance data from a business-to-consumer (B2C) e-commerce platform. We compared orders with and without express delivery promises with the same delivery time on the platform to assess the effect of the express delivery promise on the formation of online ratings. Using multiple econometrics specifications, including multiple fixed effects, propensity score matching, and a quasiexperimental setup, we found that orders with express delivery promises receive lower ratings than regular orders, holding everything constant, including actual delivery lead time. This difference persists regardless of whether the express delivery promise is fulfilled or not, indicating that heightened expectations likely lower customer satisfaction and, hence, the valence of the rating. Therefore, in line with the expectation dis confirmation theory [7,8], given an express order that arrives at the same time as a regular order, the heightened expectation for the express order leads to lower satisfaction than the regular order. Furthermore, we examined the effect of express delivery promise on review propensity to study the volume of ratings. Here, we found that express orders are less likely to receive online ratings than regular orders with the same lead time, potentially due to underreporting resulting from lower satisfaction [12]. Thus, the rating volume may decrease with the express delivery promise. Finally, we examined the changes in customers' expectations by studying the types of express delivery promises on the e-commerce platform. Specifically, the platform offers two types of express delivery promises: same-day delivery and next-day delivery. Among these of ferings, customers promised a shorter delivery time $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , same-day de livery) are likely to have higher expectations of delivery speed than customers promised a longer delivery time $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , next-day delivery). We showed that the negative effects of the express delivery promise on the ratings and review propensity are mitigated by offering a longer prom ised time of express delivery. This, again, aligns with the expectation disconfirmation theory [7,8], which suggests that the negative effect of heightened expectations can be lowered by promising a longer promised delivery date.

Our findings have significant implications for e-commerce platform and independent sellers. This paper contributes to the literature related to decision support systems and biases in online ratings, which can distort both the valence and volume of online ratings. Specifically, our study shows that a faster delivery system with promises could poten tially reduce the accuracy of rating systems and lower the overall satisfaction sentiment of the platform. This work has a few notable theoretical and practical implications.

Firstly, our study is one of the few empirical analyses that examine the interplay between two systems: the warehouse automation systems supporting the express delivery speed promises feature and the logistics service rating systems building a reputation for sellers and helping buyers make better decisions [4,13–17]. Unlike most studies in the extant literature on DSS, which consider only the effect of the express delivery promise on the purchase decisions $[ 5 , 6 , 1 8 ]$ , our research delves into the impact of express delivery promises on consumers' postpurchase decisions, specifically their rating behaviors, which has sig nificant implication to the seller's reputation. Notably, our study lever ages a unique dataset to compare express orders to regular orders with the same actual delivery time, allowing us to examine the effect of ex press delivery promises on online reputation systems. Our findings reveal that the express delivery promise has a negative influence on both the rating valence and volume, highlighting an unintended consequence that e-commerce platforms should be aware of when offering express delivery promises, providing an additional consideration for firms devising an express delivery service strategy.

Secondly, our study attempts to deepen our understanding of the sources of bias in online reputation systems [19]. Past literature related to online review discusses the potential pitfalls for underreporting bias, suggesting that customers tend not to report a rating in case of a negative experience [12]. In our case, we show that the express delivery promise has a negative effect on the propensity of leaving a rating. That is, the sellers offering more express delivery promises can potentially receive a less accurate rating. We argue that the express delivery promise heightens the customers' expectations and lowers the satisfaction of the customers with the actual delivery. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to examine how the seller's strategy $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ offering an express delivery promise) affects the underreporting bias, which is rare in the literature. Furthermore, we show that compared to regular orders with the same delivery time, the customers are less likely to rate even when the delivery arrives earlier than the promised delivery date, suggesting that the underreporting bias related to the express delivery promise is persistent. By understanding how customers leave a review on an online platform, platform owners can be better informed and design better mechanisms to incentivize customers to give accurate feedback, which is crucial for improving consumer satisfaction on a platform [20]. For example, platforms can offer higher incentives for customers to leave a rating for express delivery orders.

Lastly, our analyses take into account the effect of improved delivery time by controlling for the order lead time. This enables us to distinguish between the speed effects and the promise effect of the express delivery service. Notably, we observe that orders with express delivery promise receive lower ratings compared to orders without such promises, when they have the same delivery time. This finding highlights the criticality of managing customer expectations when offering express delivery services. When offering express delivery, sellers should be cautious about providing delivery promises, particularly when the promised de livery time is very short. Such promises can create high buyer expecta tions and lead to increased customer dissatisfaction. Therefore, sellers should carefully evaluate the necessity of offering a delivery promise and avoid setting excessively short delivery times to prevent potential backlash from heightened expectations.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Online ratings for product-related service

Online reputation systems play an essential role in online users' decision-making process $( \mathrm { e } . 8 . , [ 9 - 1 1 , 2 1 - 2 5 ] )$ ). For instance, Gu et al. [9] suggest that the valence of online ratings from customers significantly impacts sales. Some earlier research suggests that the volume of reviews or ratings can enhance product awareness [10,11]. Motivated by the importance of online ratings for e-commerce retailers, research has focused on the relationship between online user-generated content and business strategies (e.g., [26]). Clemons et al. [27] show that online reviews may help evaluate a firm's product differentiation strategy.

Existing research acknowledges biases within online reputation systems (e.g., [28,29]), and these biases lead to more extreme ratings [19]. For example, Gao et al. [12] analyzed the impact of perceived service quality on review propensity. The authors show that the offline population's perception of a physician's quality may positively affect review propensity on an online medical review platform. Physicians with lower quality are less likely to be rated on the platform. This leads to underreporting bias in online reviews and makes reputation systems less accurate. Underreporting bias exists when a consumer is less likely to report a rating after encountering a negative experience [28]. While most of the studies solely focus on biases present in the ratings of a product or service, the services associated with the product, such as customer services or, in our study, logistics services, are largely ignored in the literature. For example, Hu et al. [29] focus on biases in online reviews related to product quality, and Li and Hitt [28] focus on biases present in online product reviews due to the timing of the review. However, none of the prior studies on online reviews study biases from a product-related service perspective. Our study fills this gap and differ entiates it from much of the literature related to online reviews by examining biases present in product-related services such as logistics services. Specifically, we are one of the first studies examining how promises offered on logistics services may interplay with online rating formation. Finally, unlike most of the studies in the online review literature focusing on the effect of platform features (e.g., [30–32]), we examine how the pre-purchase seller strategy, such as delivery speed promises, affects the customers' post-purchase logistic service rating behaviors.

## 2.2. Impact of express delivery on Customer's behavior

The existing literature predominantly emphasizes the speed effect of express delivery, focusing on the improved delivery speed and its impact on customers. However, the promise effect of express delivery, which pertains to the commitment of faster delivery, has received less atten tion. On the one hand, faster delivery leads to higher consumer surplus by reducing waiting time and allowing customers to utilize the product sooner. Timely delivery enhances customer loyalty [2,3] and boosts sales [5,6,18]. In fact, reducing delivery time by one day has been shown to increase average daily sales by 13.3% for online retailers. Moreover, faster delivery improves customer satisfaction with the logistics service, leading to better ratings [6]. Online retailers can also charge price premiums and enjoy higher product margins when offering faster de livery commitments [17]. Lastly, expedited delivery reduces the risk of lost or damaged parcels by minimizing the time spent in the logistics system [33], further enhancing customer satisfaction.

On the other hand, while the benefits of improved delivery speed of express delivery have been explored, the impact of the commitment aspect, specifically the promise of express delivery, has received limited attention. Particularly, a service commitment sets expectations about the value customers can expect from the service, reduces anxiety and uncertainty related to product-related services, and implies a guarantee of service quality [34]. It may also enhance the convenience of online shopping [35]. To the best of our knowledge, existing research ha primarily focused on how failure to fulfill the delivery promise di minishes customer satisfaction, affects loyalty, and influences future sales $[ 2 , 4 , 3 6 ]$ . It is possible that the promise of express delivery creates expectations and shapes customer perceptions of the service. Our study contributes to this line of research by investigating the lesser-discussed aspects of express delivery, namely the promise aspect, and its impact on customers. We specifically examine the express delivery promise influ ence on rating formation, considering that the online reputation system is a key attribute of e-commerce sellers. ## 3. Theoretical foundations and hypotheses development

## 3.1. Promised delivery date (PDD) of express orders and rating score

It is widely accepted that customers tend to be less satisfied as they spend more time waiting for their orders to arrive [6]. However, it is less clear how offering an express delivery promise may affect customer satisfaction. The introduction of an express delivery promise has the potential to alter buyers' expectations, influencing their perception of service quality and, consequently, their satisfaction. According to the expectation disconfirmation theory [7,8], customer satisfaction arises from the comparison between perceived service performance and initial expectations. In this context, perceived performance refers to a cus tomer's assessment of the actual delivery time of their orders, whereas the initial expectation is derived from either the promised delivery date for express orders or the customer's past experiences when no promised delivery date is given. Post-purchase satisfaction is influenced by the discrepancy between the perceived performance and the expectation, also known as disconfirmation. Specifically, when perceived perfor mance exceeds customer expectations, a positive disconfirmation of belief occurs, leading to increased satisfaction. Conversely, when perceived performance falls short of expectations, a negative disconfir mation of belief arises, resulting in customer dissatisfaction [8,37]. Importantly, the level of satisfaction is predicated on the magnitude of the disconfirmation in the customers' expectations. This suggests that the degree of satisfaction or dissatisfaction is determined by the differ ence between the customer's initial expectation and their perceived performance of the service.

In the context of our study, customers on the e-commerce platform Tmall typically expect a delivery lead time of about four days for regular orders.<sup>2</sup> However, for express orders, sellers promise delivering in only one to two days. This promise significantly raises customers' expecta tions and subsequently alters their perception of delivery performance. Even when the realized delivery time for both express and regular orders is the same, the heightened expectations resulting from the express de livery promise lead to a less positive disconfirmation of the customer's belief. This, in turn, results in lower satisfaction and, consequently, lower ratings for express orders compared to regular orders. Given that ratings often reflect customer satisfaction [38–41],<sup>3</sup> the rating given to an express order should, systematically, be lower than that for a regular order, assuming the same realized delivery time. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1. Compared to regular orders with the same realized delivery time, consumers are more likely to give a lower rating for orders with an express promise.

## 3.2. Promised delivery date (PDD) of express orders and rating propensity

While online ratings reflect customer satisfaction, the credibility of those ratings depends on how customers leave reviews on the platform. Particularly, consumers do not leave online ratings for products or ser vices in a random fashion. Instead, research shows that consumers may leave reviews in response to their consumption experiences (e.g., [12,28–30]). In our context, since consumer expectations change (or shift) when firms offer an express service commitment, one might naturally wonder how it also interacts with existing online rating biases.

Many studies suggest that e-commerce consumers tend to underre port their dissatisfaction [19,42,43]. Specifically, there is strong evi dence that positive ratings are more dominant than negative ratings (i. e., the inflation effect or underreporting bias). Customers may be more likely to leave reviews when they have favorable experiences due to possible fear of retribution (e.g., [12,30]). For instance, Chevalier and Mayzlin [24] find overwhelmingly more positive reviews than negative reviews on online booking platforms. Hu et al. [40] report a J-shaped rating distribution peaking near 5-star reviews. The above evidence suggests that consumers are more silent when they have negative ex periences; as such, there is an under-reporting bias in online reviews.

Since consumers exhibit a tendency towards under-reporting nega tive experiences when providing reviews for a service, the express de livery promise may lead to an under-reporting bias. As previously mentioned, consumer satisfaction is derived from the disconfirmation relative to their expectations of service delivery. When express delivery is offered, promising a shorter delivery time, it can raise consumer ex pectations, subsequently lowering the magnitude of positive disconfir mation and hence the satisfaction levels compared to regular orders without such promises. This decrease in satisfaction levels contributes to the under-reporting bias, as consumers become less inclined to report their experiences. As a result, the rating propensity for express orders must be lower than that for regular orders. Thus, we posit:

Hypothesis 2. Compared to regular orders with the same realized delivery time, consumers are less likely to leave a rating for the orders with an express promise.

## 3.3. The role of promised delivery date in express orders

Finally, using the same theoretical framework of expectationdisconfirmation [7,8], we discuss how the promised delivery time of an express order impacts customer satisfaction. As previously discussed, customers evaluate logistics performance based on their expectations that is shaped by prior experiences or, in the case of express orders, the promised delivery date. Altering the promised delivery date for the ex press orders can shift customers' expectations and subsequently change their disconfirmation with their expectations. For instance, customers promised an express order with a two-day delivery are likely to have lower expectations than those promised an express order with one-day delivery. Following the expectation disconfirmation theory, given the same delivery day (i.e., perceived performance), when the expectations are lowered (e.g., changing from same-day delivery to next-day de livery), the customers' disconfirmation of their expectation becomes more positive and leads to increased satisfaction with the longer promised delivery time. Extending Hypotheses 1 and 2, we propose that while express orders receive lower rating scores and fewer reviews, the negative effect of an express delivery promise can be alleviated as the promised delivery time is longer. Thus, we posit that:

Hypothesis 3. Compared to regular orders with the same realized delivery time, a longer promised delivery time for express orders reduces the negative effect of delivery promise on the rating score.

Hypothesis 4. Compared to regular orders with the same realized delivery time, a longer promised delivery time for express orders reduces the negative effect of delivery promise on the rating propensity.

## 4. Study context and data

In this study, we use a dataset provided by Alibaba's Tmall.com, one of the world's largest retail online marketplaces. Tmall is a business-toconsumer (B2C) marketplace run by the Chinese e-commerce giant Alibaba and is currently one of the largest e-commerce platforms in China [44]. To achieve a faster delivery service, Alibaba has invested in building warehouses in cities across China. In 2012, Alibaba also part nered with an information-sharing platform, Cainiao, to improve its logistics handling services [45]. The Cainiao platform tracks and man ages inventory movement from sellers and warehouses to the final customers while various third-party logistics companies do the actual delivery. Following the service experience, customers can provide feedback as online ratings for individual product quality, seller service, and logistics service of the transaction. In this paper, we are specifically interested in studying the logistics service ratings of the transaction.

To provide customers with a better logistics service experience, Tmall allows sellers to stock their products in Cainiao warehouses. This enhances delivery speed and provides a reliable delivery promise esti mate for each order. The platform will automatically determine whether to show a delivery promise to the buyer primarily based on the distance between the warehouses and the buyer's location. Apart from regular orders, Tmall offers two types of express orders: “same-day delivery” and “next-day delivery.” If an express order is late, the platform explicitly states that the buyer will receive 10 RMB (\~\$1.5 USD). It's worth noting that there is a service charge to sellers for stocking their products in Cainiao warehouses, but there is no additional shipping fee to buyers for express orders.

In this dataset, for each order, we can identify the payment time, sellers, buyers, items sold, price and quantity of the items, the type of delivery speed, and the shippers' identity (i.e., the third-party logistics company). Also, if a customer has rated the logistics service, the rating is available on a scale of 1 (worst) to 5 (best). Furthermore, we compute the realized delivery lead time for each order by utilizing the detailed activities recorded about the order fulfillment process. Specifically, we use the time difference between the final package receiving time and the order payment time as the realized delivery lead time.

According to the platform, sellers can meet the express delivery promise if their orders arrive by 11:59:59 PM of the promised delivery date. Therefore, orders arriving in the morning or night on a given day are considered delivered on the same day. Accordingly, to be consistent with the delivery date promises, we ignore the clock time when computing realized delivery lead time in this study

It is worth noting that this dataset has two distinct advantages for addressing our research questions. First, on Tmall, the ratings given by customers for their logistics service experience are distinct from the overall product ratings. Thus, unlike a typical single rating system (e.g., Yelp, Amazon) that collectively aims to capture the overall service experience. we can unambiguously analyze logistics performance from the customer perspective through the ratings associated with the logistics services. Second, unlike Amazon's Prime service, buyers on Tmall do not sub scribe to premium delivery and do not pay an extra subscription fee. Instead, it is possible that the cost for express shipping is baked into the product price by sellers that offer the express shipping option. So, a customer purchasing a product with an express delivery may pay an extra price compared to a customer purchasing a regular order. The extra price can be reflected as the price premium for the order and can be controlled in estimation. Also, in e-commerce platforms, sellers strate gically price their products and show loss‑leader and dynamic pricing behavior to attract customers [46].

We access a random data sample of the Tmall transactions from January 1, 2017, to July 31, 2017. We have roughly 5 million records (one order per record) of 3,424,470 unique buyers from 526 unique sellers with 119,098 unique items in 334 different product categories delivered by 49 logistics companies. Approximately 7% of the orders in our sample are express orders. We also note that 37% (38%) of the ex press orders (regular orders) receive an online rating for logistics per formance. We also observe that the express orders have a more skewed distribution than the regular orders. Thus, it is more likely for the ex press orders to get a positive rating (5 out of 5) compared to the regular orders. However, such an observation does not indicate that giving a promised delivery date will increase the ratings because the delivery lead time and the decision to stock the products in the Cainiao warehouses could also be correlated with the location of the buyers and sellers.

## 4.1. Main variables

This section defines all the main variables for our analysis. The two dependent variables for this study are $R a t i n g _ { s b j }$ and $R a t i n g I n d _ { s b j } .$ . The online rating given by buyer b for order j with seller s is represented by $R a t i n g _ { s b j } ,$ and $R a t i n g I n d _ { s b j }$ indicates whether order j from seller s will receive a rating from the buyer b.

The key independent variable for this study is $E x p r e s s _ { s b j } ,$ which is an indicator of whether order j purchased by buyer b from seller s is promised express shipping (i.e., same day or next-day delivery with a PDD). The PDD of an express order can be said to form a baseline for a customer's expectation in terms of when an order would be received at the order's final destination. We use $E x p r e s s S p e e d _ { s b j }$ to capture the dif ference between the two express order types, ‘same day delivery’ and ‘next day delivery.’ As such, $E x p r e s s S p e e d _ { s b j } = 1$ indicates that the seller has promised same day shipping service; and $E x p r e s s S p e e d _ { s b j } = 2$ indicates that the seller has promised next day shipping service. $E x p r e s s S p e e d _ { s b j } = 0$ when there are no delivery speed promises (e.g., regular orders). ## 4.2. Control variables

In this section, we describe other transaction-variant characteristics used as a control in the model. These are categorized into four groups: seller characteristics, customer characteristics, product characteristics, material handling variation, and time-specific factors.

a) Seller Characteristics: One of the empirical challenges we have is that the service quality of each seller could be different. For instance, an established seller may spend more effort on customer service, which in turn improves their seller's rating. To capture seller characteristics and alleviate such concerns, we use a seller fixed effect $( \gamma _ { s } )$ . While seller fixed effects controls for time-invariant heterogeneity in sellers, a seller may improve their logistics performance ratings over time and affects customers expectations. Therefore, we capture this effect by including an aggregated average logistics rating of the seller $( A \nu g R a t i n g _ { s b j } )$ . We use $A \nu g R a t i n g _ { s b j }$ a day before the purchase trans action to be exogenous of the current purchase.

b) Customer Characteristics: Each buyer may have a different expectation of the shipping time. Some buyers may view the shipping time as a key attribute guiding their purchasing decision. Therefore, they may be more sensitive to the shipping time, affecting their rating behavior. We use a buyer fixed effect (θ ) to capture buyer expec tation differences and control such heterogeneity. We also include time-varying buyer characteristics such as buyers' total number of completed orders $P r i o r B u y e r O r d e r s _ { s b j }$ and buyers' prior logistics ex periences captured by their ratings given to prior orders AvgBuyerRating .

c) Product Characteristics: Each product on the platform has an associ ated product category and sub-category. We use a product subcategory $( I t e m C a t e g o r y _ { s b j } )$ as a fixed effects to capture unobserved product characteristics such as the size, type, vulnerability, or customer urgency associated with the product. In addition, we also consider some other transaction-specific product factors in the model, such as the minimum price of a single unit of an item, $I t e m P r i c e _ { s b j } ,$ and if sellers increases the price over time, charging a premium on their products, PricePremium . Furthermore, the order size may also influence the sensitivity of the shipping time. A buyer may pay more attention to the shipping time of larger orders. Therefore, we control for the item quantity $( Q u a n t i t y _ { s b j } )$ for each order.

d) Logistical Quality: To examine the impact of express delivery promise on the rating behavior, we capture the delivery lead time of the order using a variable lead time $( L e a d T i m e _ { s b j } )$ as a quality of the logistics service.<sup>4</sup> Recall from the previous section that we do not consider the clock time for computing the lead time. Thus, $L e a d T i m e _ { s b j } = k$ if the customer receives the shipment on the $( k { - } 1 ) ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ day after the purchase, where $k \geq 1$ . That is, when $k = 1$ , the shipment arrives on the same day; when $k = 2 ,$ , the shipment arrives on the next day, and so on.

Apart from the delivery lead time $( L e a d T i m e _ { s b j } )$ , we also control for the potential factors that may affect buyer perception. Particularly, given that the delivery services on Tmall are handled by third-party shippers, the quality of their service directly affects the consumers satisfaction with the logistics service. Therefore, to control for the thirdparty logistics handling heterogeneity, we use a categorical variable for shippers, $( S h i p p e r _ { s b j } )$

e) Time-specific Factors: Our dataset ranges from January 2017 to July 2017. It is common knowledge that there are some particular busy periods for the e-commerce industry in China, such as the Chinese New Year or the 6.18 shopping festival. Therefore, we use a cate gorical variable $P a y w e e k _ { s b j }$ to capture the seasonal difference by considering the systematic difference by purchasing time in weeks.

## 5. Main analysis

We examine the hypotheses through two separate empirical models discussed in this section. Eq. (1) focuses on the express orders sample and tests H1 and H3 by examining how the ratings given by the buyers may be influenced by the offering express delivery promise using $R a t i n g _ { s b j }$ as the dependent variable. Eq. (2) studies H2 and H4 by analyzing how offering an express delivery promise may affect customer likelihood of giving a review. We leverage the high-dimensional panel structure of our data to control for unobserved heterogeneity, including the unobserved buyer, seller, product, time, and shipper characteristics.

In Eq. (1), the variable of interest is the binary variable $E x p r e s s _ { s b j } .$ $E x p r e s s _ { s b j }$ indicates whether order j is an express order, regardless of whether the express order arrives earlier or later than the promised delivery date (PDD). That is, $E x p r e s s _ { s b j } = 1$ if seller s offers an express order j to buyer b – otherwise, $E x p r e s s _ { s b j } = 0 .$ . Our goal is to estimate whether the binary variable $E x p r e s s _ { s b j }$ affects $R a t i n g _ { s b j }$ while holding the shipping performance and other transaction heterogeneity constant. Furthermore, to examine H3, we use $E x p r e s s S p e e d _ { s b j }$ to capture the effect of the promised delivery time in the express orders. Thus, we present a fixed-effect model in Eq. (1) as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Rating } _ {s b j} & = \beta_ {1} \log \text { LeadTime } _ {s b j} + \beta_ {2} \text { Express } _ {s b j} + \beta_ {3} \text { ExpressSpeed } _ {s b j} + \theta_ {b} \\ & + \gamma_ {s} + Z _ {s b j} \mathbf {B} + \varepsilon_ {s b j} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\varepsilon _ { s b j }$ is the idiosyncratic error. $\beta _ { k } \mathrm { ^ { \prime } } s$ are the coefficients of key variables. The buyer fixed effect is captured by $\theta _ { b } ,$ and the seller fixed effect is captured $ { \mathbf { b y } } \gamma _ { s } . \ Z _ { s b j }$ is the matrix of the control variables described in section 4.2. We control for the price, price premium, quantity, buyer prior purchasing and rating behavior, and seller's aggregated logistics rating score. We also include $P a y w e e k _ { s b j } ,$ $I t e m C a t e g o r y _ { s b j }$ and $S h i p p e r _ { s b j }$ as categorical variables. While these categorical variables are not estimated directly in our analysis, a multidimensional fixed effect regression model [47] captures all these important unobserved factors while preserving the tractability of the model. Therefore, the estimated coefficients of the other transactionvariant variables are consistent. Additionally, since sellers do not receive a rating in a random fashion, Model (1) may suffer from a se lection bias in that customers may leave reviews based on certain characteristics. To capture such a selection effect, we use the Heckman selection model. Specifically, first, we use a Probit model to predict the propensity of receiving a review for each order using the customer and buyer characteristics $Z _ { s b j }$ . Then we follow the Heckman method to generate an inverse mills ratio (InverseMillsRatio ) and include it in the rating models.

If H1 is supported, we posit that orders with express delivery promise will receive a lower rating compared to orders with no such promise with the same realized delivery time due to the shift in consumer expectation. Therefore, the coefficient $\beta _ { 2 }$ will be negative. Furthermore, if H3 is supported, we expect the magnitude of the negative effect due to the shift in consumer expectations to be reduced if the promised delivery time is longer. In other words, when the promised delivery time is longer, the negative effect of $E x p r e s s _ { s b j }$ will be reduced. Therefore, the coefficient $\beta _ { 3 }$ will be positive.

As posited by ${ \mathrm { H } } 2 ,$ express orders will be less likely to receive ratings compared to regular orders. Similar to the above model, H2 compares express orders $( E x p r e s s _ { s b j } = 1 )$ with regular orders $( E x p r e s s _ { s b j } = 0 )$ to study a buyer's likelihood to leave a rating. While in Eq. (1), we only focus on the orders with ratings, in Eq. (2), we include all the sampled orders, with and without ratings, for our analysis. The goal of Eq. (2) is to examine how $E x p r e s s _ { s b j }$ might influence buyer b leaving a rating for particular order j from seller $s ,$ i.e., the dependent variable $R a t i n g I n d _ { s b j } .$ The model is presented in $\operatorname { E q . }$ (2):

$$

$$

where $\varepsilon _ { s b j } ^ { ' }$ is the idiosyncratic error.

If H2 is supported, we expect $\alpha _ { 2 }$ to be negative. In other words, given the same lead time, together with some other transaction-variant char acteristics, express orders are less likely to receive a rating than regular orders. Similar to eq. ## 8.2. Empirical identification & selection effect

While our results suggest that the promise of express delivery lowers customer ratings, the results can be driven by two effects: the selection effect and the expectation effect. On the one hand, in line with our hy pothesis, the express delivery promise can heighten the expectation of speedy delivery, resulting in a lowered buyer's satisfaction with the delivery services. However, on the other hand, if the selection effect plays a role, we expect that the express delivery promise attracts buyers with higher expectations, resulting in a lower rating. In this section, we attempt to further tease out the expectation effect, as hypothesized, from the selection effect. We should note that while the selection effect is an alternative mechanism, we have controlled for the buyer and the time fixed effect. In other words, the buyer's preference in delivery speed should be captured, especially during the short study period (six months). Furthermore, any platform-wide changes affecting buyer de cisions would have been captured in the time fixed-effect. To further minimize the selection effect and alleviate endogeneity concerns due to selection, we use a quasi-experimental design by combining it with a Look-Ahead Propensity Score Matching approach (LA-PSM, see details in Bapna et al. (2018) [49] and Kumar et al. (2018) [50]). In this design, we sample a set of buyers who purchased an item with regular delivery on their first purchase and had at least one express delivery on their second or later purchases. We use the first purchase as a baseline and a quasi-experimental approach to compare the buyers' rating behavior of their first and second purchases. To further lower the selection effect in purchasing the items with promised delivery, we use the subsequent purchases (the 3rd or later purchases) to match the characteristics of the buyers such that we can compare the rating behavior of a similar set of buyers. Below, we describe the identification approach and present the overview of the subsample of the buyers in Fig. 1.

Specifically, we first categorize the purchase history of these sampled buyers into two sets: the examination set and the look-ahead matching set. The examination set contains all the orders with the first and second purchases (within our study time span) of the buyers. As mentioned previously, to create a consistent comparison, the first purchase of these buyers contains only a regular order. However, the second purchase can be either an express order or a regular order. Our goal is to examine the rating difference of the buyers if they have an express order in their second purchase relative to their first purchase. Here, we use ExpressBuyer to indicate whether buyer b has an express order for their second purchase.

Based on the LA-PSM method, to further alleviate the selection effect, we match buyer characteristics based on a look-ahead matching set con structed by the 3rd and later purchases of the buyers. To avoid the subsequent effect of express orders similar to network formation described in Kumar et al. [50], we use the purchases from the look ahead matching set to match the characteristics between the buyers with an express order for their second purchase ( $\mathrm { i . e . , } E x p r e s s B u y e r _ { b } = 1 )$ and the ones without $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ ExpressBuyer = 0). Specifically, using a Probit model, we estimate a propensity score of getting an express order in the second purchase based on the fraction of orders offering express delivery promise by the seller, the item price, price premium, quantity of the order, buyers prior average ratings, review propensity, and review score of the orders in the look-ahead matching set. Then, we match the propensity score between two groups using the nearest neighbor algo rithm (with replacement). To ensure the matching quality, we discard matched pairs for which the propensity score difference is higher than 20% of the propensity score's standard deviation. Overall, out of $^ { 3 1 5 , 9 4 7 }$ buyers, we identify 17,607 buyers with a promised delivery for their second purchase and 13,164 buyers without a promised delivery. The matching method provides a set of buyers with similar character istics. Table 6 shows that the characteristics of the buyers are close to each other after the matching. These findings suggest that the distri bution of the selection effect and buyer characteristics for the two groups are very similar.

After matching the buyers' characteristics, we examine the delivery commitment effect by comparing these buyers' rating behavior for their second purchase using a quasi-experiment specification shown in Eq. (3).

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Rating } _ {s b j} \text {   or   RatingInd } _ {s b j} & = \zeta_ {1} \log \text { LeadTime } _ {s b j} + \zeta_ {2} \text { Second } _ {s b j} + \zeta_ {3} \text { Second } _ {s b j} \\ & \quad \times \text { ExpressBuyer } _ {b} + \zeta_ {4} \text { ExpressSpeed } _ {s b j} + \theta_ {b} + \gamma_ {s} \\ & \quad + Z _ {s b j} \mathbf {H} + \epsilon_ {s b j} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $Z _ { s b j }$ is the matrix of all the control variables described in section 4.2. Here, $S e c o n d _ { s b j }$ is a binary indicator capturing the second purchase made by buyers as noted in our aforementioned quasi-experimental setup. We should note that the main effect of $E x p r e s s B u y e r _ { b }$ has already been absorbed by the buyer fixed effect $\left( \theta _ { b } \right)$ . As such, it is not included in the model. Keeping the realized delivery lead time $( l o g L e a d T i m e _ { s b j } )$ and all control variables constant, the coefficient of the interaction term $S e c o n d _ { s b j } \times E x p r e s s B u y e r _ { b } ,$ denoted by $\zeta _ { 3 } ,$ captures the impact of the express delivery promise on the second purchase by comparing buyers whose first purchase was not an express order but whose second purchase was, with the buyers whose both purchases were regular orders. In general, $E x p r e s s S p e e d _ { s b j }$ is the promised delivery time (in days) for order j delivered to buyer b from seller s. Hence, $\zeta _ { 4 }$ repre sents the effect of promised delivery time on the rating and rating pro pensity of the express order.

Table 7 presents the result for the estimation of Eq. (3). Columns (1) and (2) show the estimation using the whole sample, whereas columns (3) and (4) show the estimation using the matching sample. Table 7 shows that the coefficient of the interaction term Second × ExpressBuyer is negative for all columns suggesting that the express delivery promise has a negative effect on both the rating propensity and the rating score,<sup>6</sup> which is qualitatively similar to the main results reported in Table 2.<sup>7</sup>

## 8.3. Matched seller sample

To further minimize the selection effect of offering express service from the seller side, we match all the sellers in our dataset into two groups: sellers offering express service and sellers not offering express service. In our sample, we have a total of 526 unique sellers. Out of these sellers, 258 sellers did not offer express delivery in the study period, and 268 sellers offered express delivery at least once during the data collection period. Using a probit model, we estimate the propensity of a seller to offer express service when it didn't offer an express promise during the study period. We estimate this based on available seller characteristics. In the probit model, we included the seller's overall lo gistics rating score, the propensity of getting a review, and the average price, quantity, and premium charged on items. We also included buyer characteristics of those who purchased from these sellers, such as the buyer's average rating and their total orders on the platform. Then, we match the propensity score between the two groups of sellers using the nearest neighbor algorithm (with replacement). To ensure the matching quality, we discard the matched pairs for which the propensity score difference is higher than 10% of the propensity score's standard devia tion. In total, we discarded 57 sellers who were off the common support. The matching method provided a set of 468 sellers with similar char acteristics. Table 8 shows that the characteristics of the two seller groups are similar after the matching.

After matching the sellers, we re-run our analysis on only the matched sample and reported in Table 9. The results are qualitatively consistent with the main results reported in Table 2. This confirms that the seller's decision to offer an express delivery promise is not the reason for getting a lower propensity of reviews or lower rating scores.

## 9. Conclusion and implications

Our study reveals that express delivery promises have a negative impact on customer rating scores and propensity by raising expecta tions. This effect is observed not only for delayed orders but also for orders delivered on time. These findings have important managerial implications. While firms use express delivery promises to increase customer willingness to pay [6], our research suggests that such prom ises can harm online reputation. The negative effect persists even when orders are delivered earlier than promised, indicating that customer dissatisfaction stems from heightened expectations. Sellers must be mindful of shifting customer expectations when offering express de livery promises, especially as shorter promised delivery times exacer bate the negative effect. Therefore, it is crucial for sellers to carefully consider the level of delivery promise, focusing on over-delivering rather than over-promising, to mitigate reputation damage while still attracting customers.

We acknowledge that our paper has several limitations. For example, in our analysis, despite we control the buyer and seller characteristics through fixed effect approach, we do not observe additional buyer and seller characteristics, such as the buyer's geographic location or seller's tenure. Due to the short six-month timeframe of the transaction data, it is expected that these characteristics should not play a critical role in our analysis. However, when a longer duration of data is available, future studies should investigate the potential heterogeneous effects of the express delivery promise, particularly with regard to buyers and sellers. For example, buyers from larger cities may rate differently than those from rural areas, and buyers may perceive an established seller differently than a new seller. These differences create potential chal lenges in how firms interpret ratings at the aggregated level. Our work lays a foundation for future researchers to study the impact of buyer and seller characteristics at a more fine-grained level.
