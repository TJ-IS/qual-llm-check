# How social cues mitigate advertising intrusiveness on social media: Heterogeneous cues, mechanisms, and conditions

## A B S T R A C T

While advertising sustains social media platforms, its intrusive nature threatens both user experience and plat form sustainability. Social newsfeed advertising (SNA) intrusiveness denotes users' psychological reactance eli cited when unsolicited ads disrupt their processing on newsfeeds. Beyond delivering product information, SNA embeds social cues—such as Likes, Shares, Ratings, and Sales—into users' social contexts. However, prior research provides limited insights into how heterogeneous social cues mitigate intrusiveness, particularly regarding the mechanisms and conditions underlying these effects. Drawing on attitude function theory, this study examines how distinct social cues reduce intrusiveness via sociability and informativeness, and delineate the product and user conditions that amplify these effects. Two between-subjects experiments (N = 304; N = 306) on a custom-built platform show that proximal cues reduce intrusiveness for social products and high selfmonitors by enhancing sociability, whereas remote cues benefit utilitarian products by increasing informative ness. Attitude-based cues primarily foster sociability, whereas action-based cues strengthen informativeness. These findings clarify how social cues operate within SNA and offer actionable guidance for reducing intrusiveness.

## 1. Introduction

Social media platforms rely heavily on advertising revenues, yet advertising often elicits perceptions of intrusiveness among users [1]. Excessive intrusiveness provokes negative affect and avoidance, eroding user trust and engagement [2]. While intrusiveness occurs across for mats such as video, in-app, and notification ads, it is particularly pro nounced in social newsfeed advertising (SNA) [3]. Social feeds operate as personal spaces governed by interpersonal norms; ads violate these expectations [4] and impose cognitive load [5]. Moreover, ads embedded in social newsfeeds often mimic native content, further intensifying intrusiveness. Consistent with this view, prior research shows that SNA is often perceived as intrusive and triggers advertising avoidance [6], while Statista (2024) indicates that about 72% of con sumers feel frustrated by ads [7]. For users, intrusiveness undermines autonomy; for advertisers, it weakens persuasion; for platforms, it threatens sustainability; and for regulators, it raises concerns about manipulation. These tensions position intrusiveness as a socio-technical governance dilemma, in which users' social expectations conflict with platforms' monetization imperatives, underscoring the need to under stand its causes and mitigation strategies.

Advertising intrusiveness refers to the psychological reactance users experience when unsolicited ads disrupt ongoing tasks and cognitive processing [2]. Existing research has sought to alleviate intrusiveness via two main approaches. The first adjusts ad presentation to reduce perceptual salience, such as by limiting repeated exposure [8], reducing visual prominence (e.g., size or animation) [9], or optimizing ad placement and thematic alignment with surrounding posts [10]. The second enhances user control through timing prompts [11] or blocking options [12]. While these approaches may temporarily reduce intru siveness, they primarily address perceptual symptoms and often diminish advertising exposure without resolving the core tension in SNA: the conflict between users' social expectations and marketers' commercial objectives. Addressing these limitations requires strategies that embed ads more effectively within social contexts.

Beyond presentation-level adjustments, social cues provide a lens through which intrusiveness can be understood as a socially constructed perception rather than a purely cognitive reaction. Social cues signal engagement with ads or products and vary relationally—from close ties (e.g., friend Likes) to distant audiences (e.g., aggregate Ratings or Sales) [13,14]—and informationally, from explicit attitudes (e.g., Likes, Re views) to implicit actions (e.g., Clicks, Purchases) [15]. Despite their heterogeneity, cues are often applied indiscriminately, obscuring their distinct effects. Accordingly, this study differentiates social cue types and examines how they mitigate intrusiveness via distinct psychological mechanisms, as well as the product and user conditions under which these effects occur.

Research on the role of social cues in shaping intrusiveness remains limited. Prior studies primarily focus on content-based evaluations such as clicks, attitudes, and purchases [16–18]. However, unlike these out comes, intrusiveness arises from cognitive interruption rather than content evaluation and depends on users' cognitive state during expo sure [2], a factor largely neglected in existing work. Moreover, prior studies often assess only a single cue [18] or treat multiple cues as ho mogeneous [13], producing fragmented findings and limited guidance. Although some studies provide preliminary statistical evidence that different cues elicit heterogeneous responses [13,14], the mechanisms driving these differences and the conditions under which each cue is most effective remain unclear.

Motivated by these limitations, this study examines social cues along two dimensions—relational distance (proximal vs. distant) and infor mational nature (attitude-based vs. action-based)—to capture their distinct effects on intrusiveness. Drawing on attitude function theory (AFT) [19], which connects attitude formation to the alignment between advertising appeals and users' motivational functions, we identify two dominant functions in SNA. The social function reflects users' desire for interaction and social approval, while the utilitarian function corre sponds to the pursuit of decision-relevant information. Social cues aligned with these functions are proposed to enhance either sociability (facilitating interaction) or informativeness (providing useful informa tion). Along the relational dimension, proximal cues enhance sociabil ity, whereas distant cues increase informativeness; along the informational dimension, attitude-based cues strengthen sociability, and action-based cues enhance informativeness. Aligning cues with the dominant attitude function facilitates smoother processing, thereby mitigating intrusiveness.

The salience of these attitude functions varies across contexts and individuals. Product type is a key contextual driver by shaping external motivations: social products (e.g., gift wrap) tend to activate the social function, whereas utilitarian products (e.g., medicine) engage the util itarian function [20]. For mixed products (e.g., athletic shoes), the dominant function depends on users' self-monitoring, a disposition reflecting social sensitivity and behavioral regulation. High self monitors prioritize social approval, activating the social function, whereas low self-monitors focus on personal value, engaging the utili tarian function [19]. Thus, product type and self-monitoring jointly determine whether social cues mitigate intrusiveness via sociability or informativeness, explaining heterogeneous cue effects across products and users.

To test these hypotheses, two between-subjects factorial experiments (N = 304 and N = 306) were conducted using a custom-built platform. Results demonstrated that social cues significantly reduced SNA intru siveness relative to baseline conditions, with effectiveness varying by product type and self-monitoring through distinct mechanisms (socia bility and informativeness). Proximal cues maximized intrusiveness mitigation for social products and high self-monitors by enhancing so ciability, whereas remote cues were most effective for utilitarian prod ucts by increasing informativeness. Likewise, attitude-based cues strengthened sociability for social products and high self-monitors, while action-based cues enhanced informativeness for utilitarian products.

This research contributes to the literature in four ways. First, by focusing on intrusiveness, it extends social cue research beyond contentbased processing to encompass automatic defensive responses, offering a fuller view of users' resistance to unsolicited ads. Second, it proposes a framework organizing social cues by relational distance and informa tional nature, enabling comparative analysis and clarifying prior in consistencies. Third, the two mechanisms—sociability and informativeness—synthesize existing research and extend AFT to explain how social cues mitigate SNA intrusiveness. Fourth, it identifies key moderators of cue effectiveness: product type shapes the external context by defining the dominant attitude function, while selfmonitoring captures internal motivation, pointing to more targeted cue strategies. Practically, these findings guide platform governance by leveraging social cues to manage SNA intrusiveness, aligning users' ex pectations with marketing objectives without compromising engagement.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Social newsfeed advertising (SNA) intrusiveness

Advertising intrusiveness is commonly defined as a psychological reaction triggered when ads interrupt users' ongoing, goal-directed processing [2]. It induces negative affect (e.g., ad irritation) [1], hos tile cognition (e.g., adversarial attitudes) [8], and defensive behaviors (e.g., ad blocking) [12,13], ultimately undermining user experience and advertising effectiveness. Rooted in psychological reactance theory, intrusiveness arises when ads threaten users' perceived control over their media consumption [21]. It thus reflects disruptions to attention and cognitive flow rather than isolated evaluations of ad content. As Li et al. [2] note, “ads are not themselves intrusive; rather, they become intrusive only when perceived as interrupting viewers' goals.”

Building on this conceptualization, prior research identifies two broad approaches to mitigating intrusiveness, as summarized in Table 1. The first reduces cognitive disruption by optimizing ad execution along concrete design dimensions. For example, limiting repeated exposure lowers cognitive interruption [8] and native formats blend with feed content to reduce abruptness [9]. The second enhances perceived au tonomy, framing exposure as self-directed despite fixed presentation and duration. For instance, providing ad choices or using timing prompters can increase perceived control and offset autonomy loss induced by ad insertion [11].

Strategies for mitigating intrusiveness can be effective, but often at the cost of diminished attention capture or enabling avoidance. More over, intrusiveness may depend not only on ad attributes but also on how well they fit contextual expectations. However, existing research provides limited insight into the mechanisms underlying such contex tual alignment. Specific to social media, ads appear within socially oriented streams where interpersonal norms and peer-driven in teractions shape users' cognitive processing [3,4]. In such contexts, conventional interventions may have limited influence, as users attend to social meaning rather than perceptual or autonomy-related features. Social cues provide a context-sensitive alternative: by signaling others' engagement, they may integrate ads into social cognition and reduce perceived intrusiveness. Yet their underlying mechanisms and condi tions remain insufficiently understood. This motivates the following review of social cues and their functional properties.

## 2.2. Social cues

Social cues are concise, user-generated signals—such as Likes, Rat ings, and Sales—that shape recipients' perceptions and behaviors [13,17]. They represent observable engagement with ads and products, reflecting the interactive nature of social media as advertising envi ronments. By integrating product-oriented information directly into feeds, social cues act as a “social passport,” prompting users to interpret ads as socially endorsed rather than purely persuasive [31,32]. Prior research highlights their persuasive influence [33], capacity to trigger herding [17], and support for decision making [34]. To enhance thei effectiveness, platforms increasingly display diverse signals—for example, Likes and Comments under ads (Facebook), tagging (Weibo), or exposure counts among contacts (WeChat)—which systematically vary along two core dimensions: relational distance and informational nature.

## (1) Relational distance: proximal cues vs. remote cues

Relational distance refers to the perceived psychological closeness between the cue source and the recipient. As social media expands in teractions from strong to weak ties [35], cues can be classified as proximal or remote. Proximal cues originate from close friends, whose relational closeness confers strong influence but who are encountered less frequently. Remote cues, by contrast, draw on broader audiences, making them more accessible, objective, and especially informative for observational learning [14]. These differences vield distinct behavioral outcomes: for instance, Lee et al. [36] found that users relied more on public choices (remote cues) for movie selection, whereas ratings converged with close friends (proximal cues).

## (2) Informational nature: attitude-based cues vs. action-based cues

A second dimension is informational nature, which distinguishes attitude-based cues from action-based cues. Attitude-based cues (e.g., Likes, Ratings) capture explicit evaluations and articulated opinions [37]. However, such expressions may be constrained by the cognitive burden associated with deliberate self-expression. Therefore, platforms increasingly rely on action-based cues—implicit behavioral traces such as clicks or purchases—which require no deliberate expression [38]. Additionally, these cues are often regarded as more authentic and credible because they mirror actual engagement [39]. Prior work sup ports these functional differences: for instance, attitude-based word-ofmouth influenced decisions through diagnostic information, whereas action-based clicks triggered herding driven by social approval [38].

Despite this diversity, prior research often treats social cues as an umbrella term, obscuring important distinctions across cue types. For example, some studies equate sales volume and Likes [40], while others find heterogeneous effects, such as friends' Likes increasing clicks but strangers' Likes not [3]. However, these findings largely document empirical differences based on statistical or field data, with limited focus on mechanisms and conditions. Accordingly, this study investigates the mechanisms by which different social cues reduce SNA intrusiveness and the product- and user-related conditions that delineate their effective ness, thereby providing a coherent framework for understanding how social cues operate in SNA contexts.

## 2.3. Attitude function theory (AFT)

This study adopts attitude function theory (AFT) to explain how social cues mitigate SNA intrusiveness. AFT posits that individuals evaluate stimuli based on the extent to which they fulfill salient psy chological needs—attitude functions—activated in a given context [19]. When external inputs align with the dominant function, processing is fluent; when misaligned, inputs may be experienced as intrusive [33]. Prior research has identified multiple functions, including utilitarian [41], value-expressive [20,42], and social identity functions [33], but two are particularly salient in SNA contexts: the social function, reflecting users' motivation to maintain relationships and gain ap provals, and the utilitarian function, reflecting the need for decision relevant information. Accordingly, SNA may be processed either as a socially embedded interaction or as informational input, thereby shaping users' perceptions of intrusiveness.

Consistent with AFT, social cues mitigate intrusiveness by engaging psychological mechanisms corresponding to the dominant attitude function. Specifically, they enhance sociability (ads convey relational warmth [43]) or informativeness (ads provide decision-relevant input [5]), aligning with social or utilitarian functions. However, social cues often favor one attitude function over the other, creating a trade-off between social relevance and informational utility. Consequently, their effects on intrusiveness depend on the mechanism that is activated, highlighting the importance of distinguishing these psychological pathways.

Moreover, AFT suggests that the salience of attitude functions is contingent on contextual and individual factors [19]. Product type shapes external motivation by emphasizing either social or instrumental value [19,20], while individual differences such as self-monitoring in fluence whether users prioritize social approval or diagnostic accuracy during evaluation [20,33]. By determining which attitude function is activated, these factors condition the alignment between social cues and users' evaluative goals, thereby moderating the extent to which socia bility or informativeness effectively mitigates intrusiveness.

AFT offers a distinct perspective on how social cues mitigate intru siveness. Prior studies have largely examined cue effects through inferential or learning-based lenses, such as implicit social influence [13], observational learning [15], or persuasion knowledge [44]. They focus primarily on post-exposure inference and offer limited insight into why certain cues feel appropriate or disruptive at the moment of pro cessing. In contrast, AFT emphasizes functional alignment between external cues and users' active motivational goals during ad exposure. By explicitly linking social cues to attitude functions, underlying mecha nisms (sociability and informativeness), and conditions (product type and self-monitoring), AFT provides a unified framework for explaining why and when social cues reduce intrusiveness in social feeds.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

## 3.1. Research model

Grounded in AFT, we propose the research model depicted in Fig. 1. This model posits that social cues reduce intrusiveness relative to a nocue baseline, with effectiveness contingent on product type and users' self-monitoring. Specifically, proximal cues are expected to mitigate intrusiveness more effectively for social products and high self-monitors by enhancing sociability. In contrast, remote cues are anticipated to increase informativeness, thereby benefiting utilitarian products and low self-monitors. Similarly, attitude-based cues are likely to alleviate intrusiveness for social products and high self-monitors through socia bility, whereas action-based cues primarily support utilitarian products and low self-monitors by enhancing informativeness.

To control for potential confounds, we include user demographics (age, gender), SNA entertainment, and advertising skepticism. Enter tainment reflects users' hedonic responses to ads [5], which may vary across product types (e.g., social product ads may appear more enjoy able) and be influenced by cue manipulations. Advertising skepticism reflects general distrust toward advertising, affecting reactions to ad stimuli [44]. Controlling for these factors ensures that the effects of social cues are estimated independently of hedonic responses or ad trust.

## 3.2. Direct effects of social cues on SNA intrusiveness

According to AFT, external inputs facilitate cognitive processing when they satisfy the dominant attitude function guiding evaluation, thereby attenuating perceived intrusiveness [19,20]. In SNA, social cues emphasize either the social or informational aspects of advertising exposure, aligning content with users' active evaluative goals. This functional alignment reduces perceived advertising intrusiveness in so cial feeds.

From the perspective of social functions, users engage with social media to maintain relationships, seek approval, and manage interper sonal impressions [5]. Explicit commercial messages violate social expectations, and are therefore perceived as intrusive. Social cues mitigate this disruption by embedding advertising within users' social environments. By signaling others' engagement or endorsement, such cues restore social relevance and frame advertising as an extension of ongoing interactions rather than persuasion attempts [31]. Prior research also demonstrated that cues reflecting friends' reactions acti vate social goals, thereby reducing perceived intrusiveness [4]. From the utilitarian perspective, users seek diagnostic information that supports efficient evaluation of ads and products [39]. Marketer-generated mes sages are often perceived as biased or exaggerated, increasing skepti cism and intrusiveness. In contrast, social cues, typically originating from ordinary users, provide credible information on product popularity or aggregated behavior [17]. By delivering decision-relevant input, these cues satisfy the utilitarian function, reduce uncertainty [13], and mitigate perceived intrusiveness [37].

Taken together, by fulfilling users' underlying attitude functions— whether social or utilitarian—social cues are expected to reduce SNA intrusiveness. Accordingly, we propose:

H1. SNA incorporating social cues (proximal vs. remote, attitudebased vs. action-based) leads to lower levels of intrusiveness compared to SNA without social cues.

## 3.3. The mediating mechanisms of sociability and informativeness

Having established in H1 that social cues mitigate SNA intrusiveness by satisfying attitude functions, we further examine how cues differing in relational distance and informational nature exert their effects. Based on AFT, we focus on two psychological mechanisms—sociability and informativeness—that capture the alignment of social cues with social and utilitarian functions. Sociability reflects interpersonal warmth, relational closeness, and network embeddedness; when high, ads are perceived as part of social interactions rather than disruptive messages [34]. Informativeness reflects the extent to which SNA provides decision-relevant information, supporting utilitarian evaluations and reducing perceived intrusiveness [5].

Social cues do not uniformly enhance sociability or informativeness; their effectiveness depends on relational distance and informational nature. Regarding relational distance, proximal cues that reference close friends or strong ties emphasize interpersonal engagement and rela tional warmth, making them particularly effective at enhancing socia bility. In contrast, remote cues signal large-scale adoption and collective endorsement [36]. Although less personally intimate, they provide aggregated and credible information, thereby strengthening informa tiveness. Similarly, informational nature differentiates cue effects. Attitude-based cues convey evaluative opinions and shared preferences, highlighting social approval and value alignment, which enhances so ciability [32,37]. Action-based cues reflect observable behaviors such as purchases or clicks, offering concrete evidence of product usage and validation [38,39]. By supplying decision-relevant information, these cues primarily enhance informativeness.

Taken together, the relational and informational characteristics of social cues determine which mechanism—sociability or informativeness—is engaged, thereby shaping their effects on perceived intrusiveness. Accordingly, we propose:

H2. The mitigating effects of social cues on SNA intrusiveness are mediated by sociability or informativeness. Specifically, the effect of proximal cues on SNA intrusiveness is mediated by sociability (H2a); the effect of remote cues on SNA intrusiveness is mediated by informative ness (H2b); the effect of attitude-based cues on SNA intrusiveness is mediated by sociability (H2c); the effect of action-based cues on SNA intrusiveness is mediated by informativeness (H2d).

## 3.4. The moderating role of product type

Conceptually, different social cues reduce intrusiveness through distinct psychological mechanisms (e.g., sociability or informativeness) aligned with corresponding attitude functions. Cues emphasizing rela tional proximity or evaluative opinions primarily enhance sociability, satisfying the social function, whereas cues highlighting aggregated endorsement or observable behaviors mainly enhance informativeness, fulfilling the utilitarian function. Their effectiveness further depends on whether the activated mechanism aligns with the function emphasized by the product [19,20]: when alignment occurs, processing is more fluent and cues are more effective; when misaligned, their impact is weakened.

Social products are characterized by relational value and the pursuit of social recognition, which makes the social function particularly salient [41]. In these cases, proximal cues that convey relational warmth and attitude-based cues that signal shared values are especially effective in enhancing sociability and reducing perceived intrusiveness. By contrast, utilitarian products emphasize functional performance and instrumental value, thereby activating the utilitarian function. For such products, remote cues indicating collective endorsement and actionbased cues reflecting observable behaviors better satisfy users' evalua tive goals, facilitating efficient judgment and mitigating intrusiveness. Accordingly, we propose that product type moderates the mitigating effect of social cues on intrusiveness, with social cues reducing intru siveness via sociability for social products and via informativeness for utilitarian products:

H3. Product type moderates the mediating mechanisms through which social cues mitigate SNA intrusiveness. Specifically, the indirect effect of proximal cues via sociability is stronger for social (vs. utilitarian) products (H3a); the indirect effect of remote cues via informativeness is stronger for utilitarian (vs. social) products (H3b); the indirect effect of attitude-based cues via sociability is stronger for social (vs. utilitarian) products (H3c); the indirect effect of action-based cues via informa tiveness is stronger for utilitarian (vs. social) products (H3d).

## 3.5. The moderating role of self-monitoring

While product type provides a contextual cue for the activation of attitude functions, the growing prevalence of mixed products—those combining social and utilitarian value—reduces the determinism of product-based effects. In such contexts, whether the social or utilitarian function is activated depends on individual characteristics. Within AFT, self-monitoring is a key dispositional factor that determines which function users adopt when forming evaluations [19]. Consequently, selfmonitoring is expected to moderate the mitigating effects of social cues on SNA intrusiveness for mixed products by shaping which function is applied.

Self-monitoring is a personality trait reflecting individuals' sensi tivity to social contexts and their tendency to regulate behavior in line with social expectations [33]. High self-monitors, being particularly attuned to these expectations, are more likely to activate the social function when processing advertising content. As a result, they respond favorably to cues that enhance sociability, such as proximal or attitudebased cues, which align with their socially oriented goals and facilitate smoother processing. In contrast, low self-monitors rely more on inter nal standards and diagnostic information, making them more inclined to activate the utilitarian function during ad evaluation [33]. Conse quently, they are more responsive to cues that enhance informativeness, such as remote or action-based cues, which provide objective, performance-relevant input that mitigates intrusiveness. Accordingly, we propose the moderating role of self-monitoring:

H4. Self-monitoring moderates the mediating mechanisms through which social cues mitigate SNA intrusiveness. Specifically, the indirect effect of proximal cues via sociability is stronger for high (vs. low) selfmonitors (H4a); the indirect effect of remote cues via informativeness is stronger for low (vs. high) self-monitors (H4b); the indirect effect of attitude-based cues via sociability is stronger for high (vs. low) selfmonitors (H4c); the indirect effect of action-based cues via informa tiveness is stronger for low (vs. high) self-monitors (H4d).

## 4. Experiment 1: the moderating role of product type

Two between-subjects factorial experiments were conducted using a self-developed platform. Experiment 1 employed a 5 (cue type: baseline, proximal, remote, attitude-based, action-based) × 2 (product type: so cial vs. utilitarian) design to test the effect of social cues (H1), their mediating mechanisms (sociability and informativeness; H2), and the moderating role of product type (H3).

## 4.1. Experimental design and manipulations of Experiment 1

WeChat newsfeed advertising was selected as the experimental context due to its dominance in China (1.34 billion active users). To simulate realistic SNA exposure, stimuli adopted $\mathbf { a }  \mathbf { 3 } + \mathbf { 1 } + \mathbf { 3 } ^ { \flat }$ layout: three simulated social posts appeared above and three below the target ad (Appendix A). The ad featured a fictitious brand, logo, copy, and image to minimize brand bias. The surrounding social posts acted as contextual primes to facilitate social processing [45]. To enhance engagement, each post combined authentic information (e.g., avatars and nicknames of participants' friends) with experimenter-generated content, including personal events and images aligned with common WeChat themes such as music, sports, and caf´es.

Ten experimental conditions were created (Fig. 2; see the original Chinese version in Appendix B, Fig. B1) to manipulate social cues and product type in a controlled setting. Social cues were adapted from real SNA scenario and conveyed through captions reflecting social infor mation. Each participant was exposed to only one cue type, preventing cross-cue contamination. Product type was based on established mar keting literature [19] and manipulated using two ad versions: a utili tarian product (family medicine) and a social product (personalized Tshirts). The two versions were matched in layout, color, and slogan to control for design confounds. All participants encountered systemati cally manipulated stimuli, ensuring consistency and experimental con trol. This design enabled us to assess the causal effects of social cue and product type manipulations on perceived intrusiveness.

## 4.2. Participants and procedures of Experiment 1

A custom platform was developed to implement a three-phase experimental procedure. First, participants familiarized themselves with the interface and submitted social network information, including screenshots containing at least six friends' avatars and nicknames, along with demographic data. These inputs were manually converted into configuration files used to generate personalized newsfeeds. Next, par ticipants engaged in simulated WeChat browsing on the platform, where they were randomly assigned to one of ten treatment groups and exposed to newsfeeds containing ads with manipulated social cue and product type. Finally, participants completed a questionnaire evaluating their browsing experience, using scales from validated instruments (Table 2), rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree).

Participants were recruited online and screened for WeChat famil iarity. After providing informed consent, they proceeded through the guided experimental procedure individually at their convenience. A total of 304 participants completed Experiment 1, with 30–31 per con dition. Each session lasted approximately 30 min, and data collection occurred over one month (October 2023). All participants had prior exposure to SNA, and the sample was broadly balanced in age and gender. Additional demographic details are provided in Appendix C. Participants who completed the experiment and passed an attention check received 20 RMB.

## 4.4. Discussion of Experiment 1: alignment of social cues and product type in intrusiveness

Experiment 1 confirmed the direct, mediating, and moderated effects of social cues on SNA intrusiveness (H1− H3). All social cues mitigate intrusiveness relative to the baseline conditions (H1), with proximal cues and attitude-based cues operating via sociability and remote cues and action-based cues via informativeness (H2). Furthermore, product type moderated these effects (H3): social products were sensitive to cues enhancing sociability, while utilitarian products were influenced by cues enhancing informativeness. Notably, several non-significant effects in the moderation analyses (Section 4.3.5) are theoretically informative: sociability mediated only proximal cues and attitude-based cues for social products, and informativeness mediated only remote cues and action-based cues for utilitarian products. These patterns align with the conditional nature of our model, highlighting the functional specificity of sociability and informativeness and delineating the boundaries within which social cues operate effectively.

These findings align with AFT, suggesting that the effectiveness of social cues depends on matching the activated attitude function with the underlying psychological mechanism. Specifically, social products tend to activate the social function of attitudes, making cues that enhance sociability particularly effective in mitigating intrusiveness. In contrast, utilitarian products activate the utilitarian function of attitudes, so cue that enhance informativeness are more effective. Meanwhile, the study underscores the importance of distinguishing social cues by both rela tional distance and informational nature, as this nuanced classification enables more accurate predictions of their differential impact on intru siveness, consistent with AFT principles.

Experiment 2 extended the findings of Experiment 1 by incorpo rating mixed products and examining the moderating role of self monitoring (H4).

## 5.1. Experimental design and social cue manipulations for mixed products

The design of Experiment 2, including the context and platform, remained consistent with Experiment 1. Notably, we retained the structural newsfeed as the stimuli but randomized the order of social posts to minimize contextual confounds. Athletic shoes, a wellestablished mixed product, were selected as the advertising content to control for the impact of product type on receivers' attitude functions. In addition to these experimental manipulations, we measured par ticipants' self-monitoring, a stable personality trait that cannot be experimentally manipulated. Participants were randomly assigned to one of five cue conditions, browsed the designed newsfeed, and completed a questionnaire. A total of 306 newly recruited participants completed the experiment (30–31 per group), each performing the task individually at their convenience. Sessions lasted approximately 30 min, and all data were collected in November 2023. Demographic characteristics are re ported in Appendix C.

This framework explains the observed mediation patterns via sociability and informativeness and highlights the complex interplay of cue characteristics, product type, and individual traits, providing a nuanced understanding of social cues in SNA.

## 6. General discussion

## 6.1. Main findings

Across two factorial experiments, most hypotheses were supported. Social cues consistently mitigated SNA intrusiveness, with effectiveness contingent on contextual and individual factors. In Experiment 1, proximal cues and attitude-based cues were most effective for social products via enhanced sociability, whereas remote cues and actionbased cues were optimal for utilitarian products through informative ness. In Experiment 2, high self-monitors favored proximal cues and attitude-based cues via sociability, whereas low self-monitors exhibited weaker differentiation across cue types. Unexpectedly, informativeness did not mediate cue effects for low self-monitors, suggesting that while they prioritize informational utility, they have limited ability to distin guish among cues. Overall, both external context (product type) and internal disposition (self-monitoring) shape the mechanisms through which social cues mitigate intrusiveness.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

First, this study extends understanding of SNA intrusiveness beyond conventional ad evaluations by emphasizing processing disruption and automatic defense responses. To mitigate intrusiveness, we introduce a social-interaction-driven model that shifts focus from commercial ob jectives to user-centered dynamics. This model embeds advertisements via social cues within the social network and offers guidance for balancing effectiveness with user experience.

Second, the study synthesizes prior research to organize social cues along two dimensions—relational distance (proximal vs. remote) and informational nature (attitude-based vs. action-based)—providing a structured map for comparison. This integrative framework clarifies previously fragmented findings and enables systematic analysis of how cue variations influence SNA intrusiveness, highlighting nuanced dif ferences among social cues.

Third, we identify two mediating mechanisms—sociability and informativeness—through which social cues reduce intrusiveness. These mechanisms operate conditionally, with effects moderated by product type and self-monitoring traits. Clarifying these contingent pathways helps resolve the theoretical “black box" of cue-driven intrusiveness reduction and provides a systematic understanding of the functional processes underlying social cue effectiveness.

Fourth, the study extends AFT to social newsfeed advertising. Our findings demonstrate that social cues activate specific attitude functions (social vs. utilitarian) to shape user responses. Notably, low self monitors relied primarily on informational utility but showed limited differentiation among cues. This nuance refines AFT by highlighting how individual dispositions modulate attitude function activation, emphasizing the need to consider product type and personal traits in context-specific advertising.

## 6.3. Practical implications

The findings provide actionable guidance for reducing SNA intru siveness via optimized social cues. Platforms and advertisers are sug gested to align social cues with product type and inferred user tendencies. Specifically, for social products, cues that enhance sociabi lity—proximal or attitude-basedare most effective. For utilitarian products, remote cues or action-based cues that enhance informative ness are preferable. For mixed products, user self-monitoring can guide cue selection: high self-monitors benefit more from sociability-focused cues, low self-monitors from informativeness-oriented cues. Although self-monitoring is an internal trait, platforms can infer tendencies through observable behavioral patterns. For example, high self-monitors frequently update profile elements, curate socially adaptive content, and display trend-sensitive expressions, whereas low self-monitors maintain more stable profiles and adopt utilitarian communication styles. By integrating product context, inferred user tendencies, and cue design, platforms and advertisers can strategically reduce intrusiveness while improving user experience and campaign effectiveness in social com merce environments.

## 6.4. Limitations

Three main limitations exist in this study. First, although the ex periments were rigorously controlled, the key constructs were measured through self-reported surveys, which may introduce structural biases. Future research should incorporate field experiments and behavioral data to strengthen the objectivity of the findings. Second, the experi mental setting was limited to WeChat newsfeeds with specific social cue designs, which may constrain ecological validity. Comparative studies across different SNA platforms and cultural contexts are needed to assess generalizability. Finally, the study deliberately examined each social cue in isolation, whereas real-world SNA environments involve multiple cues operating simultaneously. Investigating how different social cues interact offers a fruitful direction for future research.

## 6.5. Conclusions

This study investigates how heterogeneous social cues—varying in

Across two experiments, proximal cues and attitude-based cues reduce intrusiveness for social products and among high self-monitors via sociability, while remote cues and action-based cues are more effective for utilitarian products and low self-monitors through infor mativeness. These findings extend SNA intrusiveness research by linking AFT with social cue characteristics, clarifying underlying mechanisms and conditions. Practically, the results offer guidance for designing so cial cues that match product contexts and user motivations, mitigating intrusiveness without reducing ad exposure.

Building on these findings, our study offers practical implications for key stakeholders. From a user standpoint, greater awareness of how advertising intrusiveness emerges and can be mitigated helps in dividuals recognize sponsored content and make more deliberate de cisions. At the platform level, intrusiveness can be managed as a design outcome through the provision and fine-grained management of inter action mechanisms that trigger different social cues, balancing com mercial effectiveness with user satisfaction. For advertisers, reducing intrusiveness requires the strategic use of social cues, including adjust ing ad copy and message framing to align cues with product type and target consumers' self-monitoring traits. At the regulatory level, pro moting transparency, developing measures of intrusiveness, and moni toring advertising practices can safeguard user experience and contribute to a more sustainable advertising ecosystem.
