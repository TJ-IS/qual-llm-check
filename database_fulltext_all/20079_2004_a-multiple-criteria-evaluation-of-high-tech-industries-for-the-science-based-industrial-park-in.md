# A multiple criteria evaluation of high-tech industries for the science-based industrial park in Taiwan

Chung-Jen Chen<sup>a,\*</sup>, Chin-Chen Huang<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Graduate Institute of Business Administration, National Cheng Kung University 1, University Road, Tainan, Taiwan, R.O.C. <sup>b</sup>Graduate Institute of International Business, National Cheng Kung University 1, University Road, Tainan, Taiwan, R.O.C.

Received 28 November 2002; received in revised form 9 June 2003; accepted 1 August 2003

Available online 19 November 2003

## Abstract

Science-based industrial parks have been recognized as an effective way of promoting technology development, urban renewal, and economic growth. However, little has been done in discussing the selection strategy of high-tech industries to locate in such a park. As the space availability in the new park in Taiwan is limited, the selection of firms with higher efficiency and growth potential has become an important issue for the Taiwan government. Accordingly, this study adopted the AHP method to obtain professional’s opinions on this issue. The effort resulted in seven evaluation criteria with one, the ‘‘market potential’’ having the highest weight, followed by ‘‘technology level’’ and ‘‘government policy’’. Biotechnology and photo-electronics were the two most favorable high-tech industries chosen for the introduction and development in the new park. A sensitivity analysis was carried out to determine the critical factors that affected the priority of the alternatives. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Published by Elsevier B.V.

Keywords: Science-based industrial park; High-tech industry; Analytic hierarchy process; Sensitivity analysis; Taiwan

## 1. Introduction

The first science-based industrial park was introduced in Taiwan in Hsinchu in 1980; it has been found to have the merits discussed in past studies [3,17,18,24]. The Hsinchu Science-based Industrial Park HSIP has contributed to the introduction and development of Taiwan’s high-tech industries, especially semiconductor and computer production, and these have driven Taiwan’s economy toward a remarkable growth for the past two decades [4,12]. For example, in 1999, 292 high-tech firms at HSIP hired more than 82,000 skilled employees to create annual sales of about NT 650 billion [8].

Because HSIP was fully occupied and yet there was still a demand for space in the science-based industrial park by high-tech firms, the Taiwan government established a second science-based industrial park in Tainan city in southern Taiwan. As the space availability was limited, selection of firms with higher growth potential in specific high-tech industries became an important issue for the government. However, little work exists on the selection process.

During the past two decades, there has been growth in the number of multiple criteria decision-making methods for assisting decision-making. These allow decision-makers to evaluate various alternatives for achieving their goal. Among these, the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) is one of the most popular [2,6]. This paper applies AHP to high-tech industry evaluation. This formal analysis allows decision-makers to rank order high-tech industries for inclusion in the new science-based industrial park.

## 2. High-tech industries and science-based industrial parks in Taiwan

In early 1980s, the Taiwan government recognized the limitations of Taiwan’s natural resources and the need to develop high-tech industries in order to maintain economic growth [5]. Since then, the Taiwan government has focused its attention on stimulating the development of technology-intensive industries by a variety of policy measures including establishing sciencebased industrial parks. The efforts resulted in some extraordinary outcomes. For example, Taiwan’s technology-intensive sector has grown fast for the past decade. In the years 1986–1997, the average growth rate was 10.7%, greatly surpassing the 6.2% of the traditional manufacturing sector [15]. Taiwan’s success in semiconductor and computer fabrication has been particularly recognized [14]. Other than computer and semiconductor, Taiwan also has been trying to develop other technologies, including communications, photoelectronics, precision equipment, and biotechnology.

The Hsinchu Science-based Industrial Park (HSIP) is the epitome of the development of Taiwan’s hightech industries. It was established in 1980 in Hsinchu city, about 1 hour drive to the south of Taipei. The purpose of this park was to attract and gather high-tech firms to create industry clusters. With adequate incentives, including tax credits, excellent infrastructure, convenient official services, and abundant supplies of technologies and skilled engineers, HSIP has grown rapidly in sales and numbers of firms and employees, as shown in Table 1.

There are six high-tech industries in the park, semiconductor, computer, communications, photoelectronics, precision equipment, and biotechnology. Their sales growths are shown in Table 2. Semiconductor and computer have been the two largest industries in the park. Before 1992, the computer industry was the largest of the six industries but it was then surpassed by the semiconductor industry. Nevertheless, both have grown dramatically. In 1999, their annual sales reached NT 360.8 billion and NT 200.8 billion, respectively, accounting for 55 and 31% of the annual sales of the whole park. The data indicate that both have been the major high-tech industries in Taiwan.

Communications and photo-electronics industries have been the third and fourth largest of the six industries. Before 1996, the communications industry was the third largest among the six high-tech industries, but it was then surpassed by the photo-electronics industry. Precision and biotechnology industries have been the last two among the six industries. Annual sales of both industries account for less than 1% of the annual sales of the whole park.

## 3. The analytic hierarchy process

The AHP is an analysis methodology supporting rational decision-making by simplifying a complicated problem. It was developed to solve a class of problems that involves prioritization of alternative solutions. This is achieved through a series of pairwise comparisons [19,20]. AHP has several advantages including over-specification of judgment, built-in consistency tests, use of appropriate measurement scales, and applicability in the elicitation of utility functions [1,7,9,11]. Numerous applications of AHP have been made and it has been used in many problems ranging from investment risk to resource allocation and organization planning [10,13,16,21,22,25]. AHP allows decision-makers to make qualitative decisions more objectively and it supports systematic decision-making by expressing the interaction and hierarchy of factors, thus reducing the danger of a rough estimation. Due to these advantages, we adopted AHP to evaluate the high-tech industries for the new sciencebased industrial park in Taiwan.

AHP usually involves three stages: decomposition, comparative judgment, and synthesis of priorities. The first stage requires the construction of a hierarchical network to present the problem, with the top representing the overall objective, the middle representing the criteria, and the bottom representing the alternatives. In our study, the objective was to evaluate what high-tech industry would be best in the new science-based industrial park. Therefore, the main objective ‘‘selection of high-tech industries’’ is placed at the top level of the analytic hierarchy shown in Fig. 1. The second step is to identify key evaluation criteria for assessing the objective. Here, there are seven key criteria, identified by interviewing the experts. They are: utility consumption, technology support, industry relevance, land supply, technology level, government policy, and market potential. Finally, six high-tech industry candidates were placed at the bottom of the AHP model. These included semiconductor, computer, communications, photoelectronics, precision equipment, and biotechnology.

Fig. 1. The AHP model.

The next stage was to compare the factors at the same level in pairs and measure their comparative contribution to the main objective. A comparison matrix was set up by comparing pairs of criteria or alternatives. A scale of values ranging from 1 (indifference) to 9 (extreme preference) was used for acquiring experts’ preferences. This pairwise comparison enabled the decision-maker to evaluate the contribution of each factor to the objective independently, thereby simplifying the decision-making process. Here, the seven criteria were compared in pairs to measure their impacts on the objective. Also, the six alternatives were compared in pairs to measure their importance under each criterion.

In the final stage, synthesis of priorities was conducted to calculate a composite weight for each alternative, based on preferences derived from the comparison matrix. Following the calculation of the composite weight, we obtained the relative priority of the high-tech industries for introduction into the park.

A sensitivity analysis was carried out to determine the critical factors. Triantaphyllou and Sanchez [23] proposed a methodology for performing sensitivity analysis on the weights of the decision criteria and the performance values of the alternatives in terms of the criteria for deterministic multiple criteria decisionmaking methods. Their methodology can be used to determine the criteria in the model that requires only a small change in weight to cause a switch in rank of one or more alternatives. In our study, we used this method to identify the criteria and their effects on the priority of high-tech industries for the park.

## 4. Research design

## 4.1. Survey design

A survey was conducted to determine how the experts perceived the relative importance of the evaluation criteria and the high-tech industries. A questionnaire was developed based on the analytic hierarchy. The seven evaluation criteria were: utility consumption, technology support, industry relevance, land supply, technology level, government policy, and market potential. Utility consumption deals with the electricity and water consumed in the production process. Technology support describes the extents of technology supply that the firms can obtain from adjacent universities and research institutions. Industry relevance reflects the development status of the local complementary industries that are related to the high-tech industries. Land supply refers to the amount of land used for construction of production facilities and plants. Technology level reflects the level of technological capabilities possessed domestically. Government policy deals with the extent of the policy established to attract and develop specific high-tech industries, while market potential refers to the future growth potential of the industries.

Each question consisted of a pairwise comparison of two elements at the same level of the hierarchy. Therefore, the seven criteria resulted in a total of 21 questions. For each, the respondents were asked to indicate the relative importance of the two criteria with respect to the objective. Then the respondents were asked to compare the high-tech industries with respect to each criterion. There are six high-tech industries for the seven criteria, resulting in a total of 105 questions.

## 4.2. Data collection and analysis

The participants included government officials, industry analysts, and academic researchers experienced in the development of the science-based industrial parks and high-tech industries in Taiwan. The Government officials were in the agencies involved in planning or managing the industrial parks, such as the Council for Economics Planning and Development and the Bureau of Science-based Industrial Park. Industry analysts and academic researchers were from research institutes and universities, such as Industrial Technology Research Institute and the National Cheng Kung University.

The survey was conducted in two stages: first, six experts were interviewed for formulating the hierarchy with the evaluation criteria and the alternative high-tech industries. Then questionnaires were sent to the targeted experts and 47 responses were received. A script was included to ensure consistency and eliminate any biases that could be caused by the phrasing of the questions.

The analytic hierarchy was programmed in Expert Choice—a decision support system that implements the process. We first calculated the geometric means of the marked responses in each question from each of the survey respondents. Then, the comparison matrix was input into Expert Choice to produce local weights at each level of the hierarchy. These were then combined using an additive value model to produce a set of global weight or priorities for the alternatives. Finally, a sensitivity analysis was performed to determine the critical evaluation criteria that affect the selection strategy.

## 5. Results and discussion

## 5.1. Comparison of the evaluation criteria

We first examined the criteria with respect to the overall goal. This was done by assessing the importance of each criterion with respect to the choice of high-tech industries. Following the AHP methodology, pairwise comparisons were performed to obtain the relative importance of the factors. Table 3 shows the pairwise comparison matrix for the seven evaluation criteria with the goal. The normalized weights and the rank for the criteria are given in the last two columns. Fig. 2 shows a graphical plot of the weights as a bar chart.

The results indicate that the criterion market potential has the highest weight of 0.222, followed by the criteria technology level and government policy which both have relative weights of 0.169. These indicate that besides government policy, the market potential of the individual high-tech industry and the level of technology that local firms and research institutes possess are the most important criteria to consider in the introduction of high-tech industries into the science-based industrial park.

Technology support and industry relevance have weights of 0.129 and 0.123, respectively. This indicates that they are comparatively perceived as the secondary level of factors and are tactically important. When a specific high-tech industry is perceived as having huge market potential and appropriate local technology capabilities and is encouraged by government policies, then the creation of abundant technology support and the development of the complementary industries are tactical issues for the introduction and development of this high-tech industry. Land supply and utility consumption have relative weights of 0.097 and 0.092, respectively and are comparatively unimportant.

## 5.3. Sensitivity analysis on the weights of the criteria

The results of the sensitivity analysis were summarized in Tables 5 and 6, where the symbols A1 through A6 denote the six high-tech industries in decreasing order of their overall weights, i.e. biotechnology (A1), photo-electronics (A2), precision equipment (A3), semiconductor (A4), communications (A5), and computer (A6). Table 5 shows the changes in absolute value of each criterion necessary to cause the reversal of ranks for all 15 pairs of high-tech industries, while Table 6 presents the changes in percentage value of current weights necessary to cause the reversal of ranks.

From Table 5, we observe that two criteria, market potential and industry relevance, may cause the best alternative biotechnology (A1) to switch rank with the second best alternative photo-electronics (A2). The absolute value changes in these two criteria are similar, i.e. market potential (0.306) and industry relevance (0.312). However, we find from Table 6 that the market potential requires a much smaller change in weights (139%) than that required by industry relevance (251%). Hence, we conclude that market potential appears to be the most critical criterion.

## 6. Conclusions

This study applied the Analytic Hierarchy Process method to evaluate the high-tech industries for the introduction and development in the new sciencebased industrial park in Taiwan. Several conclusions can be made as follows: Firstly, the results of the evaluation criteria indicate that among the seven evaluation criteria, the criterion market potential has the highest weight, followed by the criteria technology level and government policy in the second and third place, respectively. Secondly, based on the subjective judgments made by experts, this comparative study shows that biotechnology and photo-electronics industries are the two most favorable high-tech industries selected for the introduction and development in the new science-based industrial park. Thirdly, as suggested by the sensitivity analysis, market potential and industry relevance are the two critical criteria that may cause the best alternative biotechnology to switch rank with the second best alternative photo-electronics. The application of AHP provided an avenue for government policy makers and researchers to evaluate the high-tech industries for science-based industrial parks.
