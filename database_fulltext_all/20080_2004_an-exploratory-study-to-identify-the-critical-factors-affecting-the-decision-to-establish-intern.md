# An exploratory study to identify the critical factors affecting the decision to establish Internet-based interorganizational information systems

Khalid S. Soliman<sup>a,\*</sup>, Brian D. Janz<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Hofstra University, Hempstead, NY, USA <sup>b</sup>The University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, USA

Accepted 25 June 2003

Available online 30 October 2003

## Abstract

The Internet, with its unprecedented growth, is a promising platform to exchange information along the business channels. The purpose of this study was to determine the factors that are critical to organizations in their adoption decision of Internet-based interorganizational information systems (IBIS). Using a field-based survey, the researchers obtained findings that suggested that the factors that significantly affect the adoption decision of IBIS are pressures felt from trading partners, pressure felt from competitors, establishing costs, network reliability, data security, scalability, complexity, support from top management, and trust between trading partners. This research highlights the similarities as well as the differences between IBIS and Electronic data interchange (EDI). The study showed that IBIS and EDI are similar in terms of the significant effect of factors such as pressure from trading partner, pressure from competition, establishing cost, top management support, and trust on the adoption decision. However, the results show that there are differences between IBIS and EDI in terms of establishing costs, scalability, and complexity. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic commerce; Internet; Interorganizational information systems; EDI; Supply-chain management; Business-to-business

## 1. Introduction

In today’s business environment, most organizations are facing significant pressure to make their operational, tactical, and strategic processes more efficient and effective. Information technology (IT) has become an attractive means of improving these processes. Consequently, organizations have implemented several strategies to improve effectiveness and to enhance efficiencies through the use of IT. Until recently, the main focus of many organizations was on improving internal operations. However, establishing strategic alliances between trading partners along the supply chain through the utilization of IT may result in great benefits. Electronic commerce (EC) is a viable option in improving the supply chain in order to remain competitive. EC is the use of electronic means to exchange information and conduct business transactions within and across organizational boundaries. It allows organizations to identify consumers’ requirements and trends and subsequently communicate the gathered information throughout the supply chain quickly.

Supplier–customer relationships have undergone radical changes in recent years. Organizations now realize that successful implementation of internal justin-time (JIT) practices alone will have limited effect on improving the overall performance [11]. Accordingly, organizations are beginning to emphasize closer supplier–customer relationships in order to create more efficient operations. Such close relationships may offer technical, financial, and strategic advantages over spot market transactions [12,24]. Increased closeness in supplier–customer relationships creates the basis for the simplest form of supply chain. According to Handfield and Nichols [15], ‘‘the supply chain encompasses all activities associated with the (bi-directional) flow and transformation of goods from the raw materials stage (extraction), through to the end user, as well as the associated information flows.’’ The main objective of supply-chain management is to unite trading partners along the supply chain into a seamless integrated flow of information and physical distribution.

Interorganizational information systems (IOIS) provide organizations with capabilities to improve linkages between trading partners along the supply chain. Electronic data interchange (EDI) is a traditional form of IOIS. EDI allows electronic communication of business information with trading partners across a company’s borders: it permits organizations to generate electronic purchase orders, invoices, bills of lading, and a variety of other documents and sends them instantly to trading partners anywhere in the world. The reduction in communications, labor, and material costs as well as the gain in competitive advantage are among the reported benefits of implementing an EDI environment [18]. However, there have been several reports of disadvantages. First, the additional costs associated with the value added network (VAN) services typically fall in the range of US\$ 5000–6000 per month [3]. This in turn becomes an excessive obstacle for small companies wanting to adopt an EDI environment to communicate with business partners. Second, to avoid difficulties, establishing EDI between trading partners requires compatible hardware at both ends in order to have seamless processing [17]. Third, preagreed-to standards and protocols are required. Several common industry-specific standards are required to establish EDI: the Transportation Data Coordination Committee (TDCC) used in the transportation industry,

Uniform Communication Standards (UCS) used in the grocery industry, and ANSI X.12 developed by the American National Standard Institute [21]. Given the different standards, a company that is a member of multiple supply chains in different industries would have to deal with multiple standards of communication, resulting in costly operations and system redundancies. Finally, because of the significant initial and operational investments, both the customer and the supplier are typically contractually tied to a long-term relationship. Although this may be beneficial, it has some drawbacks. Over time, other suppliers may come up with higher quality, lower priced, and/or better services and products. In such cases, switching costs associated with establishing a new linkage may prevent companies from making otherwise prudent changes.

The reported disadvantages of EDI have resulted in the idea of exploring the Internet as a viable alternative to establishing an IOIS. The recent growth of the Internet has been nothing short of phenomenal. In fact, B2B transactions over the Internet are expected to reach US\$ 1.33 trillion by 2005 [2].

The growing familiarity and practicality of the Internet make it a strong candidate to establish Business-to-Business (B2B) communications that can capture market share from EDI. Internet-based interorganizational information systems (IBIS) appeal to many businesses as an efficient way to link trading partners along the supply chain. IBIS can be established in different forms. Virtual VANs or extranets are examples of ways to utilize the Internet to establish B2B communication. According to Pincince [23], a virtual VAN can work like a traditional VAN, the only difference being the Internet-based nature of virtual VANs, which can be owned by either one of the trading partners, or even a third party. Extranets are considered a special case, where a company allows its trading partner to utilize the Internet to have access to its private network and internal applications. In our study, IBIS represents both examples of Internet use to communicate with trading partners.

Although end-users might not experience significant differences between EDI and IBIS in terms of interface and transparency of technology, the true impact of the IBIS is felt at the organizational level. IBIS offers advantages based on the characteristics of the Internet. These advantages are what differentiate IBIS from other IOIS such as EDI.

First, the Internet has one communication standard (TCP/IP) regardless of the industry as opposed to one of the main obstacles of EDI, which depends on industry-specific communication standards. Second, in addition to reduction in expenses, initial investment and operating expenses for IBIS can represent tremendous savings to organizations when compared to that of EDI. Third, the size of organizations plays an interesting role in putting more weight on the tendency to adopt IBIS. The high costs of establishing EDI with trading partners have prevented many small organizations from implementing EC with their partners [6], and has limited them from expanding their operations to include new alliances. Finally, scalability is another factor that makes IBIS different. This is the ability of the information systems (IS) to accommodate organizational change. IBIS accommodates growth demand better than EDI due to the low demand for additional technical components, less training for employees, and better chances of adding new trading partners.

IBIS and other IOIS share some common factors that influence the adoption decision. Factors such as trading partner pressure, competitor pressure, trust between trading partners, and top management support have been reported to affect the IOIS decision including that related to the adoption of IBIS.

Nevertheless, EDI has one advantage over IBIS when it comes to data security and network reliability concerns. The Internet is a public network. Thus, by placing strategic data such as financial reports and manufacturing schedules online, companies open themselves to potential security breaches. Therefore, the EDI environment alleviates data security and network reliability concerns that many organizations might have, especially those which deal with sensitive data.

## 2. Research objectives

To understand the business applications of the Internet, researchers have studied the factors influencing the adoption of the Internet in the workplace [32] and in small businesses [24]. However, no empirical study has been found that sought to determine the factors affecting the adoption of Internet technology to establish interorganizational information systems.

Determining how to take advantage of the opportunities of the Internet will not be easy for most executives [13]. Adopting an IBIS will involve several factors that must be considered. Identifying the critical factors affecting the adoption decision would provide managers with a more focused list of factors to be considered in adoption decisions relating to IBIS.

## 3. Research methodology and research design

Several research efforts have addressed the adoption and impact of new technology in organizations. Grover and Goslar [14] identified the factors that influence initiation, adoption, and implementation of telecommunication technologies. A stream of recent work has examined the EDI adoption decision. Iacovou et al. [19] studied EDI adoption decisions in small businesses. Premkumar and Ramamurthy [25] examined the interorganizational and organizational factors that influence the decision mode for adoption of EDI. Hart and Saunders [16] developed a theoretical framework to address the roles that power and trust play in EDI adoption and use. Few research studies were found that focus on business adoption and use of the Internet. Vadapalli and Ramamurthy [34] studied the factors that influence adoption and use of the Internet in general. Teo et al. [33] continued the efforts to identify factors influencing decisions to adopt the Internet in the workplace. Our study focuses on the critical factors influencing the adoption deci sion derived from the closely related literature in areas such as telecommunications, EDI, and the Internet. In addition, there have been concerns about several technical factors when establishing an IBIS. Network reliability [8], security concerns [30], systems scalability [28], and systems complexity [9] are issues of concern that are unique to the environment of conducting EC over the Internet and can influence the adoption decision of IBIS. Thus, this study includes these factors. A set of questionnaires was developed from a pool of survey instruments that have been tested and used in these studies. Drawing from such a pool improves content validity of the adapted items. Furthermore, new items were developed to address issues related to the Internet. The new items in the instrument are related to: network reliability, data security, scalability, and complexity. The question naires were sent out for review by a number of academics and practitioners. Based on the results, the questionnaires were refined. Appendix A represents the final set of questions.

The subjects in this study were carefully selected from IS and logistics managers in organizations that are involved in supplier–customer relationships. The members of the Council of Logistics Management (CLM) in the United States were selected as appropriate subjects for this research. The selection was based on two criteria. First, all subjects should be involved in a managerial position related to supplychain management. Second, it was important to maintain quality of the respondent surveys.

Members of the CLM are generally managers and directors of logistics, supply-chain management, and interorganizational communications in their organizations. Therefore, they are knowledgeable about the major issues related to establishing IOIS in order to manage their respective supply chains.

A total of 420 questionnaires were directed to the subjects, drawn at random from all members of CLM. Subjects were briefed at the beginning of the survey about the scope of the study and the intention to measure their perceived importance of items presented in the survey. The respondents were asked to answer the questions on a five point Likert scale (where 1 ¼ strongly disagree; 2 ¼ disagree; 3 ¼ neutral; 4 ¼ agree; and 5 ¼ strongly agree). Among all returned questionnaires, 87 were found to be complete and usable; this represented a response rate of 20.7%. A wide range of demographic characteristics were covered by the returned surveys, which were submitted by managers in sales, procurement, and purchasing departments in companies that operate in different geographic locations in the United States. These companies play different roles in their respective supply chains. These characteristics help in providing more generalizable results.

## 4. Data analysis and results

A factor analysis was conducted both to assess the construct validity of the measures and to determine the underlying factors influencing the adoption decision of IBIS. For factor analysis, there should be four to five times as many observations as there are items to be studied; this is the case in our study. Principal components analysis was used as the extraction technique with varimax rotation. Without specifying the number of factors, seven factors with eigenvalues greater than one emerged. However, in reviewing the results, it was clear that some factors appeared to contain two different types of items (e.g. costs and scalability). To achieve more interpretable constructs, the data was re-examined by specifying nine factors. The factor matrix is presented in Table 1.

Nunnally [22] recommended that in order to assess the fit between the items and their construct, all of the primary factor loadings should be greater than 0.5. In this test, all factor loadings were equal to or greater than 0.7 (with the exception of $\mathrm { S } 2 = 0 . 6 9 8 )$ , which demonstrates a good match between each factor and related items. The factor identified by the factors analysis are: (1) pressure from trading partner, (2) pressure from competition, (3) costs, (4) network reliability, (5) data security, (6) scalability, (7) complexity, (8) top management support, and (9) trust.

Several items were based on previously developed instruments. The factors related to these were given the same labels as the original source. They are pressure from trading partner, pressure from competition, cost, top management support, and trust. The factors that included items that were specifically designed to measure network reliability, data security, scalability, and complexity were given names related to the area being measured.

## 5. Discussion of findings

The results of the study support many of the findings of previous research. Pressure from trading partners, also known as channel power, has been studied intensively. Williams [35], who defined it as ‘‘the influence one channel member has over another channel member,’’ found that there is a significant relationship between channel power and the adoption of IOIS such as EDI. This study extends previous findings of the significance of pressure from trading partners in EDI to IBIS. Pressure from competition represents the second factor, which is consistent with previous IOIS literature. Competitive pressure can affect the adoption of IOIS [1].

The cost to establish Internet communications can represent tremendous savings. Organizations establish an EC environment with their trading partners over the Internet thus eliminating the high costs associated with the VANs in traditional EDI environments. This finding supports previous findings that the lower the costs and the higher the benefits, the better the adoption of IOIS [26]. Furthermore, Scala and McGrath [27] found that one of the major disadvantages of implementing EDI is that it requires high initial capital investments. Accordingly, IBIS emerged as an economically viable alternative to create electronic links with trading partners. First, in an Internet-based environment, trading partners eliminate the use of intermediaries and the associated high costs of subscribing to the VAN. Moreover, Internet-based systems require less investment in hardware and software to establish communication with trading partners than those in EDI.

Factors 4–7 deal with technical issues that are important to organizations with respect to IBIS: network reliability, data security, scalability, and complexity. In fact, bandwidth and network reliability are routinely considered to be among the main factors affecting Internet communications between trading partners [29]. Network reliability can be defined as the ability of a company to rely on the transfer of its confidential and critical data over the Internet. The fact that the Internet is a network of networks of computers forces many organizations to think twice before adopting IBIS to communicate with trading partners. Most of the risks are due to the existence of outdated web servers or applications that were not carefully installed initially [20]. Today, business communication requires increased bandwidth to accommodate the transfer of large multimedia files such as video and audio files. Accordingly, bandwidth, or more generally reliability, becomes a concern to many organizations—especially with the current communications conditions inherent with the Internet.

Data security represents a major concern to companies. The concerns are typically characterized in terms of confidentiality and fraud [31]. By placing strategic data, such as financial reports and manufacturing schedules, online, companies open themselves to potentially damaging security breaches. With control over a computer or router, a hacker can easily access critical information. Not surprisingly, the results suggest that IBIS adoption may hinge on data security-related issues, since IBIS can expose sensitive information for potential hackers to steal, alter, or make the systems malfunction. Other data security concerns stem from privacy issues. Companies along the supply chain are concerned about information gathered and/or mined by trading partners as well as the intent behind the use of the collected data [5].

The research also suggests that scalability and complexity are two more factors of concern. Scalability is an organization’s ability to easily modify the systems supporting an EC environment as business requirements and relationships change. The last two major factors of concern relating to IBIS adoption are top management support and trust. The result of this study with respect to top management support is in line with previous IOIS literature. IOIS research has emphasized that a key success factor for EDI is management support [4,21] and consistently found a significant relationship between top management support and the decision to adopt EDI. Moreover, Finnegan et al. [10] found that top management support was second to EDI planning on a list of non-technological success factors.

IOIS literature has also researched trust between trading partners and confirmed its importance in cooperative relationships [18]. In general, a trusting relationship is a critical factor for the success. The results here emphasize the importance of having a trusting relationship in an IBIS implementation. Given that the IBIS environment takes full advantage of the features provided by the Internet, it goes far beyond EDI in terms of business communications. IBIS allows companies to utilize the Internet to coordinate the entire purchasing cycle: from product information to low-level transactions such as purchasing orders to customer support.

## 6. Research implications

Many organizations are reengineering their business processes in order to take full advantage of this new communication medium. Our study provides researchers and practitioners with a set of factors that are perceived to affect the decision to adopt IBIS. Moreover, there are similarities as well as differences between IBIS and EDI. They are similar in terms of the significant effect of factors, such as pressure from trading partners, pressure from competition, cost, top management support, and trust on the adoption decision. However, the results highlight differences between them in terms of costs, scalability and complexity. The study shows that, when taking these three factors into consideration, organizations favor IBIS. Finally, the study stresses that factors such as network reliability and data security are unique to the IBIS environment and they are significant in the adoption decision.
