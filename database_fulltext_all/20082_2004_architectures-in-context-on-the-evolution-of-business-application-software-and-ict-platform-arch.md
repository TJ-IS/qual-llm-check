# Architectures in context: on the evolution of business, application software, and ICT platform architectures

A.T.M. Aerts<sup>a,\*</sup>, J.B.M. Goossenaerts<sup>b</sup>, D.K. Hammer<sup>a</sup>, J.C. Wortmann<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Mathematics and Computer Science, Eindhoven University of Technology,

P.O. Box 513, 5600 MB Eindhoven, The Netherlands

Available online 28 October 2003

## Abstract

This paper distinguishes between the business domain, the application software domain, and the Information and Communication Technology (ICT) platform domain. It analyses historical developments in each of these three domains and shows that they experienced parallel development. The parallelism can be explained by mutual influence and alignment. Innovation in one domain may enable or drive developments in another. In order to be able to analyse alignment patterns, the notions of business architecture, application software architecture, and ICT platform architecture are introduced and defined. Interdependent historical developments sometimes demonstrate a radical change. Each can be described as a shift in ‘‘dominant design’’, and we identify six such changes in the history of the modern enterprise. Professionals and scientific researchers working in Information and Management can benefit from these insights by assuming that radical changes in dominant designs will affect their field in the future according to the same pattern.

## 1. Introduction

The capacity of modern enterprises to compete is largely based on application software and Information and Communication Technology (ICT) platforms. Understanding modern enterprises requires the contribution of specialists from various engineering disciplines. In this paper we distinguish business, application software, and ICT platform engineering.

Each has its own description of architecture with interfaces. Whereas the relationship between business and ICT development is generally characterised as one of symbiosis, there exists no comprehensive description of the patterns that lead to mutual advantage in the association among them. Nevertheless the continuing development of ICT allows for new business strategies, and vice versa. Concepts and solutions emerging in one domain diffuse into others where their application provides the seed for new developments.

We regard the ICT enabled enterprise as an evolutionary system that is subject to the developments in its market. It interweaves stable behavioural patterns termed ‘‘business as usual’’ with changes ranging from small and incremental to radical. The enterprise moves into increasingly advanced uses of ICT and application software to achieve its business goals. It must keep developments in the various disciplines in tune to succeed. This process is called alignment.

Ettlie et al. [7] gave an early summary of the dichotomy between radical and incremental innovation. They focus on the organisational strategy and structural aspects of the introduction and adoption of innovation and ask questions such as: ‘‘Does the innovation incorporate technology that is risky, or a departure from existing practice? Is it new to the adopting unit and to the referent group of organisations? Does it require change to both throughput (process) and output (production and service)? What is the magnitude or cost of change?’’

Whereas they and many other authors focus on organisational strategy, we give a more prominent role to the aspects of innovation. For this we use the more fine-grained framework proposed by Henderson and Clark [11], who distinguish four types of innovation:

1. Incremental: an established design is reinforced by improving individual components.

2. Architectural: the way in which the components of an artefact are linked together changes, while leaving untouched the core design concepts and the basic knowledge underlying the components.

3. Modular: only the core design concepts of a technology are changed, not the linkages.

4. Radical: a new dominant design is established with a new set of core design concepts embodied in components that are linked together in a new architecture.

A dominant design ends a period of experimentation and confusion. It is characterised by a set of core design concepts that correspond to the major functions performed by an artefact, as embodied in components, and by an architecture that defines the ways in which these components are integrated. It incorporates a range of basic choices that are not revisited in every subsequent design.

Once a dominant design has emerged, new component knowledge becomes more valuable to the firms than new architectural knowledge, which appears relatively stable. Active problem solving as part of incremental and modular innovation becomes a recurring task and gets embedded in the practices and procedures, frameworks, and routines of the organisation.

Our work enriches the Henderson–Clark Framework by considering it in the context of enterprises and their multi-domain architectural description (AD).

## 2. Related work and scope, definitions

## 2.1. The purpose orientation

The importance of information technology for senior management has been investigated from the viewpoint of achieving competitive advantages through ICT using Porter’s competitive strategy framework [22]. However, early management approaches and tools suffered from several shortcomings: (a) the misconception that technology, in and of itself, can provide advantage; (b) the fact that models, such as the strategic grid and the strategic opportunity matrix, are too general to guide users to specific opportunities; (c) the failure to capture the change and dynamism of the competitive climate; and (d) the neglect of the nature of technological change. Industry ‘‘foresight’’ has been addressed by ‘‘core-competence’’ thinking and work on sustaining ICT-based advantage.

Core-competencies are ‘‘the collective learning of the organisation, especially of how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technologies’’ [21].

In an attempt to incorporate all the best features of earlier models Philip and Booth [19] defined a six ‘S’ framework. The six roles of information systems (IS) in relation to IS management competencies are: Survival, Sources and Resources, Strategy, Service Value Analysis and S(c)yberspace, with Sustainability as the sixth role, concerned with the management processes associated with the others. Our work on the evolution of business, application and ICT platform architectures adds a systems engineering dimension to the sustainability role. Radical alignment is required as a business moves into increasingly innovative uses of IS, e.g. from the Sources and Resources role to the Strategy role.

## 2.2. Architecture approach applied in business

Nadler and Gerstein [17] define organisational architecture as ‘‘the art of shaping ‘behavioural space to meet the needs and aspirations of a business.’’ Drawing on analogies with the dynamics of change to building architectures, they describe organisational change using the categories of purpose, structural materials, style, and collateral technologies. Style is defined as the holistic solution an architect develops to integrate a building’s purpose and its materials. It is one aspect of a dominant design.

Whereas management science [4] approaches can support active problem solving as part of incremental and modular innovation, the problem is: how can we deal with the periods preceding the consolidation of a new dominant design?

The alignment practices for architectural and radical innovations require a process that creates a new fit [12] from a consideration of the full scope of the business, from strategy to operations, from product and service functions to technology and market trends. Systems architecting is the discipline that strives for such a fit, balance, and compromise among the tensions of client needs and resources, technology, and the interests of multiple stakeholders [23]. Design decisions must be reconsidered, and purposes must be articulated in a process involving the stakeholders. IEEE 1471–2000 [13] defines a conceptual framework for the architecting process. It defines architecture as the fundamental organisation of a system embodied in its components, their relationships to each other, and to the environ ment, and the principles guiding its design and evolution. Models participate in architectural descriptions, which identify concerns that are important to stakeholders. Usually, a model will be a description of the structure, the relations, and the interactions of real world objects or processes. Modelling is the creation of abstractions or representations of a system to predict and analyse performance, costs, schedules, and risks and to provide guidelines for systems research, development, design, realisation, and management.

## 2.3. Engineering domains and architecture descriptions

ICT deployment in business is both a multi-stakeholder and a multi-disciplinary issue. The involvement of multiple stakeholders will imply the need for different models under multiple disciplines. From this stems the architect’s problem of keeping the models consistent [30].

It is useful to distinguish between the specific occurrences of the architectures and the models from which they are derived. From the models one can identify common architectural patterns that help us understand existing structures. They provide a medium for transferring knowledge from one domain to another and support alignment practices. Patterns also promote reuse of concepts, insights and knowledge.

Here we are concerned with three domains in which architecture matters:

1. The business architecture defines the business system in its environment of suppliers and customers. The system consists of humans and resources (including ICT), business processes, and rules. It belongs to the disciplines of industrial engineering and management science.

2. The application architecture details the software application components and their interaction. Its details can be described using object or component models, or application frameworks. It belongs to the discipline of computer science.

3. ICT platform architecture is the architecture of the generic resource layer, which describes the computers, networks, peripherals, operating systems, data base management systems, UI frameworks, system services, middleware, etc. that will be used as a platform for the construction of the system for the enterprise. Its description includes various platform paradigms such as mainframe– terminal, n-tier client–server, and mobile or wireless architectures. It belongs to the discipline of computer systems engineering.

The business architecture is derived from the business vision, goals and strategies. The use of a formal description is advisable to enable a proper support by the underlying ICT system.

Given a number of architecture occurrences and their descriptions, we distinguish patterns along two dimensions. Intra-domain conceptualisation is supported by the abstraction techniques of the engineering disciplines. Conceptualisation patterns link the architecture occurrences and the models by which they are described. Alignment deals with the coevolution of patterns in different domains and thus it exists in an inter-domain context.

## 2.4. Intra-domain patterns: conceptualisation

The AD is both the result and an enabler of a conceptualisation process (Fig. 1). The first stage is abstraction, during which concepts are deduced from or made explicit in real-life developments. The result is (part of) an AD. The second phase is instantiation or execution: concepts are used to generate or reconfigure systems or components for a particular context. In the third stage, monitoring and co-evolution, these processes alternate to provide a dynamic adjustment of the systems or components and the ADs. The analysis is first limited to a single domain and its AD, but subsequently will cross domains.

In the business domain, abstraction produces a description of the current configuration of the enterprise in terms of its resources, processes, and business rules (the ‘as-is model’) of the enterprise. This can serve as the basis for the analysis of current bottlenecks or for expressing new requirements. The analysis subsequently may result in a redesign into a ‘tobe’ model. Instantiation takes place when the ‘to-be model’ is enacted to produce an operational enterprise that can meet the new demands. We speak of coevolution, when the business architecture parallels the business processes as new goals need to be achieved. This implies high demands on the application software and ICT platform architecture domains [18,25].

In the software domain, conceptualisation patterns are very common. For instance, abstraction can be regarded as capturing the structure of the information being processed in a data model. Advanced CASE tools allow the generation of (parts of) the application for a particular database management system. Changes in the requirements cause adaptation of the data model, allowing the application and its model to co-evolve.

Promising conceptualisations arise from modelbased computing and simulation (see, e.g. [14,20]). A prominent example is the automatic generation of application software from business process models. Given a particular ICT platform this can even include an efficient allocation of processes to processing units, based on the requirements and a performance model. Changes in the processing requirements and capacities are analysed by use of the simulation model.

Such a model can be refined on the basis of measurements of the system for a suitable set of performance criteria. If the system and its simulation have a sufficiently modular structure, the model can also be used to perform what—if analyses of alternative system configurations and their performance.

## 2.5. Inter-domain patterns: alignment

The developments in the various domains naturally influence one another. A change in business strategy may require a redesign of the business architecture, with a reorganisation of the company. This change in turn will impose new requirements. We call this a drives-relation. Similarly, independent developments in the ICT platform or application software may lift obstructions and thus open up new opportunities. This we call the enables-relation. An alignment-relation occurs, when a balanced mutual influence takes place between domains.

Co-evolution between domains is a common form of alignment. An example is the introduction of a new version of a software component that triggers a modification of the business process because of its enhanced capabilities.

The investigation of the enables, drives and alignment relations reveals another kind of architectural development. Architecture descriptions (ADs) from one domain must be mapped onto those of another. Changes in one, which are abstractions from changes at the occurrence level, may then be translated into required changes for the mapped AD. Enactment of these required changes will bring the architectures in line again. The alignment dimension (see Fig. 2) is orthogonal to the dimension of conceptualisation.

A business strategy targets the goals of business processes, while architecture addresses their realisation. Strategic choices at the business architecture level may determine decisions about supply chains, e-markets, outsourcing, or distribution channels.

The strategic alignment model (SAM) [10] defines alignment perspectives for strategic-level drivers and purposes in terms of internal and external components in the business and IT domains. SAM builds on two building blocks: strategic fit for the alignment between external and internal components within the same domain, and functional integration for the integration of business and ICT domains. Our concept of alignment is rather closely linked with SAM’s functional integration. Strategic fit corresponds to an intradomain pattern. SAM’s IT domain covers both the application and ICT platform domain.

## 2.6. History as a constitutive element

By using architecture descriptions and their mutual relationships, we can derive patterns that characterise different alignment situations. Computers and software, initially considered as tools for solving isolated computational problems, have evolved to be pervasive systems taking over a large range of activities from the human worker. With this expansion, the need for architecture has become clear to the business, the application, and ICT platform engineering disciplines. New dominant designs have crossed multiple domains and have involved innovations in ICT support for business. For example, the three-schema architecture for database systems relates the business, application and ICT platform by its separation of the external (user-related), conceptual (semantic) and physical (storage related) models.

## 3. Historical development

Below we give a somewhat abstract outline of the historical development of the various architectures. We characterise each development phase by the dominant architectural model and a crude indication of the decade in which it became important, if not generally accepted.

Before the industrial revolution, manufacturing was based on craft. Ordering was always a local process. Trade required many links, was not predictable, often not based on money, and not included in ordering.

The history of modern organisations is rooted in two evolutionary lines [9]:

\- The principle of the division of labour, as described by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776), and improved by Ford and Sloan, who applied this to the assembly of the automobile.

\- The invention of the business bureaucracy [28], e.g. by railroad companies: in order to prevent collisions on single-track lines, railroad companies invented formalised operating procedures and the organisational structure and mechanisms to carry them out.

3.1. The functional hierarchy without automation: the 1950s

## 3.1.1. Business architecture

The dominant model of the organisation after the industrial revolution was based on the functional hierarchy and the general ledger. Until the middle of the 20th century, the enterprise structure was dominated by the machine bureaucracy [15,16].

## 3.1.2. Application architecture

There was no application architecture as computing systems were not available. There was a (manual) database for each function.

## 3.1.3. ICT platform architecture

There was no ICT platform. People processed information. Communication required humans to exchange documents or messages.

In summary, the functional hierarchy offered a dominant design and a single dimension for strategy execution. Innovation was confined to the functional units. Modular innovation occurred when units were being merged or reorganised. The introduction of computing facilities (hardware and software) was a radical innovation.

## 3.2. The functional hierarchy with function oriented automation: the 1960s

## 3.2.1. Business architecture

As the first computation and data storage applications became available, the business architecture remained the same, showing a hierarchy composed of self-contained functional units under central control. Smooth communication within organisations was mainly vertical. Horizontal communication was not organised and tended to be slow.

## 3.2.2. Application architecture

Software applications were introduced for calculation and data storage. For each function there was a self-contained application. Communications were not affected: messages were (manually) exchanged in the form of paper listings or magnetic tapes.

## 3.2.3. ICT platform architecture

The hardware consisted of a mainframe accessed by terminals. Processing, storage, and usage were performed centrally, often under the control of the finance department.

The business architecture was still based on the functional hierarchy. However, automation emerged, and improved horizontal communication for improved effectiveness and efficiency [2].

The computer was added to the technology base as an innovation. The enabling role of ICT for the enterprise was established: the speed and reliability of all data related activities and calculations were improved.

3.3. The functional hierarchy with shared data base on mainframe: the 1970s

## 3.3.1. Business architecture

The seventies showed a small change in the organisational structure in manufacturing and distribution of physical goods. Special co-ordinators become responsible for order promising and goods flow control. The geographical scope was enlarged, but not fundamentally changed. The functional hierarchy was still in place. Similar developments occurred in other sectors. For primary business processes, speed and quality became important. This led to ad-hoc adaptations in the organisation.

## 3.3.2. Application architecture

The application software became more integrated with the business processes. Integration was mainly ad hoc, and mirrored the functional structure of the business. However, a distinction between the data and application layer became visible. It was noted that data was common to multiple applications, resulting in database management systems (introduced in the mid 1960s) running on the mainframe that allowed efficient and controlled sharing of the data.

## 3.3.3. ICT platform architecture

The hardware architecture was still mainframeoriented. Processing no longer was exclusively central, with minicomputers supplying local support for functions that controlled their own databases. Communication between these systems was still mostly manual.

Business models were still hierarchic, with vertical flows of information. Basic logistical models emerged, and their appearance drove the need for a single integrated database, an architectural innovation, though this was ignored in most industries and com panies.

The application AD was built-up around a single data architecture. External schemas were mapped on a single (conceptual) schema, which in turn was mapped on a storage structure definition (The ANSI/ SPARC DBMS Model, see for instance [5]). Integration of information from different applications was gradually changed from manual to automatic. The relational model was implemented at IBM and appeared to be viable [3]. In the business domain, modular innovation continued. The ICT platform architecture was largely implicit, as only the components were visible.

Information modelling and data sharing solutions enable the emergence of business process re-engineer ing (BPR).

## 3.4. The process oriented enterprise: the 1980s

## 3.4.1. Business architecture

The 1980s show a change in the business world towards business units. Business process re-engineering appeared. Value-added processes (such as logistics in industrial enterprises) started to become the principle of organising the business. However, there was still a local geographic attitude in organisations.

## 3.4.2. Application architecture

Simple client–server enterprise applications began to appear. The first use of graphical user interfaces (GUI) was triggered by the graphical capabilities of the workstations, to allow the presentation of more information on the client system. The systematic design and generation of UIs was supported by the introduction of user interface management systems (UIMS), which enabled the separation of presentation aspects from the business logic. A three-tier software architecture emerged. GUI systems were the first application of object-oriented technologies.

## 3.4.3. ICT platform architecture

Smaller hardware systems were added to the ICT: workstations and PCs with their own processing capabilities were connected to powerful departmental systems and each other by fast local area networks. Towards the end of the decade, the hardware architecture became network-based and open. The computing workload was partially shifted to the smaller systems resulting in a two-tier, client–server hardware architecture. Processing was done at the server-side, while usage was now distributed to the clients.

A radical change occurred in the business domain with a shift from functions to processes. The business model started to stress the importance of integration of the various business functions into an overall business process.

Also the application and ICT platform models exhibited radical innovations. The software application model was built on the data-architecture of a single conceptual data model, crossing the boundaries of functional departments. Integrated client–server applications enabled enhancements in support of cross-functional business processes. ERP-software was introduced to support standard integration between applications on different functions.

The ICT platform solution changed to a client– server architecture, a modular innovation. Communication facilities, however, were mostly locked into proprietary solutions, limiting their scope to local area networks.

3.5. The supply chain process oriented enterprise: the 1990s

## 3.5.1. Business architecture

The early 1990s enlarged the scope of the business units from local to regional. Warehouses, factories and distribution centres were included, as well as different parts of legal and financial enterprise units. Supply chain processes became the focus of bilateral commercial process reengineering efforts. Internal integration was followed by supply chain integration, linking together ‘‘functions’’ of the supply chain members. However, this supply chain integration was primarily with parties belonging to the same global company, so that some standards could be enforced. In other cases standards were enforced by the dominant party in the supply chain (generally its head).

## 3.5.2. Application architecture

Enterprise-level client–server applications matured. The approach widened from expanding and integrating applications to the integration of applications based on different architectures. Communication between these components was necessarily asynchronous (messagebased). The infrastructure (middleware) enabled inte gration into a common environment based on the OMG’s CORBA or Microsoft’s distributed component object model ((D)COM). The UI paradigm shifted from the GUI paradigm, providing graphical presentation, to an object-oriented user interface (OOUI) para digm supporting the direct manipulation of objects on the screen. Object and component reuse was promoted by integrated development environments, in which applications are constructed by the configuration of templates [24]. Attempts to factor out the processdependency of applications led to workflow management systems.

## 3.5.3. ICT platform architecture

Wide area networks gained enough critical mass to enable global access. The ICT solution incorporated a multi-site aspect. Organisational units were either treated as parts of a cluster with a common database server or use a separate local server. The hardware architecture evolves from a two-tier architecture via a three-tier architecture to an n-tier architecture where applications and data have been partitioned over several servers.

Developments involved externalisation and globalisation. New concepts and paradigms led to modular innovation at all levels.

Long-term relationships between business partners were supported by the standardisation of messages and protocols. Frameworks were established for entire business sectors. These developments drove the need for external integration over the supply chain.

The scope of integration consequently widened from expanding or integrating applications based on a single, homogeneous information model to the integration of applications based on different, heterogeneous information models.

Standard telecom infrastructure was used to support electronic data interchange with different companies. This was supplemented by directory and message forwarding services.

3.6. Next: web-enabled agile process-oriented enterprise

## 3.6.1. Business architecture

Organisations are expanding their scope to become global and differentiate their patterns of co-operation to encompass collaborative activities. Multi-site and multi-enterprise problems permeate every aspect of the enterprise. Each unit differentiates between local and global responsibilities. We are on the verge of a re-engineering of engineering and commercial processes as well as market infrastructure. New applications include multi-enterprise estimating and resource planning simulations for joint bidding, and collaborative planning, forecasting, and replenishment.

## 3.6.2. Application architecture

The application architecture has become highly distributed, consisting of interoperable components that encapsulate standard functionalities. The mapping of these components and their persistent counterparts on the ICT-infrastructure is flexible and explicitly described in an ICT-implementation repository. The paradigm for communication and collaboration has changed from message-based to the deployment of intelligent agents. These provide a dynamic form of application partitioning.

## 3.6.3. ICT platform architecture

Besides powerful personal computers, a multitude of new peripheral devices allow increased mobility and place new demands on presentation services. High bandwidth networks and low cost communications enable the application of new communication and collaboration paradigms. More decentralised architectures emerge based on peer-collaboration.

In addition to supporting traditional businessto-business relationships, a globally accessible communication infrastructure allows new relationships among market players such as virtual enterprises. These developments drive the need for new, collaborative technologies.

The ICT-architectures enable many of the current innovations. Application based integration becomes less attractive, as it cannot provide flexibility. The UI paradigm becomes based on task modelling. The definition of data-structures relies on instance mark-up. All data is likely to become self-describing and carry its own meta-data, based on standards such as XML. Processing flow and logic will be stored in models, in preference to an implementation as executable code. These models will be self-describing. Application sessions will be generated based on a specific scope. An early attempt can be found in [26].

## 3.7. Summary and outlook

Over time, both the business requirements and the technological components, and their enabling role have dramatically changed. The need for architecture—as a tool for managing strategic business development, alignment of the different domains and complexity—has expanded from the business over the application towards the ICT platform domain. The ICT system that supports an enterprise has grown into a complex entity that includes hardware and software for data-storage, computation and communication, as well as data and information models.

The increasing need of businesses for flexibility and agility to cope with change may be provided by architectures supporting reflectivity. Reflection is a system’s ability to represent and control its own metalevel, i.e. its own specification. With such a capacity it can control its own evolution and dynamically adjust to changing circumstances [6]. Such architectures will coexist with the more traditional, i.e. non-reflexive, architectures, which will provide less flexibility but more performance in stable environments.

## 4. Alignment and its results

Alignment practices must take into consideration the relation between the various architectures.

Henderson and Venkatraman noted the dynamic nature of strategy: managers should recognise the need to evolve from one alignment perspective to another, based on shifts in the business environment. Our concepts of incremental and radical alignment are widely applicable, as they are independent of purposes, drivers, areas of choice, and of the level of the activity, be it strategic, tactical, or operational.

Ensuring that flexibility is preserved is a major challenge.

## 4.1. Incremental alignment

A new dominant design must preserve flexibility in order to graciously evolve in the presence of incremental or modular innovations.

The following architecting principles are important for preserving flexibility through periods of incremental alignment.

## 4.1.1. Modularity

A system that consists of moderately complex components with maximum cohesion, minimal coupling, and well-defined interfaces restricts the impact of changes to a few components.

## 4.1.2. Composability

The characteristic of a component or system to function not only stand-alone, but also as part of a larger system or in combination with new components.

## 4.1.3. Pattern extraction

The extraction of domain-specific generic models in order to support the reuse of designs and implementations. Patterns are important for the separation of domain-specific from common functionality.

Flexibility is an intra-domain matter, as long as proper interfaces between domains have been defined to decouple them (see Fig. 4). Modularity within each of the domains and abstract interfaces towards the others will enhance co-evolution within a domain.

## 4.2. Radical alignment

Developments in one domain may be enabled or driven by developments in another. ADs mediate these relationships. The business domain drives the developments in the other two. The growing enabling power of ICT systems, however, is based on the increased performance and functionality that can be supported by modern technology. The application domain adds increased functionality and adaptability. The ICT platform adds speed of computation, connectivity (space and time independence), and additional variability.

Fig. 4. Three architectures viewed as a layered system.

The necessity for a new dominant design is indicated by architectural break-down in more than one domain, because the architectural concepts have been stressed beyond their limits. What follows is usually a period of instability, of trial and error until a new dominant design, that is able to cover all three domains, emerges.

## 4.3. A moving window of flexibility and performance

Flexibility is an important architectural quality concern because it supports the adaptation of a business or ICT system to different circumstances. Two types of flexibility can be distinguished. Component flexibility is the possibility to support new use cases by adaptation of existent components or addition of new components, but without changes to the architecture. Architectural flexibility is the possibility to support new use cases by changes to the architecture (i.e. the component configuration) without changes to the components. The need for flexibility and adaptability implies an important trade-off between modularity and integration. Erens’ [8] view of modularity and integration is that:

\- Modularity is a mechanism for supporting the upgrading and reuse of existing functions or components. It reduces the costs of system development and maintenance at the expense of higher operational costs. A module or component is any of a set of units designed to be arranged or joined in a variety of ways. A broken module can simply be replaced by a correctly functioning one. The more components in a product, however, the more operations are needed to assemble it.

\- Integration of functions, components and technology, on the other hand, trades stability and performance optimised operations against higher initial costs and reduced flexibility. Integration in a product design involves merging components.

This reduces the number of components and inter-component operations, but fixes the relationships between components.

In the inter-domain context of business, application, and ICT platform architecture, adaptations must be determined from the needs of the enterprise and enabled by the technology. Enterprises can nowadays only survive when they can cope with changes in their environment or even cause these changes.

The same is true for the ICT systems supporting the business processes. Also the allocation of components to a site becomes dynamic. Components may disappear, enter, or change functionality and location in the system. Mobile agents, which move through the system, are an extreme example. Inter-domain and radical alignment require integration of components, definition of shared resources, common models, and common interfaces between components or systems. The consequence of integration is that one has to give up local specialisation and intra-component efficiency in favour of uniform and overall efficient solutions.

Ulrich and Tung [27] state that most architectures evolve from being modular to being integrated. We gain precision by interpreting this statement in a multidomain context of evolving systems. From a crossdomain perspective modularity should prevail within any particular domain. Integration across domains will strengthen the system as the architectures in the different domains enable function, component, or technology sharing in order to make the system more efficient. As interactions among business-, ICT-, and application-components are better understood, conceptualisation has taken place, leading to more generally applicable concepts. Integrated systems embodying these concepts have emerged, crossing domain boundaries and enabling radical innovations (see Fig. 4).

A common recipe for failure is the inability to strike a new balance: ICT professionals and providers, in their eagerness to push new technologies, tend to underestimate or ill-position the window of flexibility. The business managers tend to emphasise the performance criteria of mature solutions. Hence our recommendation to walk through a well-defined architecting process as part of the radical alignment practices.

## 5. Conclusions

We have discussed the relations among the ADs for business, software application, and ICT platform architectures and their role in incremental and radical alignment.

Flexibility by modularity and performance optimisation by integration are principles relevant to several engineering disciplines, and several architecture generations characterised by dominant designs. The historical development of enterprises and their ICT support illustrates co-evolution and alignment, and underscores the following statements:

\- Radical alignment is required for integral solutions.

\- Incremental alignment requires modularity within a domain and the definition of proper interfaces between the ADs that describe the dominant design. Management should be aware that incremental alignment practices for a given dominant design do not warrant radical innovation.

\- For management a major challenge is the preservation of flexibility and integration as new dominant designs are deployed. Another one is the assessment when conditions are favourable to take on radical alignment.

From the consideration of the phases of enterprise development and ICT deployment, it is obvious that ICT has considerably contributed to the integration of enterprises along (business) functions and (business) processes. In the past, this integration—a radical innovation—has often been accompanied by a poor ability to respond to changes: ill-conception of the window of flexibility in combination with poor incremental alignment practices lead to a technology lock in and to solutions becoming obsolete rapidly.
