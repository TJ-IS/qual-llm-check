# The use of computer-based information systems by German managers to support decision making

George E. Vlahos<sup>a</sup>, Thomas W. Ferratt<sup>a,\*</sup>, George Knoepfle<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>University of Dayton, 300 College Park, Dayton, OH 45469, USA

<sup>b</sup>Wiesbadener Str. 4, 81243 Munich, Germany

Received 28 August 2001; received in revised form 31 March 2003; accepted 20 June 2003

Available online 13 November 2003

## Abstract

We surveyed German managers to investigate their use of information technology (IT), including their perception of the value provided by computer-based information systems (CBIS) and their satisfaction with CBIS in supporting decisions. Participants were predominantly male, the majority were older than 45, most had taken two or more college computer courses, and over 90% were middle and top-level managers. They represented a variety of functional areas. Results show that these managers were relatively heavy IT users, with over 10 h of use per week. Overall, there was a significant correlation between the amount of use of IT and perceived value of the CBIS, as well as between satisfaction with and perceived value of the CBIS. Hypothesis tests for task-technology fit (TTF) showed that the greatest TTF was related to resource allocation, evaluating alternatives, identifying problems, and making short-term decisions. For middle and top managers, information reporting systems (IRS), which provide regularly scheduled reports, had greater TTF than more flexible inquiry and analysis systems, such as decision support systems (DSS).

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of IT has had a substantial impact on managers in all functional areas and at all managerial levels. Although managerial use of IT has been studied in several countries (e.g., [3,5,10,11,14,16,18– 20,22,23]), available literature lacks such studies of German managers. The focus of this study is therefore to investigate IT use by German managers, including the managers’ assessment of the value of their computer-based information systems (CBIS) and their satisfaction with their CBIS.

There are three main reasons why German managers were chosen for the study: (1) German managers are important players in the European and world economy. Germany, as a major member of the European Union (EU), has influence in forming and implementing economic and technical policy decisions about the EU. (2) The economic and technical environment in which German managers work has resulted in effective CBIS. The versatility and acceptability of German software for solving various types of business problems is a strong indicator of its importance in the development and use of IT. (3) Despite the fact that there has been an extraordinary expansion in the research and development of information technology (IT) in Germany, there is very little published research on its use by German managers.

## 1.1. Prior research on IT use

Since WWII much research has been conducted on the implementation and the use of CBIS to support decision making [15]. Studies have reported that managers who use IT do so for less than 2 to more than 14 h per week. The most commonly used software packages are spreadsheets, word processing, and database management. Reasons for using CBIS include planning, assisting in decision making, budgeting, forecasting, communicating with others, looking for trends, controlling and guiding activities, and finding problems.

A few studies address differences and similarities in IT use between cultures [4,12]. However, these studies have not typically used common conceptual bases or measures to support the development of cumulative knowledge. This makes it difficult to understand the importance of cultural difference on the value and satisfaction that managers perceive from their CBIS use. Only a few studies report on the perceived value of and satisfaction with the various types of CBIS in supporting managerial work. The most extensive survey of value and satisfaction is found in two separate studies with Greek and US managers and a subsequent comparison of these two samples of managers [6].

## 1.2. Research hypotheses

To provide an understanding of German managers’ attitudes toward CBIS relative to managers in other cultures, this study tested four hypotheses based on concepts, measures, and findings from prior studies of US and Greek managers. As reported in those studies, task-technology fit (TTF) for resource allocator and entrepreneurial decision making roles was at least as strong as that for other decision roles for both Greek and US managers at all management levels. To test whether German managers are similar to Greek and US managers, the following hypothesis was tested.

Hypothesis 1. The TTF for the managerial decision roles of resource allocator and entrepreneur is at least as strong as that for the decision roles of disturbance handler and negotiator.

Similarly, TTF for the decision steps of evaluating alternatives and identifying problems and issues was at least as strong as that for other decision steps for both Greek and US managers at all management levels. Thus, the following hypothesis was tested for German managers.

Hypothesis 2. The TTF for the decision steps of evaluating the outcomes of each alternative and identifying problems and issues is at least as strong as that for the steps of generating alternative courses of action, ranking the alternatives and choosing one, and implementing the chosen alternative.

In addition, TTF for making short-term decisions was at least as strong as that for other management functions with both Greek and US managers at all management levels, leading to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. The TTF for making short-term decisions is at least as strong as that for making tactical and strategic decisions.

Finally, a relatively surprising finding was that information reporting systems (IRS), which provide fixed, regularly scheduled reports, had a higher value, or TTF, than any of the other types of CBIS for both Greek and US managers at all three management levels. This is particularly surprising, given prior research that reported managers’ informational needs would be better served by more flexible inquiry and analysis systems and the expectation that DSS would provide this flexible capability [1]. Such a prior result leads to the final hypothesis to be tested with German managers.

Hypothesis 4. The TTF of IRS is stronger than that of other types of CBIS.

## 1.3. Specific research objectives

Our reason for testing these hypotheses comes from an attempt to meet the following three objectives:

1. to develop a contextual profile of German managers’ use of CBIS, including the degree of IT use, types of hardware and software used, the security of information provided by the CBIS, and their satisfaction with the CBIS;

2. to examine the value of the CBIS in supporting various managerial activities; and

3. to examine the relative value and importance of various types of CBIS.

Meeting the first objective provides a foundation for understanding how German managers use CBIS. Meeting the second provides a basis for testing Hypotheses 1–3, whereas the third allows for testing Hypothesis 4.

## 2. Methodology

## 2.1. Survey instrument and sampling procedure

The data for the study was gathered via a survey questionnaire. It included demographic data and items needed to develop the profile described in the objectives, including the degree of use of IT, value of its use, types of hardware, software, and CBIS used, relative importance of and satisfaction with existing CBIS, adequacy of investment in IT, security of information, and plans for changing existing systems. Similar versions of this questionnaire were used in the previous studies of Greek and US managers, except that questions on adequacy of investment in IT and security of information were new to this version. The questionnaire was reviewed carefully by academic and corpo rate MIS professionals with extensive international experience. The comments and suggestions of these MIS professionals regarding the clarity, validity, and consistency of the questions were incorporated into the survey instrument. The resulting changes were minor and involved moving the demographic questions from the beginning (where they were in the previous surveys) to the end.

The final English version of the questionnaire was translated into German by one of the authors, a native German with fluent command of English. The translation made appropriate changes and adaptations to reflect the educational, cultural, and business characteristics of German managers. The translation was further reviewed by two German linguists from the University of Augsburg. The final version of the German questionnaire was mailed to a randomly selected sample of 1000 German managers. The sample list was purchased from Schober Direct Marketing, a professional organization that frequently updates and validates the names in its data bank. A cover letter, in German, explaining the purpose of the survey, and a pre-paid business reply envelope were attached to each mailed questionnaire. The responses were mailed to a rented post-office box in Augsburg, Germany. One hundred and seventeen (117) managers responded. This response rate compares favorably to previous Greek and US studies, which had 55 and 64 respondents to 1000 mailings.

## 2.2. Demographic profile of respondents

The German respondents were overwhelmingly male: there were only 4 women out of a total of 116 who responded. The majority of the respondents (63.2%) were over the age of 45 and only 11.4% were under 34. The respondents were equally divided as to the number of college computer courses they had taken: exactly one-third of the respondents had 0–1, 2–3, or 4–5 college computer courses. Respondents were well educated: 80% were college graduates. Although a comparison of respondents with nonrespondents would be helpful, a profile of those on the mailing list could not be obtained to make such a comparison.

## 2.3. Scales

## 2.3.1. Use and types of hardware and software

Participants indicated the number of hours they used various types of hardware each week. The times for all hardware were combined to yield a total; similarly, a total was computed for software. (See Appendix A, questions 13 and 15.) Another question asked respondents to indicate total hours of use per week (see Appendix A, question 12); as opposed to the questions on hardware and software use, this question provided categories of hours for respondents to check. These categories were recoded as follows:

a. >16, recoded to 20;

b. 9–16, recoded to 12.5;

c. 5–8, recoded to 6.5;

d. 1–4, recoded to 2.5;

e. <1, recoded to 0.5; and

f. none, recoded to 0.

Although the recoding of values greater than 16 truncates the upper end of the distribution of responses, it prevents outliers from skewing the mean. These recoded values for the overall question, the sum of hardware hours, and the sum of software hours were averaged for each respondent. The average of these three totals provided a reliable measure of use. Cronbach’s alpha (coefficient of reliability) was 0.84.

## 2.3.2. Appropriateness of investment in IT, security of information, and involvement in specifying CBIS

Single questions were used for each of these items. See Appendix A, questions 16, 6, and 7.

## 2.3.3. Satisfaction with CBIS and identifying planned changes

Overall satisfaction was based on a single item (see Appendix A, question 20). The identification of planned change focused on whether further development of the CBIS was to support informational needs of managers or something else (see Appendix A, question 19).

## 2.3.4. Value of CBIS

Usefulness was measured for specific aspects of managerial work, managers’ mental models, and a broad view of value. Measures of the value of CBIS in supporting managerial work included the following items:

(1) each of Mintzberg’s [17] decision roles—entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, and negotiator (see Appendix A, question 3; responses of zero (0) were not included in the analyses);

(2) each of five specific decision steps [8]—identifying problems or issues, generating alternative courses of action, evaluating the outcomes of each alternative, ranking the alternatives and choosing one, and implementing the chosen alternative (see Appendix A, question 4);

(3) each of Anthony’s [2] specific decision-making tasks—long-term (strategic), mid-term (tactical), and short-term (operational) decisions (see Appendix A, question 5); and

(4) two aspects of the value of CBIS for managers’ mental models [13]—one for forming the mental model and the other for the use of the mental model in making decisions (see Appendix A, questions 8 and 9).

The measures of value of CBIS for the first three types of items—decision roles, decision steps, and decision-making tasks—serve as measures of tasktechnology fit (TTF). A broad view of the value was measured by an overall importance question (see Appendix A, question 10).

For consistency with prior studies using these scales, an overall value index was based on the mean of five questions. Two items were questions on the value of CBI used to support long-term and mid-term decision making. Since these items were on a 5-point scale and all others on a 7-point scale, responses were recoded to a 7-point scale by dividing each by 5, multiplying by 6, and adding 1 when computing the index. Another two items were on the value of the CBIS related to the mental model. The fifth item was the overall importance question. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.77.

## 2.3.5. Use and relative importance of various types of CBIS

Two listings of types of CBIS were presented to respondents. The first consisted of both fixed and flexible systems; the second of six commonly known types of systems: information reporting system (IRS), decision support systems (DSS), and transaction processing systems (TPS). (See Appendix A, questions 14 and 17.) Respondents ranked the listed sources of CBI in order of importance in decision making, with 1 the most important. The percentage of respondents ranking an item was computed. In addition, ranks were recoded to yield weights from 1 to 7, with 7 being high (i.e., 1 recoded to 7, 2 recoded to 5, 3 recoded to 3, 4 and higher recoded to 1, and no ranking coded as 0). These weights were then averaged across respondents to yield a measure of relative importance, which also serves as our measure of TTF for a specific type of CBIS.

## 2.4. Data analysis

Tests of significant differences are based on analysis of variance. Some tests compare means of different groups, e.g., management levels. Others are tests of mean differences in responses to different variables, e.g., the differences between the TTF of CBIS in supporting different decision roles in testing Hypothesis 1.

## 3. Results

3.1. Research objective 1: contextual profile of German managers using CBIS

## 3.1.1. Amount of IT use

Table 1 shows that the average use of IT for the entire sample of German respondents is at the upper end of the use reported in prior studies, indicating that the German managers participating in this study are heavy users of IT. It is not known if this heavier use is the result of changes over time, but prior studies obviously obtained their data in earlier years of CBIS. There is a significant difference $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ in the number of hours of IT use per week in the functional areas of German managers with Human Resources, Marketing/Sales, and Other managers (which do not include Accounting and Finance)—they are significantly lower than IS managers.

Assuming an 8 h work day and a 5-day work week, the average German manager in this study reported using CBIS about 25% of the work day. When the composition of the sample across management levels was taken into consideration, this usage was similar to results reported for Greek (29%) and US (32%) managers studied with a similar methodology; the German sample has the lowest percentage of first-line managers, who are the heaviest users in all three samples. Admittedly, the managers who participated in the study are those who use CBIS, so this considerable percentage of the work day devoted to CBIS use should not be generalized to all managers. Nevertheless, these results show that the German managers participating in this study, similar to managers participating in comparable studies in Greece and the US, are relatively heavy users of CBIS.

## 3.1.2. Types of hardware used

The top part of Table 2 shows the percent of respondents using various types of hardware and their average hours of use per week. The majority of respondents use a PC, terminal, or workstation connected to a network or central computer rather than a stand-alone PC. Among the three levels of managers using a networked system, the average number of hours per week is 19.8 for first-line, 14.2 for midlevel, and 11.2 for top-level managers. Considering the functional areas, the average weekly use for this type of hardware is the highest for IS managers (22.5 h) and the lowest for marketing/sales managers (10.7 h) and others (10.4 h). There is a strong significant difference among the functional areas in using this type of hardware $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ .

## 3.1.3. Types of software applications used

Table 2 also shows that spreadsheet/financial report preparation is the first choice of software used by the majority of German respondents. Among the three levels of managers using spreadsheet/financial software, the average number of hours per week is 4.6, 4.4, and 4.3 for first-line, mid-level, and top-level managers, respectively. At the 0.05 level, there is no significant difference among the three levels of managers regarding their use of the different types of software. Among the functional areas, the highest weekly average use of spreadsheets is by the accounting/finance managers (8.2 h) followed by the human resource (4.3 h) and other managers (4.3). The marketing/sales managers are the lowest users of spreadsheets (2.5 h). There is a significant difference $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ in the average weekly use of spreadsheets among the functional areas. Word processing is the second choice of software followed by database applications and electronic mail/communication. It is interesting to notice that the use of a browser for accessing the Internet or an intranet is relatively low.

## 3.1.4. Appropriateness of investment in IT, security of information, and involvement in specifying CBIS

Eighty-four percent (84%) of the responding managers rate their corporation’s amount of investment in IT as adequate or better. There is a significant difference $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ among the managerial levels regarding their corporation’s investment in IT. Top-level managers rate their corporation’s investment the highest (5.1), and the other two levels rate it lower (4.5 for both). There is a significant difference $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ across functional areas regarding investment in IT. Marketing/sales managers rate the adequacy of investment the lowest (4.1) and the rest rate it in the range of 4.9–5.2.

Sixty-two percent (62%) of the respondents report they are very confident that the CBI they use is not compromised due to lack of adequate security. There is a significant difference $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ among the functional areas regarding the security of CBI. IS managers are the most (6.2) and marketing/sales the least (4.9) confident about the security of CBI.

About half of the responding German managers report that they helped specify more than 40% of the content or format of the information presented to them by CBIS. There is a significant difference among the managerial levels $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ and the functional areas $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ . First-line supervisors rated their involvement the highest (3.3) and top-level managers rated it the lowest (2.2). Considering the functional areas, IS managers rated their involvement the highest (4.0) and a combination of functional area managers categorized as ‘‘other’’ rated it the lowest (2.2).

## 3.1.5. Satisfaction with CBIS and planned changes

Table 3 shows that the entire sample of German respondents in all levels of management and most functional areas rated their overall satisfaction with the CBIS higher than (and in all cases at least as high as) its overall value. None of the differences across levels or functional areas was statistically significant at the 0.05 level for either satisfaction or the overall value index.

Fifty-seven percent (57%) of the responding German managers indicated that they intend to initiate changes to their corporation’s existing CBIS. Fortynine percent (49%) of these reported that the intent of these changes was to develop a CBIS that supports the informational needs of managers instead of another purpose. Almost 69% of the responding German managers indicated that the CBIS most important to them was developed to support the informational needs of managers (decision makers) instead of another purpose.

## 3.2.2. Hypothesis 1

The results of testing Hypothesis 1 verified that the role of resource allocator had greater TTF than the role of negotiator $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ , and it was not significantly different from the TTF for the role of disturbance handler. The TTF for the role of entrepreneur was not significantly different from the TTF for the role of negotiator, but it was lower than the TTF for the role of disturbance handler. These results held across management levels and for the functional areas as well. Thus, with this sample of German managers, Hypothesis 1 was supported for the resource allocator role but not the entrepreneur role.

## 3.2.3. Value of IT support for steps in decision making

In the second area, respondents rated the value of CBI for each of the five decision steps. For the whole German sample and all three levels of managers the value of CBI was highest for the evaluation step with the identifying problems step next. The CBI value rating was substantially lower for the steps of generating alternative courses of action and implementing the chosen alternative by the top- and mid-level managers. At the 0.05 level, there was no significant difference among the managerial levels and functional areas of the respondents regarding their ratings of the value of CBI within each of the decision steps.

## 3.2.4. Hypothesis 2

The results verified Hypothesis 2, showing that the decision steps of evaluating the outcome of each alternative and identifying the problems and issues had greater TTF than the steps of generating alternative courses of action, ranking the alternative and choosing one, and implementing the chosen alternative $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results held across management levels and for the functional areas as well. The difference in TTF between evaluating the outcome of each alternative and implementing the chosen alternative was even more significant $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ for top managers than for middle and first-line managers, with top managers having TTF scores of 5.80 and 3.88, respectively for these two decision steps, while middle managers had scores of 5.33 and 4.40, and first-line managers had scores of 5.88 and 4.88. Thus, Hypothesis 2 was supported.

## 3.2.5. Value of IT support for short-term, mid-term, and long-term decisions

In the third area of Table 4, respondents indicated the percent of CBI used for each of three decisionmaking tasks. The whole sample as well as all three managerial levels rated the value of CBI the greatest for short-term decisions. First-line supervisors rated the value of CBI the highest for all decision tasks and mid-level managers rated it the lowest. At the 0.05 level, there was no significant difference among the managerial levels and functional areas of the respondents regarding their ratings of the value of CBI within each of the decision-making tasks.

## 3.2.6. Hypothesis 3

Testing Hypothesis 3 verified that making shortterm decisions had greater TTF than making tactical and strategic decisions $( P < 0 . 0 1 )$ . These results held across management levels and for the functional areas as well. Thus, Hypothesis 3 was supported.

## 3.2.7. Value of IT support for mental models

Table 5 shows that the whole sample, as well as all managerial levels and functional areas considered the information provided by CBIS to be of greater value for guiding planning, controlling, and operating decisions than for forming or revising the mental model of the corporation. All levels and functional areas of German managers valued the importance of CBIS at a level approaching the high end of the rating scale (5.2 or greater on a 7-point scale) for the success and effectiveness of their managerial duties. At the 0.05 level of significance there was no significant difference among the managerial levels and functional areas for all three of these questions.

## 3.2.8. Relationship among amount of use, satisfaction, and value

Correlation analysis shows a significant relationship between overall hours of use and the overall value index $( r = 0 . 3 7 , ~ P < 0 . 0 1 )$ This result stands in contrast to that of previous studies of Greek and US managers, where this relationship was not significant. The relationship between overall satisfaction and the overall value index is also significant $( r = 0 . 2 2 ,$ $P < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The relationship between overall hours of use and overall satisfaction is not significant $( r = 0 . 1 1 )$ . These two relationships are similar to those of previous studies.

## 3.3. Research objective 3: relative importance of various types of CBIS used

Table 6 presents the relative importance of various types of CBIS in supporting managerial decision making. The first classification was based on Alloway and Quillard’s fixed and flexible systems. Although the percentage of respondents ranking a system was approximately the same for each of the four identified systems, the weighted mean rank of relative importance was highest for regular reports. For the second classification of Commonly Known Systems, a greater percentage of respondents ranked IRS as the most important source of information for decision making. In addition, top-level managers ranked IRS the highest (5.1) and first-line supervisors the lowest (3.9). From the functional managers, accounting/finance ranked IRS the highest (5.6) followed by marketing/sales (4.6) with IS managers providing the lowest ranking (3.9). The German managers ranked CSCW/OIS, EIS, DSS, and TPS as relatively equally important for decision making. At the 0.05 level, there was no significant difference among the levels of management and functional areas of the German respondents regarding the importance of each specific type of CBIS.

## 3.3.1. Hypothesis 4

Testing Hypothesis 4 for the whole sample verified that the TTF for IRS was greater than that for other types of CBIS $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This result held when comparing IRS to other commonly known systems (i.e., CSCW/OIS, EIS, DSS, TPS, and AI/ES) or when comparing the regular reports to the other fixed and flexible systems. Significantly $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ , management level made a difference when comparing IRS with TPS. The TTF for IRS was greater than the TTF for TPS for top managers (5.12 versus 3.34) and middle managers (4.16 versus 3.60), the vast majority of this sample (n ¼ 41 and 67, respectively); however, for first-line managers (n ¼ 8), the TTF for IRS was lower than the TTF for TPS (3.88 versus 5.13). Also when management level was taken into account, the contrast between IRS and DSS was no longer significant, even though the TTF for IRS was still greater than that for DSS for all management levels (i.e., 5.12 versus 4.07 for top managers, 4.16 versus 3.36 for middle managers, and 3.88 versus 3.25 for first-line managers). Functional area was not significant in explaining differences between the TTF for IRS and other types of systems, but using functional area when contrasting the TTF for IRS and CSCW/OIS as well as for IRS and TPS resulted in those differences no longer being significant. Thus, this hypothesis was only partially supported. Importantly, though, within this sample, the TTF for IRS was at least as strong as that for DSS and greater than that for EIS across management levels and functional areas.

## 4. Conclusion

## 4.1. Discussion

One of the surprising results is the relatively high ranking of systems that provide regular reports. This finding is contrary to Gorry and Scott Morton’s seminal work touting the value of DSS for managers more than three decades ago [7]. Similarly, it brings into question a widely shared conviction underlying the work on DSS and EIS reflected in Alloway and Quillard’s statement almost two decades ago that managers would be better served by more flexible inquiry and analysis systems. One explanation is that the greatest marginal value for CBIS arises from reports that managers and system designers have designed and built into a regular reporting system. Ad hoc or specific requests for inquiries or analyses are perceived as having lower marginal value, particularly for higher level managers, than the time to develop the needed information themselves or explain their requirements to system designers. A corollary to this explanation is that, contrary to conventional wisdom, the majority of managerial decision making is best supported through CBIS by more structured reporting systems.

The finding that task-technology fit for the role of entrepreneur is not as strong in German managers as with Greek and US managers and, indeed, is lower than for disturbance handler, is intriguing. One explanation could be that cultural differences on Hofstede’s uncertainty avoidance dimension [9] have led German managers to perceive greater value for handling unexpected disturbances.

Results from examining German managers in different functional areas indicate that interesting differences in use and perceived value occur across functional areas. Managers in information systems and in accounting and finance are the heaviest users and perceive the greatest value, based on the overall value index and the single item measuring overall importance of CBIS. Managers in marketing/sales and human resources are the lightest users and have relatively low perceived value. Even though there is a significant correlation between use and value within this German sample, previous studies using this same methodology did not find a significant correlation except for top managers. Assuming that top managers have greater discretion in choosing to use CBIS, the relation between use and value may be more likely when use is highly discretionary, consistent with the revised technology acceptance model [21].

## 4.2. Contributions

This study provided new knowledge on IT use by German managers and the perceived value and satisfaction that they obtain from their CBIS in helping make effective decisions. The results provide the basis for comparative studies on the use and value of IT in the decision making of managers from different countries. The research instrument used in previous studies was translated into German and could be used in future surveys on the use of IT by German managers. A note of caution that arises from anecdotal evidence in our study is that managers may find it increasingly difficult to recognize distinctions among the commonly known types of CBIS because of the greater integration of systems.

## 4.3. Summary

The German managers were relatively heavy users of CBIS. The greatest task-technology fit occurred for resource allocation, evaluating alternatives, identifying problems, and making short-term decisions; in addition, CBIS that provided regular reports were at least as important as systems that provided more flexible inquiry and analysis capabilities. These results hold for the overall German sample as well as different management levels and functional areas. Since these findings are similar to those found in previous studies using a similar methodology for Greek and US managers at different times and with a different language version of the research instrument, we conclude that these findings are robust. Key differences show that TTF for disturbance handling is greater for German managers and that there is a significant correlation between use and perceived value of CBIS in supporting managerial decision making.
