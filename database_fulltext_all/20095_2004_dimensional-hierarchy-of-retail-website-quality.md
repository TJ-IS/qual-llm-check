# Dimensional hierarchy of retail website quality

Soyoung Kim<sup>a,\*</sup>, Leslie Stoel<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Textiles, Merchandising, and Interiors, University of Georgia, 352 Dawson Hall, Athens, GA 30602-3622, USA <sup>b</sup>Department of Consumer and Textile Sciences, 265 Campbell Hall, 1787 Neil Avenue, Columbus, OH 43210-1295, USA

Accepted 4 July 2003

## Abstract

This study used Loiacono’s WebQual<sup>TM</sup> scale to examine the dimensional hierarchy of the quality of apparel websites. A total of 273 female online apparel shoppers responded to a mail survey. We compared five alternative models proposing relationships among 12 dimensions of website quality. The results supported our hypothesis that website quality for sites selling apparel products was conceptualized as a 12-dimensional construct rather than as a general construct. Our results did not support Loiacono’s five-dimensional second-order factor structure or our proposed six-dimensional second-order factor structure. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Website quality; E-tailers; Apparel; Dimensional hierarchy

## 1. Introduction

As companies have become more effective in their use of websites, they have become more complex. For some organizations, they serve as repositories of information for various stakeholders and the public. For others, websites also offer transaction capabilities, providing an additional mechanism from which to serve customers. Since websites serve as an important point of contact for most companies, assessing their effectiveness or quality of the website is important as a way to understand whether the company is providing the type and quality of information and interaction to satisfy website users. This is especially true for companies selling goods and services on their websites. Customers must be satisfied with their experience with the website or they will not return. Thus, the assessment of website quality has become a priority for companies. Researchers from several disciplines have developed and tested instruments to measure website quality (e.g., [2,5,25]). All have found website quality to be a multi-dimensional construct. In these studies, a large number of items have been generated to measure website quality, they have been tested on a variety of selling and non-selling sites, and quality of the websites has been assessed by experienced online shoppers, student website users, and website developers. Few studies, however, have explored the dimensional hierarchy of these items so that little is known about the relations between the various scales or the overall construct of website quality.

For companies using their website for transaction generation, website quality may have a major impact on sales. Therefore, it is important to understand the dimensions of website quality so that sites can be developed to facilitate the online interaction between a consumer and the company. However, the information and interaction desired by a consumer may differ depending on the type of product or service [11]. Thus, our purpose was to use an established, comprehensive website evaluation instrument to examine the dimensionality of the quality of websites selling a single type of product. The Loiacono WebQual<sup>TM</sup> scale is such an instrument. Development of a 36-item scale included an extensive literature review and interviews of consumers. Analysis and validation of the instrument showed 12 distinct dimensions representing website quality, significantly more dimensions than other known instruments. This work reveals to e-tailers the dimensions, from a consumer perspective, that characterize website quality. This information is needed when developing and revising websites to increase quality, which is positively associated with customer satisfaction and purchase intention [26].

Since the characteristics of a website desired by consumers may differ by the type of product, we restricted analysis to websites selling one product category: apparel. According to M.B. Whitfield, Senior Vice President of Retail Forward, Inc., a retail consulting firm, clothing has recently emerged as one of the top categories of merchandise sold on the Internet. Analysis of data collected from their proprietary Internet Users Consumers Panel showed that in November 2001, clothing was the most frequently purchased item, with 30% of Internet users purchasing clothing online in the US, as compared to 29% purchasing books and 25% purchasing music products. In a recent Ernst & Young survey, 37% of respondents indicated that they had purchased apparel and accessories online in comparison to 49 and 28% for computer products and toys, respectively [19]. Forrester Research estimates that apparel sales online will rise from \$5.9 billion in 2000 to US\$ 20 billion by 2003 [10,18].

In addition to the emergence of the apparel category as a leader in online sales, researchers have also noted that consumers associate a high level of risk to the purchase of apparel via home shopping methods. This is due to the consumer’s inability to touch or try on the garments [16,20,22]. It has been proposed that online apparel merchants should provide a higher quantity and quality of product and customer service information on their websites to decrease risk perceptions and increase purchase intention [28]. For websites selling apparel products, this suggests that understanding customer perceptions of website quality is a priority.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Dimensionality of website quality

Every study assessing website quality that we have located provides some empirical evidence that website quality is a multidimensional construct. This is true whether website users or website designers evaluated the website or whether the site was selling or not. However, the vast majority of these studies are exploratory, so most have not confirmed the multidimensional nature of the construct.

Two studies created a priori dimensions based on literature review, but did not test them. Zhang and von Dran’s study [31] was motivated by Herzberg’s hygiene and motivator factors and suggested that certain website features are necessary but not sufficient to elicit positive perceptions or prevent negative perceptions of website quality, while other features are not necessary, but do increase positive perceptions of website quality. They compiled an extensive list of 42- scale items grouped a priori into eleven dimensions: (1) information content, (2) cognitive outcomes, (3) enjoyment, (4) privacy, (5) user empowerment, (6) visual appearance, (7) technical support, (8) navigation, (9) organization of information, (10) credibility, and (11) impartiality. Student respondents ranked the importance of website features for an online news service to validate the measures. A second group of students assessed six types of sites (selling and nonselling) and analysis showed that different dimensions were important for different types of sites. For example, the navigation dimension was important for all sites, whereas privacy was critical only for e-commerce. While the results suggested that perceptions of website quality was composed of multiple dimensions, the authors did not perform exploratory or confirmatory factor analyses to support the dimensionality.

Lin and Lu [23] tested the effect of website quality on student perceptions of usability and usefulness of an electronic newspaper. They operationalized website quality as a three-dimensional construct composed of (1) information quality, (2) response time, and (3) system accessibility. Path analysis supported the effect of the three website quality dimensions on usability and usefulness as antecedents of intention to reuse the site in the future. However, they did not test the dimensionality of website quality using exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis.

Several other studies used exploratory factor analysis to investigate the dimensionality of their measurement instruments. Liu and Arnett [24] surveyed webmasters of Fortune 1000 companies about factors that contributed to website success. They originally proposed six dimensions of website quality, but exploratory factor analysis revealed four: (1) quality of information and service, (2) system use, (3) playfulness, and (4) system design quality.

Another study examined experienced online shoppers’ perceptions of dimensions critical to the success of business websites selling to consumers [27]. A range of website attributes were collected from the literature, but dimensions were not proposed a priori. Exploratory factor analysis revealed four dimensions of website quality: (1) information content, (2) design, (3) security, and (4) privacy. Discriminant analysis showed that these four dimensions were useful in discriminating between low and high purchase intention of shoppers. The authors did not provide the percentage of variance explained by the four dimensions.

Aladwani and Palvia also examined website quality from a user perspective, but used student samples in a two-phase study. They proposed three dimensions of website quality: (1) technical adequacy, (2) web content, and (3) web appearance. In the first phase of the study, respondents evaluated websites in general to generate valid scale items. In the second, respondents evaluated two selling and two non-selling sites to confirm the scale items. Exploratory factor analysis in the first phase found four dimensions: (1) technical adequacy, (2) content quality, (3) specific content, and (4) appearance. These dimensions accounted for 67% of the variance in perceived website quality. Their second phase confirmed the reliability and convergent and discriminant validities of the four dimensions. In addition, the four factors were correlated with a rating of overall quality of the website.

Barnes and Vigden have conducted extensive work on an instrument to measure website quality of both non-selling and selling websites. Early efforts focused on technology and information as indicators of quality for university websites [8]. Five dimensions were proposed a priori and reliability analysis showed four dimensions to emerge: (1) ease-of-use, (2) experience with site, (3) information, and (4) communication and integration. However, as work continued to apply the scale to selling sites, the authors recognized a need for more items capturing the service provided by the site, or the interaction between the customer and the site. To address this need, development incorporated elements of the ServQual instrument at the expense of information items [7]. Reliability analysis confirmed that the five dimensions were acceptable: (1) tangibles (of aesthetics and navigation), (2) reliability (reliable and competent), (3) responsiveness (responsive and accessible), (4) assurance (credible and secure), and (5) empathy (communication and individualization). Later work on the scale balanced content used in previous versions to incorporate technology, information, and service/interaction dimensions [6]. Exploratory factor analysis was used in this study and found five dimensions of website quality: (1) usability, (2) design, (3) information, (4) trust, and (5) empathy. The authors grouped these dimensions into three conceptual groups based on previous categorizations. Usability included the design dimensions, information continued to stand alone, and interaction included trust and empathy dimensions.

Attitude towards a website is a construct similar to website quality, indicating a predisposition to respond favorably or unfavorably to a website. A pair of studies measuring attitude found three dimensions underlying it. In the first, exploratory factor analysis of student responses generated three underlying dimensions: (1) entertainment, (2) informativeness, and (3) organization [12]. These dimensions explained 54% of the total variance in attitude toward a website. Regression analysis showed that the three factors predicted attitude towards the website. The second study used the instrument generated in the first study and asked web developers to respond [13]. Exploratory factor analysis showed identical dimensions. Confirmatory factor analysis also supported the three dimensions underlying attitude towards a website.

Loiacono explicitly measured website quality of sites selling goods and services (books, music CDs, airline tickets, hotel reservations) and suggested that website quality is represented by 12 unique dimensions. In her study, 14 dimensions of website quality were originally proposed as a result of an extensive review of the marketing and IS literature and interviews with shoppers and website designers. Confirmatory factor analysis, however, provided evidence that the 36 items assessing website quality converged into 12 dimensions and these showed predictive validity for purchase intention. The dimensions included: (1) informational fit-to-task, (2) tailored communication, (3) ease of understanding, (4) intuitive operations, (5) response time, (6) visual appeal, (7) innovativeness, (8) emotional appeal, (9) trust, (10) online completeness, (11) relative advantage, and (12) consistent image.

## 2.2. Website quality as multiple second-order factors

In her later study, Loiacono proposed a hierarchical model of website quality containing 12 first-order factors which represent five second-order factors. She suggested that five distinct factors constitute consumer perceptions of website quality at a higher conceptual level. These higher-order dimensions identified within the WebQual<sup>TM</sup> scale included usefulness, entertainment, ease-of-use, response time, and trust. Four first-order factors (informational fit-to-task, tailored communication, online completeness, and relative advantage) formed a second-order dimension identified as usefulness. Another set of four first-order factors (visual appeal, innovativeness, emotional appeal, and consistent image) represented a secondorder factor: entertainment. Ease of understanding and intuitive operations loaded on the third second-order factor: ease-of-use. Lastly, response time and trust formed the fourth and fifth- second-order factors. Loiacono concluded that this five second-order factor model provided a good fit to the data.

A careful examination of factors identified in past studies as well as their measurement scale items, however, led us to believe that the WebQual<sup>TM</sup> scale might conform to a hierarchical model with six second-order factors. As illustrated in Table 1, most studies proposed fewer dimensions than Loiacono and although their names for website-quality dimensions differed somewhat across studies, their content was remarkably similar. It appeared to us that all but one of the first-order factors of WebQual<sup>TM</sup> constituted sub-dimensions of broader constructs. To be specific, it seemed that the scale comprised six broader dimensions: information, ease-of-use, entertainment, trust, transaction, and consistent image. Zeithaml et al. [29] also identified information, ease-of-use, entertainment, and trust as four out of five common criteria in the literature evaluating website quality. Consistent image has not been identified in prior website quality studies, but is a critical issue for retailers conducting business in multiple formats. In our study, we examined the dimensional hierarchy of the WebQual<sup>TM</sup> scale by testing both Loiacono’s five second-order factors model and our proposed six second-order factors model. The dimensions were tested only on apparel-selling websites to control for differences in consumer perceptions by type of product.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Sample

Due to the domination of online apparel purchases by females [30], and because research has shown gender differences in level of risk associated with purchasing various products online [9], females were chosen for this study. A recent Ernst & Young survey also revealed that females accounted for the majority of US online shoppers regardless of the type of product purchased.

## 3.2. Instrument

The survey instrument consisted of three sections. In the first, respondents were asked to name the online apparel retailer that they had accessed most often in the past year. In the second section, they were asked to rate that retailer on a variety of website quality attributes. The Loiacono multi-item scale was employed as a means of measuring retail apparel website quality. The authors obtained prior authorization from Loiacono to use this scale in their research. This consisted of 36 items measuring users’ perceptions of website quality. Each item was measured on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). In the third section, information on each respondent’s age, employment status, income, and marital status was obtained. Additionally, respondents were asked about (1) the number of years they had been using the Internet and (2) how many times they had used the Internet to purchase a product or service during the past 30 days.

In collecting data, a mail survey was chosen over a web survey for three reasons. First, a paper-and-pencil mail survey is relatively easier to complete, because slow modem speeds, unreliable connections, and connect-time costs may inhibit web survey completion from home. Second, it is harder to assure respondents of their privacy and confidentiality in web surveys than in mail surveys [15]. Third, we wanted to include only those who had previously purchased apparel online and were able to obtain a mailing list of those shoppers. As we were contacting our sample by mail, we could easily include a self-administered survey with the letter explaining the purpose of the study.

A mailing list of 1000 female online shoppers was purchased from a brokerage firm. This firm maintained a large database of consumers who participated in the company’s recent survey and indicated they had purchased apparel online in the past 3 years. We asked the firm to select 1000 female names randomly from its database of online apparel shoppers.

Three mailings were employed using a revised version of the Dillman Total Design Method [17]. The first mailing included a cover letter, self-administered questionnaire, and postage-paid envelope. A week after the first mailing, a postcard reminder was sent both to thank those who had returned the questionnaire and to encourage those who had not yet done so. This was followed, a week later, by a third mailing of the same questionnaire. Of the 1000 questionnaires distributed, 38 were returned due to incorrect address and eight were returned by those who either (1) refused to participate, or (2) reported that they currently did not have access to a computer. A total of 298 of the 954 questionnaires were returned yielding a 31% response rate. Of these, 25 were eliminated because they either appeared unreliable or were completed by male shoppers. The total of 273 surveys provided the data for our structural equation modeling analyses.

A total of 84 different websites were named as those where the respondents had shopped most often in the past year. J.C. Penney’s was the most frequently mentioned $( n = 2 1 )$ , followed by gap.com $( n = 1 7 )$ jcrew.com (n ¼ 16), oldnavy.com (n ¼ 15) and victoriassecret.com (n ¼ 15).

The demographic characteristics of respondents were: Respondents’ age averaged 36 years and ranged from 19 to 77; 70% of the sample were employed and 44% were married; 43% of the sample indicated an annual household income of US\$ 50,000 and over, and 23% gave their total household incomes as US\$ 75,000 or more. According to the 2000 US Census Bureau, the average age of the US female population was 38 years; 53% were married; and 54% were employed. Compared to the overall US female population, the sample here contained a higher percentage of women who were employed and a somewhat lower percentage of women who were married.

Almost 84% of respondents reported that they had purchased a product or service over the Internet at least once in the past 30 days; the average number of online purchases in the past 30 days was three. The respondents had been using the Internet for an average of 5 years.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Scale refinement

The scales of the 12 dimensions of website quality were purified using the following procedures. First, each of the 12 scales was subjected to a confirmatory factor analysis via LISREL 8 [21]. Five items with low factor loadings on their expected factors and six items with large standardized residuals were deleted. This resulted in the retention of 25 items, with two or three items per factor (see Table 2). The plot of residuals appeared normal. Internal reliability for each factor was then assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Reliabilities for all factors ranged from 0.61 to 0.92.

To test for discriminant validity among the factors, the fit of a correlated two-factor model was compared to that of a single-factor model for each possible pair of dimensions [3]. A $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference test was employed on the values obtained for the two-factor and the one-factor solutions for each pair. The results indicated that a two-factor model produced a significantly better fit than a single-factor model for all 66 possible pairs of factors, providing evidence of discrimination among all twelve factors. The internal consistency and discriminant validity of the factors were therefore supported.

## 4.2. Comparisons of alternative models

Four alternative models for the dimensionality of the WebQual<sup>TM</sup> scale were compared to each other. Model A was a totally disaggregated first-order model. In this, 25 items were proposed to measure a single first-order factor named website quality. In other words, model A hypothesized that one first-order factor accounted for all the common variance among the 25 items. This model yielded a poor fit. The goodness-of-fit (GFI), the adjusted goodness-of-fit (AGFI), and the comparative fit index (CFI) were 0.64, 0.58, and 0.68, respectively, and the standardized root mean residual (SRMR) was 0.08.

The original Loiacono model (model B) hypothesized that the 25 items formed into 12 correlated firstorder factors: informational fit-to-task, tailored communication, online completeness, and relative advantage, visual appeal, innovativeness, emotional appeal, consistent image, ease of understanding, intuitive operations, response time, and trust (see Fig. 1). In this model, each of the 12 factors was treated as a separate factor, but correlated with the other 11. The first indicator for each construct was fixed at 1 and the remaining indicators were freed. The value of $\chi ^ { 2 }$ for the model was 555 (d:f: ¼ 209, P < 0:001) and the SRMR was 0.04. The GFI and the AGFI were 0.87 and 0.79, respectively. Because sampling considerations and deviations from normality influence these indices, the CFI is often recommended as a more robust and stable measure of the overall model fit. Model C was a fully disaggregated second-order model proposed by Loiacono (see Fig. 2). A secondor higher-order factor model was defined as consisting of one, or more than one, higher-order factors modeled as causally impacting a number of first-order factors. Bagozzi [4] argue that second-order models are especially useful when the first-order factors are distinct but contain a significant shared variance. A good model fit demonstrates that a general, more global factor exists that explains all the covariation among the first-order factors [14]. In model C, we hypothesized that the 12 first-order factors loaded on one of the five correlated second-order factors: (1) usefulness, (2) entertainment, (3) ease-of-use, (4) response time, and (5) trust (see Table 5).

The value of $\chi ^ { 2 }$ associated with model C was 718 $( \mathrm { d . f . } = 2 5 3 , P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and the SRMR was 0.05. The GFI, AGFI, and CFI were 0.83, 0.78, and 0.91, respectively. The relationships between the first-order factors and the second-order factors were captured by the gamma coefficients and all were significant. Strong positive correlations existed among the five second-order factors.

A comparison of the fit statistics for models B and C showed that model B had a significantly better fit to the data. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ difference was significantly large $( \Delta \chi ^ { 2 } ( 4 4 ) = 1 6 3 )$ and, thus, model C was rejected.

## 5. Discussion

The results indicate that model B represented a valid framework for making website quality assessments. Website quality for sites selling apparel products was best conceptualized as a 12-dimensional construct rather than as a general construct (model A). One, therefore, should not assume that website quality is a single general construct and, should differentiate between its different dimensions when assessing website quality and its impact on a web user’s behavior. Assessing website quality should involve web-user perceptions of 12 different dimensions rather than a single overall measure. This approach would allow apparel retailers and their website designers to identify specific areas of website quality that need attention to improve users’ perceptions and facilitate their subsequent purchase decisions. Such information could be used by website designers to effectively allocate resources to those areas that need improvement. In addition, in-depth evaluation of competitors’ websites would provide a much clearer picture of how a company’s website compares to others.

Model D provides some interesting insight into the relationship among the six higher-level dimensions of website quality (see Table 8). The strongest correlation was between information quality and ease-of-use $( \phi _ { 1 5 } = 9 1 )$ , and indicates that shoppers’ perception of the quality of information contained in a website is transferable to their perception of how easy the website is to understand and navigate.

Unlike other website-quality studies where respondents were instructed to rate specific websites, we asked our respondents to evaluate their favorite apparel websites. In doing so, we intended to obtain information from actual frequent shoppers of the websites instead of potential shoppers who might never have visited them. This approach, however, limited the generalizability of the findings. Another limitation of this study is that only women were included in the sample and, therefore, the study results cannot be generalized to men. An additional limitation is that the respondents were asked to consider their favorite store website. While such a sampling technique ensures that respondents have adequate knowledge and experience to evaluate the website, it fails to give a representation of non-frequent shoppers.
