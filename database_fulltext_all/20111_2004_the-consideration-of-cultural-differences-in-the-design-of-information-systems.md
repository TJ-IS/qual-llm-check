# The consideration of cultural differences in the design of information systems

\* Jong-min Choe

Department of Accounting, School of Business, Kyungpook National University, Buk-ku Sankyuk-dong 1370 Daegu 702-701, South Korea

Received 14 October 2002; received in revised form 9 June 2003; accepted 12 August 2003

## Abstract

This study empirically examined cultural differences in the amount of information provided by management accounting information systems (MAIS). To demonstrate these differences, data were collected from samples of Australian and Korean firms. The results of this study showed that much more flexibility performance information is provided by Korean firms, while Australian firms provided more quality performance and traditional cost control information (TCCI). We also investigated whether the interaction effects of advanced manufacturing technology (AMT) and information on the improvement of production performance were culturally different. According to the results, we suggest that with a high level of AMT, Korean firms require more types of information (i.e. quality performance information, flexibility performance information, financial performance information, advanced cost control information, and TCCI), while Australian firms generally demand flexibility performance, quality performance, and advanced cost control information.

## 1. Introduction

Considerable information system (IS) design research has been conducted by Western researchers based on observations in Western organizations. However, the results of IS design research in Western society do not necessarily apply to other cultures. Since the general values, norms, and beliefs of managers in one country can be different from those in other countries and those values and norms affect the types of information demanded, managers’ information requirements may be expected to be culturally influenced and determined [42,51]. Therefore, different cultures require different kinds of information, process differently, and ultimately demand different design configurations of their ISs.

Although prior studies [13,15,26,49,50] have examined the cultural impact on the design and implementation of IS, most have not empirically investigated cultural effects on information characteristics (content, amount and format), that are also key design elements of IS. In this study, we empirically examined cultural differences in information characteristics of management accounting information systems (MAIS). To identify these differences, data were collected from samples of the Australian and Korean firms since the Australian culture seems to be very different from that of Korea [23].

This study also considered the levels of advanced manufacturing technology (AMT) adopted by manufacturing firms; this has a significant impact on information requirements of managers. Accordingly, we empirically analyzed whether there exist cultural differences in the interaction effects of AMT and information on production performance. Through these analyses, the types of information that are both culturally appropriate and well matched to AMT can be identified. To control the industrial and environmental effects on information characteristics of MAIS, sample firms were selected from the same industry (the automotive industry) and the same function (production) was considered.

## 2. Theoretical backgrounds

## 2.1. National culture

Culture is the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another. Hofstede identified five work-related cultural dimensions along which countries differ. His five dimensions are: individualism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, masculinity, and Confucian dynamism. Individualism (collectivism) relates to people’s self-concept: ‘‘I’’ or ‘‘we’’. Power distance is the extent to which the less powerful members of organizations within a country expect and accept that power is distributed unequally. Uncertainty avoidance refers to the degree to which the members of a society feel comfortable with uncertainty and ambiguity. Masculinity (femininity) reflects preferences for achievement and material success as opposed to emphases on relationships and the quality of life. Confucian dynamism

Thus, his results suggest that Australian culture is quite different from Korean culture. In fact, for each dimension, the scores of the two countries are on opposite sides of the mean.

## 2.2. National culture and management accounting information

MAIS collect, classify, summarize and report information to managers to assist them in their planning, control and evaluation of production activities [8]. However, planning is an ex-ante form of control [17], since it defines performance goals and expectations in terms of budget or forecasts. Evaluation represents an ex-post control in that it involves assessing the performance of individuals or groups against pre-established goals and standards. Hence, the information produced by MAIS is control information. The control information of MAIS can be grouped into two types: financial and non-financial [1,19]. A typical type of non-financial control information is non-financial performance information. The kinds of financial control information include financial performance and cost control. Types of cost control information produced by MAIS can also be classified into traditional and advanced cost control information [9].

In a country with a high collectivism and femininity culture, an individual is motivated by group interests and people emphasize the maintenance of interpersonal harmony or relationships [22]. In a collectivist society, people view their actions as an important contribution to their group’s well-being, and they gain satisfaction and feelings of accomplishment from group outcomes. Therefore, in a highly collective and feminine culture, to control employees’ activities, team- or group-oriented performance evaluations and work arrangements are more often required [4]. Teamor group-oriented performance measurements are likely to cross-departmental lines and, consequently, to be comprised mainly of non-financial performance information. High Confucian dynamism implies that people take a long-term and future-oriented perspective [7]. If managers’ time perspective is long, they generally demand and use non-financial information for control and decision making [38].

In an individualistic culture, people look primarily after their own interests, needs and preferences. In a highly masculine society, people also place more emphasis on their own success and achievement than relationships and modesty. Therefore, if individualism and masculinity are high, to control employees’ behavior, individual-oriented performance evaluation and pay are preferable to team- or group-based evaluation [30]. Low Confucian dynamism is another cause of the demand for financial information. Under low Confucian dynamism, managers’ time perspective is short. As a result, they usually prefer financial to nonfinancial information. Therefore, non-financial performance measures seem to be the high collectivism and Confucian dynamism-oriented information [28]. On the other hand, financial performance information is considered to be the high individualism and low Confucian dynamism-oriented information.

Traditional cost control information (TCCI) concentrates on managing cost by means of standards, variances and measurements established at the individual unit or level [34]. Under TCCI, the fundamental assumption is that maximizing individual performance will lead to organizational success [31].

Therefore, through TCCI, the achievement of the group cooperation and objectives cannot be supported and promoted. However, advanced cost control information (ACCI) considers interrelationships among functions or activities in controlling cost. The focus of ACCI is not an individual unit, but interrelated processes and activities. Hence, TCCI is more likely to be individualism-oriented information, while ACCI is regarded as collectivism-oriented information.

## 2.3. Information requirements under AMT

The current competitive business environment has increasingly led firms to focus on new manufacturing technologies that may be termed AMT. As a source of competitive advantage and the strategic means to cope with the hostile environment, many firms have adopted it [18]. AMT relates to the physical hardware of the manufacturing process and consists of technological advancements in automation able to be used in the production process [20].

The diversification of consumer tastes in modern society has made product life-cycles shorter, and this has led to firms producing multiple products in a smaller volume. AMT makes it possible for a firm to respond more rapidly to changing technological and market environments by introducing more new products and offering broader product lines [44]. It also can facilitate the planning and execution of products in real time; product design and the manufacturing cycle can be set in motion quickly to meet changing market demands. A firm should employ a strategy of cooperation between its operations (e.g. R&D, engineering and manufacturing) on the one hand and the external constituents relevant to product and process development (e.g. suppliers, equipment vendors and scientific community) on the other [37].

Therefore, MAIS must provide a large amount of planning information and future-oriented trend information to cope with frequent changes in the production processes and products [35,36]. To manage the integration and collaboration within and across functions, AMT requires a large amount of control and coordination information. When it is employed, the utilization of activity-based costing (ABC) and product life-cycle cost increase to control interrelated activities. ABC collects information on the basic activities of an organization as well as their costs.

This information provides the basis for understanding how changes in one area of an organization affect the activity base of others. With it, a firm can properly identify and control the complex interrelationships among functions and activities. In product life-cycle costing, integrations both across business functions and across organizational boundaries are considered [21]. Life-cycle costing provides information about the interrelationships among functions and the firm’s suppliers and customers.

It has been argued that financial performance measures lack relevance to AMT, in that they do not reflect, and are inconsistent with, the strategic factors of quality, flexibility and dependability of supply, which have now become critical to firm success [5]. Under new manufacturing systems, traditional financial performance measures have many problems or shortcomings. They are usually short-term in scope and are prone to foster suboptimal performance. For the advanced manufacturing environment, nonfinancial performance measurement systems are more appropriate [2]. AMT provides various strategic benefits such as quality improvement, economies of scope, and shortened lead and delivery times. To support and evaluate the achievement of these strategic advantages, non-financial performance infor mation is required. Under AMT, performance measurements must cross-departmental lines to assist and encourage cooperation between functional departments. The performance measurement systems required for the adoption of AMT are more team- or group-oriented. In conclusion, when AMT is adopted, non-financial performance information and ACCI are preferable to financial performance information and TCCI.

## 3. Hypotheses formulation

3.1. Impact of national culture on the amount of information

National culture as shared norms and values of people naturally influences managers’ preferences for specific information characteristics. Culturally appropriate information characteristics may be more preferred and even required. Hence, Hypotheses 1 and 2 were proposed as:

Hypothesis 1. Overall, a greater amount of nonfinancial performance information and ACCI is provided in Korean firms than in Australian firms.

Hypothesis 2. Overall, a greater amount of financial performance information and TCCI is provided in Australian firms than in Korean firms.

## 3.2. Interaction effects of AMT and information

Types of non-financial performance information and ACCI may be culturally inappropriate information for Australian firms, while culturally appropriate in Korean firms. If types of information or ISs are not culturally suitable, they are apt to be not well accepted and even not utilized [12]. Systems that conflict with the culture of an organization can foster resistance of members to a degree that results in its underutilization. Additionally, Romm et al. [43] have asserted that a mismatch between the culture of an organization or society and the cultural assumptions embedded within the IS can cause implementation failure. Hence, it is likely that in Australian firms, the provision of types of non-financial performance information and ACCI under the levels of AMT cannot contribute to the improvement of production performance to the same extent as in Korean firms. Hypotheses 3 and 4 were therefore:

Hypothesis 3. The positive interaction effects of AMT and non-financial performance information on production performance are greater in Korean firms than in Australian firms.

Hypothesis 4. The positive interaction effects of AMT and ACCI on production performance are greater in Korean firms than in Australian firms.

When AMT is employed, provision of types of financial performance information and TCCI that do not fit with it can decrease production performance. Since the kinds of financial performance information and TCCI are culturally appropriate for Australian firms, provision of types of financial performance information and TCCI can encourage and reinforce the short-term perspective and individualistic behaviors of its members. However, the adoption of AMT requires integration and collaboration among employees.

Thus, the individualistic behaviors of members encouraged under a high level of AMT are likely to have a negative impact on the production performance of Australian firms.

Provision of types of financial performance information and TCCI under the levels of AMT can also decrease production performance in Korean firms but, because they are culturally inappropriate for Korean firms, they may not be well accepted and thus underutilized. As a result, they may not promote individualistic activities of members. It seems that provision of types of financial performance information and TCCI under AMT has a greater negative effect on the production performance in Australian firms than in Korean firms, and thus we proposed the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 5. The negative interaction effects of AMT and financial performance information on production performance are greater in Australian firms than in Korean firms.

Hypothesis 6. The negative interaction effects of AMT and TCCI on production performance are greater in Australian firms than in Korean firms.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Questionnaire development and data collection

All our questionnaire items had been developed in previous research [3,47]. Thus, the instrument was first written in English and then translated into Korean by a translator familiar with both the language and research area. The Korean questionnaire was then translated back into English by another bilingual translator. Comparison of the original English version and the back translation revealed no significant differences.

Data were collected by this survey questionnaire administered to chief production or plant managers in Korean and Australian automotive manufacturing firms. Thus, the same industry and function were chosen to control for industrial and environmental effects. Only chief production managers or plant managers were selected as respondents, since they understand both the utilization of management accounting information and the firm’s AMT level and performance.

The number of firms in the Korean automotive industry was about twice that in the Australian automotive industry. Thus, 385 and 186 organizations were selected from Korea and Australia, respectively. An initial letter was sent to the chief production manager of each firm explaining the nature and purpose of the research. About 1 week later, a questionnaire with a cover letter was mailed to each. A self-addressed stamped envelope was included with the questionnaire to ensure anonymous responses. One or 2 weeks later, a reminder letter was also sent to non-respondents.

For the Korean and Australian firms, 79 and 49 responses were returned, respectively. However, five Korean and two Australian responses were excluded from the study, either because of incomplete data or because the respondent did not meet both the country of birth requirement and the post conditions (i.e. chief production manager or similar). Finally, 74 (Korea) and 47 (Australia) usable data were collected yielding a response rate of 19% (Korea) and 25% (Australia), respectively. All respondents were born locally (i.e. in their working country) and on average had worked for their firm for 11 (Korean) and 13 years (Australian). The combined sample of 121 responses represents 21% of the total sample of 571 selected firms.

Testing for non-response bias is important and often involves additional data collection. Since such data are not easily obtainable, bias is often approximated by comparing the answer patterns of early respondents with those of late respondents [52]. The final Korean and Australian subsample were partitioned into two groups of early and late responses. The non-response bias was then examined through t-tests. In each subsample, the results showed no significant differences between the two groups regarding number of employees (Korea: t ¼ 0:87, P > 0:3; Australia: t ¼ 0:03, P > 0:9), organization age (Korea: t ¼ 0:64, P > 0:5; Australia: t ¼ 1:38, P > 0:18), respondent’s working period (Korea: t ¼ 0:35, P > 0:7; Australia: t ¼ 0:25, P > 0:8) and sales volume (Korea: t ¼ 0:38, P > 0:7). ## 4.2. Measurements

## 4.2.1. National culture

Based on the Hofstede’s results, nation can be used as a proxy for culture in hypotheses testing.

## 4.2.2. AMT level

Generally, the level of AMT was measured with a 10-item scale assessing the extent to which a firm has implemented and integrated computer technologies for manufacturing [24]. This measure operationally defines the general domain of advanced manufacturing technologies, without regard to their prevalence across industries. The items are: MRPII, CAD, numerical control (NC), computer NC, direct NC, FMS, robotics, automated materials handling, computeraided test and inspection and computer-aided process planning. The level of AMT was measured on a sevenpoint Likert-type scale.

## 4.2.3. Management accounting information

Thirteen non-financial performance pieces of information suggested by previous research were utilized to measure non-financial performance information. They were: product defects, quality improvement, rate of introduction of new products, evaluation of the ability to vary product characteristics, number of product returns, set-up and changeover times, rate of material scrap loss, length of cycle time from order to delivery, on time delivery performance records, machine utilization and down time, number of customer complaints, customer satisfaction with product, and degree of cooperation of production employees with other departments. Respondents were asked to indicate on a seven-point Likert-type scale, anchored by ‘no amount of information, none supplied’ and ‘very large amount of information, very high extent of provision’, that is provided to the production department.

To measure types of financial performance information, six questionnaire items developed by prior studies [10] were used. They included inventory turnover ratio, return on investment, divisional revenue or cost, contribution margin or profit of each product, return on sales and assets, and budget variance analysis. Types of financial performance information were measured on a seven-point Likert-type scale.

Typical traditional management accounting practices include standard, variable and full costing [45]. Three questionnaire items were used to measure TCCI. It was measured on a seven-point Likert-type scale. Cost of quality, activity-based costing and product life-cycle costing are contemporary management accounting techniques [25]. Three questionnaire items were used to measure ACCI. Respondents were asked to indicate, on a seven-point Likert-type scale, the amount of information produced by these newer techniques.

## 4.2.4. Production performance

The strength of the manufacturing function is determined by the degree to which it meets the tasks placed on it by a firm’s strategic goals or plans. The ultimate goals that can be attained through AMT are low cost, improved quality, increased flexibility, and high dependability of supply [6]. These four variables compose the core elements of production performance in AMT. The importance of production performance measurements is determined by the degree to which they support the attainment of strategic manufacturing goals [55]. Therefore, the measurements of manufacturing performance under AMT should reflect the degree of realization of these four strategic goals.

Since a firm’s performance may be positively or adversely affected by various internal and external factors aside from AMT, it is difficult to isolate and measure the true impact of AMT on performance. Therefore, to evaluate a firm’s production performance, this study measured the degree of improvement in cost, quality, dependability, and flexibility through AMT. According to the measures of Vickery et al. [54] and Agarwal [3] improvements in the four dimensions were assessed by asking respondents to indicate the extent of improvement experienced since introducing AMT.

The degree of improvement was measured on a seven-point Likert-type scale that ranged from ‘not improved, worse’ to ‘highly improved’. Sixteen question items were utilized to measure cost reduction, quality improvement, increased flexibility, and dependability of supply. The 16 items were: new product, speed in new products, product changeover and R&D (four items for flexibility), lead time, delivery, production lead time and customer requirements (four items for dependability of supply), product performance, design and engineering, product features and perception of quality (four question items for quality), production cost, material cost, labor cost, and overhead cost (four items for cost).

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Reliability and validity test

The questionnaire items measuring research variables had been used in previous empirical studies. However, the construct validities of these items were questionable. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was used to determine if all items measuring a construct cluster together or not. That is, whether all items measuring a construct loaded onto a single factor or divided into multiple factors. Four separate joint factor analyses for AMT, non-financial performance information, financial performance information, TCCI, ACCI, and production performance were carried out to acquire a more stable solution by increasing the ratio of the sample size to the number of items.

Using the 0.4 criterion for significant item loading on a factor, the results show that all items within each index, except for non-financial performance information and production performance, were represented by a single factor.<sup>1</sup> In the case of nonfinancial performance information, two factors with eigenvalues greater than one were extracted. However, in factor 2, item 12 (customer satisfaction with product) was confounded with the item of factor 1. Item 12 was removed and the factor analysis for nonfinancial performance information was repeated. In this second factor analysis, the items of each factor were not confounded with the items in the other factors. Factor 1 comprises product defects, quality improvement, product returns, material scrap, on time delivery, machine utilization, and customer complaints. Thus, its title was quality performance information.<sup>2</sup> Factor 2, which is composed of new products, product characteristics, set-up and changeover times, length of cycle time, and degree of cooperation, represents flexibility performance information.<sup>3</sup>

For production performance, three factors with eigenvalues greater than one were extracted. In factor 2, item 3 (product changeover) was confounded with the item of factor 1. Thus, item 3 was removed. In the second factor analysis, no item was confounded. Factor 1 (lead time, delivery, production lead time, customer requirements, product performance, and perception of quality) was quality and dependability of supply.<sup>4</sup> Factor 2 (production cost, material cost, labor cost and overhead cost) represented cost reduction.<sup>5</sup> Factor 3 (new product, speed in new product, R&D, design and engineering, and product features) showed increased flexibility.<sup>6</sup>

A single scale for the research variable was created by averaging a respondent’s scores over the items measuring each variable. ## 5.2. Cultural differences in the amount of information

To analyze the effect of national culture on the amount of information provided, t-test and MANOVA were employed. Since AMT, organization size and age can influence the amount of information produced [14,40,48]; therefore, to eliminate the impact of these three covariates, MANOVA was performed. The results are presented in Table 3. In terms of quality performance information, flexibility performance information and TCCI, the effects of national culture were significant. No significant cultural impact was found in financial performance information and ACCI. However, in the case of quality performance information, the mean and t-value in Table 3 show that a greater amount of information was provided in Australian firms than in Korean firms. This result is contrary to our expectation; the amount of quality performance information produced in Korean firms was significantly greater than in Australian firms.

Although activities for the improvement of product quality are the strong group- or team-oriented management processes, quality improvement is a key determinant in the growth and survival of a firm [41]. Thus, it seems that significantly more quality performance information is intentionally provided in Australian firms to reinforce the cooperation and interaction among functions for the realization of continuous quality improvement. This inverse cultural impact on the use of performance measures was found in the study of Awasthi et al. They empirically suggested that US subjects (high individualism) seem to select more team-based performance measures under a high level of task interdependence to complement their cultural misfit.

## 6. Conclusion

It has been argued that national culture has an impact on the design of an IS. This study empirically investigated a cultural impact on information characteristics of MAIS. The results of our research are presented in Table 9.

Our results showed that much more flexibility performance information is provided in Korean firms, while in Australian firms the greater amount of quality performance information and TCCI is produced. Much more quality performance information in Australian firms seems to be the result of an inverse cultural impact that supplements a cultural misfit (i.e. high individualism). The greater amount of TCCI in Australian firms seems to be related to the high individualism culture of Australians. For the no cultural differences in the amount of types of financial performance information and ACCI, two possible explanations may be suggested. One is the convergence effect of globalization and the other is the compensatory effects of cultural dimensions. Recent globalization and intense competition are apt to make all firms employ the identical best or universal management practices [39,53]. Because of this, organizations world-wide are becoming more similar in some management systems or techniques utilized. The compensatory effects of cultural dimensions imply that the positive impact of some cultural variables can be neutralized by the negative effects of other cultural dimensions [27].

We also investigated whether the interaction effects of AMT and information on the improvement of production performance are culturally different. In cases of non-financial performance information and ACCI, the positive interaction effects of AMT and information under high levels of AMT are greater in Korean firms. Moreover, in terms of financial perfor mance information and TCCI, when the level of AMT was high, the interaction impact of AMT and information was positive in Korean firms. In Australian firms, under high levels of AMT the interaction effects of AMT and information (i.e. flexibility performance information and ACCI) were positive, while the interaction impact of AMT and financial performance information or TCCI was negative.

In a society with a strong uncertainty avoidance culture, people’s intolerance for ambiguity and the demand for precision and formalization are very high. Ambiguity is defined as uncertainty of meaning [29]. Individuals intolerant of ambiguity perceive ambiguous situations as a source of threat; thus, they attempt to reduce the threats by manifesting a preference for readily interpretable information (i.e. financial information). They tend to judge financial information to be more important than conceptual or non-financial information [16]. Therefore, in a high uncertainty avoidance society, a large amount of financial information such as financial performance information and TCCI is required. When the level of AMT is high, because of the frequent changes of production processes and products and the new level of technology in AMT, the degree of uncertainty is also high. Organizations that face uncertainty must seek and acquire more information to cope with uncertain circumstances. In Korean firms, the positive impact of financial performance information and TCCI on the improvement of performance under a high level of AMT is likely to be caused by both the high degree of uncertainty in AMT and the strong uncertainty avoidance of Korean culture in general.

Therefore, we suggest that in Korean firms, when the level of AMT is high, MAIS must provide a large amount of types of information (i.e. financial and nonfinancial performance information, ACCI and TCCI), while Australian firms generally require non-financial performance information and ACCI under a high level of AMT. The large amount of information demanded in Korean firms may be related to both the high degree of uncertainty caused by AMT adoption and the strong uncertainty avoidance of Korean culture. The greater amount of non-financial performance information and ACCI in Australian firms seems to reflect the information requirements of AMT.

It is evident that firms in different countries tend to demand some different types of information for the improvement of organizational performance. Recently, because of globalization, many firms in the world have become alike or similar in some management practices and techniques. Globalization is a convergence process through which cross-cultural and national differences are reduced. However, it is still important and necessary to identify what particular characteristics of IS are nation-specific or culturally different in order for a firm to develop a culturally appropriate IS.

Jong-min Choe is a professor in the Accounting Department at the Kyungpook National University in South Korea. He has received his Masters degree and PhD in management science and accounting information systems from the Korean Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). His current research interests include the design of accounting information systems (AIS), EDP auditing, the effect of national culture on the design

of AIS and strategic applications of information systems, etc. He has published his paper in the leading journals of Korea: Information Processing and Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, Information and Management, European Journal of Information Systems and International Journal of Management Science, etc.
