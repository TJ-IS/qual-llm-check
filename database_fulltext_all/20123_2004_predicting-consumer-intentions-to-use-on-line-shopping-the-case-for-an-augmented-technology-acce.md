# Predicting consumer intentions to use on-line shopping: the case for an augmented technology acceptance model

Leo R. Vijayasarathy \*

College of Business, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, CO 80523-1277, USA

Received 18 July 2002; received in revised form 16 January 2003; accepted 12 August 2003

Available online 30 October 2003

## Abstract

Derived from the theory of reasoned action, the technology acceptance model (TAM) focuses on two specific salient beliefs— ease of use and usefulness. It has been applied in the study of user adoption of different technologies, and has emerged as a reliable and robust model. However, this has not discouraged researchers from incorporating additional constructs to the original model in their quest for increased predictive power. Here, an attempt is made in the context of explaining consumer intention to use on-line shopping. Besides ease of use and usefulness, compatibility, privacy, security, normative beliefs, and self- efficacy are included in an augmented TAM. A test of this model, with data collected from 281 consumers, show support for seven of nine research hypotheses. Specifically, compatibility, usefulness, ease of use, and security were found to be significant predictors of attitude towards on-line shopping, but privacy was not. Further, intention to use on-line shopping was strongly influenced by attitude toward on-line shopping, normative beliefs, and self-efficacy.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid demise of many Internet retailers in the past 2 years has undoubtedly quelled the overly optimistic expectations for on-line business to consumer (B2C) commerce. According to the United States Department of Commerce, for the year 2001, total retail sales was US\$ 3.50 trillion and e-commerce retail sales was US\$ 32.57 billion. These figures are a stark reminder that B2C commerce is still very much in its infancy and that there will be no overnight revolution in retailing. Further, it is quite evident that traditional retail channels are in no danger of becoming obsolete or being replaced by electronic commerce. However, the potential for this new retail medium to complement, supplement, and even replace other media has not diminished.

The relative longevity and continued growth enjoyed by Amazon, eBay, and Travelocity offer evidence (albeit anecdotal) of viable on-line business models. Electronic retailing can overcome time and spatial barriers, provide greater customer reach, enhance the customer experience by offering a wealth of product information, expert advice, and customized service, facilitate faster order processing, and enable quicker delivery of digital products. But there are also many challenges, especially in the areas of store/interface design, order fulfillment, payment methods, and the safeguarding of customer information. A related and arguably a more pressing problem for on-line re tailers is identifying, attracting, and keeping customers.

Recent research is providing more insights into who adopts electronic shopping and why (e.g. [35]). In this effort, intention-based theories including the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [27], the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [3,4], and the technology acceptance model (TAM) can be valuable tools. This model, which has been used to explain IT adoption in a variety of contexts, is particularly attractive because of its parsimony and consistently good predictive record. However, for the adoption and use of on-line shopping, a number of other relevant factors, besides ease of use and perceived usefulness have been suggested. Some of these factors include salient beliefs about compatibility, privacy, and security, normative beliefs, and self efficacy.

The sparse set of variables in TAM may be adequate to explain the adoption of technologies in the workplace, where the use of such technologies may, in most cases, be unavoidable, if not mandatory. In such settings, the perceived usefulness of the technology and its ease of use may be the dominant predictors of its use. On the other hand, in decisions involving the choice of a particular shopping medium, a consumer, who typically enjoys more discretion and more alternatives (than in a work-setting), may be guided by a host of salient criteria other than whether the medium is useful and easy to use. By incorporating and testing the relevance of some of these other beliefs in the context of using the Internet for shopping, a richer understanding of why consumers accept or reject this electronic medium for purposes of commerce can be gained.

Most empirical studies on Internet shopping have used student subjects or have collected data from the pool of Internet users. This sampling strategy tends to ignore consumers who are non-Internet users (but who could be potential adopters in the future), or do not have fast and reliable Internet connections (as university students typically do), or whose beliefs about the perceived consequences of on-line shopping (i.e. its usefulness, compatibility, etc.) may be just as relevant as those of Internet users for explaining the variance in intentions to use on-line shopping.

Insights gained from the applicability of an enhanced TAM that is tested with data collected from the general population of consumers can better inform practitioners about the issues that are relevant to consumers in their decision to use the Internet for shopping, and also contribute to the cumulative knowledge about IT adoption. Therefore, the objectives of this study are to present a theoretically justified research model that extends TAM, and to empirically test it using data collected from a sample of consumers chosen from a population that includes both users and non-users of the Internet.

## 2. Literature review

Introduced by Davis [20], TAM seeks to explain and predict user acceptance of IT in the workplace (Fig. 1). It is based on TRA [5], and contends that behavioral intention to use an information system is contingent on two salient beliefs, namely perceived usefulness and ease of use. Perceived usefulness is defined as the ‘‘prospective user’s subjective probability that using a specific application system will increase his or her job performance within an organizational context’’ and ease of use ‘‘refers to the degree to which the prospective user expects the system to be free of effort’’ [21].

Fig. 1. Technology acceptance model (adapted from Davis et al. [21]).

These two beliefs are expected to influence an individual’s attitude towards using the system, which in turn, together with perceived usefulness are posited to explain the variance in intention to use the system. TAM also incorporates a causal relationship between ease of use and perceived usefulness, suggesting that an individual’s perception of how easy or difficult it is to use a system will influence his/her perceptions about the usefulness of the system.

Using data collected from student subjects, Davis et al. tested and found support for their TAM model. A key finding of their study was the predictive power of perceived usefulness that explained more than half of the variance in intention to use the system. Since this seminal study, a number of other researchers have employed TAM or variations thereof, to study user acceptance of a variety of information technologies including e-mail, word processors, spreadsheets, graphical software, and voice mail (more than 400 journal articles have cited the two original TAM articles by Davis and his co-authors [52]). Studies that have incorporated TAM have consistently found support for the relationship between perceived usefulness and attitude and/or behavioral intention [33,39,43,51]. However, the results for the linkage between ease of use and attitude and/or intention have been mixed but not surprising. The early TAM studies had also reported only a small effect for the ease of use construct and found that its effect on intention diminished over time.

Recently, TAM has also been applied to study user acceptance of the Internet. For example, Lederer et al. [34] collected data through an e-mail survey from 163 respondents who used the Web for work-related tasks. Their analyses showed support for TAM when usage, as measured by the frequency of use on a seven point scale, was regressed against usefulness and ease of use. However, the percentage of variance explained, as indicated by the ${ \bf \dot { \boldsymbol { R } } } ^ { 2 }$ , was fairly low at 15%. Better support for TAM, with 64% of the variance in intention being explained by ease of use and usefulness, is reported in another study [37]. The authors collected their data from 145 undergraduate students, and concluded from their analyses that usefulness had a stronger influence on intention than ease of use.

In an attempt to explain the varying effects of ease of use, Gefen and Straub [28] theorized that ease of use would be significant in explaining intentions only when the behavior of interest was directly impacted by the intrinsic characteristics of the IT used. They made a distinction between inquiring for a product on the web as opposed to buying it, arguing that the former would be more directly related to ease of use because seeking information about a product relied on the use of IT both as a means and an end, while purchasing it involved the use of IT only as a means. From an analysis of data collected from 217 MBA students about their use of an Internet book-seller’s site, the study offered support for the hypotheses that ease of use was significantly related to intentions to use the site for inquiries but not for purchases.

In addition to TAM, researchers have adapted TPB and the diffusion of innovation literature [44] to examine user acceptance of the Internet. For example, Chen et al. [16] tested a modified TAM model that included compatibility and made a case for its inclusion for explaining consumer attitudes towards shopping at virtual stores. Agarwal and Prasad [1] tested and found support for their hypothesis that the innovation characteristics that influence initial use of the World Wide Web (WWW) differ from those characteristics that impact intentions to continue using it. Tan and Teo [47] also used innovation characteristics but in the context of explaining user adoption of Internet Banking. Their study, which also included constructs from TPB, showed that perceptions about the innovation and perceived behavioral control were significant predictors of intentions to adopt on-line banking services, but subjective norm was not. These results are contradictory to those reported by Bhattacherjee [14], who found that subjective norm did have a significant effect on intention to use electronic brokerage services, while perceived behavioral control did not.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 2 displays the research model; it indicates that, in addition to the two salient beliefs that comprise the core of TAM, three other beliefs should be relevant to the behavior of interest, namely shopping using the Internet. Further, normative beliefs, a key construct of TRA, and self-efficacy, which is an important component of TPB, are incorporated to provide a more comprehensive set of factors that could play significant roles in predicting an individual’s intention to engage in Internet shopping.

## 3.1. Research variables

## 3.1.1. Usefulness

In the context of on-line shopping, we define ‘usefulness as the extent to which a consumer believes that on-line shopping will provide access to useful information, facilitate comparison shopping, and enable quicker shopping’.

## 3.1.2. Ease of use

We define ‘ease of use as the extent to which a consumer believes that on-line shopping is free of effort’.

## 3.1.3. Compatibility

Among the perceived characteristics that have been suggested in the innovations diffusion literature as being pertinent to adoption decisions, relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility have been found to be especially relevant with respect to technology innovations [29,40]. Both relative advantage, which refers to the incremental benefits of the innovation in comparison to its existing substitutes, and complexity, which is a measure of how difficult it would be learn to use the innovation, already have comparable representations in TAM through the constructs of usefulness and ease of use. Since shopping using the Internet could be considered an innovation on account of its newness relative to other modes of shopping, we included compatibility, which is an indicator of how well an innovation fits a potential adopter’s values and needs. For our study, ‘compatibility is defined as the extent to which a consumer believes that shopping online fits/matches his/her lifestyle, needs, and shopping preference’.

## 3.1.4. Privacy

In this age of direct marketing, a majority of consumers, dubbed ‘‘privacy pragmatists,’’ have been found to be willing to divulge some personal information based on an assessment of risks and benefits [23]. However, the ease with which consumers’ personal information and their browsing and shopping habits can be captured on-line, and the potential opportunity for misusing this information may elevate the degree of privacy risks to unacceptable levels. Therefore, we included it as another salient belief that is relevant to the behavior of interest, and define it as ‘the extent to which a consumer believes that shopping on-line will not compromise his/her privacy’.

## 3.1.5. Security

Although there is a close relationship between security and privacy, they are still distinct constructs. While privacy is related to what a company consciously decides to do with consumer data, security is concerned with any inadvertent compromises of consumer data to a third party (e.g. hacker and identity thief). A consumer’s belief about security afforded on the Internet is considered salient, since his/her comfort level with transmitting credit-card information is crucial for the consummation of an on-line retail transaction. For this study, security is defined as ‘the extent to which a consumer believes that making payments on-line is secure’.

## 3.1.6. Normative beliefs

According to TRA, both salient beliefs held by individuals and their normative beliefs, i.e. their beliefs about whether or not important referent groups or individuals would approve of the behavior under study, are key predictors of intent. A similar emphasis on the influence of significant others in shaping an individual’s intentions to engage in a behavior has been postulated by TPB. While TAM ignores the role of normative beliefs, their relevance has been shown in the context of technology acceptance [49], retail store patronage [25], and on-line shopping [36]. In this study, normative beliefs is defined as ‘the extent to which a consumer believes that people who are important to him/her would recommend that the consumer engage in on-line shopping’.

## 3.1.7. Self efficacy

Social cognitive theory [9–11] contends that a person’s situational or environment characteristics, personality, cognitive, and demographic traits, and behavior reciprocally influence one another. Among the many dimensions comprising this theory, of particular relevance here is the relationship posited between cognitive factors and an individual’s behavior. The theory models the likelihood of an individual engaging in a particular behavior as a function of the individual’s expectations about the consequences of performing the behavior, and the individual’s beliefs about his/her capabilities to undertake the behavior. The latter belief is termed self-efficacy and has been extended to the context of technology use by Compeau and Higgins who define it as ‘‘an individual judgment of one’s capability to use a computer’’ [18]. While the tools and technology for accessing the Internet may be ubiquitous, the task of shopping in an electronic medium is far from intuitive and hassle-free [31,32,38], therefore, we included on-line shopping self-efficacy, defined as ‘a consumer’s self-assessment of his/her capabilities to shop on-line’, in our research model.

## 3.1.8. Attitude

Attitude has been characterized as a person’s inclination to exhibit a certain response towards a concept or object [22]. It has been suggested that attitude can be split into its cognitive and affective components [26]. The former is modeled as salient beliefs in TRA, and the latter is deemed to comprise attitude. We define it as ‘the extent to which a consumer likes on-line shopping and considers it to be a good idea’.

## 3.1.9. Intention

While an objective measurement of the extent to which an individual engages in a behavior would be ideal, it is not always easy or practical to obtain it. Luckily, both theoretical and empirical support exists for the strong correlation between intention to engage in a behavior and the actual behavior. Here, we have chosen to use behavioral intention as a surrogate for actual behavior, and define it as ‘a consumer’s intent to use on-line shopping’.

## 3.2. Research hypotheses

Empirical support for the relationship between usefulness and attitude has been provided by a number of studies (e.g. [2]). The persistence of this relationship has also been demonstrated in the context of using the Internet, i.e. individuals who believed that using this technology could lead to positive outcomes also tended to have a more favorable attitude towards it. Therefore, we extend TAM to the specific behavior of engaging in on-line shopping, and propose the following hypothesis:

H1. There is a positive association between consumers’ attitude towards on-line shopping and their beliefs about its usefulness.

Ease of use has been found to be less consistent in its relationship to attitude. Even the original TAM studies report that it appeared to be relevant when an individual first starts to use a system, but over a period of time its influence became less significant. Nevertheless, ease of use continues to be an integral part of TAM, and we chose to retain it. Moreover, since our sample was drawn from the population of consumers and not just Internet users, ease of use may be relevant to some of the respondents with little experience of Internet technologies and/or using it for shopping. Therefore,

H2. There is a positive association between consumers’ attitude towards on-line shopping and their beliefs about its ease of use.

A key element of Innovation Diffusion Theory is the notion of fit between an innovation and a potential adopter’s lifestyle and needs. Bellman et al. [12] found that individuals who spend a considerable amount of time using the Internet and related technologies such as e-mail in their job and personal life, would be inclined to use on-line shopping. Further support for the inclusion of compatibility in models that seek to explain the acceptance and use of technology is offered in other studies. Therefore, we offer the following hypothesis:

H3. There is a positive association between consumers’ attitude towards on-line shopping and their beliefs about its compatibility.

As an innovation that is rapidly evolving, shopping using the Internet carries certain risks; here, we consider those associated with privacy and security. Shopping on-line may expose consumers to privacy risks that are similar to those associated with other forms of direct marketing. When using them, the consumer has to relinquish some personal information (e.g. home address, dress size, etc.), than at an instore shop. But in the case of Internet shopping, perceptions of privacy risks may be higher as the digital media allows for surreptitious gathering of information about a customer and his or her browsing habits. We expect that a consumer who has a more positive perception about the level of privacy offered on the Internet will have a more favorable attitude towards using this medium for shopping [45] and therefore propose:

H4. There is a positive association between consumers’ attitude towards on-line shopping and their beliefs about the privacy afforded by on-line shopping.

Conceived as a medium for information exchange, the Internet promotes an open architecture that is both a boon and a bane for commercial transactions. While openness has offered a global network that is easily accessible, it also makes the Internet vulnerable to security breaches. Consumers’ perceptions about the extent of security risks, especially as it relates to the use of credit cards for making on-line purchases, can be expected to influence their attitudes towards Internet shopping [13].

H5. There is a positive association between consumers’ attitude towards on-line shopping and their beliefs about the security of on-line shopping.

TAM incorporates both an indirect link (through the mediating variable of attitude) as well as a direct link between usefulness and intention. The direct link is based on theoretical and empirical support that, irrespective of any positive or negative affect towards a particular behavior, an individual’s cognitive assessment of the outcome of engaging in the behavior (i.e. usefulness) can have a direct bearing on his/her behavioral intention [7,15,50].

H6. There is a positive association between consumers’ intention to use on-line shopping and their beliefs about its usefulness.

Unlike TAM, TRA theorizes that behavioral intentions are influenced by both salient and normative beliefs. The role played by an individual’s beliefs about what others think he/she should do is also emphasized by TPB. Researchers who have incorporated normative beliefs in their models have found support for its link with behavioral intention [30]. Further, in the context of consumer intention to use on-line shopping, studies have shown that the influence of other people is significant.

In an innovator-imitator framework, individuals might attempt to ‘‘fit-in’’ with perceived opinions of important other people who may be early adopters of an innovation. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H7. There is a positive association between consumers’ intention to use on-line shopping and their normative beliefs about it.

The explanatory power of self-efficacy in models intended to predict technology adoption has been demonstrated [19]. Recognizing that the computer could be a complex and intimidating technology, especially for novice users, Compeau and Higgins [17] suggested the inclusion of self-efficacy. Studies have shown that there is a positive association between it and Internet use [24,41,42].

While the WWW and graphical browsers have made the Internet much easier to access and use, for a novice, making a connection to the Internet, navigating it, and performing an intended task (i.e. finding relevant information, participating in an on-line chat, etc.) could be daunting. This is especially true for on-line shopping, where store-fronts tend to differ from vendor to vendor and the tasks of finding the product/service and/or retailer(s) selling it, performing product/price comparisons, placing an order, and making payments could be baffling. Therefore, we suggest that a consumer’s confidence in his/her capabilities to conduct on-line shopping is relevant, and offer the following hypothesis:

H8. There is a positive association between consumers’ intention to use on-line shopping and their self-efficacy about using it.

Most intention-based theories model attitude as a mediator between beliefs and intentions. Individuals salient beliefs about the outcomes are expected to influence their attitude towards the behavior (Hypotheses 1–5), which in turn is expected to impact their intention to perform that behavior. There is ample evidence, both theoretical and empirical, for the link between attitude and intention [8,46]. Thus,

H9. There is a positive association between consumers’ intention to use on-line shopping and their attitude towards it.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data collection procedures

Data for this study were collected as part of a larger mail survey of consumer perceptions about Internet shopping. Participants were adults residing in an upper mid-western US city with a population of about 80,000. A database of residential properties from the city’s Assessment department was used to select the target subjects. Of the 19,600 residential properties (single family, duplexes, and condominiums) listed in the database, a proportional random sampling was performed to identify 3000 potential respondents. First, using the assessed values of the homes as surrogates for actual household incomes, the 19,600 properties were stratified into eight categories. Then, to arrive at the target sample of 3000 respondents, a proportional number of households were randomly selected from each of the eight stratified levels. These proportions approximately reflected the percentages of US households by income levels published by the US Census Bureau (www.census.gov/prod/99pubs/p60-206.pdf). Further, the 3000 people selected were evenly split between males and females. Finally, of these, only 800 were targeted for collecting the data for this study.

A four-page questionnaire was used as the research instrument. It was first pre-tested in an iterative manner among a convenience sample of colleagues, students, and other consumers drawn from the general public. The 52 respondents of this test were asked to provide comments on the relevance and wording of the questionnaire items, length of the survey, and time taken to complete it. Based on the feedback, some of the questionnaire items were dropped. Further, the questionnaire layout was modified, and the wording of some of the questions was changed to improve clarity.

Multiple scale items, that were primarily adapted from Taylor and Todd [48], were used to elicit respondents’ salient beliefs, normative beliefs, self-efficacy, and attitude and intention towards Internet shopping. The instrument also included measures for assessing the level of Internet usage and other relevant demographic indicators. A complete listing of the items is provided in Appendix A.

As a pilot test to gauge response rate, an initial mailing was sent to 100 of the chosen sample of 800 respondents. The survey package consisted of a cover letter, questionnaire, and pre-paid return envelope. A drawing to win one of 10 US\$ 25 gift certificates, redeemable at any area restaurant or retail store, was offered as an incentive to respond. As a reminder, a follow-up post-card was sent to all of the respondents

a week after the initial mailing. Thirty completed questionnaires were returned for a response rate of 30%.

Following the pilot test, the remaining 700 subjects were sent the questionnaire package. The initial mailing was followed-up with a post-card encouraging participation in the study. A total of 260 responses were received for a response rate of 37.1%. Of these, nine questionnaires were returned blank or incomplete. The predominant reasons cited for these incomplete questionnaires were the lack of a personal computer at home and/or an Internet connection, and insufficient knowledge or experience using the Internet for shopping purposes. Combining the remaining 251 questionnaires with the 30 from the pilot study yielded a total of 281 usable cases for an overall response rate of 35.1%.

To test for possible non-response bias, a t-test was conducted using the assessed value of the respondents homes. The result showed no significant difference between the respondents (mean ¼ US\$ 157,485) and non-respondents (mean ¼ US\$ 167,680). Only 184 of the 281 valid responses included identification information, such as the name of the respondent, to allow us to match them with their respective properties in the mailing database. Therefore, the means for the two comparison groups (respondents and non-respondents) and consequently the t-test result may not be accurate. Subject to this unavoidable limitation, the non-response bias analysis indicates that the respondents’ appraised home values are not significantly different than those of the non-respondents. Further, the difference in response rates between females (38.8%) and males (30.8%) was small, ensuring that the data collected were representative of both genders.

## 4.3. Data analyses and results

Each of the variables shown in Fig. 2 was measured with multiple items derived from prior literature and modified to fit the context of on-line shopping. Those used to capture salient beliefs, self efficacy, attitude, and intention were measured on a 7-point Likert scale anchored by 1 (strongly disagree) and 7 (strongly agree). To ascertain normative beliefs, respondents were asked to identify three people (spouse, parent, sibling, or friend), who were important to them and whose opinions they valued. Then, they were asked to rate the likelihood that each of these individuals would recommend shopping using the Internet. A 7-point scale anchored by 1 (very unlikely) and 7 (very likely) was used.

In order to assess convergent and discriminant validities, the 19 items used to measure 8 of the 9 research variables were subjected to principal components analyses with varimax rotation. The Kaiser– Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.885, confirming the appropriateness of proceeding with the analyses. In the first round, two of the items, Useful 1 and Ease 1, were found to have strong secondary loadings (i.e. greater than 0.40 on more than one factor). Therefore, these items were dropped and the analysis was performed again. The results, which identified eight factors and cumulatively explained 80.2% of the variance, are shown in Table 2.

## 4.4. Discussion of results and guidelines for practice

The results show support for the contention that in addition to usefulness and ease of use, there are other relevant factors that explain intentions to use B2C online commerce. Among salient beliefs, consumers perception about the compatibility of on-line shopping to their lifestyle appears to be a strong predictor of attitude. Therefore, consumers whose lifestyle and shopping habits and preferences are better served by non-store alternatives can be expected to embrace on-line shopping more readily. They may be ‘‘time starved’’ and constantly exploring ways to reduce the time to complete various tasks to manage their busy schedules. Consumers who like to shop at home and/or have an aversion for in-store shopping may also find on-line shopping to be congruent with their needs and preferences. On-line retailers may be best served by tapping into these two market segments—time starved consumers and in-home shoppers.

In addition to compatibility, the emergence of ease of use as a significant variable is a reminder that although the technologies for accessing and using the Internet may be standardized and relatively easy to use, the task of shopping on-line may not be as intuitive or free of effort. Poorly designed interfaces, cluttered page layouts, inadequate and ineffective search engines, dead links, outdated information, tedious navigational structures, and complex checkout procedures may all contribute to consumer frustration with on-line shopping. Therefore, in order to attract and keep customers, it is imperative that electronic retailers do not ignore good design principles in the construction of their cyber stores.

It appears that usefulness plays a secondary role to compatibility in the extended model. Nevertheless, the perceived usefulness of the electronic medium to acquire product information and perform comparison shopping is positively related to attitudes towards online shopping. For on-line retailers, the implications are clear. In order to attract customers they have to focus on creating value and/or differentiating their offering from other retail media. Perception of value derived from on-line shopping is important and on-line retailers should leverage their capabilities to create and deliver individualized interactive experiences for customers.

One of the major inhibitors of on-line shopping has been the perception of poor security associated with payment methods; it has been reported that on-line shoppers are still wary about transmitting credit card information over the Internet. The significant positive association between beliefs about the extent of security available for on-line transactions and attitudes shows support for this assertion. Innovations in payment technologies and processes have improved the security and ease of making payments on-line. However, incidents where on-line merchants have suffered security breaches or inadvertently exposed customers’ credit card information can seriously undermine consumer confidence [6]. Use of an open, public network makes the task of ensuring absolute security difficult and maybe even impossible. Unless on-line merchants can convince prospective customers about the existence of high security standards and/or safeguards, it may be difficult to change their attitudes toward online shopping.

Many on-line retailers have also focused on reducing consumer concerns about privacy by adopting and sharing explicit privacy policies and statements. But respondents’ beliefs about the extent of privacy offered by on-line retailers do not appear to influence attitudes. This may be a reflection of consumers resigned acceptance of some level of privacy invasion in an age of database marketing, telemarketers, and spam-mail.

Consistent with intention based models, attitude towards shopping on-line was found to be significantly associated with intention to shop on-line. In addition, normative beliefs and self-efficacy were also found to have significant coefficients in the model regressing intention. Since using the on-line medium to shop is a relatively new phenomenon, an individual’s intentions to engage in this behavior can be expected to be influenced by the opinions held by people who are important to the individual.

Self-efficacy has a positive association with intention to shop on-line. However, even though a number of on-line retailers have designed their sites to mimic the experience of shopping at a brick and mortar store or using a print catalog, shopping on-line may not always be intuitive, and may require additional skills to find the desired product/service and/or the vendor offering it, evaluate product information, perform comparisons, make a selection, and complete the check-out. To build confidence, on-line retailers could follow the lead of some electronic brokerage firms that have encouraged prospective customers to engage in simulated on-line trading. Such ‘‘test-drives’’ may help to alleviate consumer concerns.

## 5. Limitations

Limitations to this study are in the areas of sampling and non-response bias. The sample was drawn from a highly homogenous group residing in a single city, probably lacking the diversity that can be expected from a comparable sample chosen from across an entire country or the world. The stratified sampling procedure used was intended to reflect the population based on income levels. However, since household income data for the target sample was not available, a surrogate measure (assessed home value) was used. Even though the correlation between income levels reported by the respondents and assessed home values was significant at the 0.01 level (Spearman’s Rho: 0.594), the substitute measure is not a perfect indicator of household income.

## 6. Conclusion

Dramatically lower than anticipated sales have dampened the unmitigated enthusiasm that characterized business-to-consumer electronic commerce a few years ago. A number of on-line businesses, that could not attract and/or keep customers, have been forced into bankruptcy or have been acquired by larger companies that could weather the uncertainty of a stalled economy. In spite of the current retrenchment in IT investments, especially in on-line ventures, the Internet has established itself as a legitimate and promising retail channel.

Results highlight the idea that salient beliefs including compatibility, ease of use, usefulness, and security are highly relevant in assessing attitude towards on-line shopping, while attitude is strongly associated with intention. Moreover, normative beliefs and self-efficacy are also shown to be pertinent in acceptance of the on-line medium for shopping. We hope that the results provide insights for researchers and practitioners by increasing their understanding of consumer intentions to shop on-line.
