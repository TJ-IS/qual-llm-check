# A theory of application service provider (ASP) use from a client perspective

Michael Alan Smith<sup>\*</sup>, Ram L. Kumar

BIS/OM Department, UNC Charlotte, Belk College of Business, 9201 University City Blvd, Charlotte, NC 28223-0001, USA

Accepted 7 August 2003

Available online 5 March 2004

## Abstract

Since the late 1990s, ‘‘application service providers’’ (ASPs) have offered telecommunication-based application services. ASP use can be considered a type of IS outsourcing as well as the creation of an inter-organizational system, such as is created for electronic data interchange (EDI). We developed a theory of ASP adoption and use from the client’s perspective based on analysis of primary and secondary data on ASP use and analysis of literature on IS outsourcing and EDI. We present a model and highlight similarities and differences between conventional IS outsourcing, ASP use, and EDI. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Application service provision; Application service providers; Grounded theory; IS outsourcing; IT outsourcing; EDI

## 1. Introduction

IS outsourcing has occurred for decades but gained attention during the 1990s as well-known firms signed large contracts, primarily to cut costs or to concentrate on their core business [40]. Client applications were transferred to vendor machines or the vendor bought the client’s systems. In the late 1990s, application service providers (ASPs) offered products, such as Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems, as services available through networks such as the Internet. We define an ASP as a single point of contact for all the telecommunications,hardware, software, and consulting necessary to deploy, run, and maintain hosted applications remotely.

One would expect that the provision of an application service (AS) shares many features with conventional

IS outsourcing as well as offering alternative ways of outsourcing. However, ASPs usually deliver services through the Internet, increasing uncertainty about availability and response time and, in contrast to conventional (non-ASP) outsourcing, AS provision necessitates coordination among network providers, hosting services, software vendors, and consultants. Also, many ASPs are newer and smaller than conventional IS outsourcers, leading to consequences for them and their clients.

In managing multiple customers on the same network, ASPs face issues similar to those faced by EDI initiators. Many EDI applications involve an initial investment by one firm in an EDI infrastructure followed by attempts to have (or force) its suppliers or customers to follow [34,59]. Individual organizations may, of course, decide to adopt EDI alone, depending on its perceived costs and benefits. Also, organizations may differ in terms of the EDI transaction sets that each adopts. This scenario is similar to that in which an ASP invests in an IT infrastructure then signs up customers. ASP adoption depends on the perceived costs and benefits, which vary by customer, which could lead to differences in the applications adopted through AS provision. Finally, issues of security and reliability may be similar to those faced by users of Internet EDI [64,66].

We use the grounded theory methodology [27] to derive a theory of ASP from the client’s perspective and to compare ASP use to IS outsourcing and EDI. There are similarities that suggest areas for applying existing knowledge. There are differences, including client motivations and strategies for adoption and use, that have implications for ASPs, their customers, and other stakeholders.

## 2. Research method

We generated a description and explanatory theory of the ASP use phenomenon using a version [14,75] of the grounded theory approach, which has been employed in social science and policy research and to study phenomena such as IS adoption of CASE tools [55] and EDI use [15]. The grounded theory approach is ‘‘a general methodology of analysis linked with data collection that uses a systematically applied set of methods to generate an inductive theory about a substantive area’’ [26]. It leads to a theory derived from and consistent with the data, and ‘‘is more likely to resemble the ‘reality’ than is theory derived by putting together a series of concepts based on experience or solely through speculation (how one thinks things ought to work)’’. Its use allows us to use findings from prior research, along with current anecdotes concerning ASP use, to create a common theoretical framework for comparing results and generating hypotheses.

## 2.1. Grounded theory generation

Grounded theory generation may use several types of data, including interviews and field observations, as well as documents such as trade and academic journal articles, newspaper articles, and other media material. Phenomena are the central ideas. In theory building, these are put into categories. For example, a phenomenon might be ‘‘desire to restructure their organizations was a prime motivation for employing outsourcing activities in four banks’’ [50], which could be conceptualized as the category aid internal restructuring. This category could become part of a larger one or be refined into subcategories.

The generation of a grounded theory can be divided into three activities: initial identification of phenomena and categories, identification of relationships among categories, and integration into a theory based on the categories and their relationships.

## 2.1.1. Identifying phenomena and categories

Researchers identify phenomena and concepts/categories that relate to an area while trying not to limit or force categories based on preconceptions. This is open coding. The researcher compares additional information with established categories. As more sources are analyzed and more categories emerge, the researcher may return to previously examined sources, reviewing them in light of new data. The researcher ‘‘dimensionalizes’’ categories by noting their attributes or properties. As evidence for findings grows stronger, the researcher may consult other sources likely to yield more information about them. This is theoretical sampling, driven by the emerging theory.

## 2.1.2. Relating categories

The researcher examines the categories for interrelationships. This is axial coding. More categories may then suggest themselves and the researcher return to the data for theoretical sampling or selective coding.

## 2.1.3. Integration

Finally, the researcher develops a narrative that integrates and refines the findings during selective coding. The researcher may still return to the data for more sampling and coding, revisiting prior work several times in the light of emerging findings. A list of categories and relationships emerges.

Analysis concludes at theoretical saturation: ‘‘the point in category development at which no new categories, or relationships emerge during analysis’’. The result is a framework that includes, without contradiction, all the observations of the sources.

## 2.2. Using pre-existing categories

Opinions differ on beginning analysis with categories in mind. Glaser insists that all categories should emerge from the data and that to do otherwise risks forcing the data to conform to preconceived notions. Strauss and Corbin, however, maintain that using prior theory is valid theoretical sampling. We chose to use general pre-existing categories from our topic area: context, causal conditions, adoption and use, and consequences, as well as some subcategories that have been used in similar research.

Because ASP is our area of interest, we generated initial subcategories by analyzing research on the related topics of IS outsourcing and EDI use, an approach that has been used in grounded theory and qualitative research [74,78]. ‘‘Causal conditions’’ became the reasons for which organizations decided to use ASP. An organization may choose from among several options for ASP adoption and use. Consequences of these actions could affect the adopter and other organizations. The context (for example, industry structure) is a collection of organizational and environmental conditions that affect each other and moderate the interactions among the factors.

## 2.3. The study

The study was conducted in two phases. To develop initial subcategories, we examined 16 articles on IS outsourcing and six on EDI that appeared in refereed journals in the years 1990–2001. These were identified through a keyword search of the ABI/Inform Global and the Business Source Elite indices as well as the indices of well-known journals. Similarly, using the CNET and ZDNet indices, we retrieved 52 articles on ASP from widely circulated trade publications. Articles were chosen to gather the points of view of vendors, consulting firms, and clients. Illustrative examples were taken from some, while others were used only to develop categories in our analysis [9,20,24,25,29,45,47,48,63,65,73,76]. We also interviewed senior executives at two IT service providers [69,70] and one large electric utility [38].

Data were gathered and analyzed in 2001 and 2002, a period that saw media coverage of ASP rise and fall. Transcripts of interviews, as well as articles regarding ASPs that were available electronically as ‘‘rich text format’’ files, were input into NVIVO, a qualitative data analysis program. Other materials were analyzed manually.

A fundamental limit of this approach is that important concepts and relationships may be missed. For example, consequences of ASP use may not have yet appeared in or may be missing from the sources studied. Also, many early articles were biased in favor of the practice and, doubtless, the authors influenced one another.

To address these issues, we engaged in three kinds of theoretical sampling. First, we sought articles that were critical of the service. Second, we sought articles published when some ASPs were going out of business and causing difficulties for their clients. Third, we sampled until theoretical saturation occurred. We also considered our ASP categories in light of those from analyses of research on related but more mature phenomena—IS outsourcing and EDI adoption and use, about which the literature presents a more complete and objective picture. Finally, grounded theory work is susceptible to bias through the preconceived notions of researchers. We addressed this by having both researchers review the data independently, meeting to discuss categories periodically, and including only those on which we agreed.

## 3.2. ASP use phenomenon causal conditions

Factors in the context manifest themselves in the organization as the causal conditions that they affect. Causal conditions also affect each other.

Faced with the costs and risks associated with hardware and software purchases and the pace of telecommunications services development, firms may turn to ASP to reduce the total cost of IS acquisition and maintenance or to balance cash inflows and outflows by avoiding the necessity of a large investment. In some organizations, managers may consider using ASPs with customized pricing schemes to make quick decisions and circumvent approval processes for capital budget expenditures [2,10,30,72].

Clients may turn to ASPs in an attempt to deal with the costs and risks of hiring IS workers, for example to create mobile applications for an increasingly distributed work force or to deal with bureaucratic constraints on action. Decision makers may believe that this will allow their organizations to take advantage of new technology. Some organizations may adopt ASP to have their IS staff focus on ‘‘higher value added’’ or ‘‘strategic’’ systems. Or the staff might be freed to develop new applications [62,68]. ## 3.3. Adoption and use of ASPs

This category describes choices clients make to address the causal conditions. These are influenced by the conditions, the features of the context, and each other. Four subcategories of adoption and use emerged: selection and renewal of vendors, selection of application(s) to outsource, writing a contract, and strategy for implementation.

Vendor selection and renewal may be influenced by factors such as the quality of the basic value-added services, including hosting, problem resolution, application upgrades, bandwidth, scalability of the network and hosting infrastructure, security, etc. [2,8,62] and their ability to grow to serve more and, possibly, growing clients. Some of these may depend on the quality of the integration between the vendor and its partners. Factors such as the quality and availability of support staff along with the vendor’s experience with similar size clients and the concentration of a vendor on service in the client’s geographic area were mentioned [23,31,57]. Some vendors marketed themselves on the quality of their service level agreements (SLAs) and speed of implementation.

Lack of capital is a major cause of start-up failure, including ASPs; the vendor’s financial status may bear on client decisions, although accessing this information can be difficult when the vendor is privately held [33,51,53]. Fundamental is the vendor’s business plan, including its focus, pricing, and revenue models. For example, evidence of focus on a reliable customer base may be important, as would be the vendor’s ability to provide services that are difficult to replicate [46,67]. Also, vendors with staffing problems may have difficulty servicing clients.

The ease with which an application can be made discrete from other applications may influence its selection for outsourcing. However, clients are often reluctant to hand over critical applications to a third party.

While the revenue models of many ASPs depend on spreading the cost of an application among many clients, some implementations require customization. This increases the potential profitability of the ASP but also the complexity of operations [4].

The contract is the primary means of ensuring that the vendor will fulfill its role in addressing the causal conditions. Features may include: duration, for periods of a month to several years; pricing, which varies from periodic fixed charges to per-transaction to revenue sharing; and cost of consulting services [52]. Examples of quality of service aspects include connection speed, security, penalties for failure, userperceived service parameters, and back-end service parameters. In addition, vendor performance monitoring by the client, the vendor, or a third party could be specified. There may be provisions for ending the relationship, including recovery of the client’s data and establishing the ownership of applications [43,80].

Finally, an implementation strategy may factor into ASP adoption and use, including the use of pilot projects and the creation of mirror facilities [56] and the existence of client personnel who are familiar with the processes. The client may have to hire additional employees or consultants for support and training, but should not rely totally on the vendor. If the vendor fails, relationships between client and vendor personnel may ease the transition. The third table in Appendix A provides dimensions and illustrative examples.

## 3.4. Consequences of the ASP use phenomenon

The ASP use phenomenon has consequences for organizations adopting IS and for stakeholders in the IT industry, including hardware and software manufacturers, telecommunications firms, and service providers. The fourth table in Appendix A presents dimensions and illustrative examples.

For client firms, one set of consequences concerns whether causal conditions were addressed. Clients may benefit from changes to the level or predictability of costs and IS quality, which may affect the ROI for their ASP project. However, this may be affected by the client’s importance to the vendor and whether the vendor’s business model leaves cost saving to be passed to the client. Consequences for the client also include side effects, such as role changes for IS management and employees.

The emergence of ASPs may affect the business models of software and hardware makers. Application software makers may need to adapt their packages to work in an n-tier Web-based environment—a Webbased interface differs from a host-based one. There may also be opportunities for new products, such as middleware, particularly for systems software makers who also may have to change their applications to accommodate the performance and security requirements of WAN-based n-tier applications. Since ASPs may serve a large customer base with differing customization requirements, software designed to facilitate customization may be easier to deliver through ASP [17,77,81].

Hardware manufacturers could see changes in sales, since ASPs require high end servers while their customers’ needs may be satisfied by computers designed as thin clients. As well, there may be changes in hardware distribution channels as ASPs replace their clients as buyers of servers [7,11,69].

For telecommunications providers, ASP use may be an opportunity to sell bandwidth. Although conventional IS outsourcing has pushed hardware and telecommunications capabilities, vendors did not develop equipment or capacity in response to the needs of the outsourcing market. In the case of ASPs, however, hardware makers such as Compaq and telecommunications companies such as Qwest Communications have sought partners to take advantage of the new demand for their products.

Service providers, including ASPs as well as portals, ISPs, VARs (value-added resellers), and consulting firms can add value to existing services by helping integrate applications in addition to providing their primary services. Some have allied with telecommunications companies to offer application and consulting services. Internet portals that provide basic applications may consider hosting applications to become ASPs. ISPs could find AS provision a natural extension of their access service [19,54]. Software makers, hardware makers, and service providers may also face pressures to merge or to enter into alliances [21].

## 4. A comparison of ASP use and related phenomena

We compare ASP use with two related phenomena: conventional IS outsourcing and EDI.

## 4.1. Context

The ASP phenomenon context resembles that of conventional IS outsourcing. The fundamental environmental condition that drives both is the increasing cost and risk associated with hardware, software, and personnel [5,42,50]. Organizational and IS management concerns associated with ASP use also mirror those of conventional IS outsourcing: firms desire to avoid up-front investments in software systems, corporations draw on their experience with similar services, and some managers are reluctant to give up control.

The matter of risk is different. In the case of conventional IS outsourcing and, sometimes, ASP use, risk is mentioned as a reason not to use the service. But many ASP clients were startups for whom it was a way to gain access to the IS they needed to operate. This, combined with the revenue- or profit-sharing pricing models offered by some ASPs, meant that there were clients with nothing to lose by adopting ASP. These clients transferred their technology risks and difficulties to vendors who gave up control of their revenue streams by using models directly dependent on their client’s revenues. The demise of unsound clients caused difficulties for the ASPs and for their clients who were sound. So, ASP differs from conventional IS outsourcing and EDI use in that the client must concern itself with the financial stability of the vendor and of its customer base (SMEs often).

In the case of ASP use, the crucial technological enabler is the growing extent and reliability of networks. This sets it apart from conventional IS outsourcing, which does not depend on technological advances, and gives the phenomenon a common concern with EDI, especially Internet EDI. Organizations considering Internet EDI also face issues related to the reach and performance of networks. Table 1 summarizes these differences.

## 4.2. Causal conditions

ASP use shares with conventional IS outsourcing the desire of clients to reduce total costs and to balance cash inflows and outflows by reducing up-front costs [3,5,40,49]. The need to reduce cost also drove EDI adoption. Although these are general business concerns, they are more important for IS because of the environmental conditions: the increasing cost and risk associated with hardware, software, and personnel, and corporate reluctance to make major up-front investments. Along similar lines, addressing staffing problems and gaining access quickly to new technology are cited as causal conditions of both practices. Difficulty in hiring employees to focus on higher value added activities is mentioned concerning both IS outsourcing and ASP use [79].

In the case of EDI, organizational benefits, such as cost savings, were one of the reasons for adoption. Other factors, such as coercion by powerful trading partners and reaction to competition, were also important. While client organizational benefits dominated the reasons for ASP adoption, it is possible that, once a critical mass of customers use ASPs, competitive pressure will force others to use them to save money or gain access to technology. The idea of faster adoption once a critical mass of adopters is reached is supported by the theory of network externalities and has been studied in the context of EDI. It is also possible that ASPs, unlike conventional IS outsourcers, are more sensitive to sustaining a critical mass of customers for financial stability because they invest in IT infrastructures and amortize their investments over a large customer base, while conventional outsourcers almost never made IT investments common to a variety of customers. ## 4.3. Adoption and use

## 4.3.1. Selection and renewal of vendors

In the selection of vendors, IS outsourcing and ASP use share concerns appropriate for service vendor selection such as the quality and availability of support staff, the ability of the vendor to grow or adapt with the client, and indication of some vendor competence through focus and tangible evidence of success. Differences in concerns derive first from the base of AS provision in telecommunications. ASP vendor concerns include a host of network-related issues, such as the scalability of the network infrastructure, that are not mentioned as IS outsourcing concerns.

Other differences emerge from the difficulties experienced by many ASPs. This led potential clients to evaluate vendors’ financial situations, etc. In the case of IS outsourcing and EDI, in-depth analyses of the vendor were not mentioned. Many ASPs were founded in the late 1990s as pure-play ASPs or were untested partnerships of existing firms in related industries (e.g. IT consulting and telecommunications). These firms sometimes relied on risky business models and, when they failed, their customers had difficult transitions to other vendors. Until ASPs are as stable as outsourcing vendors, such as IBM and EDS, potential clients will have to pay close attention to vendors’ business models or contract with conventional IS outsourcers.

## 4.3.2. Adoption and use—selection of application

Discrete, non-critical applications that are easy to customize or that require none are candidates for IS outsourcing or for access through ASP use [22,41,42,82]. However, one could argue that certain critical applications are best left to vendors if the client staff cannot guarantee support, as with applications, such as ERP, for which it is difficult to retain personnel. This would more likely be the case if the client firm were small or had difficulty hiring and retaining IS personnel. In the case of EDI, organizations typically chose transaction sets that were discrete (e.g. purchase orders) and were slower to adopt transactions that required a higher degree of integration or process change [13].

## 4.3.3. Adoption and use—writing a contract

In both conventional IS outsourcing and ASP use, the contract is the major definition of the relationship between client and vendor and concerns about service level expectations and penalties for failure are common to both [39,61]. Conventional IS outsourcers and ASPs offer flat fee and revenue- or profit-sharing options for payment. Contract durations of around three years are common, although one ASP offered month-to-month contracts.

With ASP use come concerns of the need for performance monitoring. Although need for evidence of performance was mentioned in IS outsourcing, this may be more important for ASP use because of network uncertainty. Exit provisions have become important as ASPs have collapsed, leaving clients scrambling to reclaim their data. This is related to the environmental conditions of ASPs and their customers as well as the business models of the vendors. For conventional IS outsourcers, attention to exit provisions was not apparent though outsourcing agreements have failed.

## 4.3.4. Adoption and use—personnel and contract management

Having some expertise in-house is an issue in both conventional IS outsourcing and ASP use. In the latter case, cultivating a close relationship with the vendor has been mentioned, although, in the case of IS outsourcing, relationships between the client and vendor employees should remain ‘‘professional’’ since the creation of personal relationships between key personnel can increase the cost to the client [22,44,49,82]. Since ASP adoption often involves using a new system, training and access to expertise, perhaps through consultants, must be considered. Relative bargaining power of the SME customer base and ASPs is important as was the relative bargaining power of organizations in an EDI network.

Conventional outsourcing, as well as ASP use, involves role changes for IT employees who become contract managers. Use of EDI also involves changes for employees who, in addition to programming, must accompany sales personnel on customer calls. Also, non-IT personnel must learn to operate and trust new technologies [37]. Table 3 summarizes this comparison.

## 4.4. Consequences

For the client, the consequences of ASP use and IS outsourcing are similar; this derives from the similar causal conditions related to finance and access to quality ISs. For EDI, consequences included improved financial performance, improved customer service, and new products and services.

In the case of both IS outsourcing and ASP use, role changes for IS personnel occurred. For example, IS managers took on the responsibilities of managing relationships with their vendors. However, in the case of IS outsourcing, client personnel were sometimes hired by the vendor even though they continued to work on the client site. In this case, the former relationship may color the new one, leading at times, to dissatisfaction with the vendor.

For other stakeholders, the consequences have differed. Although some software makers changed their licensing agreements to claim a greater share of profits from IS outsourcers, no one spoke of changing the way business software was financed and software makers did not speak of integrating ‘‘forward’’ to better control the new channel.

## 5. Discussion: implications

Data concerning the unanticipated, unpleasant consequences of ASP use and the concerns of EDI users suggest that ASP clients have additional factors to consider in addition to those associated with IS outsourcing.

A strong implication is that managers should concern themselves with the business models of their vendors. A major reason for ASP use is the cost advantage that ASPs can provide by amortizing their IT investments over multiple customers. This is greater when the application is standard and can be replicated. However, some applications, such as supply chain management, require customization for each user. This increases the system support costs and reduces economies of scale. In addition, conflicting requirements of different customers can hurt system performance and lead to customer dissatisfaction. There may, however, be room for ASP aggregators who design and deploy intelligent software that can integrate diverse applications.

Many outsourcing contracts transferred authority for maintaining existing facilities or applications to the vendor, sometimes even at the client site. There were no technical hurdles to overcome, only management ones. ASP use, however, is a ‘‘child of technology’’ and clients must be convinced of the quality of the vendor’s hosting services and support and the reliability of the network. Since many ASPs rely in the Internet, a critical part of their ability to provide consistent good service lies out of their hands. In response, ASPs have located in major metropolitan areas where they can contract with several ISPs.

The ASPs that are likely to survive are those that have financing to endure the loss of clients without having to turn to venture capital. Surviving ASPs, which will be larger and less motivated to provide services on risky terms, will often deal with small clients from a position of strength.

Large organizations seem comfortable using ASPs for niche applications. For example, a large utility company used ASPs to make engineering design software available to engineers at multiple locations [38]. We found no instance of total IS outsourcing using AS provision.

In the IS outsourcing literature, the client is advised to retain some IS expertise in house to represent the client’s interest. Even if the client outsourced to gain access to technical talent, there would be in house personnel who understood the business and the IS. The vendor would assume that the client can determine when it is being adequately served. However, one of the reasons for small firms using ASPs their lack the personnel to create and manage an IS. Thus, the client relies totally on the vendor for access to technology and advice concerning changes. The balance of power favors the vendor.

In IS outsourcing, firms sometimes use multiple vendors to reduce the power of each and to gain access to different sets of expertise [16]. In this case, a large vendor could serve also as an integrator. However, in the case of ASP use, the business is normally dealing with several applications that are smaller in scope and scale and the client depends on a single vendor. Large clients can deal with vendors and integrators as peers—they can select from a group and force them to cooperate. However, small clients will not have that leverage. They have little choice but to outsource the integration. One does not see successful ASPs buying stock or entering profit-sharing agreements with dozens of small customers. Early ASPs, such as Pandesic suffered at the bottom line from having entered into profit sharing arrangements with small (particularly dotcom) companies.

An organization with significant IT resources could create an ASP in-house and service both internal and external needs from it. This is consistent with the evolution of IS outsourcing itself, for example in the banking and aerospace industries, e.g. GM has announced an ASP service.

Contract issues may be simpler if the ASP has many customers requiring similar services. ASPs may prefer standard contracts and, from the client perspective, standard contacts can be beneficial, since they can make it easier to switch services. However, it is important to ensure that standard contracts address important issues such as service levels. The ability to negotiate contracts with adequate service level guarantees may depend on the bargaining power of individual customers or the collective power of groups of customers. Large firms with existing IS staffs are more likely to possess the resources to address this issue. Smaller firms must contract for this expertise or rely on the ASP. In the event of a dispute, the SME would have little recourse other than to threaten to terminate the agreement, a difficult course for a relatively minor client with no expertise in-house.

## 6. Conclusions

Using the grounded theory approach, we created a comprehensive theory of ASP use from the client’s perspective and, since ASP use is outsourcing, of IS outsourcing as well. We identified causal conditions, varieties of implementation (adoption and use), and consequences of ASP use. We also identified contextual moderating factors at several levels, including the general business environment, the organization, and IS management. We compared our findings to research on the related practices of IS outsourcing and EDI, relating ASP use to areas with which managers are familiar in order to help them make better decisions regarding ASP use and to suggest areas in which existing research may apply to the new phenomenon.

The comparison suggests that, while many causal conditions of all three practices are similar, the features of adoption and use and the consequences of ASP differ. These are probably a result of differing environmental factors, including the technological base of the ASP, the harsher business climate of the early 2000s, and the relatively poor bargaining power of the SMEs relative to that of the vendors. These environmental differences have brought forward the riskier aspects of ASP business models and contributed to the collapse of many ASPs. This has led to suggestions that clients should become more familiar with their potential vendors than when outsourcing or using EDI. ASPs are challenging the business models of IT vendors and consulting firms by threatening existing revenue streams even as they create new ones. ASP has created new marketing opportunities, some of which would lead these firms to merge with complementary service providers. An area in which ASP use stands out is in its effect on various stakeholders— software, hardware, telecommunications, and service providers. But as basic service providers adapt, ASPs and other service providers will change as well.

AS provision does not represent the way in which all IS will be deployed. However, ASPs offer an alternative, particularly for SMEs that want access to the latest in hardware and software but lack the time, cash, personnel or infrastructure to secure it themselves. For large firms, ASPs may simply offer opportunities to selectively outsource applications. However, the failure of many ASPs indicates the need for clients to understand the risks associated with the practice from the vendor’s perspective.
