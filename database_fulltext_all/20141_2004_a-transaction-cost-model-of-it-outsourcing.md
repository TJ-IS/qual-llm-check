# A transaction cost model of IT outsourcing

Benoit A. Aubert<sup>a,b,\*</sup>, Suzanne Rivard<sup>a,b</sup>, Michel Patry<sup>a,b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>HEC Montre´al, 3000 Coˆte-Ste-Catherine Road, Montreal, Que, Canada H3T 2A7 <sup>b</sup>CIRANO, 2020 University Street, 25th floor, Montreal, Que, Canada H3A 2A5

Accepted 2 September 2003

Available online 13 November 2003

## Abstract

This paper proposes and tests an explanatory model of information technology (IT) outsourcing behavior. Relying on transaction costs and incomplete contracts theories, the model hypothesizes that characteristics of IT operation activities—asset specificity, uncertainty, business skills, and technical skills required to perform the activities—will influence the level of their outsourcing. The model was tested using data from a survey of 335 firms. Results indicate that uncertainty is the major deterrent to outsourcing, while the level of technical skills is the most important reason to outsource. Business skills do not seem to play a significant role. Finally, asset specificity, which is always presented as a constraint to outsourcing, showed inconsistent effects. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Outsourcing; Transaction cost theory; IS operations; Asset specificity; Outsourcing decision

## 1. Introduction

The information technology (IT) outsourcing phenomenon has been expanding during the past decade and this growth is likely to continue [36]. Outsourcing deals are becoming larger and often involve partners, including offshore suppliers [32]. The relationships implied in outsourcing arrangements are increasingly complex. For example, while acting as client and supplier, Xerox and EDS were engaged in a legal battle on some aspects of their agreement, but they acted as business partners to collaborate on ventures, such as the State of Connecticut’s U\$ 1 billion outsourcing deal [21].

Researchers have analyzed IT outsourcing from several angles. Some authors compare outsourcing to insourcing [15]. Some evaluate if and how in-house services could be reorganized to provide firms with benefits similar to those of outsourcing [12,17]. Others focus on the client–supplier relationship, analyzing its characteristics, its partnership quality, and the impact of these on outsourcing success [19]. Some studies analyze IT outsourcing with a political or social lens, providing alternative explanations (different from economic motives) for outsourcing decisions [16]. Finally, a number of researchers focus on the determinants of outsourcing, that is, on those variables that will influence the outsourcing decision [20,28].

Our study fits in this last group. As IT outsourcing continues to expand, firms have to decide which types of activities are good candidates for outsourcing and which should be kept in-house. Relying on transaction costs and incomplete contracts theories, our study examined the influence of IT operation activities characteristics—asset specificity, uncertainty, business skills, and technical skills required to perform the activities—on the level of outsourcing of these activities, in 335 firms.

## 2. Theoretical background

Outsourcing is the handover of an activity to an external supplier; it is an alternative to internal production. It was first described by Coase [6] who outlined many of the avenues subsequently explored by researchers in transaction costs and incomplete contracts theories. The essence of the argument is that using a market is not frictionless. When buying a service or a product, one incurs costs. If these costs become too high, relying on self-production is more appropriate.

Transaction costs theory has two underlying assumptions: bounded rationality and opportunism. Bounded rationality is the inability of the human mind to find or process all the information about a transaction; therefore, it is conducted with a certain level of uncertainty. Opportunism is more than the simple defense of one’s interest or value maximization; it is self interest seeking with guile [41]. The combination of these assumptions results in information asymmetry. When parties do not have the same information, they will not share the information they possess, because they wish to use it strategically. In order to strike a better deal, sellers will hide negative characteristics of their products, and buyers will not reveal how much they are prepared to pay. Since both parties know that the other is opportunistic, each will engage in information seeking activities, for example having a product tested before buying it, or asking for warranties and safeguards to protect themselves from potentially false allegations from the other party. All these actions generate transaction costs.

The factors determining the importance of transaction costs are grouped into three broad categories: (1) the specificity of the assets required for performing the transaction, (2) the uncertainty surrounding the transaction, and (3) the origin of the critical investments associated with the transaction and their alignment with the allocation of residual rights.

## 2.1. Asset specificity

The nature of assets varies. They can be machinery required to manufacture a product, knowledge needed to perform a service, or even appropriate location convenient for dealing with the other party. While some assets are common, others are dedicated to a particular use and are said to be specific. The degree of specificity can be measured by the difference between the cost of the asset and the value of its second best use [40]. For example, money is not specific. One thousand dollars might be needed for a given transaction but should the transaction not be completed, the U\$ 1000 could be used for another transaction with no loss in value. Here, asset specificity is null. At the other extreme, knowledge is often a specific asset: in acquiring knowledge, one makes an irreversible investment (one cannot ‘‘unlearn’’ and recuperate the time spent). If the knowledge becomes obsolete, it is a lost investment. Similarly, if the knowledge was unique to one trading partner and the relationship terminated, the investment is lost.

Investments in specific assets lead to transaction costs. A party investing in a specific asset will seek a promise from the other party before actually making the investment. Failure to secure such a promise would put the first party in a vulnerable position, since the other party could renege on the agreement by asking for a lower price, for instance. Indeed, accepting a lower price, even if it would make the transaction unprofitable, would be better than losing the specific investment. The investing party could end up paying a quasi-rent to the other party [30]. Generally, the promises take the form of long-term contracts, bonds, and volume guarantees, which enable the investing party to recoup its investment without risk [39]. Implementing all these promises creates transaction costs. As asset specificity increases and contract protection clauses become more numerous and complex, they become increasingly costly to implement. When these costs are too high, it would be more appropriate to conduct the transaction in-house.

## 2.2. Uncertainty

All transactions are conducted under a certain level of imperfect information. There is demand uncertainty, when parties do not know ex ante the exact volume of product that will be required or ignore the form the service will take. This leads to many problems which increase transaction costs. If these costs become too high, the transaction will not occur and will be internalized by one of the parties. Uncertainty about the quantity or the exact nature of the deliverables will make the contract difficult to write. When contingencies are too numerous, it might be impossible to agree on a contract, thereby preventing parties from concluding the transaction. Internal governance is more flexible with respect to this type of uncertainty. When using employees, an employer can reassign the tasks and change the specification of the output at will, providing that the changes do not exceed the agreed upon zone of indifference, as described by Simon [34].

Another type of uncertainty is linked to measurement. Contracting parties need to be able to evaluate the service or product being exchanged. Such evaluation, in terms of quality, quantity, timeliness, etc. can be laborious. This is an even greater problem when the services involve joint efforts by several team members. Then, assessing individual contributions might be impossible [1]. Using the market presupposes that the parties can establish a price for the goods exchanged. If such a price is difficult or impossible to establish, the market will not be viable and internal production will be superior. Therefore, increased uncertainty in a transaction will lead to increased use of internal governance.

## 2.3. Critical investments and residual rights

Accepting that some contingencies are impossible to foresee, the parties can decide to allocate to one contracting party the ability to decide, ex post, the appropriate actions. This is what happens when a company buys a supplier (this is vertical integration). It acquires the decision rights over the actions. Grossman and Hart [10] showed that although it would not lead to optimal behavior (which would only be attained if parties would cooperate), the parties would be better off if they allocated the decision rights to the party making the most important investment.

Grossman and Hart also demonstrated that the party owning the decision rights will tend to over-invest in the transaction while the other will under invest. Because the party making the over investment is also the one making the crucial investment, this lowers the overall inefficiency.

This argument is often applicable in cases when expertise is involved. This would suggest letting the expert party decide on the course of action for a given activity. It is analogous to Pisano’s argument that companies with less experience in specific areas were better off outsourcing these activities, since the supplier could bring the investment (expertise) they lacked. Expertise and knowledge have been a key factor in explaining the success of outsourcing arrangements [18].

## 2.4. Previous tests of the theory

Empirical efforts resulting from this theoretical foundation are numerous. We mention some here to illustrate the explanatory power. For a more extensive review, see Rindfleisch and Heide [31]. Asset speci ficity has been the most studied variable, notably in the automobile parts industry [22,23,25]. Among these, Monteverde and Teece stressed the importance of specific expertise in the integration/outsourcing deci sion. The automobile parts production requires human efforts, depending on the piece produced. By evaluating the engineering effort for each piece, the authors demonstrated that the production of highly specific pieces was significantly more vertically integrated than that of non-specific parts. Joskow [13,14] analyzed the contracts between the electrical utilities and the coa producers in the United States. The coal extracted from the eastern regions has uniform characteristics and can be utilized by a wide variety of equipment. In the West, coal quality fluctuates and installations needed to burn it must be specially adapted to each type of coal. As predicted, the contracts in Western US have a significantly longer duration than those in the East, reducing the risk associated with the specific investments. Hennart [11] studied the aluminum and tin industries. He found that the quality of the bauxite varied widely from one extraction site to another. The equipment needed to transform bauxite into aluminum is highly dependent on the type and quality. As predicted, aluminum is a highly integrated industry. The tin industry is different. The mineral is cheap to extract and tin production utilizes a widely used technology. There is, therefore, no apparent reason for this industry to be highly verti cally integrated. As predicted, this market is active and fluid, involving numerous, non-integrated small firms.

Some studies incorporated the effect of uncertainty on the decision to use internal governance or outsourcing. In the sales force industry, Anderson [2] evaluated the effect of opportunism. He verified that asset specificity and performance measurement problems were positively related with it. Furthermore, the goal alignment of different parties (clans) significantly reduced the degree of opportunism.

However, the results obtained were not always aligned with the predictions drawn from the theory. Walker and Weber [38] measured the impact of uncertainty on governance, obtaining mixed results. Murray and Kotabe [27] used three measures linked to human asset specificity. They obtained counter-intuitive results. Asset specificity was linked positively with outsourcing.

Empirical work exists in IT but the results are more puzzling. For instance, Nam et al. found that uncertainty played a significant role in the decision to outsource. As expected, higher uncertainty leads to less outsourcing. However, they did not find any significant link between asset specificity and outsourcing. Ang and Straub [3] evaluated the overall impact of transaction costs on the degree of outsourcing and found no significant relationship. Production economies were the key driver explaining the degree of outsourcing.

The puzzling results in the IT field, along with the fact that no previous study evaluated simultaneously asset specificity, uncertainty, and the origin of the investments provided a motivation for our investigation.

## 3. The model

Transaction cost theory enables us to formulate four hypotheses about the level of outsourcing of IT operation activities. The first pertains to asset specificity. Transactions requiring specific assets will bear higher transaction costs; hence, they will be more likely to be retained in-house.

H1. The degree of asset specificity will have a negative effect on the level of outsourcing of IT operation activities.

Similarly, uncertainty will be a deterrent for organizations trying to outsource IT operation activities. The difficulties associated with writing contracts or with measurement will induce them to prefer internal governance for highly uncertain activities.

H2. The level of uncertainty will have a negative effect on the level of outsourcing of IT operation activities.

Finally, the last two hypotheses are related to the origin of the investments. Two types of skills are required to carry any given activity. Business skills refer to the knowledge of the business environment of a firm, while technical skills are more related to the generic components of a given activity. According to Grossman and Hart, activities presenting a high level of business content should be kept inhouse, because the members of the organization are more likely to master this content. Conversely, highly technical activities should be outsourced, since suppliers can nurture the critical technical skills.

H3. The amount of business skills required to perform IT operation activities will have a negative effect on their level of outsourcing.

H4. The amount of technical skills required to perform IT operation activities will have a positive effect on their level of outsourcing.

## 4. Methodology

The data presented here come from three sources: two surveys and a ranking exercise.

## 4.1. Questionnaire development and validation

Existing measures pertaining to the various variables were identified and new ones were built when required. The measures were subsequently validated in three ways: a prior assessment with a classification exercise, an evaluation by practitioners, and finally a statistical evaluation.

The measures forming the asset specificity construct used several variables. First, the investments in various forms made by the client and the suppliers in order to work together and the delays observed when recruiting and training employees for different types of jobs (the more specific the knowledge, the more difficult to find and train the employees and the longer the anticipated delays) were assessed using [4]. The structural liaison devises were measured using a measure proposed by Miller and Dro¨ge [24]. Finally, a direct evaluation of human resource specificity was done by asking respondents to evaluate the specific language or work procedures required to do the work.

To evaluate uncertainty, first, a list of formal measures used to control IS operations was measured and then the level of formalization of the department was evaluated using Miller and Dro¨ge’s measure. The tasks themselves were also assessed. Questions were asked to determine their level of standardization [37], their complexity [5], and their level of difficulty.

To evaluate the origin of the most important investments, respondents were asked to assess the relative importance of business skills and of technical skills for a list of activities related to IS operations. Finally, the governance mode was assessed by asking the respondent a series of questions regarding a list of the generic IT operation activities listed in Table 1. This was developed on the basis of a framework of information services related activities proposed by [33], which distinguished between management, development, and operations activities. The questions pertained to who was responsible for executing each activity (the firm employees or the supplier), where the activity was executed (on the firm’s premises or on the supplier’s premises), and who in the firm or of its supplier was the owner of the required equipment.

The initial questionnaire was subjected to three validations. First, all items of the questionnaire were printed on individual cards and sorted by seven expert judges. To evaluate the concordance between the judges and the intended questionnaire, the Kappa coefficient (range: -1 to 1) was used [7]. The results obtained were considered appropriate, the coefficient scores ranging from 0.68 to 0.89, with a standard deviation of 0.07 [26].

The second validation step was an assessment by two experienced practitioners to determine face validity and to ensure that questions were easy to understand and unambiguous.

Finally, after questionnaires were received, a subsample of 250 questionnaires was used to further refine the measures. Four criteria were used in reliability assessment: the Cronbach alpha (the lowest alpha was 0.52, all the others were above 0.70), the individual item reliability as evaluated by partial least squares (PLS, all items having loadings above 0.5 on their respective construct), the Rho coefficient (lowest, 0.77; all others above 0.82), and the average variance extracted (all over 50%).

Average scores were computed for each measure. These were then included in PLS to compute the loadings of each measure on each of the four constructs. High loadings of a measure on its corresponding construct suggested convergent validity, while low loadings on the other constructs were indications of discriminant validity. All variables were found to load significantly higher on their respective constructs than on the other ones, with the exception of structural liaison devices, which loaded on both asset specificity and uncertainty/measurement problems variables. Although this was puzzling, the variable was kept in the asset specificity construct for further analysis. The average variance extracted for each variable was also compared with the variance shared between the constructs. The largest correlation between two different constructs was 0.26 (12 of the 15 coefficients lower than 0.15). The lowest AVE squared root was 0.61 (3 of 5 over 0.72). Therefore, the variance explained for each construct was notably higher than the shared variance between variables. These results support the adequate properties (discriminant and convergent validity) of the measures.

## 4.2. First survey

A survey was conducted to test the model, 630 usable questionnaires were returned completed from a sample of 1410 IS executives (response rate of 44%). No non-response bias was found, either by comparing respondents’ firm size with non-respondents firm size or by comparing the responses from late and early respondents (as suggested by Oppeinheim [29]).

Data analysis was conducted using partial least squares. PLS enable the evaluation of a causal model [35]. Here, three main variables were expected to influence the governance mode (outsourcing): asset specificity, uncertainty/measurement problems, and the type of skills (technical or business) required. All were measured by several items. From these items, PLS compute latent variables and evaluate the links between the independent variables and the dependent variable. PLS compute a regression coefficient for each relationship specified in the causal model. Special cases of PLS models overlap well known models like first principal components or first canonical correlation [42]. PLS simultaneously evaluated both the measurement model and the theoretical model, and adjusted the relationships between the variables accordingly [8].

In our study, PLS were appropriate, considering the size of the model. They enabled us to obtain meaningful results from the number of responses. The rule of thumb for determining the smallest sample size required to perform PLS analysis is that the sample must comprise 10 times the number of items present in the largest construct (or the number of links to one variable) [9].

## 4.3. Expert ranking of IT activities and the follow-up survey

In order to better understand asset specificity, two additional actions were taken. First, a panel of eight experts was formed to rank IT operation activities in terms of asset specificity. The results were analyzed and a judge was removed from the panel, since his ranking was negatively correlated with the rankings of the others. The panel results gave an average rank for each activity. The Friedman test was performed and the agreement was significant (Chi-square: 38.9, d:f: ¼ 15, $P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The Kendall W-coefficient was computed (it is a normalization of Friedman’s). The coefficient computed was 0.37 $( P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). It indicates a moderate but still significant agreement between the judges.

Second, respondents from the first survey were again contacted and asked to evaluate directly, on a 1–7 Likert scale, the level of specificity of each IS operation activity. One hundred and twenty persons responded to this additional request. The results of the 120 responses were compiled and average scores were computed for the IT activities. This measure of specificity was completely different from the first survey. In the first, respondents were asked to answer a series of reflective items that were deemed appropriate to measure specificity (see Appendix A). In the second, they were asked directly to rate (on a 1–7 Likert scale) the level of specificity of each IS operation activity (using a single item).

## 5.1. Analysis

Fig. 2 presents the results of the PLS analysis. All the links were significant $( P < 0 . 0 5 )$ except for business skills. The most surprising finding is the link between asset specificity and the outsourcing level. It was anticipated that the presence of specific assets would deter companies from outsourcing IT activities. The results obtained suggest the opposite. The link between asset specificity and outsourcing level is strong and significant. As anticipated, increased technical skills tend to favor outsourcing. Similarly, the presence of uncertainty and measurement problems seems to preclude outsourcing of IT activities. Overall, 19.4% of the variance was explained.

When looking in more detail at the measures, the negative and significant link between uncertainty and measurement problems and the level of outsourcing indicates that participating firms outsourced more readily when activities were less complex, easier to measure, and more standardized. They also relied on outsourcing when they had a more formalized setting (having rules and guidelines for IT activities and roles) and when they had formal tools to measure the services rendered.

The link between technical skills and outsourcing confirms the hypothesis that more important technical skills are needed to perform activities; more companies rely on an external supplier for these activities. The link between asset specificity and the outsourcing level is significant but in the opposite direction from that expected. This result is puzzling and warrants more investigation. From the results, it seems that more human resource specificity, higher hiring and training delays, more structural liaison devices and more efforts, from both the client and the supplier required to adapt systems and hardware for dealing with each other will entice companies to rely on outsourcing for IT provision.

Many of the components of asset specificity are related to human asset specificity. This approach was taken because IT itself is non-specific. Most companies use the same equipment. The equipment is, however, used in many different ways (often specific) and these uses should translate to specific knowledge for workers, longer training periods, and more difficulties to find workers having some of this specific knowledge (or being qualified to learn them). This focus on specific knowledge might be a partial explanation for the results. It might take a long time to hire and train new employees because of intense technical training. In a very fluid market, technical skills would be readily available. However, maybe there is a shortage of qualified resources, as often stated in the popular press. This would mean that delays are important in order to bring employees to the appropriate level of technical expertise, and not because of specific training. In this case, it would make sense to outsource these activities, since no specificity is involved and technical skills should be available from vendors.

Two components of asset specificity were the extent of changes in configurations and procedures (on the client and on the supplier sides) required for outsourcing. These changes were measured on Likert scales. However, they were not measured relative to the value of the contract. It is possible that even if changes were deemed important, the costs or efforts associated with these changes remained marginal when compared to the contract value. This could explain why they would not deter companies from outsourcing.

Another possibility that would explain the ambiguous role of asset specificity is the non-optimal beha vior of some firms which keep all of their IT activities in-house, thereby distorting the results. This was verified by conducting the same analysis with the subsample of firms that had an outsourcing score above the median. The results were similar, the links were in the same direction as for the complete sample and all links but business skills were significant. The variance explained was 32%. While this suggests that there might be an inefficient bias toward in-house provision of services, it does not change the essence of the results.

## 5.2. Asset specificity revisited

Two additional steps were taken to analyze asset specificity: a panel of experts classified the activities for asset specificity and survey respondents were again contacted and asked to evaluate, on a 1–7 Likert scale, the level of specificity of each activity. Table 2 presents these results.

The first observation is the high correlation between the ranking (from the most specific to the least specific) of the activities by the panel of experts and the evaluation of the 120 respondents in the follow-up survey. The 0.87 correlation coefficient indicated that both evaluations were very similar and it reinforced our confidence in the assessment of asset specificity performed by both groups.

The correlation between the percentage of outsourced activities and the average rank (in terms of asset specificity) of each activity was computed. The coefficient was 0.59. This supports the hypothesis that specific activities (higher ranks) tended to be conducted in-house (less outsourcing). The evaluation of the correlation between the respondents’ evaluation and the percentage of the activities outsourced gave almost identical results (0.54).

These results were obtained with the 16 activities of the survey, which had a small n. However, these activities represent the core ones linked with IT operations. This indicated that the activities involving more asset specificity were less prone to be outsourced (higher percentage done in-house) than those involving less asset specificity. This is in line with theoretical expectations but in contradiction with the survey findings.

## 5.3. Understanding the measure of asset specificity

The contradictory results obtained from the survey and from the subsequent analysis of asset specificity may be attributed to different causes. First, it was possible that the measures used to tackle asset specificity were not adequate. Asset specificity has been very difficult to assess. Nam et al. obtained non-significant results with respect to asset specificity and questioned that whether the concept was partly captured by a tacit knowledge variable. In their case, the measure focused on specific knowledge and ownership of assets.

The items chosen might not have captured the essence of the specificity construct. Problems with the instruments used cannot be discounted since other means of assessing the role of specificity (judge rank ings) led to more conclusive results.

Another option to explain the reverse link between asset specificity and the outsourcing level is the interaction between uncertainty and asset specificity. Williamson stated that if no uncertainty was associated with a transaction, asset specificity was not a major constraint. Long-term contracting could be used to alleviate the problems introduced by the presence of specific investments. Maybe the IT operations outsourced presented a very low level of uncertainty and measurement problems (in absolute terms), and therefore long-term contracts could be used to manage asset specificity problems.

The remark made by Ang and Straub also cannot be ignored. Assessing the weaker role of transaction costs compared to production costs, they note that one possibility may be that ‘‘. . .managerial decisions are far from the normative ideal represented in theory. . .’ The analysis of the subset of companies having outsourcing levels above median would tend to support this possibility. These suggested a more efficient management. While it is doubtful that inefficiencies alone could explain the reverse and significant effect of asset specificity, they cannot be discarded. However, the results obtained from the expert judges would tend to refute the possibility that managers were inefficient.

Finally, another explanation might be the character of the IS operations, which were probably not specific.

Maybe their variance is not sufficient to lead to significant differences.

## 6. Conclusion

The objective of the study was to test an explanatory model of IT outsourcing behavior. Based on transaction costs and incomplete contract theories, the model embedded hypotheses that constitute the foundations of these theoretical domains. The results obtained support, atleast in part, the transaction costs model.

From the results, it is clear that uncertainty and measurement problems play a role in the IT outsourcing decision. Firms outsource more readily activities having low uncertainty. These results support the transaction cost hypothesis. The market is seen as less efficient to ensure the execution of transactions prone to high uncertainty or severe measurement problems. The internal governance is a more efficient alternative.

The presence of technical skills was positively related to the outsourcing decision. It supports the idea from incomplete contract theory stating that activities should be under the control of the party making the critical investment for the transaction. However, the business skills did not seem to play a significant role in the decision to outsource. It may be attributed to the rather low level of business skills required to perform IT operations.

Asset specificity showed conflicting results. The first survey provided a puzzling link between asset specificity and outsourcing, suggesting that more specific assets lead to more outsourcing. This contradicted the transaction cost hypothesis. Subsequent data sources, both from general respondents and experts, provided opposite results.

For managers, outsourcing should be regularly reassessed as the most appropriate governance mode for their firm’s IS activities. Managers should be aware of the characteristics of their outsourcing portfolio. Uncertainty and measurement problems may prevent contracts with enforceable performance clauses. Asset specificity may lead to costly lock-in situations. Managers should evaluate the skills required to perform the activities. The activities involving a technical skills probably need more efficiently performed by outsourcers. The ones requiring business skills should be kept in-house.
