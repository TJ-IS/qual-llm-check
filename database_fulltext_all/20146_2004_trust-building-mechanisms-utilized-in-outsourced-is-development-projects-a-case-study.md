# Trust-building mechanisms utilized in outsourced IS development projects: a case study

Maria Cristina Lander<sup>a</sup>, Russell L. Purvis Gordon E. McCray<sup>c,1</sup>, William Leigh<sup>d,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Communications & High Technology, Accenture, Reston, VA, USA

<sup>b</sup>Department of Management, College of Business Administration and Public Affairs,

Clemson University, 123D Sirrine Hall, Clemson, SC 29634-1305, USA <sup>c</sup>The Wayne Calloway School of Business and Accountancy, Wake Forest University, Winston-Salem, NC 27109, USA

<sup>d</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, College of Business Administration, University of Central Florida, Orlando, FL 32816-1400, USA

## Abstract

This article describes the trust-building mechanisms used between top-level managers, project team members, users, and outsourcers over the course of a project offering outsourced information systems development (OISD). Previous studies of trust were used to identify potential trust-building mechanisms for participants in OISD projects over three potential stages of trust building: deterrence-based trust, knowledge-based trust and identification-based trust. A case study approach was then used to explore the development of trust for individuals involved in an Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) implementation for a US\$ 2.5 billion international manufacturing company. The results indicate that the various stakeholders did not encounter previously identified stages of building trust, but significant differences were found in the use of trust-building mechanisms among top-level managers, project team members, users, and outsourcers. Implications for scholars and practitioners are discussed. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Published by Elsevier B.V.

Keywords: Trust; Outsourcing information systems development; Enterprise resource planning

## 1. Introduction

Previous research has identified trust amongst stakeholders as a key success factor in outsourced information systems development (OISD) projects [21]. Trust contributes to the ability of team members to work collaboratively [4], and, by direct extension, improves the ability of the firm to adapt to complexity and change [25,31]. Trust also mitigates perceptions of opportunistic behavior between the outsourcing vendor and the client firm, thereby easing the transfer of knowledge and resources in the partnership [19,21]. Ultimately these dividends of trust may precipitate enhanced OISD project performance by increasing the quality of output, increasing efficiency by lessening the need for structural controls, providing a more flexible environment in which decisions and work are performed, and heightened strategic focus [21,28].

Despite the growth of OISD projects and the recognized role of trust in effective project execution, there nonetheless exists a paucity of research targeted at understanding the trust-building mechanisms employed by various stakeholder groups over time within those projects. The objective of the present research is twofold. First, we investigate whether different methods and strategies are used amongst upper-level managers (IS and non-IS alike), project team members, users and outsourcing vendors involved in OISD projects. Second, we explore whether different methods and strategies are used to build trust at different times or stages over the course of the project.

This research is carried out by identifying the various mechanisms conferred within the literature to build trust in work relationships structured within the staged-framework of trust development [15,28]. The staged-model posits that three levels of trust are linked in a sequential iteration. Development of trust at one level enables the development of trust at the next higher level; a violation of trust can also reduce the level of trust, and therefore also change the mechanisms used to develop trust [15,28].

In the following sections we consider the staged model of trust building and the mechanisms that can be used to build trust within these stages. We then discuss the methods and specifics of the case that was evaluated. Finally, we assess the results of the study and potential ramifications for practitioners and researchers alike.

## 2. Background

The notion of trust generally is associated with one party having confidence in another and this often implies some degree of alignment between relevant value systems [2,4]. Even though the very act of deliberately trying to create trust can lead to mistrust, results from previous studies indicate that certain social mechanisms can be used to create a context where trustworthiness might be perceived [1,2]. Indeed, certain social mechanisms can be used to create environments in which trust can gain a foothold and flourish [1,2]. Trust-building mechanisms can be employed to provide individuals with information necessary to allow them to determine when trust in another party is warranted [16]. Further, information gained from various trust-building mechanisms can lead to better decisions regarding the use of structural controls in relationships.

## 2.1. Stages of trust development

It has been theorized that trust within the business context exists at three levels [15,28]. The lowest form of trust, termed deterrence-based trust, exists when both parties can be trusted to keep their word. The primary motivation in keeping one’s word at this level is that deterrents exist (for example, discontinuing the relationship or likelihood of retribution) that outweighs the benefits of acting in a manner that abuses trust. The second level of trust, knowledge-based trust, is based on the predictability of the other party developed through knowing the other sufficiently well that their behavior is predictable [28]. Knowledge-based trust relies on information rather than fear of punishment or rewards of being trustworthy. The final and highest order of trust is identification-based trust. This level of trust is developed when one party has ‘‘fully internalized the other’s preferences ([28], p. 371).’’ Trust exists because the parties effectively understand and appreciate the other’s wants, and this mutual understanding is developed to the point that each can effectively act for the other.

These three levels of trust are believed to be linked in sequential iteration such that one level enables the development of trust at the next higher level, as the relationship evolves and matures [15]. Although no specifics are given as to when such an episodic shift will occur, the movement from one stage to another stage may require a fundamental shift in the dominant perceptual paradigm [15]. The decline of trust is also theorized within this stage model. If a party violates the norms and mechanisms of a particular stage, the trust between the parties could decline to the extent that a lower stage or level of trust is warranted. This three-stage model assumes that the parties are entering into a new relationship with no prior history to overcome, feel vulnerable if disclosure comes too quickly, and are uncertain about the future of the relationship.

Certain trust-building mechanisms are considered to be more appropriate and therefore likely to be used during certain stages within the trust-building model. Shapiro et al. [28] assert that repeated, concurrent interactions, multifaceted relationships and reputation are useful strategies in building deterrence-based trust. They further suggest that regular communication and courtship (i.e. good research before a relationship is engaged) are important mechanisms within the knowledge-based phase of trust development. Finally, they argue that joint products and goals, a common name, proximity and shared values are more appropriate developing identification-based trust.

While this provides a rich set of trust building mechanisms with insights into how and when they are employed, it is limited, and does not tap into the rich body of research offered within other contexts (for example, trust amongst coworkers, between subordinates and supervisors, within teams and so forth). These studies potentially provide additional mechanisms and rich insights into the development of trust in OISD environments.

## 2.2. Development and placement of trust-building mechanisms within the phases

Many trust-building mechanisms have a high degree of interrelatedness as described within the literature, making it difficult to fully distinguish one method from another. In addition, because many of these mechanisms have not been placed within the aforementioned stages of trust development, a dilemma arose in deciding into which stage of trust development a particular mechanism best fit. The process of differentiating mechanisms and placing those mechanisms within the phases of trust development involved a three-phase process.

Phase 1 involved identifying the various mechanisms within the literature. The literature review was conducted to identify trust-building mechanisms described in extant research; the organizational or experimental context within which this research was conducted was no0t considered. Some of these mechanisms might be of little relevance but all were identified and described in Phase 1 to avoid eliminating potential mechanisms a priori.

In Phase 2, guided by arguments and themes offered within the literature, redundancies were eliminated and similar mechanisms were grouped into a smaller, more workable yet rich set of overarching constructs. Two of the authors each performed this independently. Comparing results, one deviation arose, consisting of one researcher seeing an overlap between two constructs and the other researcher viewing the constructs as separate. A conservative approach was taken and the construct was left as two separate constructs. Presented in Table 1 is an overview of these mechanisms that offer potential application in OISD environments. Each mechanism is discussed in detail directly.

Phase 3 consisted of placing the list of trust-building mechanisms into the phases of trust development. Like the second phase, the effort was guided by previous research on the staged model of developing trust. As before, two researchers independently arranged the mechanisms into the three stages. One difference was encountered when the lists were compared; one researcher viewed ‘‘initial interactions’’ within the first stage while the other placed the mechanism into the second stage because it included using ‘‘reputation’’ as a subconstruct. The difference was discussed, and the conclusion was to place ‘‘initial interactions’’ in the first phase as deemed most appropriate within the literature bases and the other subconstructs within the mechanism. As a measure of inter-rater agreement, the coefficient Kappa was calculated ([3], p. 496). A Kappa of 0.93 was obtained indicating that the strength of agreement between the two researchers was substantial. The mechanisms within each phase are shown in Table 2.

## 3. Case study

Because the goal of the present research was to understand the complex development of trust, a qualitative research method was employed. Such an approach preserves and collects information on the subtle nuances that can be lost in quantitative analysis. The suitability of the case study method in information systems research has been well noted when the focus is on organizational behavior rather than on technical issues [27].

Following the guidelines of Glaser and Strauss [8], Eisenhardt [6], Yin [30], and Hunt and Ropo [12], standard practices for qualitative data analysis were employed. Rather than apply Glaser’s [9] approach to grounded theory in which the researcher engages the subject without pre-existing ideas or frameworks, the approach of Strauss and Corbin [29] was adopted, thereby allowing the use of existing theory to guide the research. The research reported on herein reflects a single in-depth case study.

## Initial interactions

Because OISD projects can involve many new relationships, building trust effectively and quickly amongst project participants can be an important determinant of project success [31]. This initial building of trust can involve three trust-building mechanisms: (1) consideration of reputation, (2) developing familiarity amongst stakeholders well prior to project initiation, and (3) using early team building efforts.

Within the context of OISD projects, reputations typically derive from past behavior as determined by second-hand information gathered prior to contact between the client and outsourcing vendor [2,4,17]. Because reputation impacts the attractiveness of entering into the exchange between client and outsourcer, it is considered to have economic value [6]. Recognizing this, actors in the OISD agreement may seek to manage their reputations (for example, by seeking both positive and negative feedback in the spirit of continuous improvement). To the extent that actors recognize the economic value of reputation in a trust–reliant relationship, efforts to manage reputation reflect efforts to manage trust and distrust [16]

Trust often is enhanced by repeated positive interactions between actors [20]. Efforts to increase the frequency and richness of interaction between client and outsourcer early in the OISD project, then, may precipitate higher levels of initial trust [20]. Widely used in IS organizations, team building exercises represent one potent mechanism for facilitating this interaction [21].

## Integrity

Beginning during the initial stages of newly formed relationships, individuals make efforts to determine the trustworthiness of other individuals in that relationship [17]. Before trust can develop, individuals must have strong confidence in one another’s values and integrity [13,16]. Previous research has identified two mechanisms that positively impact trust [10,11]: (1) being forthright and truthful in interactions with others, and (2) fulfilling promises. When consistently practiced, these behaviors provide team members with direct evidence of trustworthiness and go toward the development of a reputation for honesty and truthfulness.

## Predictability

Outsourcing projects are famous for their unpredictable costs and schedules. Indeed, this uncertainty surrounding OISD projects has, in recent years, driven many outsourcing contract schedules from a once standard ten years to as little as one year [26]. Although many of the factors that drive this unpredictability are systemic (e.g. rapidly evolving technological markets, scope creep, and poorly defined requirements), others are linked to individual attributes. While project cost and duration often is highly dynamic in OISD settings, several mechanisms have been identified that appear to mitigate these individual contributors to uncertainty in OISD environments [4]: (1) individual consistency, (2) individual dependability, (3) holding individuals accountable, and (4) appropriate use of motivators.

Trustworthiness, though difficult to prove, can be suggested via individual consistency of word and deed, as well as through dependability [2]. Further, holding individuals accountable has been used successfully to maintain focus on intended outcomes as team members work collaboratively [11]. Finally, the utility of leveraging proven motivators such as rewards (monetary and otherwise) and punishments is well documented, as its positive downstream impact on predictability and trust [21].

## Communication

The trust literature is replete with studies focusing upon the role of communication in the development of trust between parties. Within the context of OISD projects, the importance of communication is expected to be magnified since the client and outsourcing vendor are separated by organizational boundaries, cultures and policies. Trust borne of communication in OISD settings should improve the ability of actors from both organizations to work toward shared goals. Furthermore, this same trust may lead to improved efficiency in knowledge transfer mechanisms aimed at the bi-directional sharing of relevant knowledge [19].

Researchers have identified at least eight distinct communication-oriented trust-building mechanisms [14], including: the encouragement of communication, the sharing of relevant information and knowledge, the provision of timely feedback, the creation of a common language, the creation of a shared vision, the offering of explanations for decisions, the creation of an open communications environment, and the presence of receptive actors. To the extent these mechanisms are enacted between outsourcing vendor and client, heightened trust is expected.

## Sharing control

Individual perceptions of control or influence of decisions are associated with perceptions of the ability to protect one’s own interests [24]. The sharing of control also is associated positively with the perceived fairness of decisions [14]. The sharing of control within OISD projects would appear particularly important since these projects often have far reaching implications for business processes and employees associated with them.

It has been shown that as team leaders entrust group members with responsibility and authority, group members’ feelings of trust in the team leader increase [4,14]. The sharing of control has the additional benefit of yielding more frequent interactions and communication within the team, leading to a further heightening of trust [19]. These findings suggest two mechanisms that may increase the sharing of control: the delegation of obligations and responsibilities across team members, and the sharing and delegating of control across team members.

It is, of course, a maxim of effective management that these two mechanisms be used in concert; the assignment of responsibility absent requisite control or authority bodes poorly for team member success.

## Concern for others

Beyond their direct impact on business processes, strategies, and the like, the decisions made by upper-level managers are important communicators of the concern held for the well-being of other organizational members. Within the context of OISD projects, demonstrating concern for others may prove critical. Previous research has shown that trust in CEOs and other senior management usually is based less on direct observation than on the efficiency and perceived fairness of organization-wide systems created by individuals in these positions. For OISD projects, trust in senior business unit managers is expected to develop similarly since the contact between senior management and individual project team members often is minimal, both within and across the organizations involved in the OISD arrangement [4,5]. Noteworthy, too, is the finding that individuals are, in fact, more concerned with the fairness of decision making processes than with the fairness of the outcomes of those decisions [14].

More overt expressions of concern for individual OISD team members also may be employed. In addition to demonstrating fair decision making processes, three such mechanisms have demonstrated efficacy [24]: (1) displaying overt respect for team members, (2) when appropriate, quickly and genuinely apologizing for unpleasant events or consequences, and (3) demonstrating concern for specific stakeholder interests. Collectively, these mechanisms may lead to increased levels of trust as they show consideration of and sensitivity to the needs and interests of individual team members.

## Joint identification

While the notion of communication suggests the exchange of information potentially over significant distances, group interaction herein implies rich close interaction amongst individuals, most likely in face-to-face settings. Within the context of OISD projects, group interaction may only be possible via co-location. While co-location may place burdens upon actors and carry with it significant financial costs, extant research indicates strongly that face-to-face interaction is critical in the development of trust because it reduces the risk of misinterpreted actions on the part of individuals and provides an efficient environment in which to move beyond superficial information exchange toward more substantive levels of mutual understanding [17,22]

Further, physical contact increases favorable perceptions of others and leads to an increase in-group cohesiveness [13,17]. This suggests, then, cooperative acts beget further cooperative acts. Five trust-building mechanisms are believed to generate this positive feedback loop [13,17]: (1) co-location of group members, (2) the ready availability of group members to participate in activities, (3) the degree of attachment to the group, structural and psychological, (4) meaningful participation in group activities, and (5) the frequency and duration of group interactions.

In effect, then, trust in OISD environments may be enhanced via close interactions amongst project actors as those interactions engender shared vision and meaning and foster cohesiveness within the interorganizational OISD team.

## Commitment

Commitment generally is viewed as a strong indicator of the intent of an individual to continue participation in an organization or team [4]. To the extent trust amongst participants is strengthened by a sense of shared commitment, the development or preservation of individual commitment is important to OISD project success. Extant research indicates that commitment is instrumental in ensuring that team members accept decisions and work cooperatively in carrying out those decisions [14]. In collaborative environments, individual actors must trust that colleagues possess a shared commitment. Factors are associated with individual commitment include: (1) individual loyalty, (2) individual job satisfaction, and (3) a focus on the long-term interests of individual participants.

Loyalty has been described as the willingness of an individual to support, protect and encourage others [10]. Leader-member theory holds that this willingness is an essential element in the development of high quality exchanges between team members [5]. Job satisfaction, too, contributes positively to commitment, and offers the ancillary benefit of contributing to the trust placed in upper-level managers [4]. Finally, a demonstrated interest in the long-term interests of relevant stakeholders has been shown to contribute positively to trust in vendor–client relationships [1,21]. This finding is expected to reflect the relationship between outsourcing vendor and client in an OISD setting; to the extent the long-term benefits of the contractual relationship are articulated for both parties, trust should be bolstered between outsourcer and client.

The manufacturing industry was chosen because of the intense competition over the last decade; gains in efficiency have become essential to the long-term viability of companies within this sector. This particular international manufacturing firm, headquartered in the southeastern United States with sales of US\$ 2.5 billion, was selected because of the importance of the successful implementation of the ERP system as well as the near limitless access offered to the researchers by the firm.

In the interest of protecting the identity of the firm, it is referred to herein as Alpha. Alpha employs 18,000 people at more than 300 locations on five continents. The study focuses upon the enterprise resource planning (ERP) implementation project conducted within a division of Alpha. The division is relatively new, consisting of three plants acquired from as many companies. Since each plant employs a different IT infrastructure, the implementation of the ERP system was intended to provide a common system across the three plants. The principal goals for the ERP implementation, reflective of most such projects, were to:

(1) relieve the plants of the burden of systems administration by passing it on to the IS department at Alpha headquarters, (2) create an environment of information integration, data accuracy and availability, and understandable systems aligned with business needs, (3) integrate the shop floor with the ERP system, and (4) produce management reports reflective of the specific needs of the various businesses.

Alpha ensured that users understood the benefits and ramifications of the system by conducting challenge sessions in which user groups critiqued system assumptions and design, providing change management workshops for the main stakeholders of the system, and training on the system for all users of the system. A regular newsletter also was distributed to project stakeholders.

Outsourcer. Outsourcer selection was based upon prior experience with similar projects in the same industry in which Alpha operated.

There were four primary stakeholder groups in the project: (1) upper-level managers, (2) project team members, (3) users, and (4) employees of the Outsourcer. Upper-level managers included the vice president of IS and the division vice president. Project team members were fully allocated to the project. These members, although they possessed no experience with ERP implementations, were viewed as the most capable IS professionals employed by Alpha. A user was considered to be any Alpha employee needing information provided via the ERP system.

Implementation of the system at the three targeted plants proceeded sequentially in a phased implementation. For the majority of the project, project team members, the project leader and the outsourcer collocated at temporary facilities established at the plant currently experiencing the deployment. From its initiation, high levels of communication amongst all stakeholders characterized the project.

Several approaches were taken to gather data regarding the deployment of ERP technology at Alpha. Memos, reports, presentations and internal correspondence were content analyzed to clarify the process through which Alpha had progressed from initial consideration of ERP to actual deployment of the technology at the first plant. This data was augmented with extensive stakeholder interviews and surveys aimed at uncovering the trust-building mechanisms employed by stakeholders. Interviews were semi-structured and made strong use of openended questions targeting stakeholder development of trust with other project participants. Stakeholders were then asked to complete surveys that employed a seven point Likert scale to determine the influence of various mechanisms on the development of trust for the respondent. Results from all data sources were aggregated for each stakeholder group.

All study participants were stakeholders at the first plant at which ERP technology was deployed. Alpha management and the research team reviewed the stakeholders for appropriateness for inclusion in the study, iteratively refining the list of participants. Ultimately, seventeen project participants were solicited to participate in the study: four upper-level managers, four project team members, five users and four members of the outsourcer’s contingent. These individuals were intensely with the OISD project. For various reasons, one each of the upper-level managers, project team members and outsourcer participants was unable to take part in the study, resulting in a total of fourteen participants. Of the participants, ten were male, four were female; three of the participants had no IS implementation experience, four had less than ten years of experience while six had greater than ten years of experience.

Using the mechanisms described in Table 2 as our coding scheme, each of the first two authors coded all the taped interviews in the sample independently. As a measure of inter-rater agreement, the coefficient Kappa was calculated [3]. A Kappa of 0.91 was obtained indicating that the strength of agreement between the two raters was substantial. Where there were disagreements, we reviewed and discussed the interview in order to reach a consensus on the most appropriate code.

## 4. Findings and discussion

Analysis of data revealed that while all of the mechanisms were utilized by the various stakeholders, differences were found in the use of trustbuilding mechanisms by upper-level management, project team members, users and outsourcers. Further, while some trust-building mechanisms find favor across all four stakeholder groups, other mechanisms are heavily used only by selected groups (see Tables 3 and 4). A discussion of each mechanism and the extent to which each of the trust-building mechanisms is used by each of the stakeholder groups, together with analyses of the rationale behind the use of the mechanisms, is provided below. While these mechanisms are discussed independently, it should be noted that the mechanisms most likely interact to build a cumulative effect for enabling or disabling trust development.

## 4.1.2. Communication

The importance of communication as a trust-building mechanism had the highest overall rating as the most important of the ten mechanisms examined. Study participants identified communication as a trust-building mechanism more frequently than any other mechanism. Specific stakeholder group perspectives show general agreement:

\- Upper-level management: Senior managers repeatedly emphasized the benefits of ‘‘listening sessions’’ with users at multiple points within the project life cycle.

\- Project team members: While project team members report several aspects of communication are important to the establishment of trust (e.g. the provision of timely feedback, the sharing of knowledge, and the creation of a shared vision), communication generally is believed to be more a requirement for effective project work than an effective trustbuilding mechanism. Possessing no prior experience with ERP projects, other trust-building mechanisms may be more crucial to the establishment of trust for this internal IS group, as is discussed directly.

## 4.1.3. Joint identification

The second most important determinant of trust, joint identification ranked highly amongst all stakeholders except upper-level managers. Both interview and survey results indicated a strong belief in the critical role played by group interactions and group identification in the development and maintenance of trust amongst stakeholders. Specific observations among stakeholder groups include:

\- Upper-level management: While expressing less enthusiasm than other stakeholder groups for joint identification as a trust-building mechanism, upperlevel managers nonetheless report group interaction, a major component within this mechanism as very important between users, project team members and outsourcers. Upper-level managers may place less emphasis on group interaction relative to their own roles owing to the ‘‘directive’’ nature of those roles in hierarchical settings such as that within Alpha. More likely, however, is that upper-level management exclusion from the colocation teams prevented their observation and experience of the value of face-to-face interactions in the OISD setting.

\- Project team members: Long familiar with team environments, project team members note specifically the importance of group interactions. Again, however, project team members view group interaction as something of a standard approach to achieving quality results rather than an explicit trust-building mechanism. As with communication, group interaction may somewhat be taken for granted by these stakeholders.

\- Users: Among all stakeholder groups, users express the strongest support of group interaction as a trustbuilding mechanism. Too often disenfranchised in project settings, users report group interactions afford them a sense of belonging to an overall team effort that is cognizant and supportive of their specific needs and concerns.

\- Outsourcers: Outsourcers are particularly concerned with engaging in group interactions with upper level managers, reporting that only via such interaction can they build a sense of trust in organizational commitment to the project. Notably, upper-level management does not share this felt need, highlighting a potential disconnect between management and outsourcers in OISD settings.

Ultimately, all stakeholder groups acknowledged the importance of face-to-face interaction as a trustbuilding mechanism. As noted earlier, both Alpha and outsourcer stakeholders (except upper-level management) co-located for the ERP implementation project in an explicit effort to create joint identification and shared commitment. It is perhaps not surprising, then, that group interaction amongst stakeholders whom, in many cases, are working together for the first time, was valued highly. Further, despite the personal inconveniences of co-location, the practice was strongly supported by all stakeholders except a single project team member who took exception to its high personal costs.

## 4.1.4. Sharing of control

Ranked eighth among trust-building mechanisms, the sharing of control was not viewed as a critical determinant in the creation of trust amongst OISD stakeholders. The principal reason for this low ranking is not because the participants felt that sharing control was unimportant. In fact, the majority of interviewees agreed that the sharing of control is necessary to a successful implementation effort:

\- Upper-level management: Upper-level managers report the sharing of control is important for meeting project milestones, but plays little role in their development of trust.

\- Project team members: Team members note the importance of shared control as an enabler to the transfer of mission-critical knowledge between internal IS staff and the outsourcing vendor. Shared control implies the need for the coordination of effort, which further implies shared knowledge and understanding.

\- Users: Because they are delegated less work and have less to delegate than other stakeholder groups, users have a lesser need to share control across those stakeholder groups. As a result, the sharing control is not a critical factor in the evolution of relationships between users and other stakeholders.

\- Outsourcers: Similar to upper-level managers, members of the outsourcing team view the sharing of control as an operational necessity.

In the end, almost all stakeholders explicitly described the sharing of control as necessary to project completion but relatively unimportant in the creation of trust amongst stakeholders. In fact, several respondents noted that too much sharing of control can lead to group disintegration as individual responsibility becomes diluted.

## 4.1.5. Concern for others

Aside from a relatively weak overall importance rating (seventh among the 10 mechanisms examined), exhibiting concern for others also was viewed in widely differing ways across stakeholder groups:

\- Upper-level management: Upper-level managers point to the importance of change management sessions as opportunities to demonstrate concerns for other participants via listening, explaining and engaging in overt self-evaluations. These same managers note the value of admitting mistakes and being generous with apologies when appropriate. These characteristic behaviors are believed to contribute a sense of fairness to the project environment.

\- Project team members: Project team members express strong concern for overall project success, even at personal expense and team harmony. They note all stakeholders will view few decisions as fair.

\- Users: While users acknowledge the attention paid by upper-level managers and project team members to user interests, they report only relatively weak concern for these gestures. These same users, however, acknowledge they may have been far more focused upon expressed concern for others had it, in fact, been absent. The fact that users report so little concern for this mechanism may reflect the successful focus on expressing concern undertaken by the other stakeholder groups.

\- Outsourcers: With regard to this trust-building mechanism, the outsourcers reported sentiments toward this mechanism nearly identical to upperlevel management.

While the various stakeholder groups expressed disagreement as to the importance of showing concern for others, they unanimously agreed that demonstrating mutual respect was critical to project success, particularly when navigating equivocal or contentious issues.

## 4.1.6. Integrity

Like showing concern for others, possessing perceived integrity was of significantly varied importance to the various stakeholder groups. Interestingly, project team members and outsourcers were diametrically opposed with respect to their ranking of integrity as an important determinant of trust: project team members ranked integrity first while outsourcers ranked it eighth:

\- Upper-level management: Upper-level managers report that following through on promises is crucial to the development of trust.

\- Project team members: Like upper-level managers, project team members report that following through on promises is crucial to the development of trust. These team members also underscore the importance of upper-level managers being particularly diligent in their fulfillment of promises and commitments, underscoring not only the criticality of upper-management support but also hinting at the negative outcomes previously experienced when that support faltered.

\- Users: Failure on the part of any other stakeholder group to deliver on promises has an admitted effect of reducing the motivation of users. Users are staunch in their observation that commitment to the development, deployment and use of the system wanes in such environments.

\- Outsourcers: The outsourcers view the fulfillment of promises more as a pragmatic operational matter than as a behavioral concern. The fulfillment of promises, from the perspective of the outsourcers, is important primarily as a contributor to bringing the project in on time and within budget. The trustbuilding relevance is less emphasized.

A notable characteristic of Alpha was its strong emphasis on promise fulfillment as a key component of its corporate culture. The outsourcing vendor did not share this emphasis, perhaps accounting for the distinctly different attitudes exposed in the study.

## 4.1.7. Predictability

Within the context of the OISD project at Alpha, several objectives were created against which individual and team performance was assessed: project timelines, weekly task lists, role definitions, etc. Overall, predictability received a particularly high ranking as a trust-building mechanism. Only users regarded predictability as relatively unimportant in the creation of trust:

\- Upper-level management: Upper-level managers were particularly concerned with the organizational-level impacts of the project and view predictability as a key determinant to insuring intended outcomes.

\- Project team members: Concerned with project duration, cost, scope and system functionality, project team members rely on predictability to achieve interim and final project targets. Achieving these team-based goals begets trust amongst the project actors.

\- Users: Users acknowledge the likely importance of predictability, but see little impact in their organizational roles.

\- Outsourcers: Lacking deep knowledge of the subtleties of the Alpha organizational context, the components of which have the potential to derail a project, members of the outsourcing group rely upon a predictable project environment in which to contribute their unique skills and knowledge and, in the end, to garner favorable feedback from Alpha. By achieving the goals with which they are associated, the members of the outsourcing group gain the favor—and trust—of their Alpha partners.

Users consistently commented on the high reliability of both project team members and outsourcing personnel. However, because users are not evaluated against project success or failure, their definitions of project success are necessarily somewhat different and their trust is enmeshed with the ultimate impact of the new system on their roles and responsibilities rather than with the predictability of the project as it unfolds. This may account for the weak ranking of predictability given by users.

## 4.1.8. Commitment

Commitment ranked lowest overall among all trustbuilding mechanisms. Curiously, outsourcing personnel attach little importance to commitment.

\- Upper-level management: Upper-level managers find commitment only somewhat important in the formation of trust.

\- Project team members: Similarly, project team members report relatively little emphasis on commitment as a trust-building mechanism.

\- Users: While not placing very strong emphasis on commitment, users nonetheless report that loyalty to the project is an important determinant in their trust of all other stakeholders. It is mentioned that even the departure of a project team member has a distinct demoralizing effect on user trust.

\- Outsourcers: The outsourcers note explicitly that commitment is not necessary to the establishment or maintenance of trust. This is in direct conflict with the trust literature, but may be explained by the outsourcer’s reliance on formal contracts to specify rules of engagement.

Not surprisingly, all stakeholders agreed that loyalty and commitment was more important for stakeholders within Alpha than for outsourcing personnel. Internal stakeholders consistently reported that their long-term interest in Alpha motivated their personal investment in the project—a sentiment not shared by the outsourcers.

## 4.1.9. Potential for success

Tied in overall importance with stakeholder integrity, this trust-building mechanism exhibited the lowest variance of all the mechanisms in the study. Not surprisingly, all stakeholder groups felt having capable individuals assigned to the project was an important success factor. At the same time, however, most agreed that a positive attitude and a willingness and ability to learn could, to some extent, mitigate some degree of weakness in initial capability.

\- Upper-level management: Upper-level managers consistently comment that every OISD project team appointment is critical across all stakeholder groups. This may, of course, be driven by the nature of the project: an ERP deployment many examples of which have faltered at other companies in the past. Exceptionally strong staffing increases the likelihood of project success and, by extension, generates a positive project environment in which trust can develop, ceteris paribus.

\- Project team members: Project team members report that their trust in other team members and stakeholders is tied strongly to their perceived competence of those individuals.

\- Users: Similarly, users are concerned about the trustworthiness of other stakeholders who are seeking to impact the work processes in which they are personally invested. These users make a determination as to the qualifications and competence of these other actors and thereupon establish a level of trust.

\- Outsourcers: Members of the outsourcing contingent note that Alpha members must believe in the competence and criticality of any external knowledge resources in order for those resources to be efficacious in OISD projects, but that this confidence is, to some extent, already implied by the presence of the contractual relationship.

All stakeholder groups noted the importance of reaching project milestones for creating momentum and trusting relationships early in the project.

## 4.1.10. Managerial decisions

Within the context of the OISD project at Alpha, managerial decisions were focused on (1) staffing and training issues, (2) change management strategies, (3) vendor selection, and (4) contract negotiation. Stakeholder groups varied significantly in the importance placed upon the impact of these decisions upon the formation of trust:

\- Upper-level management: Upper-level managers believe that their close scrutiny of outsourcing vendors conveys to other stakeholders their level of commitment to the OISD project and contributes to higher levels of trust. Further, the resources they allocate to change management sessions and training are thought to have a similar effect.

\- Project team members: Project team members use managerial decisions as a proxy for uppermanagement support of their project-based initiatives.

\- Users: Users note the positive impact of the change management sessions and report that these indicate the grasp by upper-level management and project team members of the challenges inherent in the adoption of complex technologies. These users report that, as a result, they have trust in these other stakeholder groups. Users gave this mechanism their highest rating, underscoring the importance not only of these decisions, but also of their visibility to users.

\- Outsourcers: The majority of the managerial decisions associated with the OISD project at Alpha were made prior to the outsourcing contingent joining the project effort. Consequently, the effects of these decisions are less visible to the outsourcing stakeholder group, perhaps accounting for the lower ranking of this mechanism. Further, managerial decisions have a decidedly less direct impact on the Outsourcer once it and Alpha have entered into a contractual relationship.

All stakeholder groups acknowledged the important role of managerial decisions in assuring project success. But all stakeholder groups except users also noted that most of these decisions are made early in the life of the project and that memory of them— although not their impact—is soon diminished.

## 4.2. Mechanism use within the stages

Table 5 provides salient free responses given by the various stakeholder groups. While the first question validated the importance of developing trust on the project, the other questions offer insights into whether different mechanisms were used within different stages of the project. We did not detect different stages within the OISD project as theorized by the phased model of trust development. Several points should be noted that corroborated this finding. First, the respondents did not identify specific stages of trust development within the OISD project within any of the interviews conducted. Second, while many of the stakeholders viewed trust as fairly constant or fluidly changing metaphorically like a ‘‘sine wave’’ over the course of the project, distinct downward movements within phases were not mentioned as offered by the staged model of trust development. Third, while deterrence-based trust mechanisms, the lowest and first level of trust development within the phased model of trust development were noted as being used by the various team members, mechanisms from the later stages of knowledge-based trust and identification-based trust were also mentioned as being used initially within the project. Finally, there was little difference found between mechanisms used at the beginning of the project and those mentioned to be used over the course of the project.

While it could be argued that not finding phases within the trust development process is due in large part to a small sample, other possible explanations should be considered. First, it should be noted that while several articles have theorized about the development of trust over phases, this model has not been proven empirically. Secondly, while the normative model offers an interesting and parsimonious view of trust development, it could be inefficient to explain how trust is developed within complex environments such as that found surrounding large-scale OISD projects.

Another alternative is that it has been argued that development and maintenance of trust within temporary groups, or ‘‘swift trust’’, has unique properties and is quite different than trust experienced in more conventional forms of organization. Temporary groups, such as those working on OISD projects, are a set of diversely skilled people working together on a complex task over a limited period of time. Temporary systems have several characteristics that are potentially important in developing trust, including: (1) participants with diverse skills are often assembled to enact expertise they already possess, (2) participants have limited or no history of working together, and limited prospects of working together again in the future, (3) participants often are part of limited labor pools within overlapping networks, (4) tasks are often complex, involve interdependent work, and have a deadline, (5) assigned tasks are sequential, nonroutine and not well understood, and (6) continuous interrelating of team members is required to produce an outcome [18].

## 5. Conclusion

As evidenced in a large and substantial base of extant literature focused upon trust, together with the findings gleaned from the OISD project reported upon herein, trust amongst stakeholders is critical to the success of OISD projects. Creating trust amongst these participants in OISD projects is difficult, however, because individuals involved in these projects often have little or no prior experiences working with the other stakeholders involved with the project, yet must rely on their expertise and judgement for a successful project. Thus, arises the challenge of identifying strategies for developing trust in these intense project-oriented contexts.

The extant trust literature provides insight into the utility of a variety of mechanisms in building trust between individuals. This body of literature does little to inform organizations about the appropriateness of these mechanisms in complex interorganizational contexts such as those implied by OISD environments. The in-depth case study reported on herein sought to explore the perceived importance of generally accepted trust-building mechanisms in an OISD setting. Differences surfaced in the perceived importance of the mechanisms across stakeholder groups.

The data collected from the interviews and surveys of top-level managers, project team members, users, and outsourcers involved in an ERP implementation at Alpha provides potentially useful insight into the mechanisms for developing trust valued most by these participant groups. While some mechanisms were viewed similarly, other mechanisms such as communication, predictability, managerial decisions, integrity, concern for others, and commitment were perceived in significantly different ways across stakeholder groups, indicating substantial differences in their mechanisms used to build trust within the four groups. The findings also shed light upon the relative importance of the trust-building mechanisms across stakeholder groups.

Other insights potentially useful to practitioners can be gleaned from an examination of the data collected from the various stakeholder groups. The slight emphasis placed on mechanisms that can build initial trust was a surprising finding, for example. Team building activities are widely used in information systems organizations, if not within the context of OISD projects. Would not OISD project team members benefit from socialization activities at the outset of the project? To the extent that such team building activities have proven efficacious in IS development environments historically, their use in OISD settings would seem reasonable; their absence may represent a missed opportunity. Also noteworthy is the finding that there is a potential disconnect between senior management and outsourcing vendors with regard to the importance each places upon face-to-face interaction: While outsourcing vendors value such interaction highly, it is discounted by senior management. Inasmuch as this interaction begets trust, members of senior management may want to consider engaging in this rich form of interaction with the outsourcing vendor in the interest of strengthening the relationship, despite their own perception that such interaction is not important. If the service provider—the outsourcing vendor, in this case—will become more invested in the project as a result of face-to-face interaction, then it seems prudent to invest some measure of organizational resources in that activity. Relatedly, senior management and project team members report that the fulfillment of promises is crucial to the development of trust, yet the outsourcing vendor does not share this view. For the outsourcer, fulfillment of promises is an artifact of a contractual relationship. It would seem, then, that there exists significant potential for client and service provider to each value a trusting relationship, yet operate on differing—and conflicting—assumptions regarding what signals trust-building behavior. Each will do well to appreciate what the other values in the trust-building process and manage project-related actions such that what is intended is perceived.

For researchers, there are exciting new avenues to pursue in our endeavor to understand how trust develops. This case study offers potentially a new perspective on developing trust within temporary groups. Instead of developing over a series of phases, time constraints require using mechanisms in a parallel or simultaneous manner. In this fashion, mechanisms are being used much earlier than previously theorized in the trust development process. Additionally, it raises questions into the validity of the phased model of trust development, particularly in developing ‘‘swift trust’ within temporary groups. Empirical research within these areas would offer additional insights into these areas.

A common limitation of any qualitative research is the relatively small sample size. However, stakeholder groups across OISD projects typically are of similar composition. This similarity between the roles of participant groups in this OISD project and others suggests the insights gleaned from the present study are applicable to other OISD projects ceteris paribus. Possessing a better understanding of the development of trust among participants in OISD projects may assist managers in creating an environment that allows each participant group to use trust-building mechanisms most important to them. In addition, time spent on activities that do not develop trust for those participants can be rethought.

While a single case study was deemed an appropriate strategy for the present study, future research involving a larger sample representing many different OISD projects would be beneficial. Study of projects within differing organizational contexts would also prove useful to understand the anticipated influence [18]. Further, it would be interesting to compare successful OISD project with unsuccessful projects to consider the ramifications of trust. The understanding of trust in OISD projects gained from this study could be used as a foundation for conducting these future studies involving more individuals working on different projects in different organizations.
