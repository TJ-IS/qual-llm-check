# Perceptions about the quality of web sites: a survey amongst students at Northeastern University and Erasmus University

Jos van Iwaarden<sup>a,1</sup>, Ton van der Wiele<sup>a,\*</sup>, Leslie Ball<sup>b,2</sup>, Robert Millen<sup>b,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Erasmus University Rotterdam (EUR), Room H15-06, P.O. Box 1738, 3000 DR Rotterdam, The Netherlands <sup>b</sup>Northeastern University (NEU), Boston, USA

Accepted 12 October 2003

Available online 29 December 2003

## Abstract

A survey was undertaken to identify the quality aspects perceived to be most important in the design and use of web sites. The questionnaire utilized was based on preliminary research by Cox and Dale who had previously developed a model for assessing the quality of web sites. The results were compared to the SERVQUAL dimensions as developed by Zeithaml, Parasuraman, and Berry for bricks-and-mortar service environments. Our results indicated that the quality dimensions found applicable in the service sector are also applicable to web sites.

No major differences have been found between the attitudes of students at Erasmus University Rotterdam (EUR) and those at Northeastern University (NEU), Boston on web quality aspects. Minor differences can be explained by age, gender and field of study. Overall, NEU students make more use of the Internet than EUR students. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Web site quality; SERVQUAL; E-business; Survey research

## 1. Introduction

In the early 1990s three performance characteristics of ICT (communication speed, memory capacity, and computer speed) were rapidly improving as, indeed, they had over the previous thirty years. However, their combined S-curve growths suddenly enabled one of the greatest technology revolutions—the Internet. Originally designed as an information conduit, entrepreneurs soon saw its great power as a selling channel that enabled browsing, selecting, and buying without leaving the comfort of the home. Businesses realized that they could interact directly with others over the Internet also. Additionally, government agencies began delivering services online, collecting payments for licenses and taxes, providing information, etc.

The history of this explosion is well documented [24,30,31,41]. Since 1997, computer use has grown at a rate of 5.3% on an annualized basis. Internet use has grown at a rate of 20% per year since 1998, and, in the 13 months prior to September 2001, a survey by the US Department of Commerce found that over 26 million more Americans went online.

Thousands of companies were formed to sell goods and services over the Internet during this period, creating the ‘‘New Economy.’’ Subsequently, many of those companies are now gone bankrupt [4]. However, there is still a huge growth of trade over the Internet. Traditional companies of the ‘‘Old Economy’’ turn out to be the engine behind e-business. These companies are procuring and selling more and more products and services over the Internet. Trade is expected to grow from US\$ 43 billion in 1998 to US\$ 7 trillion in 2004 [17,21,40]. Indeed, the growth in ebusiness is tremendous. Despite this growth, surveys suggest that a large proportion of users have only taken the first steps in e-business adoption [8].

But the value of the Internet goes beyond adding another selling channel. Researchers at McKinsey and Company report that the retention of customers online is easier than in traditional bricks-and-mortar companies and that the online company spends three to five times less to retain them. Companies that retain customers exhibit traits of reliable basic operational execution. Their sites download quickly; they respond to customer queries quickly; they deliver more than 95% of their orders on time; and they make it easy for customers to return or exchange purchases. One company raised its on-time delivery rates from 60 to 90%, and cut customer churn in half [2].

The Internet also can play a pivotal role in enhancing brand-name relationships and corporate reputations. Nike, Disney, Coca-Cola, and Toyota are all well-established brands well known by their products. Branding is equally a critical component of the design of web sites: building a corporate reputation to create relationships with customers [7]. ‘‘Branding is redefined online,’’ said Caroline Riby, vice presidentmedia director at Saatchi & Saatchi Rowland. ‘‘We are moving beyond representing a brand to experiencing it.’’ The web site must capture the attention of those people who know nothing or very little about the company but are interested in it. It must also build awareness of what the company does within the context of the industry in which it is competing.

Early web sites were developed by large corporations that required the corporate logo and color scheme, attaching to the corporate databases, and complying with several other requirements. This translated to high cost and significant development time. Others (those created for ‘‘Mom and Pop’’ operations and early entrepreneurial operations) were built over the weekend, did not connect to large databases, and had no standards. While the company was in operation fast, the sites were often unattractive and difficult to use. Is it surprising, therefore, that the companies that are most successful selling over the Internet today are the former and not the later? Certainly, we can find examples of new-starts that have succeeded, but they have usually adapted to the model of a corporation that requires high standards.

Just as in the bricks-and-mortar world, companies have to offer excellent service on the web. Sites have become very important to companies as more products and services are being bought either over the Internet or by making use of the Internet before purchasing in a bricks-and-mortar store. Therefore, companies need to have web sites that live up to customers’ expectations. Huizingh [23] studied the quality of web sites from a company point of view. Liu and Arnett [26] took the same view and also involved the web masters of companies in their study. The purpose of our work is to provide empirical evidence on the factors that contribute to the quality of web sites from a customer or user point of view. Our most important questions are:

\- How do customers distinguish a good web site from a bad one?

\- What factors determine the quality of a web site? And because we make use of student samples at NEU and EUR, another research question is:

\- Do NEU and EUR students have different perceptions about the quality of web sites?

## 2. Customer satisfaction

Because a web site is part of the connection between a company and its customers, it is evident that it should reflect the quality efforts that are in place throughout the company. Companies therefore wish to offer quality interfaces to their customers [14].

Besides this, there is another reason why a company should provide high quality web sites: there is no human contact offered through a web site; the interaction is through technology. Although companies may try to emulate human behavior with technology, the interaction is different because some aspects of human interaction cannot be replaced with technology; e.g. courtesy, friendliness, helpfulness, care, commitment, flexibility, and cleanliness [9]. The absence of these aspects has to be replaced by better performance or excellent performance on ‘new’ specific web factors.

A key aspect is the way a customer can attain satisfaction or dissatisfaction with a company’s service. If a company wants to satisfy its customers the first question it needs to answer is: what is it that satisfies customers and what makes customers dissatisfied with the company, its products, and services? Satisfying customers depends on the balance between customers’ expectations and experience with the products and service. When a company is able to lift a customer’s experience to a level that exceeds expectations, then that customer will be satisfied.

Because customers have ever increasing expectations, it is necessary for companies to improve their quality continuously. The issue is: what should be improved to keep the customers satisfied? What customers experience is not just one simple aspect of the company but a whole range of factors. Some aspects are concerned with the way customers experience the company itself, some are concerned with the way customers experience the physical product, and some are concerned with the way customers experience the service that the company offers.

Comparing customers’ expectations and their perceptions of actual performance can be done by making use of the SERVQUAL scale of Berry, Parasuraman, and Zeithaml. This was developed for the service sector. It has five generic dimensions or factors:

\- Tangibles: physical facilities, equipment and appearance of personnel.

\- Reliability: ability to perform the promised service dependably and accurately.

\- Responsiveness: willingness to help customers and provide prompt service.

\- Assurance (including competence, courtesy, credibility and security): i.e., knowledge and courtesy of employees and their ability to inspire trust and confidence.

\- Empathy (including access, communication, and understanding the customer): caring and individualized attention that the firm provides its customers.

In the SERVQUAL instrument, 22 statements measure the performance across these five dimensions. For each statement the expectation and the experience of a customer is determined. There has been some criticism of the long-term stability of the results of the

SERVQUAL scale [25] and on the general applicability of the five dimensions (e.g. [6,12]). Although alternative models have been proposed, e.g. SERV-PERF [11], the scale has been widely used by academics and practitioners to measure service quality. Therefore, this is the model we use in our paper.

## 3. SERVQUAL dimensions in relation to e-business

## 3.1. Tangibles

Examples of the tangibles factor are has up-to-date equipment, physical facilities are visually appealing, and materials are visually appealing. These might be even more important in e-business, as there is no faceto-face contact between a customer and employee. The visual aspects (i.e., the web site) are the only contact between them. Therefore, the need to have well functioning, good-looking web sites is paramount. Many customers abandon their shopping carts on the Internet because they get frustrated with the technology or the design and lay out of the web site [20].

The visual aspects are also judged differently by people of different age. While young people may be attracted by flashy graphics, sounds, and a high-speed interface, older people do not want blinking texts that are hard to read or animations that distract them from the use of the web site [22]. Although a number of web sites offer users the opportunity to customize it to their needs, this process is mostly aimed at the content of the web site and not at the graphics, animations, and sounds.

## 3.2. Reliability

Some reliability aspects have to do with doing what is promised and doing it at the promised time. Although many organizations seem to think that the major reason why customers shop via the Internet is because of low prices, this is not always the case. Some organizations found out that there are also customers shopping via the Internet because of convenience [35]. If customers cannot trust an organization to do what they ask, they will be dissatisfied. Priceline, for example, ran into problems at the end of 2000 because of its focus on lowest prices. People could buy a plane ticket at a discount but because of inconvenient flying times there was a risk for customers. This resulted in dissatisfaction and customers went to the convenience offered by a competitor.

## 3.3. Responsiveness

One of the responsiveness factors is gives prompt service. The time to download a web page appears to be of great importance to most users of the Internet. Research in 1999 found that fewer than 10% of users leave a web site if page response is under seven seconds. However, when the time rises above eight seconds, 30% leave and when delays exceed twelve seconds a staggering 70% exit the web site. It can be assumed today that people expect sites to be even quicker, because of technological advances. Thus, it is very important to have a web site that is quick but on the other hand users expect web sites to be visually appealing. As the number and size of animations, pictures, and sounds increase to make a web page more appealing, the time it takes to download that web page will increase and this can be judged negatively by users. Hence, there is a trade-off between the looks of a web site and its speed. This trade-off is complicated by companies’ demand that their web sites convey the corporate image [28].

## 3.4. Assurance

One need of the assurance factor is knowledge to answer questions. Customers expect to find everything they want on a web site. In a bricks-and-mortar store people feel comfortable with limited inventory but Internet users are not satisfied if they cannot find everything they want. Web shops need to have complete inventory and rich and relevant product information [16].

Two other aspects are employees can be trusted and feel safe in your transactions with employees. Firstly, there is a need for trust: users have to share personal information with an organization they do not know. Research on this topic [39] shows that at least 50% of the Internet users are very concerned about: misuse of credit card information they must give; selling or sharing of personal information by site owners; and cookies that track customers’ activity.

Secondly, the same research shows that two-thirds of active web users typically abandon a site that requests personal information and one in five has entered false information to gain access. Aspects in the assurance factor that could be very important in ebusiness are [15]:

\- Availability of a formal privacy and confidentiality policy on the web site.

\- Secured access that customers are prompted to acknowledge.

\- General reputation of the supplier.

\- Certification or guarantee of assurance.

\- Reports of the experience of other customers.

The first of these is acknowledged by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). The Code of Practice for Information Security Management (ISO/IEC 17799:2000) provides a basis for establishing and maintaining the handling of sensitive data.

Organizations are trying to obtain certification by an objective, consensus-based standard, just as they did earlier with quality management standards.

## 3.5. Empathy

Several aspects of empathy are missing on web sites. Because there is no human interaction, web sites normally do not offer personal attention; a number of web sites have therefore implemented a design that can be personalized by their users. This kind of web site design is aimed at giving users personal attention, assuming that the more a web site is tailored to needs, the more likely that the customer will return [18]. The most advanced sites try to create a face-to-virtual-face interaction. A virtual assistant is supposed to make customers feel more comfortable. With the use of artificial intelligence, this assistant can suggest products or services that might be of interest based on previous purchases and on reactions to questions. The purpose of all these technological gadgets is to add warmth.

## 4. Research methodology

The research used a questionnaire survey. This approach provides a relatively easy means to study the perceptions and opinions of a large group of people in a limited time frame and at low costs. The survey was undertaken with the student populations at Northeastern University (NEU), Boston, USA and the Rotterdam School of Economics at Erasmus University Rotterdam (EUR), The Netherlands. Students were expected to be familiar with e-business and the Internet. They received an e-mail containing a hyperlink to the questionnaire allowing them to respond electronically.

The purpose of the survey was to develop empirical evidence on the quality factors important to people who are familiar with and frequent users of the Inter net. The survey was intended to determine:

\- Personal information (gender, age, academic discipline).

\- Respondents’ use of the Internet (equipment, frequency of use).

\- Specific web sites visited (a predefined list of 20 categories of web sites).

\- Aspects of web quality (a predefined list of 50 aspects).

The core of the questionnaire was this list of aspects. For every one we asked the respondent to indicate its importance and for their satisfaction with it. This method was derived from the SERVQUAL scale (expectations versus experience). The list of aspects was according to the model developed by Cox and Dale:

\- clarity of purpose;

\- design;

\- communication;

\- reliability;

\- service and frequently asked questions;

\- accessibility and speed;

\- product or service choice;

\- order confirmation;

\- product purchase;

\- user recognition;

\- extra service;

\- frequent buyer incentives.

For each of these, a number of aspects were defined. The list was ranked according to that of Cox and Dale.

The questionnaire was discussed with experts in the field of quality management and a pilot was then conducted using a few students, leading to an improved questionnaire.

## 5. Survey results

## 5.1. Sample and response rate

At Northeastern University all students who had a university e-mail account (approximately 6000) were sent an e-mail discussing the research and the questionnaire. At Erasmus University all students of the Rotterdam School of Economics were sent a similar email (approximately 5000). Responses were received from 293 NEU students and 248 EUR students. Such response rates were low (approximately 5%) but acceptable for this type of research.

## 5.2. Descriptive statistics

In Table 2 and Fig. 1, the response is shown in terms of gender and age, respectively. A comparison of the NEU response sample with the total population at NEU showed that the response group was representative of the total population. This was also true of the response group at the Rotterdam School of Economics. The NEU respondents were enrolled across many academic disciplines and apparently at the undergraduate level. The EUR respondents were all from the field of economics (social economics, business and management, econometrics) and most were at the graduate level.

The 10 aspects that were perceived as most important seemed to relate to accessibility and to reliability. Customers that buy a product want web sites and their owners to be trustworthy. E-commerce web sites need to be fast, simple, and available. Customers want a clear overview and confirmation of what they bought and what they have to pay. The 10 most important aspects relate to usability (e.g., accessibility; colors and images; navigation bar) and information exchange (e.g., overview of the order;

payment options; privacy policy available; self explanatory forms for personal details). Here again, there were no major differences between NEU and EUR students, seven out of 10 aspects are the same. However, NEU students are more satisfied than EUR students.

The gap was widest for five out of the 10 most important quality aspects. The ones with the largest gaps were related to either speed or e-commerce issues. In general there are no aspects with a positive delta, meaning that for every aspect the experience was less than expected.

## 5.4. Quality dimensions of web sites

The empirical data on the importance of each of the 50 aspects of web quality was used to check for the existence of specific quality factors in the quality of web sites. A five-factor confirmative factor analysis (SPSS 10.0, Varimax Rotation, Kaiser Normalization, KMO ¼ 0:91) showed a good fit with the five SERVQUAL dimensions. These factors all have Eigenvalues greater than 1 and together explain 44% of the variance. Factor analyses with six and seven factors do not provide evidence for the existence of any other underlying factor. These six and seven factor solutions showed a break down of one or two of the SERVQUAL dimensions into two sub-factors with the same content. ## 6. Discussion

Internet users are found world wide today. One has to suspect that there are differences between United States students and those elsewhere. Language, culture, religion, and other factors may be important to a user’s impression of the web site. Our findings so far do not support this. In fact, our findings show that there are no major significant differences in the quality perceptions of web sites between NEU and EUR students. This agrees with results of Robbins and Stylianou [36]. The absence of major differences might, however, be due to the fact that the USA and The Netherlands are both Western countries with similar cultures. Moreover, the respondents were all academic students of comparable age. There were, however, minor differences:

\- NEU students made more frequent use of the Internet, and spent more time on it, both per visit and as per web site.

\- NEU students were more satisfied with their equipment and Internet connection.

\- NEU students were more satisfied with aspects of web site quality.

\- EUR students more frequently visited university, company information, and stock exchange information sites. NEU students more frequently visited web shops: books, music and movie stores, and second hand products. These differences might be related to their academic discipline differences.

## 7. Conclusion

Selection by students from a predefined list of web quality aspects led to the following as most important: fast access, easy navigation on the web site, presentation of a complete overview of the order before final purchase decision, and a simple registration process. No evidence was found that new dimensions are emerging.

It can be concluded that the quality dimensions developed by Berry, Parasuraman, and Zeithaml for service environments are equally useful in e-business. However, with the number of Internet users now over one billion, our sample is relatively small. Also, one must suspect that there are differences between a sample taken from American and Dutch students and what might be found elsewhere.

Quality is an area of critical importance for commercial companies. Businesses need to understand what attracts people to their web sites, what keeps them there, and what keeps them coming back. They need to understand the differences between the casual buyer and the user who visits their web sites on a daily basis. Web sites for companies like Dell, Cisco, Orbitz, and Covisint do millions of dollars of business each day. They need to understand the factors that keep them growing by understanding what encourages buying and what brings customers back.
