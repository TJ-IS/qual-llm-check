# A reversed context analysis of software piracy issues in Singapore

Trevor T. Moores<sup>a,\*</sup>, Jasbir Dhaliwal<sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management Information Systems, University of Nevada Las Vegas, Las Vegas, NV 89154-6034, USA <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems, Northern Kentucky University, Highland Heights, KY 41009, USA

Received 6 October 2002; received in revised form 1 June 2003; accepted 11 October 2003

Available online 6 March 2004

## Abstract

The level of software piracy and reasons underlying the behavior among students in Singaporewas investigated using the reversed context analysis previously used by Moores and Dhillon in a study of Hong Kong. This technique presented the respondents with a set of context statements that describe the buying and using of pirated software in terms of high availability, high cost, and low censure. The contexts were reversed (low availability, low cost, high censure) in order to determine whether targeting one or more of these reasons would lead the respondents to stop the behavior. The same instrument was used here with a sample of 462 students. The results showed general agreement with those of the Hong Kong study, although the level of pirating behavior was lower, with a weaker switch from agreement to disagreement when the context involved cost. A closer examination of the respondents revealed a set of respondents that frequently bought and used pirated software and seemed resistant to any of the reversed scenarios. This suggests that even in culturally similar markets different approaches may be required to combat software piracy. <sup>#</sup> 2003 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Ethics; Intellectual property rights; Software piracy; Singapore

## 1. Introduction

In a recent study, the reasons underlying software piracy in Hong Kong was investigated by using context statements to frame three particular issues that would support the buying and using of pirated software: its high availability, the relatively high cost of legal software, and the perceived lack of censure for buying or using pirated software [8]. These scenarios were reversed in order to see whether increasing censure, reducing availability, and reducing the relative cost of legal software would cause the respondents to re-evaluate their behavior and stop buying and using pirated software. The results showed that while responses to all three of the reversed context statements showed a significant shift away from intending to pirate software, cost was the prime motivating factor.

The aim of our study is to test the external validity of the Hong Kong study by applying the same instrument in a culturally and economically similar country, that of Singapore. By controlling for culture and economics, the result of reversing the context statements should determine whether the results from

Hong Kong were simply indicative of Hong Kong or are a more general sentiment held by end-users that software is just too expensive and software piracy is a justifiable crime. However, although there was general agreement between the two studies, the habitual software pirate in Singapore seemed to be much more resistant to attempts to stop them buying pirated software than their counterparts in Hong Kong.

## 2. Comparing Singapore and Hong Kong

Singapore and Hong Kong have much in common. Both are major port-cities in South East Asia where Chinese dialects are widely spoken, including Putongua, Cantonese, Hokkien, and Hakka, and both have British colonial heritages from the early-19th Century. Singapore gained self-government from Britain in 1959 and full independence in 1965. Hong Kong was returned to Mainland China in 1997.

Both are also major financial centers and members of the Asian ‘‘Tiger’’ economies that thrust the region to the forefront of developing countries in the 1980s. Like most South East Asian economies, the per capita GDP of Singapore and Hong Kong has fluctuated over the past few years, although both have a relatively high standard of living at approximately US\$ 17,500 per year per capita. Singapore’s population is approximately 3 million, compared to 6.5 million for Hong Kong.

According to Hofstede [5], Singapore and Hong Kong are also similar in terms of their national work values. Singapore and Hong Kong both score relatively high on the Power Distance Index (PDI) scale, relatively low on the Uncertainty Avoidance Index (UAI) and Individualism (IDV) scales, and moderately high on the Masculinity (MAS) scale. This suggests that workers in Singapore and Hong Kong are seen as being quiet, easy-going, and controlled, they value high earnings and advancement over job-security, bosses are respected as benevolent autocrats, and the interests of the in-group outweigh those of the individual.

Singapore and Hong Kong also have similar software piracy rates. The software piracy rate for a country is calculated as part of the Business Software Alliance (BSA) and Software and Information Industry Association’s (SIIA) ongoing battle against software piracy worldwide. By estimating the number of new personal computers sold, the degree of pirated software in use is calculated as the shortfall in expected sales for each new computer. The software piracy rate (SPR) is the percentage of pirated copies in use and the financial loss associated with this rate is the cost of these missing sales.

Hong Kong’s software piracy rate (SPR) in 2001 was 53%, with average annual losses between 1996 and 2001 estimated at US\$ 116.7 million, whilst Singapore’s SPR in 2001 was 51%, with average annual losses estimated at US\$ 53.2 million [11]. This suggests that for both Hong Kong and Singapore more than half the software in use was pirated. The difference in financial losses is due to the size of the software market in Hong Kong, which, as previously stated, is approximately double that of Singapore.

## 3. Software piracy in Singapore

Intellectual property rights in Singapore are protected in law by the 1987 Copyright Act, with Amendments in 1994, 1998, and 1999; these Amendments allow for a fine of up to S\$ 100,000 and 5 years in prison. Software piracy remains a major problem, however. In its annual Special 301 reviews of countries violating intellectual property rights, the International Intellectual Property Alliance (IIPA) noted that in 1999 Singapore lost US\$ 114.6 million dollars in revenue due to piracy, with US\$ 50.3 million in business applications, US\$ 52.3 million in entertainment software, and the rest in motion pictures, sound recordings, and books [1]. The IIPA are particularly critical of Singapore’s ‘‘self-help’’ system, where the IPR owners themselves are required to police for infringements, obtain evidence, and pursue the conviction.

Piracy hotspots include Sim Lim Square, Lucky Plaza, and Far East Plaza, as well as the night markets in the alleys around Arab Street, Bugis Street and the Geylang areas of the city. In response to police raids, counterfeiters have become mobile, appearing in housing estates and intersections and selling from vans. For example, in November 2000 the Singapore Criminal Investigation Board conducted a raid on 15 retail stores and four vans at three night markets operating in the Sembawang, Choa Chu Kang and

On 1 April 2001 the Intellectual Property Office of Singapore (IPOS) was established as part of the Ministry of Law, and charged with formulating and regulating intellectual property laws and education. Recent campaigns in conjunction with the Business Software Alliance (Singapore) included the distribution of 75,000 posters to more than 10,000 businesses, illustrating the problem of software piracy with images, such as a boomerang flying back into the thrower’s face, a man shooting a hole in his foot, and CD-ROMs cascading in domino-style with the message ‘‘Remember, what goes around, comes around’’ [6].

The BSA (Singapore) website http://www.bsa.org/ singapore/) provides ample evidence, however, that the traffic in pirated software continues at a sustained rate. In November 2000, Singapore police seized 15,000 pirated CD-ROMs after a routine lorry inspection at Singapore port, including 10,000 copies of Symantec’s Norton Antivirus 2000 with a street value of S\$ 990,000. In February 2001, eight people were arrested and 60,000 pirated CDs with a street value of S\$ 720,000 were seized. Within days of its release in October 2001, pirated copies of Windows XP Home and Professional Edition were selling in Singapore for between S\$ 5 and S\$ 10 (>US\$ 6) [12].

## 4. Research methodology

The context statements used in this study frame three (positive) issues that might explain the pirating of software: the high availability of pirated software, the relatively high cost of legal software, and a perceived lack of censure involved in buying pirated software. These issues were seen as indicative of the situation in Singapore (and Hong Kong), given the relatively high software piracy rate.

Availability refers to the ease of buying pirated software. The easier it is to come across pirated software, the more likely someone will eventually purchase a copy of it, or see nothing wrong in others doing so. Curiously, it has also been suggested that the availability of pirated software actually benefits software publishers by drawing more people into the market, increasing the user base, and thus ultimately leading to greater profits [3].

Cost refers to the perception that the price of legal software is too high, and that pirated software often becomes the only means someone has of acquiring the software. Differences in piracy rates can be partially explained by differences in economic wealth of countries [4,10].

Censure refers to the likelihood that buying pirated software will result in some punishment. Clearly, a law against software piracy is no deterrent if there is also the perception that it is rarely enforced, and a lack of censure is seen as one reason to continue with the behavior [2,7,9].

Three additional context statements were developed to test whether reversing these issues would have an impact on their intention to pirate software, in other words, if pirated software is no longer easily available, if the cost of legal software is not perceived as being too high, and if there is clear censure for buying pirated software. This reversal would indicate the likely effectiveness of campaigns directed at these issues. The six context statements are summarized in Table 1. The reversed context statements are L-AVAIL, L-COST, and H-CENSURE.

A seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1-‘Strongly Agree’ to 7-‘Strongly Disagree’ was used to measure the response to the context statements, with 4- ‘Neither’ as the midpoint. Behavior was measured by asking: ‘‘On average, how frequently do you buy pirated software?’’ A six-point Likert scale ranging from 1-‘Never’ to 6-‘Every day’ was used. Given the results of the Hong Kong study, it is expected that the positive context statements would have strong agreement (a low score), while the reversed context statements would show a significant shift towards disagreement (a higher score).

## 5. Results

The survey was distributed to undergraduate and postgraduate students in the School of Business, National University of Singapore, with 462 usable responses being obtained. As shown in Fig. 1, 48% (220 out of 462) claimed to buy pirated software on a regular basis: that is, a few times a year or more. Four people suggested they buy pirated software every day. This compares to 81% of the Hong Kong respondents that claimed to buy pirated software on a regular basis.

The results of a one-way ANOVA with scenario as the grouping variable confirms that the six scenarios do indeed capture different contexts associated with buying pirated software $( F [ 5 , 2 7 6 6 ] = 1 6 7 . 5 ,$ $\rho < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . A summary of the responses to the six context statements with a comparison to the results of the Hong Kong study is given in Table 2. As a measure of central tendency, the median was used with the expectation that for scenarios that clearly contribute to software piracy the median score should be less than the midpoint of the seven-point Likert scale (i.e. median < 4), while the reversed scenarios would have a median score of more than four.

As can be seen, the positive context statements do indeed show strong agreement (median < 4), indicating that availability, cost, and a lack of censure were clear motivating factors for software piracy. The results of a Wilcoxon Matched Pairs Test showed that reversing the context results in a significant shift away from agreement $( \rho < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . However, only the L-COST scenario showed the expected shift from agreement to disagreement, although the median score of 4.5 suggested the central tendency was at the borderline between agreement and disagreement. This suggested that campaigns focusing on all three issues are likely to have an impact on software piracy behavior, however, as in the Hong Kong study, cost remained the dominant factor.

## 6. Discussion

Have the results of the Hong Kong study been externally validated by this study? Although the Singapore sample was larger, the overall responses were generally weaker than those found in Hong Kong. This could partly be due to the fact that Singapore had a lower software piracy rate, and overall, a smaller software market. Accordingly, there appeared to be a lower level of software piracy among respondents in this study. An analysis of the responses of the hardcore or habitual software pirates in each of the samples, however, suggests a more surprising reason for the differences.

Forty-two of the Singapore respondents can be described as habitual software pirates, buying at a rate of a few times a month or more. In Hong Kong, 78 of respondents fell into this category. As a result, increasing the sample size in Singapore did little to identify the core reasons for pirating software, since most of the buying was being carried out by a small, but committed, group of respondents. This suggestion was confirmed by the fact that when re-analyzing the data using a Mann–Whitney U-test, there was a significant difference in response to all six of the context statements between the group of 42 habitual software pirates and the rest of the sample $( 0 . 0 0 0 < \rho < 0 . 0 3 6 )$ .

At first glance, this was perhaps to be expected, since this group of 42 respondents were the main perpetrators of software piracy and were likely to differ in their views of the act from the rest of the sample. After all, whatever the deterrent, there will always be people willing to break the law. The surprise came when the Hong Kong data was re-analyzed. Applying the same test to the Hong Kong sample produced the same result as that found in Singapore $( 0 . 0 0 0 < \rho < 0 . 0 1 0 )$ , except for the switched cost scenario L-COST, where there was remarkably little difference between respondents (r ¼ 0:479).

More specifically, for the habitual software pirate in Singapore the median L-COST score is 3, which suggest they would continue to buy pirated software even when the cost of legal software is not too high. This compared to a median score of 4.5 for the same group in Hong Kong. For the rest of the sample that did not buy pirated software on a regular basis, the median L-COST score was the same for both the Hong Kong and Singapore samples (median ¼ 5). What does this all mean?

In short, it means there is a radically different response from the habitual software pirates in terms of the context under which they would be willing to desist from buying pirated software. In this case, the Hong Kong pirates were willing to accept that if the cost of legal software was lower they would be likely to stop buying pirated copies. In Singapore, however, the software pirates were less willing to accept cost as a reason to stop buying. This also suggests that an antipiracy campaign that addresses cost might work in Hong Kong but may have limited success in Singapore.

## 7. Conclusions

Software piracy is a multi-billion dollar industry that deserves our attention not simply from the viewpoint of how information technology is subject to and affects our ethical standards, but also from the more practical viewpoint of the loss of financial reward for those that have developed the software and the subsequent loss of jobs and livelihood that the missing billions might otherwise have provided. Since students are often seen as typical software pirates, it is worth noting the contexts that lead them to buy pirated software.

The advantage of reversing the context is that the significance of a particular issue can be assessed in terms of the reaction of respondents to a new context, perhaps one brought about by an anti-piracy campaign that addresses one or other of the issues defined by the context statement. In other words, what would stop students from continuing to buy pirated software?

The results of our study are broadly in line with the Hong Kong study, although the level of pirating behavior is less than that found in Hong Kong. The majority of the respondents in both studies reacted in similar ways to the six context statements. While all three issues were important, cost seems to remain the dominant factor. For the most prolific software pirates in Singapore, however, little would seem to affect their intent to buy pirated software. We are left with two unappealing conclusions.

First, even though Hong Kong and Singapore may have cultural and economic similarities, those responsible for the majority of the pirating behavior have different attitudes towards the act. This would seem to suggest that before launching an anti-piracy campaign vendor organizations, such as the BSA and SIIA, should first investigate the local issues that drive software piracy. Using the same campaign across even the same region is likely to produce mixed results. This step, however, complicates the standard legislation, enforcement, and education approach currently being taken by the SIIA, adding to the cost of the campaign without necessarily impacting the behavior of the habitual software pirate.

Second, by continuing to identify the high cost of legal software as a justification for pirating software we are faced with the inevitable conclusion that software piracy is not the product of a cultural difference in attitude between East and West towards the act of copying. Rather, it is a rational, economic decision. For the majority of the students in Hong Kong and Singapore, software piracy was not an ethically charged issue of right and wrong but a calculated response aimed at acquiring software that would otherwise be out of their reach. Cost, however, is also the one issue that software vendors are least likely to address in their fight against software piracy.
