# The role of communication values in swift starting action teams: IT insights from flight crew experience

Earl H. McKinney Jr.<sup>a,\*</sup>, James R. Barker<sup>b</sup>, Daryl R. Smith<sup>b</sup>, Kevin J. Davis<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>324 BA, College of Business, Bowling Green State University, Bowling Green, OH 43403, USA

<sup>b</sup>US Air Force Academy, Colorado Springs, CO, USA

Received 14 November 2001; received in revised form 2 January 2003; accepted 13 October 2003

Available online 5 March 2004

## Abstract

We define a new subset of action teams termed swift starting action teams. These high technology groups of professional, well trained strangers perform from the moment they start working and face high stakes from the beginning. For these teams, we present evidence that the expression of communication values precedes effective task communication and team performance. IT professionals increasingly team with other professionals in high technology environments, such as swift starting action teams. Communication and communication values are important to the success of action team interactions common to IT professionals, such as requirements analysis, knowledge discovery interviews, and end user service engagements.

Keywords: Communication; Values; Teams

## 1. Introduction

Information technology is driving many organizations increasingly toward restructuring into decision making teams of highly technical, well trained, professional workers [6,11,56,71]. Facilitating the interaction of these teams has long been a goal of IT [10,62,96,97]. Despite a long term effort, our understanding of such teams is incomplete, particularly in deciding how these teams excel [44,78,86]. As IT continues to increase in sophistication, effective high tech decision making teams will be in ever greater demand [84]. As Jiang et al. [46] asserts: ‘‘We need to gain insight into the factors influencing successful high tech team performance.’’

Many technology intensive (‘‘high tech’’) teams are only together for a short time and must find common ground quickly, performing effectively as they start. However, researchers traditionally have hypothesized that teams take time to develop and become effective [47,79,87]. Our study asked the question: is there a catalyst for team development, enabling teams of strangers in high technology environments to reach high effectiveness quickly?

Clearly communication plays a major role in early team development. Therefore, understanding communication and the antecedents to effective communication may help explain why some teams quickly become highly functional. Recently, the study of antecedents of team communication became a fruitful area of inquiry, and research called for field studies to examine the critical initial stages of team formation [35,51].

Understanding teamwork, especially its initial stages, is essential for IT [53,61]. IT professionals, more than specialists in any other discipline, team as a way of life, constantly working with others to share their technical knowledge. What is important now is to extend the focus from longer term teams to early team development and the short term teams that increasingly occur in IT. Little research has been published on short duration teams, even though time together has been shown to be key variable in team performance [95] and team processes [60].

In general, IT professionals need to understand teamwork and communication to design and participate in short term teams. As designers of computer mediated communication systems, choices made by IT staffs have profound and lasting impacts on individual and organizational communication and performance [9,27,28,54,63]. IT professionals also increasingly operate as members of short term, high tech teams, and team-like collaborations that are not labeled teamwork. Understanding the initial processes that lead some of these teams to develop rapidly may improve team skills. As teamwork becomes the sine qua non of business, teamwork knowledge skills become as valuable to IT professionals as some technical skills [100].

In addition to teamwork, communication skills are essential for IT success. IT professionals depend on communication as they briefly team with others in many short term, team-like roles, such as developing requirements, discovering knowledge, and supporting end users [14]. Understandably, communication is recognized as essential for IT profession [34,38], however, new IT professional’s communication skills are generally inadequate [12,68,88,99] and are seldom included in IT curricula [73]; little communication research has been attempted to improve our understanding of how short term teams develop.

## 2. Teams and communication

We argue that one key antecedent for both team performance and effective communication is the expression of communication values: positive ideals or standards about communication.

## 2.1. Swift starting action teams

A common type of short term, high technology team is an action team [82]; it consists of ‘‘highly skilled specialist teams cooperating in brief performance events that require improvisation in unpredictable circumstances’’ [101]. Examples include short term varieties of sports teams, military combat units, surgery teams, musical ensembles, technology research teams, disaster response teams, crisis action teams, aircraft flight deck crews, law enforcement swat teams, firefighting teams, etc. Individual tasks are specialized; roles are differentiated, coordinated patterns of interdependence are specified; and expertise is distributed across individuals comprising the team.

A subset of these teams involves those comprised of professional strangers: swift starting action teams. Their hallmarks include: (1) they are composed of well trained strangers—professionals with no prior knowledge of others on the team, (2) they perform immediately—as they warm up, and (3) they face high stakes from the beginning. Such teams are receiving increased research attention [23].

The flight deck team is the quintessential swift starting action team. The consequences of not performing well immediately can be calamitous: of 37 recent air carrier mishaps, 73% occurred on the first day the pilot and copilot flew together [66]. Fortunately, the aviation community performs thousands of successful flights under difficult conditions, due, in part, to recent efforts to improve flightdeck communication [41]. As a result, this domain has matured to the point at which reliable communication measures have been developed, and theory has suggested specific team development factors [32]. The airline community has learned much about their teams. They have generalized their insights to a wide range of other action teams and team-like settings [69]. Recently, this research community has extended flightdeck insights to action teams solving organizational problems, emerging management teams, and IT teams that develop and maintain large scale computer programs [4,21].

Flightdeck crews, particularly those crews in our study including pilots, navigators, and other specialists, are similar to many IT team-like settings. Both display the distinguishing characteristics of all action teams: they involve trained specialists, are composed of strangers, conduct brief performance events, perform immediately, require improvising, and face unpredictable circumstances. For example, requirements analysis is a team-like setting that must start well if the project is to be successful [43]; it also requires improvisation, and decisions clearly can lead to unpredictable results.

## 2.2. Communication and performance

Communication can be viewed as a medium, a means to performance ends, or as constitutive, as the end in itself. Within the goal dominated domain of aviation, it is more often defined as task communication, about the goals of the team and organization. Non task communication would include innovation and social maintenance communication [24].

Thus communication is essential to effective team performance [26,37,48]. This study, which focuses on antecedents, treats task communication and performance as separate dependent variables. We examine each in turn (see Fig. 1).

Research intensified as a result of a National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) study examining crew fatigue and communication [25]. In that, tired crew members who had just finished a 3 days flying schedule outperformed their rested cohorts in a series of flight tasks in a simulator. This was attributed to task communication advantages accrued via familiarity. As a result, airline training curricula [70] and a variety of research programs tried to understand why some crews quickly develop effective task communication, while others lag. However, little definitive progress was made in identifying and evaluating antecedents to task communication [50].

With no available progress on antecedents to task communication, we turned to antecedents of action team performance to help depict early team development. One of the important early developments for a team is the emergence of norms, recurrent patterns of behavior, or thinking [74]. These shared norms or standards also regulate behavior and team dynamics [81]. Sawyer and Guinan found norms of loyalty and supportiveness to be essential for software development teams. They play a crucial role in early development for all types of action teams as they begin to develop as early as a team’s first meeting. In fact, communication excerpts taken from less than 1 min from the start of a team’s life illustrate the importance of opening norms in how the team makes sense of its situation and how it will behave [59]. In one study of flightdeck crews, first officers understood the norms imposed by their captains (authoritarian or open to suggestion) within a matter of minutes [30]. McDonough [58] suggested that team leaders can establish norms such as freedom and openness and that these play a strong role in early team development.

In addition, other research has shown that organizational culture [1,36] plays a significant early role [2,90–92]. For IT teams, centralized and mature environments are significant early cultural factors [18]. Culture here is defined as socially derived, taken for granted assumptions about how to act and think [75]. Within aviation, successful aircrews built teamwork and mutual understanding on a framework of organizational culture to avoid starting teamwork with no common way of operating [31]. Further, aircrew members come with a set of expectations that is quickly decided as a result of early events. For example, each airline culture generates a stereotype of a captain that includes such characteristics as: thorough, patient, and consistent. Using this stereotype, first officers begin to know what to expect and what is expected of them. Such cultural stereotyping is also evident in the nuclear submarine environment [3].

Just as norms and culture precede early effective performance, we suggest that expression of communication values precedes early effective team performance. Further, we suggest that expression of communication values is also an antecedent for effective task communication for these teams.

## 2.3. Expression of communication values

We defined communication values as positive ideals or standards about communication. They are standards against which acts and outcomes are judged. Examples include taking turns, questioning, candor, and acknowledging. They are a syntax used to create meaning and understand behavior and apply to all communication acts on the team and help inform members about how communication is to be employed. Once values are expressed, communication processes and structure can develop [65]. In this role, values work in a similar way to norms and organizational culture. All three can influence early communication.

Within the aviation domain, crewmembers express these communication values in verbal statements. For example, inclusiveness is embedded in statements such as ‘‘what do you think?’’, ‘‘what is your opinion?’’, and ‘‘please speak up if you have any discomfort at what I’m doing.’’ Candor is expressed in disclosures about a member’s ability or currency. That is, pilots volunteer information such as: how long it has been since they landed or have flown a particular segment of a flight. Acknowledging is expressed in ‘‘OK’’ and repetitions of messages by the receiver. This serves to close the communication loop by ensuring that both crewmembers are working together [5].

When a crew does not explicitly cultivate the development of communication values, a set of values will emerge from their interactions. Unfortunately, when communication values are allowed to emerge without explicit design, the team’s ability to achieve agreement may be rife with problems. Without first establishing a set of communication values, crewmembers will struggle to interpret each other’s actions and words. Amidst this struggle, values of safety, ego preservation, service, or deference, may compete as development mechanisms. Rather than defaulting to this haphazard approach, effective swift starting action teams seem to use a structured, values based approach.

The aviation community has recognized the particular importance of two communication values: directness and vulnerability. Directness, or assertiveness is the antithesis of vagueness. It is exemplified by ‘‘we are 10 knots slow,’’ as contrasted to ‘‘we’re a little off airspeed.’’ The impact of this communication value was measured in a study of simulated crew preflight briefings [80]. Crews that expressed and reinforced assertiveness in this early setting demonstrated higher subsequent team climate scores [45].

A second communication value is the expression by the captain of vulnerability. Helmreich [40] found this was the leading discriminator between excellent and poor crew performance; ‘‘the best captains expose the value of expecting their first officers to question them.’’ A similar communication value is tolerance for disagreement. In a study of crew effectiveness, Foushee et al. [25] found that better performing crews were those where the pilots are willing to disagree with another pilot. Outside the aviation domain, other studies suggest communication values of trust and honesty as precursors of team performance [57,94]. For teams that are not action teams, other studies have found a positive impact on performance for similar values: acceptance of conflict [13,77] and willingness of team members to question and disagree [42,76].

## 2.4. Hypotheses

In sum, norms and organizational culture have an early impact on action team performance. Here we propose that assertiveness and vulnerability are instances of a larger construct, communication values. Further, we argue that their expression is an antecedent of not only performance but of effective task communication. This leads to the following hypotheses:

H1. Swift starting action teams expressing communication values early demonstrate more effective subsequent task communication

H2. Swift starting action teams expressing communication values early demonstrate more effective subsequent team performance

To test these, we chose a set of military (US Air Force) aircrews. Three of the paper’s authors have military flying experience that made access to crews possible and aided in understanding context, language, and culture. Guzzo and Dickson [39] describe these teams:

Flight crews are in some ways like many other types of groups that make decisions . . . indeed, commercial airline flight crews perhaps most closely resemble project teams or task forces in that they are composed of persons with expertise in a specific area (navigator and captain) and work together for a limited period of time, after which members are reassigned to other flight crews. Because of this reassignment . . . training is not done with intention of strengthening that particular team, but rather with the goal of making the individuals more effective in whatever team/flight crew they find themselves. Flight crews are like other teams: they determine what the situation is, assess available options, and choose among them.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Summary of research design

To evaluate the hypothesis, we observed 19 Air Force crews in-flight. Six ride-along observers scored communication and performance measures and recorded written notes about each of the crews. The profile of all 19 flights was similar in terms of both length and mission. After all the flights were recorded, judges identified written comments that indicated communication values, splitting the set of 19 teams into three categories based on the presence or absence of communication values. Finally, we used an ANOVA to evaluate the hypotheses.

## 3.2. Context

Flight teams are not permanent or fixed. A crew might fly together for 2 or 3 days, but then reform into other crews for a new series of flights; teaming with strangers is their way of life. Formerly, such teams achieved a communication context through hierarchy, with the captain taking the dominant role. This hierarchical model transformed dramatically in recent years. To enhance effectiveness and safety, flight deck crews have become team or crew focused, though the captain continues to be the focal point.

The crews of the KC-135 refueler consist of pilot, copilot, boom operator, and navigator (the KC-135 is the military equivalent of the Boeing 707 aircraft). The boom operator’s task is mostly independent of the rest of the flight deck crew (the ‘‘boomer’’ operates in the rear of the aircraft). The navigator, pilot, and copilot work together and sit in adjoining seats on the flight deck. They infrequently make eye contact. Almost all their verbal communication is accomplished via an intercom.

The priorities of the mission are clear and consistent: safety first, then support of an aerial dogfighting exercise by refueling 20–30 fighter aircraft. Although the priorities and goals are clear to the crew, the tasks are only semistructured. Each flight is unique in several ways—avoiding other aircraft on the ground and in the air, refueling an unknown number of small aircraft with different needs, circumventing weather and winds, and coordinating actions with ground controllers and supervisors. The degree of communication to support these tasks varies from constant discussion to about three or four sentences a minute during critical refueling and landing. The synchronization with other work units contributes to the clarity of the mission. Many of the onboard tasks are highly coordinated. The tasks have similar and familiar work cycles but with unpredictable inputs and some uncertainty.

The pilot and copilot receive similar technical flying and team behavioral training. Each pilot is able to perform all of the duties, although the formal leader and decision maker is clearly the captain. In practice each assumes a portion of the piloting task. Generally, one of the two will handle the flying while the other monitors progress and perform supporting tasks, such as answering radio calls and reading checklists. During refueling, the copilot and pilot continue to specialize. One of the two is responsible for keeping the aircraft in a standard ‘‘racetrack’’ pattern while the other manages fuel transfer and completes refueling forms. The captain will have been promoted to that position after 1–3 years as a copilot. The navigator is primarily responsible for flight navigation and is the crewmember responsible for rendezvousing with the receiver aircraft. The pilot assists in this task. The community is closed, competitive, well educated, and confident. The flightdeck is semi autonomous, guidance and advice can come from both organizational superiors, who are well known to the crew, and mission directors onboard other aircraft, who are strangers. Most feedback is internal, occasionally the crew competes for awards given to crews, not to individuals.

The crewmembers were aware of our study but did not know what specific behaviors were being studied. The 19 crews were observed from their inception as a team until the completion of the flight. To collect the data, the researchers began their observations from one hour prior to departure until one hour after landing. The observers did not know any of the crews.

The crews were from five Air Force organizations, both Active Duty and Reserves. Each flew an identical flight profile, though some differences arose as a result of weather variations and degradation of aircraft systems. Each flight departed from a base near Las Vegas, Nevada, climbed to a rendezvous point (15 min), refueled in a holding pattern (90 min), and then returned to the departure base.

## 3.3. Observers

Six Air Force pilots were trained as observers. They were experienced military pilots who had flown several different military aircraft and, as crewmembers, had worked in hundreds of different crews. The observers were collecting data for another study, so all were blind to the hypotheses. They sat behind the pilot and copilot and did not provide feedback to crews or interact with them in-flight. In this flight community, it is common to have ride-along pilot-passengers in the observation seat.

## 3.4. Participants

The pilots studied ranged in flying hours in the KC-135 from 1200 to 2450 (mean ¼ 1784:6; S:D: ¼ 448);

copilots ranged from 100 to 2400 (mean ¼ 682; S:D: ¼ 625). The aircrews represented a cross section of experience levels and did not include any who were receiving training. Observed crews were assured that individual performance would not be divulged to supervisors. Based on crew self reports, crew members had not flown together before. Consent of each crew was obtained prior to observation, no one refused to participate.

## 3.5. Measures

## 3.5.1. Task communication

The instrument used to gauge task communication proficiency was the line/line oriented simulation checklist (LLC), a widely used research tool in aircrew communication dynamics [98]. There are 40 separate task communication categories. The 40 observation categories align with eight markers, each assessed during five phases of each sortie: pre departure, take off and climb, cruise, refueling, and descent and landing (see Appendix A for descriptions of the eight markers). The LLC required that ride-along observers used a four-point evaluation scale; 1 for poor, 2 minimum expectations, 3 standard, and 4 outstanding. The metric was the percentage of downgrades in the 40 communication markers. A downgrade was a score of 1 or 2 on a communication marker. A maximum of 40 downgrades was possible; actual downgrade scores ranged from 0 (a perfect score) to 13. The LLC downgrade percentage score is shown for each crew in Table 1.

An experienced assessor trained observers on the LLC. More specifically, as a pretest, the observers learned how to make assessments by evaluating videotapes. The expert assessor facilitated discussions with the observers on how the behaviors should be scored after each tape. Then other tapes were scored individually and differences in scoring were discussed. The training tapes were approximately 1.5 h in length and four tapes were used. On the final videotape, a Cronbach alpha [19] reliability coefficient of 0.90 resulted.

For the actual flights, in addition to completing the LLC the ride-along observers were told to make written, qualitative records on noteworthy behavior of the crew. Their procedure was to complete scores on each communication marker then record their written observations, if any, before assessing another item. The written record typically included 25–50 specific observations and ran to several hundred words for each sortie.

## 3.5.2. Performance

Performance was assessed by the observers with a checklist of 25 in-flight tasks. They assessed performance on in-flight checklist procedures by recording accomplishment of 25 procedures (e.g., setting navigation instruments prior to take off, briefing approach plans, checking oxygen systems, etc.). These items are common to all the aircraft flown by the observer-pilots and were not subject to interpretation (like evaluating a car driver’s use of a turn signal or setting a parking brake). For each sortie, each of the 25 separate items was scored as pass/fail. The checklist performance measure was objective, simply the number of procedures successfully completed. Crew scores ranged from 20 to 25.

## 3.5.3. Early expression of values

Three additional pilots were used to evaluate the written observations recorded prior to take off for the 19 flights. The judges read all 97 comments of the ride-along observers that were recorded prior to take off (Table 2 shows a subset). The judges were instructed to identify expressions of communication values (see Appendix B for training instructions). The judges read the instruction sheet, scored several example written observations and then discussed differences. The actual 97 observations were randomized, then each judge scored the observation as either an expression of a communication value or not.

## 4. Results

The judges were able to identify comments as expressions of communication values. All three agreed on 84% of the written observations, generating a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78. When the judges were not in complete agreement, they reached a decision via majority voting. Crews were assigned to three categories: those that expressed no values, that expressed one value, and that expressed values several times. These results are shown in Table 1. LLC downgrades and checklist accomplishment scores are also shown for each team, as well as each team’s performance score.

Overall, the data suggest strong support for both hypotheses—expression of communication values precedes effective teamwork. Swift starting action teams that express communication values demonstrate more effective task communication. LLC mean communication downgrades for teams that failed to express communication values were 0.238; teams with one value downgrades were 0.093; and teams with multiple expressions were 0.024 $( F ( 2 , 1 8 ) = 7 . 4 8 ;$ $P < 0 . 0 0 5 )$ ). Expression of communication values also led to better performance. The means for in-flight checklist accomplishment for no values, a single value, and multiple values were 21.5, 23.3, and 23.9, respectively $( F ( 2 , 1 8 ) = 8 . 1 8 ; P < 0 . 0 0 4 )$

A post hoc analysis was performed on the data using Tukey’s L.S.D. The results showed a statistically significant difference in LLC downgrades $( P = 0 . 0 4 6 )$ and checklist performance $( P = 0 . 0 2 7 )$ between the no values group and the one value group. The test further revealed statistically significant differences between the no values group and the two or more values group for both LLC downgrades $( P = 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and checklist performance (0.001). However, there were no statistically significant differences between the one value and the two or more values groups $( P > 0 . 0 5 )$ . It seems that a team that made any effort to express communication values would reap benefits in task communication and team performance.

Other aviation related factors, such as experience and training, were statistically evaluated to test for alternative explanations. These factors included: pilot and copilot flying hours; recent context experience— the number of similar sorties from Nellis AFB accomplished in the past 90 days; mission complexity; hours of team training on communication, teamwork, and decision making. None of the factors were significantly correlated with either task communication or performance (see Table 3).

The statistical analyses clearly supported our hypotheses that the expression of early communication values subsequently resulted in better task communication and team performance. Apparently just beginning the dialogue on values positively affected the crew. There were no statistically significant differences between the one value and the two or more values group. It is possible that, with a large sample size, such differences would be found.

## 5. Summary

Expression of communication values precedes effective task communication and team performance. The relationship between expression of communication of values and task communication and team performance could not be much stronger: for 17 of the 19 crews, communication of values before take off seemed to predict accurately task communication and performance (crews 5155 and 1123 were the only exceptions). It is not surprising that good teams might express communication ideals, such as acknowledging each other and being assertive. What is somewhat surprising is that these values were completely absent in four of the six worst teams. We further noted that the simple idea of communication values was so salient and clearly expressed that observers noted them, judges clearly identified them, and every effective team took time early on to display them.

## 6. Discussion

The purpose of this investigation was to understand early team development in a high technology, action team domain. For a subset, swift starting action teams, we began probing mechanisms that contributed to effective early development. Our purpose was to use the well understood domain of the flightdeck to pursue an initial investigation of communication values among teams and suggest that they were useful in generalizing to other IT action team settings.

Our study supports work highlighting the importance of early team events [33]. The need to understand the earliest events is vital, but poorly understood [72]. Researchers have repeatedly suggested that early events have a disproportionate and lasting impact on later team performance. Cohen and Bailey [17] called for field studies to examine the conditions that promote effective decisions in groups’ initial stages; Gershick [29] stated that a group’s first meeting would set lasting precedents for how the group the group would act; and Sethi and Nicolson [78] sought early processes that resulted in ‘‘charged’’ or exceptional performance in new product development teams. Our study suggested that communication values can have a synergistic effect that triggers a substantial or qualitative change in the team. Other studies have shown that IT also influences early development. In some action team settings, team members use IT systems to communicate and to share communication values.

Our finding is similar to other work within this domain that suggested these teams rapidly reach effectiveness by leveraging organizational culture and norms that are factors implicitly communicated. What makes values different is that their expression must be explicit and, unlike culture and norms, which are externally imposed, their use is at the discretion of the team. Further, expression of values, occurs after team formation, and thus may play a particularly valuable and robust role, especially on swift starting actions teams with members from different organizations with different cultures and norms.

In addition, our study begs the question: if specific communication values have a reliable impact on performance within a domain, could participants could be trained to identify opportunities to share values, and recognize when an appropriate set of values is in use?

A final implication of our study is the specification of swift starting action teams. IT teams will need to be more responsive as use of knowledge management techniques increases [67]. One of its key aspects is communication of knowledge within a team. Its growth may result in more use of action teams; thus, IT professionals will have increased need for understanding these types of teams, their information needs, and how they communicate.

## 6.1. Caveats

A number of limitations are clear. First there is generalizability. Although five organizations within the Air Force were sampled, all the observations were from a single domain with arguably one distinct organizational culture and ethos. Also the workforce was produced by one training program and the flights and tasks were similar. Obviously IT research in action teams should investigate other domains and employ more controlled, experimental settings. Many IT teams have different structures, environments, risks, and dependence on communication. Significant differences on any of these dimensions would prohibit application of the findings.

Another limitation is the small sample size; another, when judges rate communication they may grant a halo effect to teams with good communication skills, thus artificially raising other performance metrics. However, performance was not a subjective assessment, rather the accomplishment of well defined explicit and objective tasks.

## 6.2. Implications for IT

Our study has several implications for the IT community. As IT systems design affects both teamwork and performance [49,83], understanding how short term, high tech teams operate and develop teamwork is essential to effective system development. From our study it is clear that action teams require systems that can carry or convey communication values. Designers, when possible, should build high fidelity systems capable of conveying these values. Fidelity or media richness is a subject of considerable IT research [15,52]; here we have suggested a new dimension of media richness: the ability of the system to facilitate the establishment and use of communication values.

IT individuals are frequently represented on action teams and in action team-like settings where understanding the impact of communication values could be vital to success. One example is in determining system requirements with users. The social aspects are essential [8,20,22] but not well understood [7,93]. Determining requirements is an action team setting—a brief performance event with other specialists requiring improvisation. Like all action teams, specific tasks are clear, roles are differentiated, and coordination patterns have been established. IT professionals might well employ communication values, such as vulnerability, candor, acknowledging, or self deprecation early in the meeting as a catalyst for rapid development. In addition, IT professionals have many other opportunities such as soliciting knowledge from experts for a knowledge base or serving end user needs. In these settings, communication values such as responsibility taking, trust, and sensitivity to frustration could be used to enhance early communication and performance. While considerable IT research has investigated the role of various systems development tools such as RAD and object oriented methodologies [55,64], the social factors are less frequently studied.

IT is permitting new structures in teamwork in which effective starts are vital and team development time is minimal [16]. Further, new technologies are evolving collaborative organizational models that require a better understanding of how workgroups interact and include virtual teams [85,89]. Today’s technology intensive, swift starting action teams cannot rely on normal time to progress; they must start to work together quickly or risk tragic consequences. The IT community should take a lead role in action team, and action team-like research as new organizational models are built upon IT advances.

## Appendix A. NASA/AMES line/LOS checklist for communication markers

1. Team concept and environment for open communications established and/or maintained, e.g., crewmembers listen with patience, do not interrupt or ‘‘talk over’’, do not rush through the briefing, make eye contact as appropriate.

2. Briefings are operationally thorough, interesting, and address crew coordination and planning for potential problems. Expectations are set for how possible deviations from normal operations are to be handled, e.g., rejected take off, engine failure after lift off; go-around at destination.

3. Cabin crew is included as part of team in briefings, as appropriate, and guidelines are established for coordination between flight deck and cabin. Passengers are briefed and updated as needed, i.e. delays, weather, etc.

4. Group climate is appropriate to operational situations, e.g., presence of social conversation at appropriate times. Crew ensures that nonoperational factors such as social interaction do no interfere with necessary task.

5. Crewmembers ask questions regarding crew actions and decisions, e.g., effective inquiry about uncertainty of clearance limits, clarification of confusing/unclear ATC instructions.

6. Crew members speak up, and state their information with appropriate persistence until there is some clear resolution and decision, e.g., effective advocacy and assertion: ‘‘I’m uncomfortable with . . . , Let’s . . .’’

7. Operational decisions are clearly stated to other crew members and acknowledged and include cabin crew and others when appropriate, e.g., good crosstalk between pilots, everyone on the same.

8. Captain coordinates flightdeck activities to establish proper balance between command authority and crewmember participation, and acts decisively when the situation requires.

## Appendix B. Instructions to judges for expression of communication values

You will be asked to read a series of recorded observations made on board a KC-135. Please select those statements that indicate a communication value.

Communication values are positive ideals or standards about communication. Examples of communication values include taking turns, questioning, candor, and acknowledging. These values are general principles that apply to all communication acts on the team and help inform members about how communication is to be properly employed.

Examples of communication values follow. However, keep in mind that you will not be asked to label specific values correctly, rather you need only to say if a verbal statement is an expression of a communication value.

One example of a communication value is inclusiveness. Inclusiveness is expressed in statements such as ‘‘what do you think’’, ‘‘what is your opinion’’, and ‘‘please speak up if you have any discomfort at what I’m doing.’’ Candor is expressed in disclosures about their own ability or currency. That is, pilots volunteer information such as how long it has been since they landed, or flown a particular segment of a flight. Acknowledging, is expressed in repetitions of messages, or read backs of the message received. Directness, or assertiveness is the antithesis of vagueness. It is expressed in statement like ‘‘we are 10 knots slow,’ contrasted to ‘‘we’re a little off airspeed.’’ Vulnerability is expressed by an aircraft commander’s willingness to have a crew member question them.

To be clear, communication values are not routine communication actions such as discussing a departure routing before take off. These are specific communication events whereas values apply to all communication acts. Further, a communication value requires some minimal reciprocation. If directness is expressed and met with no response or a question is not responded to this fails as an expression of values.
