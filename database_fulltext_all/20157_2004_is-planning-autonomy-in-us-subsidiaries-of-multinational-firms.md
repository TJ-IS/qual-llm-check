# IS planning autonomy in US subsidiaries of multinational firms

Dinesh A. Mirchandani<sup>a,\*</sup>, Albert L. Lederer<sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup>College of Business Administration, University of Missouri–St. Louis, 8001 Natural Bridge Road, St. Louis, MO 63121-4499, USA

<sup>b</sup>425C C.M. Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, USA

Available online 27 Febuary 2004

## Abstract

Research has suggested that information systems planning is an important challenge to managers of multinational firms. Although studies have examined marketing, finance, and manufacturing, limited research has considered IS planning autonomy in such firms. A field survey of 55 US subsidiaries collected data from matched pairs of senior IS and non-IS managers to test hypotheses based on corporate governance theory and agency theory. The former described their planning autonomy and the latter described organizational characteristics. Data analysis revealed that foreign product diversity, extent of local ownership of the subsidiary, intracompany purchases, and age of the subsidiary were related to the subsidiary’s IS planning autonomy. The findings indicated that parent companies treat the IS function differently from other areas of the organization. Future researcher should investigate the proposed explanations of this difference.

Keywords: IS Planning; Multinational firms; Autonomy; Corporate governance theory; Agency theory

## 1. Introduction

Foreign investment in the US represents several billion dollars in capital and thousands of jobs to the economy [1]. However, most of what is known about the management of the subsidiaries of foreignbased firms concerns the administration of international operations from the parent company’s perspective rather than the managerial issues faced by the subsidiaries [2]. Research has suggested that US managers feel that autonomy from the parent company is necessary to build a successful operation in the US [3].

The major advantages that managers cite for more autonomy are the ability to react more quickly to changes in the American marketplace and to attract as well as retain US managers accustomed to greater autonomy. US managers of multinational firms typically have high autonomy for day-to-day operations but their strategic planning and long-term capital investment decisions tend to be controlled by corporate headquarters [3]. For instance, in Volkswagen’s Westmoreland assembly plant in Pennsylvania, managers had considerable autonomy for operational decisions but not for strategic planning and product innovation. Volkswagen’s failure to localize its global strategy and innovate new products for the US market was a major factor that led to the closing of the plant [4].

On the other hand, parent companies have found that if planning is delegated to the subsidiaries, it leads to divergence from the central model, inflexibility, and higher costs [5]. In fact, international business researchers have asserted that ‘‘headquarters must attempt to increase control over foreign subsidiaries in order to reduce the uncertainty of their investment, since such control ensures that the behaviors originating in separate parts of the organization are compatible and support common goals’’ ([6], p. 542).

Prior research has examined manufacturing, marketing and finance in multinational firms [7] but has neglected IS [8]. IS may or may not be managed similarly to these other functional areas [9]. However, a matter of growing concern is that practitioner literature is often replete with horror stories of mismanaged IS strategies in multinational firms which have led to declines in stock prices and market capitalizations for major firms [10]. In fact, a key strategic issue faced by managers of multinational firms is the decision to centralize or distribute the processing of information [5,11–14]. Therefore, an examination of the IS planning autonomy of subsidiaries may shed light on parent-subsidiary relationships in multinational firms to help IS managers better perform their responsibilities and plan their strategies.

Management scholars have identified some variables related to the organizational design of multinational firms. They have studied these variables in the context of a parent company’s decision to give a foreign manufacturing subsidiary more or less autonomy [7,15]. The purpose of the current study was to extend the prior research by testing hypotheses with variables that may predict subsidiary autonomy for IS planning. Though these variables may be outside the control of the subsidiary, this study can help subsidiary managers understand what challenges they face, and thus enable them to better present their case for more autonomy or to manage with lower autonomy. The study can also help parent managers understand the subsidiary. Since the prior studies examined manufacturing subsidiaries, the current study also looks at a sample of such subsidiaries. Further, because this study transcends national boundaries and reflects the impacts of a regional environment on IS, it supplements a body of literature that is increasingly important to researchers and practitioners attempting to understand information management within multinational firms [16].

## 2. Theoretical background

Corporate governance theory can be used to understand the autonomy of the subsidiaries of multinational firms [17–21]. A multinational’s ability to formulate and implement global strategies is influenced and constrained by existing asset configurations, its historical definition of management responsibilities, and ingrained organizational norms. These factors constitute a company’s administrative heritage or corporate governance system [22].

Corporate governance systems thus deal with the organization of ownership, management, and control of a corporation. In some corporations, ownership is dispersed among many stakeholders whereas in others, ownership is concentrated in the hands of a few stakeholders [23].

In multinational firms, the parent can be considered to be the owner of the subsidiary, and its power to exert control over a subsidiary may vary with its level of ownership [24,25]. The parent may be a single owner of the subsidiary or a majority stockholder, in which cases it might have the greatest level of power over the subsidiary. However, the parent’s power might be lower if the subsidiary’s stock is widely dispersed with no dominant owner [6].

The power to control a subsidiary can be equated with the power to make strategic decisions for it [18]. It can thus be expected that based on the corporate governance systems existing in the parent firms (i.e., the owners), the autonomy provided to their subsidiaries for strategic planning will vary [26,27]. The choice of the corporate governance system is critical because only when the proper corporate governance is exercised in multinational firms can strategies be formulated and implemented which best serve the interests of all stakeholders [22].

Agency theory can be useful in explaining the degree of management control exerted via parent corporate governance systems on foreign subsidiaries [6,28]. Among the fundamental assumptions underlying the theory are that a principal and agent have goal differences, and observing all of the agent’s actions is difficult or costly for the principal [29]. Thus, the parent firm, as the principal, cannot relinquish all decision rights to its subsidiaries because the local interests of the subsidiaries may conflict with those of the parent. At the same time, it cannot effectively and efficiently make all the decisions for its subsidiaries because it lacks their unique knowledge [30]. In fact, the use of subsidiary physical, technological, knowledge, financial, and human resources often is better determined by subsidiary management because it can better identify the particular resources needed and their appropriate deployment [31]. However, ‘‘according to agency theory, principals will generally attempt to control their agents in order to minimize the costs of the agency relationship to them’ ([6], p. 544), thereby making entrepreneurial subsidiary initiatives unlikely [32].

Agency costs are created from the conflict of interests between the principal and the agent. These costs can be defined as the sum of monitoring costs, bonding costs, and residual loss [33]. Monitoring costs refer to the principal’s expenditures to obtain information to limit the agent’s activities that diverge from the parent’s model. Bonding costs are incurred for resources the agent commits to guarantee that it will not take actions that could harm the principal, or to ensure that the principal will be compensated if the agent does take such actions. The residual loss is the reduction in welfare experienced by the principal due to decisions of the agent that would benefit it more than the principal [34].

In multinational firms, such issues as language differences, varying legal and accounting systems, and capital and labor market imperfections can lead to higher agency costs. Any subsidiary decision undertaken to promote that subsidiary’s interests at the expense of the parent’s interests can also increase agency costs. For example, a subsidiary manager’s selection of low quality personnel for the unit based on the manager’s personal relationship with these individuals might do so [6]. Reducing agency costs is thus important to these firms [35,36].

In organizational contexts, as senior executives uncertainty with respect to the IS function increases, they delegate more decision rights to the IS manager, and use IS planning as the basis to reduce agency costs [37]. Because the executives of the parent are typically unaware of (and thus uncertain about) the unique environment of subsidiaries, they may choose to delegate more planning decisions to subsidiary IS managers, and use those plans as a basis for monitoring the manager [31]. At the same time, agents such as subsidiary IS managers generally desire greater autonomy to avoid agency costs and be more effective [38]. In fact, the influence of organizational characteristics on agency costs [39] justifies the study of such characteristics in multinational firms.

## 3. Research model

Prior research on multinational firms has studied the relationship between several key organizational variables and the decision-making autonomy of subsidiaries for the functional areas of marketing, manufacturing, and finance [40–42]. Such research has yielded conflicting results making the variables interesting. On that basis, both Garnier [15] and Gates and Egelhoff [7] investigated them as a group.

Some of these variables from those studies exist at the level of the subsidiary and pertain to its relationship with the parent, other subsidiaries, and its demographic characteristics. They were selected for the current study because its focus was on subsidiaries, and respondent subsidiary managers are more likely to be knowledgeable about their own subsidiary than the parent firm and other subsidiaries. Table 1 includes the variables and their measures as derived from the prior research. Fig. 1 shows the variables in the context of the research model with IS planning autonomy as the dependent variable.

IS planning represents an example of decision making that a parent may or may not delegate to a subsidiary. It is a complex and challenging process for senior managers and thus is of considerable interest to IS researchers and practitioners [43–46]. The core of the IS planning system is the planning process, which assesses the external IS environment and the internal requirements, identifies new system applications, and develops a set of strategic IS plans to support the business plans of the organization. The primary objective of IS planning is to align IS plans with the business plans [46].

The IS planning process can be divided into phases that are well accepted in strategic business planning [47,48]. Such phases are strategic awareness, situational analysis, strategy conception, strategy formulation, and strategy implementation planning.

## 4. Hypotheses

## 4.1. Foreign product diversity

Foreign product diversity refers to the number of broad product lines the multinational company offers for sale in the subsidiary’s country. Greater foreign product diversity will be more difficult for the parent to control than standardized product lines [15] because of the larger agency costs associated with managing both heterogeneous products [53] and greater foreign market exposure [36]. In order for a subsidiary to have the IS it needs to support its product diversity and remain innovative, the parent would delegate it more IS planning autonomy.

H1. Foreign product diversity in multinational firms is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.2. Product modification differences

More product modification differences at the subsidiary level raise the level of internal complexity in the organization as differentiation and specialization will tend to occur in such subsidiaries. These subsidiaries will require more planning autonomy to identify the IS needed to be effective in their markets. If parent companies, however, choose to treat such subsidiaries homogeneously, they run the risk of creating higher agency costs [39]. For instance, controlling the innovativeness of subsidiaries could eventually prove expensive for the firm [32]. Product modification was measured on a Likert scale as the extent to which the subsidiary modified products of the parent.

H2. The extent of product modification differences in multinational firms is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.3. Extent of local ownership in foreign subsidiaries

Owners of a subsidiary may include the local government and local financial institutions that are interested in the goals and performance of the individual subsidiary over those of the parent company. Goal conflicts between subsidiaries with greater local ownership and their parent companies will increase management complexity for the individual subsidiaries as well as for the parents [7]. Agency costs will therefore be higher for such subsidiaries and the parent will grant them more autonomy to reduce the costs [36,54]. The parent, if it is a minority owner, might also not have a choice other than to give more autonomy to such subsidiaries to plan for their individual IS needs [6]. The variable was measured as the percentage of local managers on the subsidiary’s board of directors.

H3. The extent of local ownership of foreign subsidiaries is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.4. Relative size of the subsidiary

The larger the relative size of the subsidiary, the better is its bargaining position with the parent and the better its claim for autonomy because larger firms face greater agency costs [6,15,33]. Such a subsidiary also has the capability to maintain an independent IS department which can share the planning responsibilities of parent managers. This sharing would lower the agency cost for the parent, and thereby motivate it to delegate the autonomy. In the current study, this variable was measured by the subsidiary’s sales expressed as a percent of parent’s overall sales.

H4. The relative size of the subsidiary is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.5. Size of the subsidiary

A parent grants more autonomy to a larger subsidiary because the subsidiary has the ability to support a full management staff [40]. Larger subsidiaries are more complex, and their decisions more ambiguous to the parent, thus producing higher agency costs and difficulties in governing them [6,36]. Providing autonomy to subsidiary IS managers will reduce the workload on the parent’s managers and the overall agency costs. In this study, size of the subsidiary was measured as the subsidiary’s number of employees.

H5. The size of the subsidiary is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.6. Product change

Product change refers to the intensity with which new products are introduced by the subsidiary to its local markets. Subsidiaries experiencing a high degree of product change must have higher autonomy due to their need to react quickly to rapid changes and heavy flow of new products [40]. Curbing the subsidiary’s entrepreneurial culture if it clashes with that of the parent can increase agency costs for the parent [36]. This subsidiary should therefore have more autonomy to remain innovative, thus lowering the agency costs [53]. This variable was measured as the percentage change in the number of broad product lines offered by the subsidiary over the last 5 years (the same duration used by Gates and Egelhoff [7]).

H6. Product change is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.7. Competitive climate change

Competitive climate change represents the extent to which the subsidiary’s local market competition has recently been changing. Local market volatility, particularly in terms of economic, political and industry conditions, can increase agency costs and cause the parent to change its governing mechanism [31]. Therefore, a positive relationship is expected between autonomy and competitive climate change to enable the subsidiary to respond quickly to its competition [55], and control its agency costs [56]. The subsidiary will need more IS planning autonomy to be responsive to its information needs in such an environment. Competitive climate change was measured using the average of four Likert scale items that reflected the extent to which the climate of the subsidiary had become more heterogeneous, dynamic, hostile, and uncertain [57] over the last 3 years (the same duration used by Gates and Egelhoff [7]).

H7. Competitive climate change is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.8. Intracompany purchases

Intracompany purchases can create a critical type of dependence in a multinational corporation, and are an important tool with which the parent can exploit its multinationality in order to compete. They can lead to higher agency costs and to reduced autonomy for the subsidiary. This is because subsidiary-level decisions have greater consequences for the firm as a whole when interdependence is high. It is thus important for the parent to ensure that such decisions are appropriate [31]. For the effective functioning of the system of interdependent subsidiaries, more coordination would be expected in IS planning. Thus, parent managers would delegate less autonomy to the subsidiary for IS planning. In this study, intracompany purchases were measured as the percentage of the subsidiary’s purchases covered by sales from some other parts of the company.

H8. Intracompany purchases is negatively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 4.9. Age of the subsidiary

Older subsidiaries have more experience, hence represent less risk to parent firms, and therefore have more autonomy [15]. Familiarity between the parent and subsidiary IS managers can also contribute to more trust and a better working relationship between them. In fact, research has shown that employees identification with the organization becomes greater as they interact with its other members [58,59]. This interaction over an extended period of time can improve intra-firm relationships, simplify governing relationships, and lower agency costs because subsidiary managers will better understand the role that they and their subsidiary play in meeting the goals of the parent, and they will more likely engage in cooperative behavior [31]. Age of the subsidiary was measured as the number of years since it was acquired or founded.

H9. Age of the subsidiary is positively correlated with autonomy of IS planning.

## 5. Methodology

The methodology utilized a field survey consisting of two questionnaires. The first was for the chief information officer. It consisted of 5-point Likert scale items to measure the dependent variable (i.e., the extent of autonomy for the IS resources planning activities in Table 2) and general questions about the participant (Appendix A contains the relevant items). The second was intended for a senior manager from outside the IS area. It contained open-ended and factual questions to measure the independent variables, along with general questions about the participant and organization (Appendix B contains the relevant items). The purpose of the dual questionnaires was to tap the expertise of each executive and avoid any potential common method variance problem. Both instruments were sent to either the senior

IS executive or to a senior manager. Instructions in the cover letter requested the receiving manager fill out the appropriate questionnaire and forward the other questionnaire (with its own return envelope) to a manager who he or she felt could best answer the questions.

The mailing list for the instruments was obtained from Applied Computer Research (ACR) in Phoenix, AZ for a one-time fee based use. It included the name, telephone number and mailing address of CIOs of about 11,000 manufacturing companies based in the US. This list was compared to a list of foreign owned subsidiaries in the US from the Directory of Foreign Firms Operating in the US [60]. It provided the name, telephone number, and address of the CEOs about 4000 foreign owned subsidiaries along with information about the subsidiary’s industry, number of employees and parent company information. Comparison of the two directories led to the identification of contact information for 416 CIOs of foreign owned subsidiaries in the US.

To increase the sample, the Directory of Foreign Firms Operating in the US [60] was again referred to and 490 manufacturing subsidiaries were identified who did not have a corresponding listing in the ACR directory and employed at least 50 people. This group of firms thus had contact information specified only for the CEO rather than the CIO. In total thus the sample size was 906 firms ð416 þ 490Þ.

Next, the instruments were pilot tested with pairs of managers in five manufacturing subsidiaries of foreign firms. In each case, the participants consisted of the senior IS executive and another member of senior management. The senior author met with each participant independently at his or her workplace and reviewed the purpose of the survey. The participant was asked to read the sample cover letter and complete the survey, making any comments during this time. After completion, the senior author discussed the survey with the participant and asked for comments regarding the presentation of the questions and the utility of the cover letter in gaining responses. The pilot subjects suggested minor revisions to the questions and the cover letter. They were duly incorporated in the final versions before mailing the surveys.

Within 6 weeks of the initial mailing, 39 subjects (21 CIOs and 18 senior managers) had returned completed surveys. To determine why they had not returned more and to encourage greater participation, the senior author and an assistant phoned non-respondents. Each non-respondent received an average of three calls. When the manager was unreachable, the caller left no message but phoned again after a few days. On the fourth try, the caller left a message for the manager requesting participation and mentioning that another survey would be mailed to him or her within a few days. In all, of the 906 possible subjects, 137 were unreachable because the subject had moved, or the company had closed, or was no longer a subsidiary. Of the remaining 769 firms, 98 declined to participate because of corporate policy. A total of 131 IS manager surveys and 103 senior manager surveys were received from the mailings. Thus, the effective response rate was about 19.5% (131/671) for the CIO surveys and 15.4% (103/671) for the senior manager surveys, with 8% (55/671) being matched pairs.

The returned surveys were examined for nonresponse bias to confirm that the decision to respond was uninfluenced by non-random events or motives. If the decision to respond is random, then the timing of the response should not significantly influence the value of survey measures [61,62]. Multivariate analysis of variance was used to evaluate whether differences among early and late responders were associated with different responses [63]. The analysis indicated no significant differences in several key variables tested (IS resources planning activities, subsidiary employees, subsidiary sales, parent employees, and parent sales) for the top manager and IS manager surveys. This indicated absence of response bias in the top manager surveys (Wilks’ L ¼ 0:87, P ¼ 0:495) as well as the IS manager surveys (Wilks’ L ¼ 0:83, $P = 0 . 2 8 1 )$

Respondent CIOs had worked on average about 9 years with their current company, and about 15 each in manufacturing and IS. Approximately 75% had earned at least a 4-year college degree. The senior managers from outside the IS area had worked on average about 11 years with their current company and about 16 in manufacturing. Approximately 72% had earned at least a 4-year college degree. Worldwide, the average sales of the parent firms were US\$ 4.7 billion and their average number of employees was 18,400. The average sales of the US subsidiaries were US\$ 242 million and their average number of employees was

1230. For further insight to the data set, metrics for other survey measures are presented in Appendix C.

## 6. Data analysis

Validation tests were first performed to ensure that the research constructs were measured appropriately. These tests included exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and tests of reliability. This was followed by hypothesis testing using one-tailed Pearson correlations.

EFA was performed to validate the IS resources planning activities which had been applied in prior research as measures for IS planning [52]. The principal factor method was used to extract the factors, and this was followed by a varimax rotation with Kaiser normalization for the 131 CIO responses. The scree plot suggested two meaningful factors [64]. In interpreting the rotated factor pattern, an item was said to load on a given factor if the factor loading was 0.50 or greater for that factor and less than 0.40 for the other. The factor analyses revealed a number of items with cross-loadings (loadings of 0.40 or greater on more than one factor). Four items were dropped one at a time because of cross-loadings and the EFA was run again [65]. The items dropped in order were systems control planning, disaster recovery planning, data security planning and standards and procedures planning.

Table 3 shows the factor loadings resulting from the analysis. The final two-factor solution explained 73.6% of the variance in the data. Cronbach’s [66] alpha for the first factor was 0.88 and for the second factor was 0.85. Factor 1 consisted of application software planning, systems software planning, hardware planning and network communications planning. Thus, it was called the Technical Resources Planning Activities factor. Factor 2 consisted of technical training planning, personnel planning, end-user computing planning and facilities planning. Thus, it was called the Personnel Resources Planning Activities factor.

## 7. Discussion of results

The study found support for the hypothesis that the percentage of intracompany purchases correlated negatively with IS planning autonomy (H8). Also at a statistically significant level, it contradicted the hypotheses that foreign product diversity (H1), extent of local ownership (H3), and age of the subsidiary (H9) correlate positively with IS planning autonomy.

For H8, the complexity of intracompany purchases suggested that greater parental coordination of subsidiary IS planning would reduce agency costs for the firm and thus lead to less subsidiary autonomy. The strong support for H8 $\left( P < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ confirms that parent managers delegate less autonomy for IS planning to the subsidiary when more intracompany purchases take place. In marketing and finance decisions, intracompany purchases are also negatively correlated with autonomy [7,40]. Hence, for such purchases the parent treats IS management as it does other functional areas.

H1 had hypothesized that foreign product diversity in multinational firms positively correlates with autonomy of IS planning. For the parent, more product lines would be difficult to supervise, lead to higher agency costs, and thus encourage the delegation of autonomy. In marketing and finance decisions, foreign product diversity positively correlated with autonomy [7,40].

However, the current study found a significant negative correlation between product diversity and IS planning autonomy $\left( P < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ . Perhaps more research and development takes place at the US subsidiary, or the subsidiary could be one of the more successful foreign operations of the parent. Hence, the parent may be more interested in monitoring it. The use of IS enhances the parent’s ability to do so. Thus, IS planning may in fact be performed by the parent to facilitate this. The disparate treatment that IS receives in comparison to marketing and finance may also reflect the unique role of IS (i.e., the providing of information to facilitate monitoring), the lower levels of power and influence of IS in the organization, difficulties managing IS (unresponsiveness to users, runaway projects, etc.), or the parent’s mistrust of subsidiary IS management [9].

H3 had hypothesized that the extent of local ownership of foreign subsidiaries in multinational firms positively correlates with autonomy of IS planning. Goal conflicts of local owners of a subsidiary with the parent will increase management complexity for the individual subsidiary as well as for the parent company [7]. Agency costs will be higher for such subsidiaries [54]. The parent, especially if it is a minority owner, may not have a choice other than to give more autonomy to such subsidiaries for planning their individual IS needs. In marketing and finance decisions, the extent of local ownership positively correlated with autonomy [7,15].

However the current study contradicted this hypothesis $\left( P < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ . As the percentage of local company representatives increased on the subsidiary’s board of directors, the parent granted the subsidiary less autonomy for IS planning. Perhaps with less parent company representation on the subsidiary’s board, the parent needs to rely more on IS to monitor the alignment of the subsidiary with the parent. This would motivate performing IS planning at the parent rather than delegating autonomy to the subsidiary. Similarly to H1, the disparate treatment that IS receives in comparison to other functional areas may reflect the unique role of IS, the lower levels of power and influence of IS in the organization, difficulties managing IS, or the parent’s mistrust of subsidiary IS management [9].

H9 had hypothesized that the age of the subsidiary positively correlates with autonomy of IS planning.

Older subsidiaries have more experience and hence represent less risk to the multinational firm. Familiarity between the parent and subsidiary IS managers can also contribute to more trust and a better working relationship between them. This improved relationship can lower the agency costs. The subsidiary would therefore have more autonomy. In the case of finance decisions, age of the subsidiary is positively correlated with autonomy [7].

However, this hypothesis was contradicted $\left( P < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ . Perhaps greater experience in interacting with a subsidiary helps the parent understand the foreign market better, and thus gain confidence in its ability to manage this operation remotely with the use of centrally planned IS. On the other hand, an older subsidiary may tend to present problems to the parent due to its own established decision-making structures. Thus, the parent may want to control this subsidiary more closely, and centrally planned IS can play a crucial role in monitoring it. But again, as in H1 and H3, the IS function is apparently treated differently from other functional areas.

Finally, earlier research had found support for marketing, finance, and manufacturing decisions of subsidiaries for the five other hypotheses in the current study [7,15,40]. The non-significant relationships found for these hypotheses in the current study prove nothing. However, they may at least be deemed consistent with the conclusion that parents in multinational firms generally treat the IS function differently from other functional areas in their foreign subsidiaries.

## 8. Implications for future research

This study integrated the topic of subsidiary IS planning autonomy with a larger body of management research related to general subsidiary autonomy. It used corporate governance theory and agency theory to justify the hypotheses. It revealed the different treatment that the IS area receives from other functional areas in multinational firms, and its comparatively lower decision-making autonomy under certain conditions.

The study did not provide empirical evidence explaining the disparate treatment. Speculation, however, suggested several possible explanations. Among them were the unique role of IS (i.e., the providing of information to facilitate monitoring), the lower levels of power and influence of IS in the organization, difficulties managing IS (unresponsiveness to users, runaway projects, etc.), and the parent’s mistrust of subsidiary IS management. Researchers should test these reasons and explore other possible ones. Although the current study used agency theory as its foundation, failure to support more hypotheses does not disprove the theory. It confirms the difficulty in refining management theories in a multinational context [70]. In fact, to some extent the speculation about the reasons for the contradictory findings is consistent with agency theory. The unique role of IS, its lower power and influence, its difficulties managing, or parent mistrust probably produce agency costs different from the finance, marketing, and manufacturing areas of the organization.

The corporate governance system adopted by the parent might also explain the differential treatment. Future research might therefore examine the influence of such systems on subsidiary IS planning. The study of the evolution of these systems over time and their differences across functional areas might further prove useful. Future research could also seek other theories for explaining the results.

This research suggests that under certain conditions (i.e., the hypotheses) IS may have less autonomy than other functional areas in multinational firms. Future research might thus investigate if, in general, IS has less autonomy than those areas.

The study of other cultures can enhance the understanding of one’s own culture [71–73]. On that basis, the current study raises the question about the treatment of the IS function within strategic business units of corporations in a single country. Most of the hypotheses within the current study are tied to international issues. Nevertheless future research might further consider the conditions under which IS planning autonomy is granted to such business units [74,75].

Future research might investigate the extent to which parent companies provide autonomy to their domestic IS departments, and the extent to which they do likewise with foreign subsidiaries. An expectation might be that they treat their foreign subsidiaries the same way as they treat their domestic ones.

Future research might also overcome the limitation of this current study. The small sample size restricted statistical testing to non-multivariate analysis. However, the use of dual subjects and the consequent avoidance of common method variance provide some confidence in the results. Although IS researchers have analyzed small samples in the past with useful implications [68,76], future research should attempt to replicate this study with larger samples and multivariate analysis.

## 9. Implications for managers

This study found that parent managers of multinational firms treat the IS function of their foreign subsidiaries differently from other functional areas. Perhaps this is intentional, but if not, these managers might want to explore the reasons for this and how it may have come to be. Maybe the explanation can be traced to the organization’s corporate governance system, which is typically a result of its administrative heritage [22]. That is, the differential treatment may stem from a time when IS provided back office efficiencies rather than today’s strategic role. Perhaps the understanding the managers gain through their introspection, if shared with subsidiary managers, may help overcome some of the multinational IS management problems such as conflict with the parent and challenges to its authority.

The study also informs subsidiary IS managers that the parent firm treats them differently from other functional areas. These IS managers may want to be sure they know the reasons for this treatment and may want to decide what to do about it. Perhaps they are already aware of it but unsure of how to present their case for more autonomy. Knowing about the reasons may thus be valuable to these managers in determining how to react.

## 10. Conclusion

Management researchers [7,15,40,41] have studied the autonomy of decision-making for different functional areas (marketing, manufacturing, and finance) as influenced by the variables used in this study. The current study has extended that research to IS planning autonomy. It has thus contributed towards a better understanding of the management of foreign subsidiaries. It has also suggested the need to examine other factors that might influence IS planning practices in multinational firms. The identification of the differential treatment of the IS area is, moreover, a valuable contribution for managers.

Another contribution of this research was the validation and refinement of an instrument that can be used to study IS planning. This instrument identifies the resources that are the objectives of IS planning, and therefore opens up the possibility to study the predictors and outcomes of the planning of those resources. In conclusion, this research has attempted to understand IS planning autonomy in the subsidiaries of multinational firms. With little known previously about this topic, this study may motivate future research and improved practice.
