# Electronic coordination and collective action: use and effects of electronic calendaring and scheduling

Bart van den Hooff<sup>\*</sup>

Amsterdam School of Communications Research (ASCOR), University of Amsterdam, Kloveniersburgwal 48, 1012 CX Amsterdam, The Netherlands

Received 7 July 2002; received in revised form 26 February 2003; accepted 28 December 2003

Available online 9 April 2004

## Abstract

The electronic calendar is a relatively simple ICT application that can have positive contributions to the coordination of activities in organizations. In this study, the realization of these benefits is analyzed in terms of collective action. Two kinds of benefits are distinguished: connectivity (an increased ability to reach other members of the organization) and communality (more information on time, location, and activities of coworkers). A theoretical model is developed and tested explaining the use and effects of electronic calendaring. It is found that collective use of the calendar (i.e. using the system for interpersonal coordination) is crucial to the realization of the benefits, and that the coordination benefits themselves are interrelated. Use and effects of electronic calendar are found to be dependent on a combination of task, user, and system characteristics. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronic calendaring and scheduling; Collective action; Coordination; Information and communication technology acceptance; Information and communication technology effects

## 1. Introduction

Tools supporting the coordination of activities between individuals and groups can be an important contribution in managing the increasingly scarce resource of time in organizations. Coordination is central to much literature in the field of organization science (e.g., in Mintzberg’s [21] classic distinction of organization structures). Huczynski and Buchanan [14] define coordinating (following Fayol) as ‘‘making sure that activities and resources are working well together towards the common goal’’. The goal of coordination is to achieve a balance between the different parts and processes of the organization, so that they can all contribute to achieving the organization’s common goal.

Information and Communication Technology (ICT), can be helpful in improving such coordination. Hinssen [13] argues that group scheduling tools (electronic calendar systems) are important ICT tools for coordination. Andriessen [1] has a similar argument, as he mentions electronic calendars as a prime example of what he calls coordination tools. Ehrlich [9] reports empirical results supporting such arguments: electronic calendars provide simultaneous access to shared information, and easy access to the right individuals and groups, speedy and independent of distance. Basically, the electronic calendar is a relatively simple ICT application that enables users to schedule meetings, plan activities, make notes—as could a traditional paper calendar. However, electronic calendaring and scheduling (ECS) systems offer more—the sharing of calendar information facilitates organizational coordination.

Sharing information can be seen as a collective action—it results in the realization or maintenance of a public or collective good. This is the common information base that results from every user sharing his or her calendar with co-workers. The central premise of this paper is that such sharing is a prerequisite for the full benefits of the electronic calendar to be realized. Here, the focus is on the contribution of ECS to improved coordination in organizations, and on the factors that influence the extent to which this is realized. In other words, the research questions are:

1. What are the effects of ECS aiding coordination?

2. Which factors influence the extent to which and ways in which such systems are used and what can influence the realization of such benefits?

3. What is the importance of collective action in realizing such benefits?

In order to answer these questions, it is first necessary to have a clear picture of the ICT application under study: what is ECS, and which are the relevant dimensions in studying this?

## 2. Electronic calendaring and scheduling: the calendar as an information system

The electronic calendar is basically a database that is organized along temporal dimensions (hours, weeks, months, and years), in which users can enter their appointments, activities, etc. Its basic function is similar to that of the traditional paper calendar: it reminds users of the time and location of relevant activities and serves as an ‘‘archive’’ that enables them to track the history of their (work) life.

However, contemporary electronic calendaring systems go beyond this idiosyncratic, personally customized way of time management—they move personal calendars into a social space, as Palen [28] puts it. Ehrlich even claims that electronic calendars can be considered to be an extension of electronic mail rather than a replacement for paper calendars. Today, they are typically networked, and can easily be shared among users. Consequently, it is to be expected that their impact goes beyond an increased efficiency of personal time management. Rather, today’s systems are a form of groupware [23] providing simultaneous access to time-structured information and allowing users to be alerted to and manage changing contingencies. Thus, these systems can play an important role in organizational coordination. As Lee [16] argues, they can affect the sociotemporal order in an organization. All in all, this groupware aspect can play a role in a process of increasing ‘virtualization’ of organizations, substituting physical with electronic presence.

Palen [29] distinguishes three dimensions in the study of such systems and emphasizes the need to integrate them when studying ECS:

1. the individual user, the calendar as a personal artifact;

2. the socio-organizational environment, the social dimension, i.e. the groupware functionality;

3. the technology of the application, its functionalities, compatibility, and ease of use.

Palen emphasizes that the interactions between these dimensions should be taken into account in the study of the use of ECS, as shown in Fig. 1.

These perspectives are represented by the three arrows in Fig. 1, and are:

1. the single-user calendar: the individual user’s time management with the aid of the ICT application;

2. interpersonal communication: the interaction and coordination between the individual user and his or her socio-organizational environment;

3. socio-technical evolution: the interaction between and joint development of the technology of the electronic calendar and the organizational environment [26,27].

The first two of these perspectives are in line with Lee’s distinction between personal use of ECS, and use as a communication device. This leads to distinctions in the use of ECS:

\- Individual use: for individual time management, such as temporal orientation, scheduling activities and meetings, registering information for retrieval, reconstruction, and reminding.

\- Collective use: for interpersonal coordination—coordinating meetings, looking for people and finding out where to reach them, synchronizing activities, booking facilities, etc. For this, the functionality of ‘‘access settings’’ is essential: in order to determine the level of access others have to one’s calendar, each user can customizethe settings, allowing certain other users to see the contents of the calendar (or not), or even to change the contents.

All in all, ECS is likely to have effects that exceed the efficiency gains of automating the individual paper calendar. The electronic calendar offers functionalities that support coordination, insight in time allocation and availability, and scheduling meetings with groups. With this, the electronic calendar develops into an organizational IS: a source of information. Collective action is a central concept in determining the extent to which such a system can actually contribute to coordination in the organization.

## 3. Collective action, use and effects of ICT in organizations

## 3.1. Effects of electronic calendaring in terms of coordination and collective action

By sharing their calendar information, users create a shared information base that can facilitate coordination in the organization. This base can be conceptualized as a ‘‘public’’ or ‘‘collective’’ good, since it possesses two key characteristics of such goods [22]:

1. Impossibility of exclusion: members of the organization cannot be excluded from using the good even if they do not contribute to it. Any user who provides general access to their calendar contributes to the collective information base.

For others, however, it is not necessary that they provide general access to their calendars. In other words, non-contributors are not automatically excluded from using the collective good.

2. Jointness of supply: one person’s use of the good does not diminish the level of good for other users. The value of the collective information base is not influenced by the fact that many users are consulting it.

These characteristics point to an important problem in collective goods: free riding, which Olson [25] defines as the primary barrier to collective action. People can enjoy the benefits without contributing. If, however, many people choose to do this, the collective good will never be fully realized, since insufficient contributions are made. Hence, a central question in collective action is how to establish a level of gain that makes it worthwhile for enough of the members to contribute to the production of the good.

But what kind of collective goods can result from using the electronic calendar? For this, the distinction initially made by Fulk et al. [11] between connectivity and communality can be used. According to them, ICT offers two classes of public goods:

1. connectivity: the ability to reach other members of the group; and

2. communality: the collective storing and sharing of information to which all members of the have access.

Connectivity is the ability for members of a social system to contact each other directly. Physical connectivity (the existence of an infrastructure that connects all members of the social system) should be distinguished from social connectivity (the actual use of the physical connections) . Although the first is a precondition for the second, it is social connectivity in which the actual value of ICT lies.

Communality, on the other hand, exists where organizational members have access to a commonly held body of information [12]. So, data repositories which consist of information provided by all members of a social system lead to communality. Examples are shared databases, bulletin boards, best practices, etc.

It is important to emphasize that the public goods here are the results of the effective use of an ICT application, not the application itself. Also, both public goods are not independent entities—as Monge et al. [22] have shown, an interesting interplay between the two can be expected, depending on the nature of the social system and the ICT application. Such conditions can favor the provision of communal over connective public goods—and vice versa.

Both connectivity and communality are important in determining the contribution of the electronic calendar to the organization—provided each individual calendar is kept up to date and is accessible to those for which the information is relevant. The coordination benefits here are defined as:

\- increased ease in making appointments with other members of the organization (connectivity);

\- more information on the availability of coworkers (communality).

This leads to the following hypotheses:

H1. Collective use of the electronic calendar is positively related to coordination benefits in terms of increased ease in making appointments with coworkers.

H2. Collective use of the electronic calendar is positively related to coordination benefits in terms of more and better information on coworkers’ time, location and activities.

## 3.2. Factors affecting the use of electronic calendaring and collective action

Marwell and Oliver [20] identify four factors that affect collective action. First, characteristics of the good itself: a number of these have already been mentioned. Second, the characteristics of the participants in the collective action (their interest and the resources they contribute). Third, the collective group of participants—their heterogeneity, the size of the group, etc. Finally, the action processes that produce the collective action—variables such as their degree of interdependence and the amount of information required for a process.

A theoretical model concerning the adoption, use and effects of ICT in organizations [39] has some resemblances to Marwell and Oliver’s model. In this model, five factors are said to influence the adoption, use, and effects of electronic mail:

\- characteristics of the users (their innovativeness, organizational position, etc.);

\- characteristics of the medium (its ‘richness’ in terms of media richness theory [6,7] and its ease of use);

\- characteristics of the tasks performed (complexity and communication intensity);

\- characteristics of the organization (social networks in the organization, structure, and culture);

\- characteristics of the implementation strategy used to present the system.

Both models point towards similar factors affecting the process and effects of electronic calendaring. Integrating these theoretical perspectives, four factors were derived which should influence the degree to which coordination benefits are established through the use of ECS:

1. the users of the application (the participants and user characteristics);

2. the tasks they perform (the action processes and task characteristics);

3. the system (the good and medium characteristics);

4. the social environment of the users (the group and the organization).

Users: Rogers [36] distinguishes members of a social system according to their ‘innovativeness’, their readiness to use a new innovation. A person’s innovativeness is expected to be an important individual characteristic with regard to the individual and collective use of the electronic calendar—although this might be less relevant where use is mandatory. Still, a difference might be expected in extent and ways of use with different degrees of innovativeness. After all, mandatory use is difficult to enforce—individual users still have considerable discretion in the extent they actually use the calendar.

H3. A user’s innovativeness is positively related to: (a) individual and (b) collective use of ECS.

If there are more innovative individuals in an organization, a ‘critical mass’ of users is more likely to emerge. This ‘critical mass’ is directly related to the concept of collective action: in Markus [18] and Oliver et al.’s [24] view, a ‘critical mass’ emerges when a sufficient proportion of the members of a social system are willing to invest in the use of an ICT application to make it worthwhile for the majority of the members of the social system to start using the application as well.

This critical mass relates to users’ willingness to keep their calendars up to date and provide access where relevant, and not become free riders. Use of an electronic calendar implies that individuals open themselves up to peer judgement about their time allocation and time preference. So, a certain ‘‘openness’’ among individual users with regard to their personal temporal information is also expected to be an influence on collective use of the electronic calendar. This openness is defined as the degree to which a person is willing to share information on his or her activities with coworkers.

H4. A user’s openness with regard to his or her activities is positively related to collective use of ECS.

A final hypothesis with regard to users relates to the relationship between individual and collective use. In many studies concerning the use of ICT [3,17,19], experience with the application is identified as an important determinant of the way in which ICT is used. E-mail, for instance, is often found to be initially used primarily for routine, information lean tasks [40]. Over time, however, users learn to apply the medium for tasks that are more complex, group-oriented, socio-emotional etc. Kraut et al. [15] describe a similar process in the use of video telephony: in spite of an initial reluctance to use the new medium at all, over time and with sufficient shared experiences, shared understandings and behaviors developed. These, in turn, created an environment in which use of the system optimally contributed to enhanced communication and coordination in the organization. In analyses of the process of adoption, use and effects of voice mail in organizations, Rice and co-workers [31,33,35] found comparable results.

It can be assumed that the incorporation of ECS is a comparable learning process, in which users initially use the system to support their individual time management (individual use), but gradually ‘‘widen their horizon’’ and increasingly use it in a collective fashion.

H5. Experience with ECS is positively related to both individual and collective use.

H6. Individual use of ECS is positively related to collective use.

System: In the process of adoption, use and effects of an innovation within a social system, the characteristics of the innovation itself are also of great importance. The central variable here is perceived ease of use [4,8], or the degree of accessibility of the system. The easier it is to access an ECS system, the more it will be used both individually and collectively.

H7. An ECS system’s ease of use is positively related to: (a) individual and (b) collective use of that system.

Tasks: A relevant characteristic of a user’s task with respect to use of the electronic calendar is its external orientation: the degree to which an individual’s work concerns collaborating with others and how it requires him or her to be in different locations. A second task characteristic is related to this external orientation: the number of appointments a person’s task requires. A third characteristic is the extent to which the individual is likely to be the initiator of a meeting; i.e. the number of appointments organized by the user.

H8. The number of appointments a user has is positively related to: (a) individual and (b) collective use of ECS.

H9. The frequency with which a user is the initiator of an appointment is positively related to: (a) individual and (b) collective use of ECS.

H10. External orientation of a user’s task is positively related to: (a) individual and (b) collective use of ECS.

Social environment: Social Influence Theory [10] states that an individual’s choice of communication media is influenced strongly by other peoples’ opinions and behavior (through social network mechanisms [5,30,32]), as well as their own past opinions and behavior [37]. Organizational norms can constitute a direct way of influencing people’s use of the electronic calendar. The more a person’s coworkers keep their calendars up to date and provide access, the more the person will be inclined to do so. Also, explicit pressure exerted by coworkers and managers is an important factor in the social environment. Sending key communications (such as meeting times) through the new system, and requiring users to input their data into the system, positively influences adoption. This leads to two more hypotheses:

H11. The extent to which ECS is used by a user’s coworkers is positively related to that user’s: (a) individual and (b) collective use of that system.

H12. The extent to which a user experiences explicit pressure from coworkers and superiors to use ECS is positively related to that user’s: (a) individual and (b) collective use of that system.

The hypotheses are summarized in the theoretical model for the use and effects of the electronic calendar in Fig. 2.

The variables and relationships in this model were tested in a case study in a large Dutch governmental organization.

## 4. Study site and method

The organization in which the study was conducted is part of the Dutch government; it is occupied with the preparation, establishment, and execution of policy in the area of agriculture, nature preservation, and fishery. The Ministry has existed since 1935, and currently employs about 7300 people. Since 1998, Microsoft Outlook Calendar has been in use as their ECS system.

Data were collected by means of a survey that was conducted among the users and potential users of the system. The questionnaire was administered electronically: an HTML document was placed on a server, and a sample of 660 employees were sent an e-mail with a request that they fill in the questionnaire via a link to it. The sample was randomly selected from a database with all organizational personnel’s e-mail addresses. When the questionnaire was closed, 280 people had completed it for a response rate of 42%. No incentives were promised or given other than informing the respondents on the results of the study.

For the model as a whole, three statistics were found to be relevant [2]. Although AMOS produces a very large number of different statistics, the University of Texas [38] mentions that these three statistics are commonly reported fit statistics:

1. The chi square value: This indicates the absolute fit of the model to the data, and is the result of the testing of the null hypothesis that the model does indeed fit the data. For the model in Fig. 3, the chisquare test of overall model fit is 14.0 with 15 degrees of freedom, returning a probability of 0.526 that this value would be obtained by chance if the null hypothesis that the model fits the data is true.

2. Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI): Because the chi-square test of absolute model fit is sensitive to sample size and non-normality of the input variables, a number of descriptive fit statistics are available to assess the relative fit of a model to the data. The TLI is an example of this; it compares the absolute fit of the specified model to the absolute fit of the most restrictive model, in which all relationships between the observed variables are assumed to be zero. TLI values close to 1 indicate a very good fit. For the model in Fig. 3, this value is 1.001.

3. RMSEA: The root mean square error of approximation is based on a comparison of the values in the specified model to population means and covariance structures. In general, the closer the RMSEA value is to 0, the better the fit. Since the model has an RMSEA of 0.000, this indicates a very good fit of the model.

On the basis of both regression coefficients and fit indices, a number of conclusions can be drawn. First, the general premise that collective use leads to coordination benefits in terms of both communality and connectivity is supported, as well as the fact that individual use leads to collective use. Also, the two kinds of coordination benefits are interrelated: increased information on coworker availability (communality) makes it easier to make appointments (connectivity). Finally, a surprising finding is that the social environment (the percentage of coworkers using the system and pressure from coworkers) did not seem to play a role.

One characteristic of the individual user which could also be relevant, but was not included in the regression analyses, was the user’s organizational function [34]. In order to determine the influence of this variable, analyses of variance were conducted on the effect of differences between job categories on the key variables. The figures show that a number of relevant individual and task characteristics were primarily found in the categories of managers and consultants, and significantly less in the category of administrative workers and policy workers. Managers and consultants have more external tasks, more appointments, are more often the initiators of these appointments, and are more innovative and open with regard to the use of the electronic calendar than administrative workers (and, for a number of variables, than policy workers). Not surprisingly, they also use ECS significantly more—for individual and collective use.

## 6. Conclusions

The use of ECS leads to coordination benefits in terms of both communality and connectivity, and the collective use of this ICT application is an essential prerequisite for these benefits to be realized: the benefits occur in the use of the system as a means for interpersonal communication. Furthermore, communality is a prerequisite for connectivity—sharing information on time, location and nature of one’s activities leads to more, efficient contact between coworkers. Finally, the use of the electronic calendar is determined by a combination of individual user characteristics, system characteristics and task characteristics.

Although the hypotheses concerning the importance of the social environment were rejected, other results do point towards an important role of the social environment in determining use and effects of ECS: collective use of ECS and the degree of openness of a user with regard to calendar use were found to be significant influences. The benefits of the electronic calendar only manifest themselves when the application is used for organizational coordination, and users provide access to their calendars to those for whom this is relevant. Then, the application supports connectivity (people are better able to reach their coworkers) and communality (together, the different calendars form a shared information system, containing information about activities, locations, processes, etc.). In this sense, the socio-organizational environment does indeed play an important role in the process of use and effects of ECS.

## 6.1. Limitations

The research consisted of a single case study and thus the influence of differences in organizational characteristics on use could not be measured. Furthermore, the study took place in a government organization, and may not be generalizable to other types of organizations. Another issue is possible self selection of respondents: the survey was administered electronically, which could lead to exclusion of the less ICT-literate members of the organization. Finally, concerning the role of the social environment, asking respondents what percentage of coworkers they think use the calendar is not a strong measure, since it entirely depends on the extent to which a respondent can estimate such a percentage. Future research should employ different measures for this variable.

## 6.2. Practical implications

As postulated, it is important to stimulate members of the organization to use their calendars in a ‘collective’ fashion and to establish organization-wide use of the system. Users who find most value from this application are those with many appointments of which they often are the initiator: the managers who invest more in realizing the public goods of connectivity and communality than other users. These can play the role of opinion leaders, establishing a climate in which collective use of the electronic calendar is fostered and coordination benefits are realized.
