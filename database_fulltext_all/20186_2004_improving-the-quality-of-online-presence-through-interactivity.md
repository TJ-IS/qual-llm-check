# Improving the quality of online presence through interactivity

Kuanchin Chen<sup>a,\*</sup>, David C. Yen<sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Business Information Systems, Haworth College of Business, Western Michigan University, Kalamazoo, MI 49008, USA <sup>b</sup>Department of DSC & MIS, RTF SBA, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, USA

Received 1 October 2001; received in revised form 1 October 2003; accepted 19 January 2004

Available online 9 April 2004

## Abstract

Online interactivity is becoming a valuable way of improving the communication quality of business web sites. As a result, it is important that web site designers understand the concept and how it affects the quality of web site design. This study empirically validated Ha and James’ five interactivity dimensions (playfulness, connectedness, reciprocal communication, information collection, and choice) and their relationship to design quality. The findings suggested that the playfulness, connectedness, and reciprocal communication dimensions are important predictors of web site quality. While information collection tools are powerful for web engineering and user tracking, the companies surveyed here had not fully used the promising potential of such tools. The choice dimension, although it offered flexibility and customizability to users, was not significant in predicting web site quality. The results underscored the viability of interactivity dimensions in the online environment.

Keywords: Interactivity; Web design quality; World Wide Web (WWW); Playfulness; Reciprocal communication

## 1. Introduction

Its ability to display digitized text and multimedia data has made the World Wide Web (WWW) a major means of delivering business information to targeted customers. Fueled by the underlying technologies that enable interactive and vivid demonstrations of business messages, the web has grown faster than any previous communication medium [4]. Many marketers consider the web to be the fourth marketing channel because of its potential to attract, engage, and involve visitors. This is not to say, however, that a simple web presence can achieve all of its potential: a web site must deliver several essential features to foster a better online experience.

While the exact number of online users is difficult to estimate, the Internet population is large and expanding [21,25,32,39]; for example CommerceNet (http:// www.commerce.net) estimates that the number of Internet users worldwide by year-end 2005 will be over 760 million. This growing Internet population offers great online business opportunities. But without strategic development of online features to entice users and engage them at the web site, Internet marketers will soon lose potential customers to competitors. Research has shown the importance of combining traditional mass media with this new communication medium [18,37]: many brick-and-mortar businesses are beginning to include their web addresses and online offerings into their TV commercials. This hybrid approach to marketing has the advantage of attracting users to a business web site but it offers little to the understanding of which online features are linked to its quality.

Many web users experience ‘‘user disorientation’’ while navigating a web site. This is often the result of confusing navigational features or an inadequate user interface. Many researchers and practitioners, including Marcus and Gould, Neilsen, and Neilsen and Sano, have investigated the issues of site ease-of-use and intuitiveness [23,28,30]. Web usability studies have generated a long list of design guidelines, including short load time, deeper site hierarchy, shorter home pages, and better placement of company logos. Application of these by web developers has helped to make web sites easier to use, improved the user’s experience with sites, and reduced navigational confusion. However, the ‘‘established’’ usability guidelines, while removing many negative aspects, have not focused on features that engage users. For example, removing navigational obstacles from a dull web site that closely follows usability standards does not necessarily mean that the site will attract viewers, stimulate their interests and curiosity, or leave them with a positive impression.

Research has shown that interactivity engages users (e.g., [12]). Other benefits of adding interactivity to a web site include improved user satisfaction [33] and a possible increase in site visibility [7], hence better acceptance [8]. The early interactivity studies, however, did not determine what types of interactivity are most relevant to quality. Nielsen and Wagner’s recommended web usability guidelines [27] stated that scrolling should be avoided on main navigation pages and features for a particular site should not drastically deviate from the general norm. Also, the practice of ‘‘overloading’’ the display area of a web page by packing it with many interactive features was not considered wise and should be avoided. Further, a cluttered design can significantly affect the users’ ability to identify relevant information and hyperlinks [3]. Clearly, awareness of the conventions of other web sites is good practice for web designers.

Many studies primarily offer guidelines or research frameworks [5,20], but wait for others to verify and validate their findings. Some developers blindly depend on such guidelines without considering whether they have been verified [26]. Moreover, other studies have focused on identifying interactivity dimensions through qualitative assessments (e.g., Heeter’s study on interactivity in the context of design experiences [16]). Some researchers, such as Cunliffe [10], have cautioned that existing design guidelines may be inappropriate, too rigid, or overly vague to be of value. Despite its obvious value, there has been very limited research that empirically examines interactivity dimensions and their relationship with an important design outcome—quality.

In light of this, we designed our study with two research objectives:

\- To provide a theoretical foundation for the dimensions of interactivity. With a clear understanding of it, the quality of web applications will be improved.

\- To investigate and provide a preliminary assessment of the relationship of interactivity dimensions to the quality of web site design.

## 2. Background

Rice and Williams [36] indicated that the study of interactivity should focus on the nature of real-time communication. Similarly, Rafaeli and Sudweeks [34] suggested that simultaneous and continuous exchanges should occur within the scope of interactivity. However, recent computer-mediated communication (CMC) and human computer interaction (HCI) studies [9,35] have acknowledged the existence of time lag in computer-based interactivity, suggesting that some interactivity may not occur in real-time. For example, email, newsgroups, and mailing lists are all asynchronous features of modern interactive media. Asynchronous interactivity does not necessarily deteriorate the quality of their communication, but can actually facilitate the desired effect.

The scope of interactivity has not yet been defined and accepted within the research community, although recent studies have identified possible dimensions that, within context, capture its multi-facet characteristics. Alba et al. [1] indicated that two key dimensions of interactivity are ‘‘response time’’ and ‘‘response contingency’’, which refer to the degree to which the response of one party is a function of the message made by the other party. The rationale of identifying interactivity dimensions in this fashion was based on an online sales environment, where the buyers and sellers communicated in a two-way message exchange. While not every online activity involves shopping, Alba’s definition nonetheless showed that messages exchanged during the communication should be related.

Ha and James [15] further refined the definition of interactivity by identifying the different degrees of communication that web visitors may need to experience in order to have a successful online session. They indicated that both low and high levels of communication were crucial in the WWW environment. Based on the many features available on the web that can be used to characterize interactivity, they offered five dimensions of interactivity:

\- playfulness;

\- choice;

\- connectedness;

\- information collection;

\- reciprocal communication.

Downes and McMillan [11] used a qualitative approach to identify six interactivity dimensions:

\- direction of communication;

\- time flexibility;

\- sense of place;

\- level of control;

\- responsiveness;

\- perceived purpose of communication.

Based upon the user’s qualitative experience, Burgoon et al. [6] conceptualized interactivity as interaction involvement, mutuality, and individuation. Interaction involvement involved the user’s perceived level of engagement in the interaction. Mutuality was related to the users’ perceived level of relational connection, interdependence, coordination, and understanding. Individuation was associated with the user’s perceived level of identity.

Although these studies identified different characteristics of online interactivity, they all asserted that interactivity was a multi-dimensional construct. All three studies placed less emphasis on testing these dimensions empirically and assessing their effects on the quality of web sites. Our study attempted to address both.

## 3. Research hypotheses

## 3.1. Playfulness

Webster and Martocchio [43] defined playfulness as ‘‘the degree of cognitive spontaneity in microcomputer interactions’’. Ha and James indicated that it featured devices that attract the of web visitors’ attention and involve them during their visit. Thus, playful devices include online games, software downloads, and question and answer (Q&A) exercises. While it was often associated with online features, neither definition precluded software, such as games or screensavers, downloaded for later off-line use. The viewer’s use of offline playful devices can extend the impact of organizations by continuing, and possibly strengthening, the association with business offerings: a site can offer a screensaver or game if the user visits the site to download it and it thus may prolong the user’s contact with the corporate message.

Because online users tend to seek gratification in escape, entertainment, interaction, and surveillance, playful features are a strong factor in influence and engagement [22]. Furthermore, embedding enjoyable features within web sites affects user attitudes toward revisiting the web site [17] and may enhance online user experience during the visit [13]. Therefore, online playfulness is considered one of the success factors of business web sites [24]. This assertion leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. Online playful features are significantly related to the quality of site design.

## 3.2. Choice

The choice dimension does not refer simply to informational and navigational alternatives for human users—it also includes the flexibility of the web design to accommodate popular software agents (such as various web browsers). Clearly, if any site design were geared toward only one type of web browser, it could confuse and frustrate those who use different browsers. Conversely, sites that provide alternative viewing or navigation tools, customization features, printer-friendly data layout, and human language options are considered friendlier to the user, the browser, or both. Since such user- and system-friendly choices reduce user confusion and frustration, we conjecture a second hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. Choice is positively related to the quality of site design.

## 3.3. Connectedness

This refers to a software component’s capability to offer the user a feeling of being connected to the outside world. The design of a web site and the robustness of its contents can foster such a sense. Many multimedia software components can enhance the perception of connectedness by providing high quality video clips, audio clips, site tours, product demonstrations, and other relevant features. As a result, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 3. Connectedness is a significant predictor of the quality of site design.

## 3.4. Information collection

The ability to collect information is an integral part of conducting business. The underlying protocol of the WWW is stateless: there is no built-in mechanism to ‘‘remember’’ who visited a site. If users are to have seamless experiences as they navigate from page to page, some information is an absolute necessity.

Besides having users enter data via surveys and forms, user information can be collected in a way that users are unaware of its collection. For example, site designers can employ ‘‘cookies’’ to keep track of events, the user’s identity, and/or online user behavior. End users are normally unaware that a cookie has been deposited in his or her computer, because most web browsers are set to accept cookies by default; however, users can configure their browsers to warn them of or reject cookies. ‘‘Web bugs’’ are also small graphics generated by computer programs on the web server to track users. Because they are sent as normal image files designed to blend with the color scheme of a web page, they can easily go unnoticed. Modern browsers are not yet ready to alert users to the existence of web bugs in the transmission.

Although information collection mechanisms can be used to enhance a site’s design, the way that the collected information is used is at the designer’s discretion.

Assuming that site designers use the information to improve site design, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 4. Information collection mechanisms are significantly related to the quality of site design.

## 3.5. Reciprocal communication

Reciprocal communication refers to the presence of response mechanisms at a web site. This dimension differs from information collection in that users control the content of the message. Users can choose which information they wish to provide about themselves or whether they wish to remain anonymous. The nature of this dimension requires two-way communication. However, the message flow does not have to be continuous or synchronous. During the complete course of a communication, participants can choose to leave and return later to continue to discuss a topic. Email provides typical asynchronous communication; it can terminate with only one message from each participant or it can continue for several rounds. Many web features provide such a dimension: email, online shopping, chat rooms, bulletin boards, mailing lists, search engines, etc. Thus, we further hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5. Reciprocal communication is a significant predictor of the quality of site design.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Sampling and procedures

The sample for our study was a random selection of Fortune 500 Company web sites observed in 2001. We were able to access all but one of their web sites—the missing site was down during the analysis phase of our study. In total, we evaluated the web sites of 149 companies.

Although a site can be designed in a highly hierarchical manner, the home page is the one that Internet users visit most often. Because this serves as the ‘‘front door’’ to visitors, it is the point at which most web features are implemented. If visitors have to search through several levels of a hierarchical tree to find a feature, they are likely to experience frustration and leave.

Our study extended and enriched the scope of Ha and James’ model of interactivity dimensions in web site design. We measured each dimension by counting the presence of web features in a particular dimension. We term this counting mechanism the ‘‘interactivity index’’ (II), originally coined by Ghose and Dou [14]. We calculated the II for each of the dimensions of a given web site by tallying the occurrence of web features classified into the particular dimension and used it as the primary approach for quantifying the degree of interactivity in each dimension. All IIs were assessed by a senior staff control coder and two student assistants and were normalized from 0 to 5. The three coders were given an identical set of company web sites and were instructed to assess their interactivity components. Results from the three were not significantly different. The overall intercoder reliability exceeded 86%. Discrepancies were assessed and corrected.

Our benchmark for the quality of a site was the Lycos list of top 5% web sites. Prior research [19,40] adopted similar product-rating reports to assess such issues as brand equity and effectiveness of web design.

Lycos had invited independent reviewers to evaluate based on a scale from 0 to 100. They measured:

\- the content by a site’s broadness, thoroughness, and accuracy, as well as the degree to which the information was up-to-date;

\- the presentation based on the design quality of presentation related features;

\- the experience based on other intangible assets.

We used logistic regression to assess the relationships between site design quality and the five interactivity dimensions. The dependent variable, ‘‘quality of site design’’, was determined by its inclusion or exclusion in the Lycos top 5% list.

In Ha and James’ study, reciprocal communication features were found in many web sites (61.8%), followed by choice (52.7%), and connectedness (37.0%). On the contrary, our current study suggested that the reciprocal communication dimension was the most influential (b ¼ 0:83) to predict web site quality. Connectedness followed as the second most influential predictor (b ¼ 0:72). The third was the playfulness (b ¼ 0:68); however, choice and information collection dimensions were not significant.

## 5. Discussions and analysis of findings

Our study empirically tested how interactivity is related to the quality of web site design; it suggested that successful web strategies should include interactive features, especially those categorized in the playfulness, connectedness, and reciprocal communication dimensions. With this information and the techniques suggested to quantify interactivity dimensions, web site designers can compare the quality of their sites to others.

Usability studies encourage designers to mimic features on big web sites and caution designers to stay away from lengthy home pages [29]. Selection and prioritization of web features appear to be a viable but challenging task in determining usability requirements.

Our findings suggest that reciprocal communication is the most influential component of interactivity on web site quality. Rafaeli and Sudweeks’ study supports this hypothesis; they suggested that ‘‘responsiveness’’ (e.g., load time, update rate, acknowledge speed, order status, and shipment tracking [41]) was a key element. It requires participants to take control in reciprocal communications. Research also suggests that people treat their interactions with computers as social encounters and therefore unconsciously expect them to obey the rules of interpersonal behavior [31].

By suggesting that site designers employ ‘‘attractors’’ in the web site, Watson et al. [42] noted that they provided a means for interacting with the medium as opposed to merely reacting to it. However, this view is restricted to how and what communication media are used. Their comments are applicable to situations in which at least one end of the communication does not involve humans, such as simple online web-surfing and game-playing. While a firm may not incorporate all interactive web functions into its web site, increasing the number of web features identified in the reciprocal communication dimension is likely to engage visitors.

Our study also showed that connectedness was a strong predictor for web site quality. As corporate web developers improve on their use of these features, the resulting quality and interactivity will be enhanced. Multimedia demos, 3D views, and featured selections for products in addition to static product specifications will more fully engage visitors to the site. Although providing multiple stimuli in the form of interactive components may seem to be common sense, many sites did not include such features.

Ideally, users’ surfing patterns can be traced and collected to re-engineer a web site. Information col lection mechanisms may currently be under-utilized. Perhaps corporate web sites are considered as tools/ vehicles for collecting user information for marketing or other purposes only. Schonberg et al. [38] recommended that e-business intelligence (the analysis and use of information about visitors to a web site) should play a strategic role in online commerce. They further suggested that good business practice should utilize intelligence to guide the design of all web site features.

Finally, our study results suggested that the choice dimension is not a strong predictor of site quality. Possibly this type of interactivity requires more technical effort in design and maintenance which translates to higher costs with unknown and uncertain returns. Designing sites that accommodate the differences among popular web browsers is imperative. We recommend, however, that site designers consider supporting their major web browsers because incompatibility can negatively affect visitors’ experiences.

## 6. Conclusions

## 6.1. Suggestions

Identifying interactive web features can be a logica and feasible step in web site design; however, prioritizing and strategically selecting the candidate features may help firms sustain a competitive web presence. Corporate web designers can use our findings to help select the important interactivity dimensions to achieve their goals.

This study also offers contributions to existing research in several ways. First, it offers an empirical assessment on the relationship between interactivity and quality of design. Second, this study highlighted areas that will improve site quality through interactivity features, thus complementing usability and quality guidelines (such as [2]).

## 6.2. Limitations

Only home pages were considered in our study because they are generally the doors through which users visit sites. This limitation could provide challenges to the application of this study. The rationale of our approach is based on the idea that most interactive features are available through the home pages. However, designers may still elect to deviate from the norm by spreading these features across several pages. Future work can develop an assessment tool to study interactivity of the whole site, rather than on a perpage basis. Furthermore, although the presence or absence of interactivity features was our primary focus, our study may not portray the profound depth of each interactivity feature. A simple counting mechanism was employed and it did not treat multiple occurrences of an interactive feature on a given web site any differently than on another. Therefore, a web site with several email contacts was viewed as equal to another with fewer email contacts. This opens opportunities for future work to study how the weight of each interactivity feature is determined, and how it affects the design quality.
