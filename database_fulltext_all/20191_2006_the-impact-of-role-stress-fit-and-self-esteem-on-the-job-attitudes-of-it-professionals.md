# The impact of role stress fit and self-esteem on the job attitudes of IT professionals

Cynthia LeRouge <sup>a,\*</sup>, Anthony Nelson <sup>b,1</sup>, J. Ellis Blanton <sup>c,2</sup>

## Abstract

We used person-iob fit theory to examine the relationships between the match in IT developers’ preferred and perceived actual role stress (role stress fit) with job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Self-esteem was considered as either a potentia moderator or mediator of those relationships. Results from surveys completed by IT developers in 12 Fortune 500 companies indicated that role stress fit was positively related to both job satisfaction and organizational commitment and that self-esteem significantly moderated the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction. The results of our research has provided insight into staff assignment and the design of coping interventions. @.2006 F1aevien D.V A11 ricbt

Keywords: Role stress fit; Job satisfaction; Organizational commitment; Self-esteem; IT turnover

## 1. Introduction

Rasch [42, p. 38] stated that ‘‘long-term productivity is affected not only by hiring the best qualified personnel, but keeping them in the organization for long periods of time’’. An operational IT infrastructure is vital to most firms today. A loss of key IT professionals and their associated intellectual capital due to absenteeism or turnover can severely impact an organization’s restructuring and growth efforts, its competitive advantage, and ultimately its survival.

Retaining IT personnel has been a challenge to businesses. The costs of hiring and training ITemployees are high [43]; the total cost of losing an IT employee has been estimated as up to 150% of his or her annual salary [53]. As a result, retaining IT employees is an on-going central concern for managers [30].

Job satisfaction and organizational commitment play very important roles in affecting employee intention to stay with their organization [24]. Indeed, they have been identified as the two strongest attitudinal predictors of turnover behavior [34]. Even if turnover is not the end result, when core team members experience a reduction in job satisfaction or commitment, the organization loses due to diminished work quality or quantity. Job satisfaction and organizational commitment can be influenced by specific characteristics of jobs [14].

Role stress is a job-related source of organizational stress, where roles are ‘‘the behaviors and demands that are associated with the job an individual performs’’ [10, p. 37]. Negative workplace stress (including that of downsizing and technological evolution) may adversely affect an employee’s mental and physical health [25]. The organizational costs of adverse stress reactions can include additional hiring and training costs, the costs of settling worker’s compensation and/or tort claims, as well as the costs of decreased quality or efficiency [11]. The nature of the IT profession can introduce role stressor such as continuous change, long work hours, insatiable user demands, unmet deadlines, and constant demand to update skills [28].

IT attracts ‘‘the type of individual that thrives on being challenged, working hard and long, and engaging in self-motivation’’ [27, p. 79]. It seems the appropriate assessment of the consequences of workplace stressors is to address role stress fit (an equilibrium of an individual’s cognitive appraisal of stressors in relation to their tolerance or even desire for certain workplace stressors).

The purpose of our study was to address two unresolved issues in the IT context. First, we examine whether the equilibrium between IT professionals preferences for job-related role stress and their perceptions of actual role stress is positively related to their job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Numerous studies indicate that these relationships are influenced by self-esteem [37,38]. Second, we examine the potential moderating or mediating effects of selfesteem on those relationships.

## 2. Literature review and conceptual model

The research models for this study (Fig. 1) adapted selected variables and relationships originally proposed by Ivancevich et al. for investigating the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction and organizational commitment.

## 2.1. Organizational commitment

This is an attitudinal variable indicating the amount of loyalty and support an employee feels for an organization. It is a commonly used in management, marketing, psychology, and other disciplines as an antecedent to job involvement, job satisfaction, turnover, absenteeism, and organizational citizenship behavior [12,35]. Mowday et al. [39] found that performance was improved when IT professionals were committed to the organization. In fact, studies have found organizational commitment is the most important determinant of turnover intentions [23].

Prior research has indicated that organizational commitment is a multi-dimensional construct [17]. In our study, it was split into continuance and affective commitment; the first views organizational commitment from a loss standpoint, i.e. employees remain because they lose existing benefits if they leave [7]; the second refers to feelings of organizational loyalty and pride [29].

## 2.2. Job satisfaction

Job satisfaction is an attitudinal variable reflecting an employee’s overall feeling toward the job [50]. It has been found to be related positively to positive mood, job involvement, and performance [8,55] and is especially important in technical workers because of the competitive edge they provide [46]. Typical models of turnover fail to account for individual differences. Locke’s [32] discrepancy conceptualization of job satisfaction held that an employee’s job satisfaction was a function of the fit (or discrepancy) between the employee’s needs and what he or she has.

## 2.3. Role stress

The presence of role stressors does not itself evoke either negative or positive reactions. A negative reaction may be described as ‘‘stress’’ or ‘‘strain’’. Role strain is an adaptive response, moderated by individual differences. Role strain is a negative consequence of role stressors, which places special demands on a person [26]. The occurrence of unwanted, unmanageable role stress situations can ultimately result in exhaustion and burnout. Role strain can be caused by either an understimulation situation (where a worker’s skills may be underutilized) or an over-stimulation situation (where a worker has an excessive workload). Identified sources of IS role strain include: disagreement about control; differences in goals and time frames; lack of measurable IS benefit; and disagreement over roles and responsibilities during IS development [49]. Moore [36] stated that approximately 20% of the ITwork force experienced role strain to the point of exhaustion, with reduced job satisfaction, reduced organizational commitment, and greater intention to quit. Furthermore, the potential physiological (specifically, cardiovascular, biochemical, and gastrointestinal) and emotional consequences of role strain (high blood pressure), is quite well documented.

However, all stress is not negative. Selye [48] pointed out that a certain level of stress, ‘‘eustress’’, was necessary for development, motivation, change, and growth. Ivancevich et al. theorized that low levels of stress may indicate that IT professionals cope and adapt well to role stressors. As such, IT professionals may self-select into their context to fulfill a desired level of eustress.

In accordance with the transactional view, role stressors are inherently neutral and role strain can be considered to be a lack of fit between a person (in terms of personality, aptitudes and abilities) and the job environment with a consequent inability to cope with role stressors. As explained by Cooper et al., the transactional approach views role stress as an individualized process in which imbalance and equilibrium may shift over time.

## 2.4. Role stress fit

This is a measure of the difference between employees’ preferences for role stress and their perception of the actual role stress in their jobs. Role stressors lead to decreased job satisfaction and reduced organizational commitment when the stress results in strain [6]. Role ambiguity is the extent that goals and responsibilities are unclear. Role conflict is the extent to which an employee is challenged by assigned tasks due to resource constraints and competing demands.

In our study, role stress fit is determined by the IT professionals’ preferred and perceived levels of role ambiguity and role conflict in their jobs. Person–job (P–J) fit theory is our theoretical underpinning to predict whether equilibrium in an employee’s preferred and perceived role stress levels will result in favorable outcomes, such as increased job satisfaction and organizational commitment [31]. P–J fit theory assumes that jobs vary in characteristics, just as employees vary in preferences [15] and predicts that employee preferences and the job characteristics jointly determine employee attitudes and behavior. Therefore, when the preferences of IT professionals’ match the characteristics of their jobs, positive attitudes and behavior may be expected.

Thus, P–J fit theory predicts that role stress fit will have a positive relationship with job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Therefore, we hypothesized that:

H1. Role stress fit is positively related to job satisfaction.

H2. Role stress fit is positively related to organizational commitment.

## 2.5. Self-esteem

Organizational self-esteem is the extent that employees feel valued and taken seriously. Behavioral plasticity predicts that employees with low self-esteem are subject to stronger influence by external or social cues and are more responsive to situational effects, including peer-group interaction, role stress, and supervisory support [44]. Employees with low selfesteem rely more on workplace events to govern their thoughts, feelings, and actions. For example, Mossholder et al. found that role stressors had a negative impact on job satisfaction for low self-esteem nurses. Some studies have found high self-esteem may mitigate physiological consequences of job-related strain [47]. In essence, these findings indicate that self-esteem may ‘‘insulate’’ individuals from interpreting role stressors negatively or provide coping capabilities.

A conflict exists as investigations of role stress, assignments, and attitudes have found self-esteem to behave both as a moderator and mediator. Research that investigated mediational and moderational models emerged as a critical method for testing competing theories [45]. Our study followed methodological structure to test competing theories. Detailed discussion of moderator and mediator variables may be found in works by Baron and Kenny [5] and Holmbeck [20].

Abraham [3] found that self-esteem moderated the relationship between differential inequity and job satisfaction. Additionally, Mossholder et al. found self-esteem moderated the relationships of role stressors and job satisfaction. A moderator relationship is a nonlinear relationship that proposes that self-esteem indirectly affects job satisfaction and/or organizational commitment by moderating the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction and organizational commitment through the use of interaction terms. Moderator variables are important, because moderators (self-esteem) are often assumed to reduce or enhance the influence that specific independent variables (role stress fit) have on specific dependent variables (job satisfaction and organization commitment).

Several factors may contribute to the moderating influence of self-esteem. First, individuals with high selfesteem exhibit less distress from negative events [9]. Second, high self-esteem may protect one from distress by affording less vulnerability and more resilience to stressful events [4]. Third, high self-esteem may also result in executing better coping strategies. Low selfesteem individuals may employ more passive coping mechanisms due to insecurities in their ability to affect their environment, the desire to conform to social expectations, and greater tendency to permit selfcriticism and negative feedback [51]. Therefore, selfesteem could mitigate the impact of role stress fit on job attitudes. A person with high self-esteem should be less affected by role stress fit because they are less affected by and cope better with their environment. Accordingly, management may not see a need for interventions to rectify a lack of role stress fit in situations when an employee had high self-esteem. Based upon this, we postulated the following hypotheses:

H3. Self-esteem moderates the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction.

H4. Self-esteem moderates the relationship between role stress fit and organizational commitment.

It is also likely that self-esteem may act as an intervening mediator in the relationship between role stress fit and job attitudes, rather than as a moderator. A mediator relationship conveys a linear, causal chain relationship: that role stress fit has a main effect on selfesteem and self-esteem has a main effect on job satisfaction and/or organizational commitment. Some studies investigating self-esteem have found a negative relationship between the two variables [2]. Other studies investigating self-esteem as an antecedent to job attitudes have found a positive relationship between them [52]. In studying self-esteem as a mediator between role strain and expectancy of success, Abel [1] addressed both the antecedent and consequent relationships and found that higher role stress was related to negative attitudes when using self-esteem as a mediator of the relationship between role stress and the expectancy of success. Thus, poor role stress fit may have a negative impact on selfesteem, which in turn may result in poor attitudes while good role stress fit may increase self-esteem and result in positive attitudes. Thus, we proposed:

H5. Self-esteem mediates the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction.

H6. Self-esteem mediates the relationship between role stress fit and organizational commitment.

## 3. Research method

A survey instrument was used to collect data required to test the research hypotheses. Twelve Fortune 500 companies representing a cross-section of industries, such as IT consulting, manufacturing, telecommunications, fast-food, pharmaceutical, and insurance, participated in the study. A senior IT executive in each company administered the survey to a randomly selected set (10–15) of their IT professionals involved in IT development.

## 3.1. Sample

A total of 124 of 158 IT developers responded to the survey. The response rate for each company did not differ significantly from the average of 78.4%. No particular pattern was noted in non-respondents.

Demographics of the respondents (Table 1) were similar to those found in overall industry averages and previous research [16], except that the number of female respondents was approximately 10% higher than a previous industry average [22]. Correlations between the demographic variables and the attitudinal variables were determined to be insignificant $( | r | < 0 . 1 )$ and therefore demographic variables were not used as covariates.

## 3.2. The survey instrument

The questionnaire was designed and pre-tested with the assistance of several IT development managers. IT managers and subordinates were used to pre-test all items to determine readability and understandability (vs. understanding). Minor adjustments were made to a few items based upon pre-testing to increase comprehension and clarity.

Construct validity and reliability were measured and assessed during the course of instrument development. Items used to measure each of the constructs were adapted from previous studies. Appendix A contains the survey items and scales for each construct.

Job satisfaction was measured using portions of the job diagnostic instrument developed by Hackman and Oldham [18]. Organizational commitment was measured using a scale developed by Mowday et al. Scale items reflected continuance (‘‘There’s not too much to gain by sticking to this organization’’) and affective commitment (‘‘I am proud to tell others that I am part of this organization’’).

Role stress fit was measured as the difference between preferred role stress and perceived role stress. Difference scores have been used in many past studies to calculate various fit constructs [13]. Preferred and perceived role stress were determined using role ambiguity and role conflict as subconstructs. Items were adapted from an instrument developed by House et al. [21]. The same item stems were used to assess both the respondent’s preference regarding job characteristics and perceptions of actual job characteristics to support a parallel form that would allow for gap assessment. A measure of role stress fit for each respondent was determined by averaging the differences between perception and preference responses calculated for each item.

Self-esteem was measured using a five-item scale developed by Pierce et al. [41].

Standard techniques were used in validating the measures. Exploratory factor analyses were conducted to assess the number of factors, any underlying dimensions of the constructs, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. The nature of the constructs of interest did not support factoring difference scores. Hence, two sets of factor analyses were performed. The first factored perceived role stress items with commitment, satisfaction, and self-esteem. The second analysis factored preferred role stress items with commitment, satisfaction, and self-esteem. A conservative decision was made to keep items that loaded satisfactorily on either factor analysis set with no conflicting loadings.

## 4. Results

Hypothesis testing was conducted in four phases. In phase one, descriptive statistics of the constructs were calculated and examined to validate the survey instrument. Descriptive statistics were also used to provide a general characterization of the population. For example, job satisfaction had a mean of 2.46 with a standard deviation of 0.42, indicating that the average employee was slightly satisfied with his or her job.

In phase two, once it was ascertained that multiple regression assumptions were met, the direct effect of role stress fit and job satisfaction (H1) and organizational commitment (H2) were examined. Multiple regression was deemed a suitable technique for the study given our goal to test specific hypotheses rather than testing the overall model and the study sample size [33].

In phase three, the moderating (H3 and H4) or mediating (H5 and H6) effects of self-esteem on the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction and organizational commitment were tested using a multi-step regression analysis.

In phase four, based upon the results, post hoc analysis was examined using ANOVA to examine the effect of self-esteem on job satisfaction for respondents whose role stress fit indicated that they were in an under-stressed, over-stress, or good-fit role stress job environment.

Self-esteem was tested as a mediator between the relationships of role stress fit and job satisfaction and organizational commitment using a series of regression tests. Mediation would be upheld if a series of three tests of significance based upon a series of three regression models all passed. First, overall significance had to be found when regressing the mediator on the independent variable. Second, overall significance had to be found when regressing the dependent variable on the independent variable. Third, the mediator term had to be significant when regressing the dependent variable on both the independent variable and the mediator. The tests are graphically presented in Fig. 2. The first test failed, and therefore the mediator test for self-esteem failed for both job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Neither H5 nor H6 are supported.

## 4.3. Post hoc analysis of self-esteem

The results indicated that self-esteem had a significant moderating effect on the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction. Post hoc analysis was conducted in order to understand this effect in a manner similar to the analysis of Wiener et al. [54]. Respondents were classified into three groups: under-stressed, over-stressed, and good-fit. Thosewhose preferred level of stress was greater than their perceived actual level by at least 0.5 were classified into the under-stressed group; those whose perceived actual level was greater than their desired level by at least 0.5 were classified into the over-stressed group.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to determine whether significant differences existed between the three groups based upon their level of self-esteem (see Table 5). Respondents were classified into two nominal level self-esteem groups based upon the median (5.33) self-esteem score for all respondents. Respondents with a self-esteem score of 5.33 and above were classified into the high self-esteem group; those with a self-esteem score of less than 5.33 into the low self-esteem group.

Regardless of the role stress fit group, respondents with high self-esteem reported a higher level of job satisfaction than respondents with low self-esteem (see Table 5). This difference was statistically significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ for under-stressed and over-stressed groups; i.e. people who did not indicate role stress fit equilibrium. However, self-esteem did not make a statistically significant difference for respondents in the good-fit (equilibrium) group.

## 5. Discussion

Our study found that role stress fit is positively related to job satisfaction and organizational commitment and that self-esteem moderates the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction (Table 6). Because of the competitive advantage that can be provided by IT professionals to their organization improving job satisfaction and organizational commitment is important. Job satisfaction has been linked with positive mood, job involvement, and performance, while organizational commitment has been shown to improve the strategic advancement of the organization and to lower turnover costs.

P–J fit theory predicts that a match between job characteristics and the preferences of employees will result in positive attitudes and behaviors. The positive relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction and role stress fit and organizational commitment supports the application of P–J fit theory to IT professionals.

As organizations endeavor to retain key IT workers and keep them productive in both stable and turbulent times, our work suggests that IT managers and human resource professionals should consider role stress fit when determining appropriate role stress coping interventions for employees and when assigning IT developers to projects. Such an assessment could be made in a costeffective manner through written or verbal self-reports.

When assessments of equilibrium indicate an overor under-stress situation, possible coping interventions should be made. Additionally, job enlargement theory indicates that jobs should be designed to use the full capacity of the individual so that s/he will have the opportunity to achieve the maximum in terms of need fulfillment [19].

Our study suggests that IT managers should try to match IT developers’ role stress preferences with estimated project role stress levels as a means of facilitating job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Staff assignment flexibility and coping interventions should be considered separately and in tandem, as allowed by organizational constraints, in management efforts to promote role stress fit among IT personnel.

In addition to the relationship that role stress fit has with job satisfaction and organizational commitment, the findings also reveal that self-esteem behaved more as a moderator than a mediator in the relationship between role stress fit and job satisfaction. Low self-esteem IT developers were less satisfied in situations of over and under-role stress. These results are consistent with behavioral plasticity, which suggests that low self-esteem individuals are more influenced by the environment. Though post hoc findings indicate self-esteem was significant in a role strain (under- or over-stress) situation, self-esteem was not significant in an equilibrium situation. These findings imply that a good fit may offset the negative moderating effects low self-esteem has on the relationship between role-stress fit and job satisfaction. Therefore, IT developers with high self-esteem are less susceptible to job environments that do not match their preferences than IT developers with lower self-esteem.

In acknowledging the moderating effect of selfesteem on job satisfaction, coping interventions, such as counseling or training for an IT worker experiencing role strain, may focus on improving self-esteem as part of or in lieu of a focus on stress management strategies.

IT managers should also consider differences in selfesteem when making project assignments. Namely, managers may consider assigning IT developers with higher self-esteem (as opposed to IT developers with low self-esteem) to projects where a potential role stress mismatch may occur.
