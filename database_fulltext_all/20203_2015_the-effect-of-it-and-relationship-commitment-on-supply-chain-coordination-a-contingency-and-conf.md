# The effect of IT and relationship commitment on supply chain coordination: A contingency and configuration approach

## A B S T R A C T

This study investigates how information technology and relationship commitment, mediated by supply chain coordination, influence supply chain performance from a resource synergy perspective. The proposed model is tested using data collected from 617 manufacturing firms. Supply chain coordination with both suppliers and customers is found to enhance supply chain performance, and the effect of coordination with customers is higher. There is an interactive relationship between the supply chain coordination on the supplier and customer sides, indicating that synergizing supply chain coordination with suppliers and with customers improves supply chain performance. Furthermore, information technology and relationship commitment influence supply chain coordination.

## 1. Introduction

In today’s hypercompetitive environment, successful supply chain coordination is critical for firms to improve their firm and supply chain performance [26,38,41]. Supply chain coordination refers to a firm’s ability to coordinate transaction-related activities with its supply chain partners [93] and is one major dimension of supply chain integration [26,101]. Supply chain coordination is one of the key capabilities [73] that enable firms to leverage their resources to accomplish objectives that they are unable to achieve alone [44] and create a seamless, synchronized supply chain [34]. Many firms perceive supply chain coordination as integral to their strategy [52] and spend millions of dollars in creating and sustaining effective supply chain coordination [71]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the key drivers of supply chain coordination and implement them efficiently.

Information technology (IT) has become a major driver of supply chain management as supply chain partners have become increasingly integrated via IT [28,91,101,102]. The traditional supply chain management approach relies primarily on the linkage between physical processes (e.g., inventory, warehousing, and shipment). However, a key feature of today’s supply chain management is the change in focus from the linking of physical processes to the linking of information-based processes across supply chain operations [103]. IT can facilitate the coordination of inter-firm processes spanning the whole supply chain, including both upstream and downstream operations [21]. More evidence on the role of IT in improving supply chain coordination is provided in Appendix A. It is also evident in business practices; for example, many firms invest in digitalized inter-organizational systems (IOS), such as electronic data interchange, RosettaNet-based systems, and customer relationship management information systems, to help improve supply chain coordination.

Although IT can potentially boost supply chain management, many firms fail to realize this potential due to the lack of supply chain coordination through IT, or because their trading partners may not have the capability to align with them [6,72]. This poses a formidable challenge for firms to work out how IT can be leveraged to make supply chain coordination more successful [21,105]. However, most IT and supply chain coordination studies consider only two-level supply chains (a supply chain with two firms, such as a supplier–manufacturer supply chain or a manufacturer–retailer supply chain). For example, Rai et al. [72] found that IT infrastructure integration significantly improved supply chain process integration, which led to better firm performance. However, they studied integration only at a two-level supply chain and thus failed to investigate the difference between supplier-side and customer-side integration. Using a similar two-level logic, Dong et al. [21] examined the effect of digitally enabled supply chain management – in terms of backend integration, managerial skills, and partner support – on supply chain process performance and competitive position. Therefore, to better understand supply chain performance improvement through the configuration of IT and coordination with both upstream and downstream partners, we extend these supply chain management studies from the two-level to a three-level supply chain setting (a supply chain with three firms, such as a supplier–manufacturer–distributor supply chain), with the aim of discovering how IT on the supplier and customer sides differentially affects supply chain coordination.

According to socio-technical system theory, both inter-organizational relationship resources and technical IT resources [67] play an important role in improving cooperation [58] and supply chain management [100]. For example, Zhao et al. [100] found that manufacturers’ relationship commitment to their customers was positively related to the manufacturers’ integration with those customers. Zhao et al. [101] also found that relationship commitment to suppliers and customers was related to supplier and customer integration, respectively. Patnayakuni et al. [63] found that both long-term relationship orientation and asset specificity were important in improving information flow integration for supply chain coordination through relational interaction routines. More evidence of the role of relational resources in improving supply chain coordination is provided in Appendix A. Aligning with the relationship perspective, this study further tests the effect of relational resources on supply chain coordination for the supplier and customer sides simultaneously. Extending this perspective to coordination within a three-level supply chain raises the important question of how the supplier-side coordination and the customerside coordination should be configured, e.g., balanced or unbalanced, to achieve better performance. Previous studies have identified different sources of inter-firm synergy [86] and revealed an insignificant or negatively significant direct relationship between supplier-side coordination and performance [6]. Similarly, Flynn et al. [26] found that supplier and customer integration had a synergistic effect on operational performance, whereas the direct effect of supplier integration on operational performance was insignificant. Motivated by recent studies on resource synergy, we apply configuration theory as the theoretical lens through which to investigate how resources on the supplier and customer sides can be configured for overall supply chain performance improvement.

From the combined socio-technical system theory and configuration theory perspective, we address three major research questions: (1) How do IT for suppliers/customers and relationship commitment to suppliers/customers jointly influence supply chain coordination with suppliers/customers? (2) How does supply chain coordination with suppliers and customers jointly influence supply chain performance? (3) What are the patterns of supply chain coordination and their resource and performance characteristics? By examining the effects of IT and relationship resources on performance through supply chain coordination, this study contributes to the literature and provides knowledge that could improve the practices of supply chain management [101], IT [6,32] and relationship management [58] in several ways. First, we provide empirical evidence of the effects of IT for suppliers and customers and relationship commitment to suppliers and customers on supply chain coordination with suppliers and customers, respectively. The evidence reveals the relative importance of IT and relationship commitment in improving supply chain coordination, thus contributing to the IT, relationship management, and supply chain management literature. Second, we elucidate the relative roles of coordination with suppliers and customers in enhancing supply chain performance, thus contributing to the supply chain management literature. Third, we identify supply chain coordination patterns and link them with IT, relationship commitment, and supply chain performance, thus contributing to the IT, relationship management, and supply chain management literature. Finally, we provide managerial guidelines to help managers devote their IT and relationship commitment resources to supply chain coordination for the supplier and the customer sides simultaneously to improve performance.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and research hypotheses

The theoretical perspective we use to study IT and relationship commitment resources and coordination capability in supply chains combines socio-technical theory and configuration theory [22]. Theoretically, socio-technical system theory addresses how the use of two major resources affects firm performance and suggests that firms comprise both social subsystems, consisting of people, and technical subsystems, consisting of equipment and technologies [62]. Both resources should work together to achieve performance improvement. Configuration theory addresses the typological configuration of interrelated activities to form a firm’s competitive advantages. As supply chain management focuses on the externally oriented capability in response to market demand, the coordination synergy of a supply chain is the link between upstream and downstream coordination capabilities in the supply chain context.

According to configuration theory, synergy is an important power for combining the resources and skills of individual organizations across organizational boundaries to enhance coordination [55]. The synergy that partners seek to achieve through coordination is more than a mere exchange of resources. Instead, synergy is the potential ability for two business units to enjoy super-additive value by integrating the individual organizations resources so that the aggregate effect of sharing resources could be greater than the sum of the individual contributions [86]. For resource synergy to occur in supply chains requires IT outside-in resources that leverage partner linkage and market responsiveness by sharing inventory, demand, and shipment information [79], and supply chain coordination emphasizes partner linkages and market responsiveness across organizational boundaries [34,90].

## 2.1. Effects of supply chain coordination on supply chain performance

Supply chain coordination is similar to supply chain collaboration and supply chain cooperation, which are the key components of supply chain integration. According to [101], supply chain integration includes strategic alliance, information sharing, and working together. Working together involves the coordination of related activities. These three dimensions of supply chain integration are related but different. For example, whereas strategic alliance focuses on strategic-level activities, working together focuses on operational-level activities. Similarly, whereas information sharing emphasizes the technological aspects of operations, working together emphasizes the human aspects of operations. Thus, we need to investigate their effects in detail. Although strategic alliance and information sharing are popular topics in the strategic management and IT [39] and supply chain integration literature [26], coordination, particularly supply chain coordination, is less emphasized. In addition, coordination studies focus primarily on the individual effects of coordination on performance (e.g., [73]); few studies examine the joint effects of different coordination activities on performance. Therefore, this study focuses on different supply chain coordination activities.

Supply chain coordination primarily emphasizes the seamless process connection and synergy among supply chain partners such as suppliers and customers. Coordination with suppliers and customers is commonly referred to as external coordination [54], which is the degree to which a firm and its external partners structure inter-organizational practices and processes into a collaborative, synchronized process [82]. However, coordination with suppliers and coordination with customers have different effects and cannot be treated as the same thing. Coordination with suppliers emphasizes the core competencies of coordination with the critical supplier, whereas coordination with customers emphasizes the core competencies of coordination with the critical customer [11].

The relationship between supply chain coordination and firm performance has been elaborated in the literature. For example, Dangayach and Deshmukh [18] argued that efficient supply chain coordination leads to superior performance. Supply chain coordination practices are the building blocks of a supply chain strategy. When key supply chain coordination practices are valuable, sustained competitive capabilities can be acquired, which lead to performance improvement [45]. In practice, coordination with suppliers is associated with product development performance [46] and supplier communication performance [17], whereas coordination with customers is associated with customer satisfaction directly [35] or indirectly through product development and innovation [81]. When a firm has the capability to coordinate with its supply chain partners effectively and efficiently, the firm can achieve better supply chain performance. High-level coordination can help a firm to understand the needs of its partners, to meet the requirements of its partners flexibly and speedily, and to provide high-quality customer service. High-level coordination can reduce the firm’s transaction costs within the supply chain, leading to a more effective and efficient supply chain. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1a. Coordination with suppliers is positively related to supply chain performance.

Hypothesis 1b. Coordination with customers is positively related to supply chain performance.

Various sources of inter-firm synergy [86] and empirical outcomes have been shown to have insignificant or negatively significant direct relationships with supplier-side coordination and performance [6]. From the perspective of supply chain integration, Devaraj et al. [19] showed that customer integration moderated the effect of supplier integration on performance. Flynn et al. [26] found that supplier and customer integration had an interactive effect on operational performance. Supply chain management represents a holistic philosophy, and the upstream and downstream effects should be considered together to gain a holistic understanding of the whole supply chain. Coordination with suppliers can help coordination with customers to improve supply chain performance, and vice versa. For example, coordination with customers can enhance the speediness of the supply chain, and this enhancement can be reinforced by coordination with suppliers. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1c. The interaction between coordination with suppliers and customers is positively related to supply chain performance.

## 2.2. Supply chain coordination through IT

A firm’s capabilities depend on its resources, including tangible and intangible factors such as physical assets, human capital, and intra-/inter-organizational routines and procedures [85]. How resources can be leveraged to create and sustain competitive advantages for a firm is a central focus for scholars in linking various types of capabilities with firms’ financial performance [93]. Focusing on outside-in capabilities in supply chains, this study summarizes two main resources, IT and relationship commitment, through an in-depth literature review from a socio-technical system perspective.

The use of IT in supply chains has received significant attention [48,54]. Many studies have examined the role of IT, or IOS, in supply chain management [8]. Previous studies have reported positive effects of IT on supply chain performance [84]. It is generally agreed that IT used among supply chain members can reduce various transaction costs and improve supply chain performance [100]. Accordingly, recent studies have considered supply chain management as a digitally enabled inter-organizational capability [72] and have viewed IT as an enabler of supply chain management [14].

However, many firms do not have an explicit strategy for addressing IT. Instead, firms often tend to approach external system integration as a purely technical issue, which is highly likely to result in a costly maintenance nightmare [54]. This may lead managers to form the incorrect belief that IT investment will automatically bring better organizational performance. Research has suggested that IT needs to be aligned with the business environment [39], and the match between IT needs and IT capabilities improves supply chain management performance [9]. Therefore, what is yet to be investigated is how IT improves supply chain performance.

IT, as part of a firm’s resource portfolio, may not affect firms’ competitive advantages when acting alone [93]. We propose that by embedding IT in a firm’s supply chain processes, IT can facilitate the development of a higher-order organizational capability, i.e., supply chain coordination, which is firm specific and difficult to duplicate. The synergistic benefits achieved through an integrated system across the organizational boundary provide sustained competitive advantages for a firm [10]. Recent studies on this topic have focused on the ‘missing link’ during IT value creation [104]. Some studies have found that IT has an indirect effect on firm performance and that the use of IT is likely to mediate the relationship between IT investment and IT value [104,106]. Similarly, [14] investigated the inter-organizational relationships that influence IT value creation, and [21] investigated the role of firms’ process performance in the relationship between IT and supply chain performance. There is an emerging trend in the use of intermediate variables to analyze IT value creation [90]. In this study, we pay particular attention to the mediating role of supply chain coordination in the relationship between IT and supply chain performance. The basic logic is that IT resources should be transformed into supply chain coordination capability, which consequently improves supply chain performance.

In this study, we define IT resources from the vertical information systems perspective as a set of technical specifications designed to promote inter-organizational coordination and integration within vertical industry sectors [53,90]. With IT resources, firms can enable real-time information sharing to coordinate the allocation of resources across the whole supply chain [50]. Dangayach and Deshmukh [18] argued that firms that can manage their practical resources in the supply chain more efficiently are more likely to gain competitive capabilities. Hill and Scudder [31] found that electronic data exchange (EDI) can improve supply chain coordination in the food industry. Furthermore, IT helps to establish links between different resources owned by different supply chain partners, thus transferring them into bundles of co-existing resources [21] and improving a firm’s ability to address business uncertainties [9]. This is consistent with the concept of creating resource synergy [16]. The value of the supply chain can be manifested by revenue generation and cost reduction. Accordingly, technologies that help to reduce coordination costs are more valuable in intensely competitive markets. With the assistance of IT, firms are more likely to coordinate with their supply chain partners. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 2a. IT for suppliers is positively related to coordination with suppliers.

Hypothesis 2b. IT for customers is positively related to coordination with customers

## 2.3. Supply chain coordination through relationships

In this study, a relationship is defined as an inter-organizational relationship that involves the sharing and pooling of resources to reduce conflicts and transaction costs, concentrating on critical competency and seeking additional assets and/or markets [52]. Depending on the nature of the shared resources and the extent of sharing, a successful relationship must lead to an overall win-win situation for all partners and have a significant effect on supply chain performance [79]. In this study, we focus on relationship commitment, which is defined as the willingness of a party to maintain a relationship through the investment of financial, physical, or relationship-based resources [58,100,101].

Strategic management research has suggested that firms work closely with other firms to manage the flow of goods and services and increasingly rely on collaborative relationships with other firms to operate value chain activities such as new product development, manufacturing, procurement, inventory management, forecasting, and sales [61,72]. A committed relationship represents a strong tie in which the boundary spanners in a firm leverage social capital to access information and resources from their counterparts to gain value for the firm [30]. Firms in a committed close relationship form a strategic network, ‘‘a longterm, purposeful arrangement among distinct but related forprofit organizations that allow those firms in them to gain or sustain competitive advantages vis-a-vis their competitors outside the network. . .. it is a mode of organization that is based on coordination through adaptation,’’ for synergic coordination [42].

Previous studies have suggested that relationship commitment provides strategic flexibility [30] and offers an attractive means for enhancing resource bundles when an organization’s current capabilities are insufficient to achieve desired outcomes [36]. In support of this conclusion, Stuart [83] found that younger and smaller firms without cutting-edge technologies were more likely to form alliances with larger firms with leading technologies. Doh [20] argued that firms without specialized resources usually search for partners who have them. In such an environment, there is great potential in relationships that enable organizations to support each other by leveraging, combining, and capitalizing on their complementary strengths and capabilities [47]. Research has also shown that supply chain coordination encourages mutual trust, efficient operational planning, conflict resolution, and the sharing of information and provides both operational and strategic benefits [75]. In this study, we build on the literature related to coordination as the proximal outcome of relationship commitment and the literature related to IT, as discussed above, which gives firms unique advantages in supply chain coordination.

Noting that organizations often learn by collaborating with other organizations, inter-organizational learning is critical to competitive success [24]. A firm’s relationship commitment in a supply chain is in many cases one of the most important sources of new ideas and information, and it may result in performanceenhancing technologies and innovations. Thus, partners with relationship commitment can generate rents by developing interfirm information and knowledge-sharing routines, which are a regular pattern of inter-firm interactions that permit the transfer or creation of value-added activities [24]. Inter-organizational network studies suggest that a firm creates a collaborative relationship with another firm based on familiarity, trust, and commitment [29]. This relationship leads to the sharing of private information, such as a firm’s strategy and production capabilities [87].

With relationship commitment, each supply chain partner focuses on its own operations while simultaneously coordinating its operations with those of its supply chain partners. This may reduce the acquisition of duplicate resources, thus increasing resource utilization and reducing operational costs. Further cost reductions can be gained through resource synergy among partners [49]. For instance, due to the risk of supply disruptions, firms usually keep safety stocks of key materials. From the perspective of relationship marketing, Morgan and Hunt [58] empirically tested the relationship between relationship commitment and cooperation between trading partners. They found that in the context of retailer and distributor relationships, relationship commitment positively influences acquiescence and cooperation. From the perspective of supply chain integration, Zhao et al. [100,101] found that relationship commitment helps firms to achieve external integration. Zacharia et al. [97] found that supply chain partner insight improves supply chain collaboration, which in turn leads to operational and business performance improvement. From the perspective of organizational learning, relationship commitment can help a firm to develop its coordination capabilities through learning in the relationship. In this study, we develop a full and rich understanding of how this relationship factor influences supply chain coordination. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 3a. Relationship commitment to suppliers is positively related to coordination with suppliers.

Hypothesis 3b. Relationship commitment to customers is positively related to coordination with customers.

In addition to the enablers of IT, relationship commitment, and coordination, factors such as firm size can also influence supply chain performance. Larger firms tend to own more resources and thus may have better supply chain performance than small firms. Therefore, we use firm size as a control variable. ## 3. Configuration approach to supply chain coordination

Another useful perspective to address the combination of resources across organizational boundaries is the configuration approach. Configuration theory describes an organization as a set of interrelated activities [22] and emphasizes the establishment of patterns that reflect the alignment of strategies and systems or practices [56]. Thus, we need to articulate patterns of supply chain relationships and IT resources. The configuration perspective proposes that when organizational practices are consistent with each other, a holistic configuration analysis should be adopted [56,92], whereas the IT value literature commonly considers only one side of the relationship, either the supplier side or the customer side [98]. This approach is useful for handling the synergistic activities and relationships in supply chain coordination [26]. For example, Morash [57] classified supply chain capabilities into supply-side and demand-side capabilities and posited that supplyside capabilities focus on operational excellence, whereas demandside capabilities focus on customer closeness.

One important question about the configuration of coordination in a supply chain is whether coordination on the supplier side and customer side should be balanced or unbalanced to achieve better performance. Because different firms may have different levels of coordination, various configurations of supply chain coordination exist. The patterns can be defined in terms of the strength and balance of the supply chain coordination. The strength refers to the level or extent of supply chain coordination, whereas the balance refers to the extent to which a firm has a similar level of supply chain coordination for the supplier and customer sides. Firms may not possess equal supply chain resources and capabilities. Although some may have a strong capability for customers, others may have a strong capability for suppliers. The question is important for decision makers because they need insights into how to configure strategies for pursuing supply chain management through IT or relationship building. However, the literature is notably lacking a taxonomy of supply chain coordination based on the patterns of coordination with suppliers and customers.

Following the configuration logic, we argue that supply chain performance should be achieved from both supplier and customer sides to form a complete view of supply chain operations. A firm that has more relationship resources and IT resources with both suppliers and customers is more capable than a firm that has fewer such resources. Firms with more supply chain resources and capabilities are more likely to have better supply chain performance. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 4. The patterns of supply chain coordination are related to supply chain performance.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Sampling and data collection

We reviewed the literature to obtain valid measures for the constructs and adapted extant scales to measure relationship commitment [58], IT resources [59], supply chain coordination [59], and supply chain performance [7]. All items were measured using a seven-point Likert scale, with higher values indicating more resources, stronger coordination, or better performance. The indicators are provided in Appendix B. Firm size was measured by three items: the number of employees, fixed assets, and total sales. We used two steps to assess the questionnaire. First, we submitted the questionnaire to academicians and supply chain executives for review. Next, we pre-tested it in a sample of fifteen firms. Then, we revised the items based on their feedback to ensure that they were relevant to the practices.

To obtain a representative sample, we randomly selected firms located in four cities in mainland China (Chongqing, Tianjin, Guangzhou, and Shanghai) from the China Telecom yellow pages and firms located in Hong Kong from the directory of the Chinese Manufacturer Association. These five cities represent differing levels of economic and geographic diversity in China. The firms selected represent a wide range of industries, including chemicals and petrochemicals, building materials, rubber and plastics, metals, mechanical and engineering, electronics and electrical, and textiles and apparel. Further details about the sampling and data collection can be found in [26,39,101]. Of the 4569 firms contacted, 1356 agreed to receive our questionnaire. After several followups, we received 617 valid questionnaires from key informants such as supply chain managers, CEOs, presidents, senior executives, vice presidents, senior directors, and senior managers. The response rate was 13.5% based on the number of firms initially contacted, or 45.5% based on the number of questionnaires distributed.

To assess the possibility of a late response bias, we compared the early and late responses on their physical assets, annual sales, and number of employees using t-tests [2] and found no significant differences. Because there was a single informant per firm, the potential for common method bias was assessed. Harmon’s singlefactor test of common method bias [33,68,69] revealed seven distinct factors with eigenvalues above 1.0, explaining 72.2% of the total variance. The first factor explained 29.3%, not the majority, of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was also used to test Harman’s single-factor model [74]. The model fit indices of x<sup>2</sup> (299) = 5593.66, NNFI = 0.69, CFI = 0.72, RMSEA = 0.22, and SRMR = 0.15 were unacceptable, suggesting that the single factor model was not acceptable. Thus, common method bias was not a problem in this study.

To further understand the relationships among resources, coordination, and performance in the supply chain context, we tested the mediating effects of supply chain coordination on the relationships between relationship commitment, IT, and supply chain performance. Following the procedure of Baron and Kenny [5], we found that the three conditions for establishing mediation were met: (1) The independent variable (relationship commitment to suppliers/customers, IT for suppliers/customers) affected the dependent variable (supply chain performance; t = 9.17, 9.79, 7.65, 6.92, respectively), (2) the independent variable (relationship commitment to suppliers/customers, IT for suppliers/customers) affected the mediating variable (supply chain coordination with suppliers/customers; t = 6.11, 8.38, 22.22, 17.25, respectively), and (3) the mediator (supply chain coordination with suppliers/ customers) affected the dependent variable (supply chain performance; t = 6.99, 9.29, respectively). We then used the Sobel test to determine whether the mediator carried the effect of the independent variable to the dependent variable [70]. The results confirmed that coordination with suppliers and customers mediated the relationship between relationship commitment to suppliers and customers, IT for suppliers and customers, and supply chain performance (Sobel z = 3.95, 5.25, 2.62, 5.96, respectively).

## 6. Discussion

6.1. The role of supply chain coordination in improving supply chain performance

Our findings indicate that supply chain coordination is able to transform supply chain resources into value for a supply chain, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [13,26,76,93]. Close coordination between a firm and its customers improves the accuracy of demand information, reducing the firm’s design and production time and responding to customer needs quickly. Because coordination with customers can leverage the intelligence of the partner, it enables firms to reduce costs, enhance value, and cope with changes quickly. Coordination with suppliers facilitates their understanding and anticipation of the firm’s needs to better meet its changing requirements. Information sharing about products, processes, and capabilities makes it easier for firms to produce their goods and deliver them to customers on time. Understanding the firm’s operations helps suppliers to provide good customer service.

Our finding that relationship commitment can improve performance through coordination is consistent with the findings of previous studies (e.g., [26,58,101]). With relationship commitment, firms are more likely to coordinate with their supply chain partners to improve performance. The mediation effect of supply chain coordination also implies that incorporating proper mediators can help to explain the effect of IT and relational resources on firm performance. This study provides some explanations regarding the inconsistent effects of IT on net-enabled business value [6]. The findings suggest that examining the effect of IT in a specific setting, such as a firm’s supply chain system, can help to gauge the effect of IT on firm performance [4,72,94]. The literature suggests that IT value creation plays an indirect role for IT in improving firm performance [21]. The basic logic is that IT influences other resources, which leads to competitive advantages. The improvement of the supply chain, therefore, is essentially a resource synergy along the supply chain [21]. This logic is consistent with the supply chain coordination perspective in the strategy and operations management field, which is defined as a means of combining resources and skills across organizational boundaries to create an efficient supply chain [34]. Supply chain coordination also extends the realization of a firm’s competitive advantage from intermediate levels, such as the business process or project levels, to the supply chain level [90].

We also explored a critical mechanism for supply chain coordination efficiency, i.e., resource synergy. Traditionally, although coordination with suppliers is primarily the responsibility of the purchasing function, coordination with customers is primarily the responsibility of the marketing function. These two functions usually have different departmental goals and operate independently. Our study reveals that these two functions should work together in developing supply chain coordination. This synergy enhances the effects of the coordination between the upstream and downstream supply chain on supply chain performance. Our further analyses reveal that upstream IT and relationship commitment and downstream IT and relationship commitment should also be deployed in a synergistic way to achieve the best supply chain performance.

## 6.2. The role of IT and relationship commitment in improving supply chain coordination

Our findings reveal that both relationship commitment and IT resources play an important role in the development of supply chain coordination capability; however, relationship commitment to suppliers has a significant influence on coordination with suppliers at only the 0.10 level. This means that supplier relationship management is not as important as customer relationship management in improving supply chain coordination in Chinese supply chains. One possible reason may be that the supplier-side coordination is relatively simple, particularly in the manufacturing industry, where the supply of materials is the key issue for the focal firm; therefore, the tacit knowledge gained through relationship commitment is ineffective for handling coordination issues. This finding is consistent with that of Petersen et al. [66], who observed that dependence on suppliers has no significant influence on supplier integration. In contrast, coordination on the customer side is relatively complex, particularly in the manufacturing industry, where both product and service issues are involved for the focal firm; therefore, coordination with customers requires the tacit knowledge gained through relationship commitment. This finding is consistent with that of Zhao et al. [101], who found that the supplier-side relationship is less important than the customer-side relationship. IT for both suppliers and customers directly enhances supply chain coordination, reflecting the importance of technological resources in reinforcing coordination capability. Compared with relationship commitment, IT is significantly more effective in improving supply chain coordination. However, relationship commitment, as a social resource, and IT, as a technical resource, should be combined into a socio-technical resource system to improve supply chain capability.

Theoretically, cooperative relationships between firms can bring competitive advantages [61,72], in which IT and relationship commitment are two important resources and supply chain coordination is an important capability. The firm can leverage the channel-specific IT resources to enhance information sharing and relationship commitment resources to improve relational exchange, leading to better supply chain coordination. Supply chain coordination can make it easier for the firm to integrate knowledge and relationships from its supply chain partners, which gives the firm a competitive advantage over its competitors. It can also help the firm to learn from its partners and respond to environmental changes quickly. Furthermore, the specific IT and relational assets developed over time can provide sustained competitive advantages [10].

Practically, firms should leverage both relationship commitment and IT resources to build and sustain supply chain coordination capability. In developing relationship commitment, the customerside relationship is more important than the supplier side. Firms should emphasize both social and technical resources simultaneously to gain supply chain coordination capability because these two resources represent different but complementary approaches to the coordination capability. However, for Chinese supply chains, there should be a greater emphasis on IT resources because they are more effective than relationship commitment resources in improving supply chain coordination capability.

Our study identifies IT as a technical resource and relationship commitment as a social resource and finds that both of them can improve supply chain coordination capability, which improves supply chain performance, thus further enhancing our understanding of socio-technical system theory in the supply chain management field. Our findings also contribute to supply chain management practices from the combined theoretical perspective. Managers should target different competitive objectives based on their different resource constraints, identify appropriate resources, and allocate them correctly.

## 7. Conclusions and future research

With the growing importance of IT, relationship commitment, supply chain coordination, and performance, it is essential to improve our understanding of these constructs and their interrelationships. This study contributes to the literature by proposing and empirically testing a supply chain-based IT and relationship commitment resources – coordination capability – performance model from a combined perspective of socio-technical systems theory and configuration theory. Specifically, the study contributes to the IT, relationship management, and supply chain management literature by examining the joint roles of IT and relationship commitment to both suppliers and customers in improving supply chain coordination with suppliers and customers. It contributes to the supply chain management literature by investigating the joint effects of coordination with suppliers and customers on supply chain performance. It also contributes to the IT, relationship management, and supply chain management literature by identifying supply chain coordination patterns and their resource and performance characteristics. The findings also provide some guidelines for managers to direct their managerial actions to supply chain resources and capabilities.

Although this study makes significant contributions to the literature and practices, it has some limitations and opportunities that can be addressed in future research. First, whereas this study took the manufacturer’s perspective, future research could investigate the constructs and the relationships among them concurrently from the supplier, manufacturer, and customer perspectives. Second, like many previous studies, we measured coordination with suppliers and customers in a similar manner; future research may distinguish between supplier-side and customer-side coordination. Third, we used limited items to measure IT and coordination; future research could include more items, which would provide greater insight into these aspects and the relationships among them. Finally, this study used crosssectional data. Future longitudinal studies could investigate the evolution of supply chain IT and relationship commitment resources, coordination capability, and their interactions to determine how they improve supply chain performance over time.

## Appendix B. Measurement items

## Relationship commitment to suppliers

RCS1: It is very important for our firm to maintain its relationship with our major supplier.

RCS2: The relationship that our firm has with our major supplier is something we are very committed to.

RCS3: The relationship that our firm has with our major supplier is something my firm intends to maintain indefinitely. RCS4: The relationship that my firm has with our major supplier deserves our firm’s maximum effort to maintain.

## IT for suppliers

ITS1: The level of information exchange with our major supplier through the information network.

ITS2: The establishment of a quick ordering system with our major supplier.

ITS3: Stable procurement through the network with our major supplier.

## Coordination with suppliers

CS1: The participation level of our major supplier in the process of procurement and production.

CS2: The participation level of our major supplier in the design stage.

CS3: We help our major supplier to improve its processes to better meet our needs.

## Relationship commitment to customers

RCC1: It is very important for our firm to maintain the relationship with our major customer.

RCC2: The relationship that our firm has with our major customer is something we are very committed to.

RCC3: The relationship that our firm has with our major customer is something our firm intends to maintain indefinitely.

RCC4: The relationship that our firm has with our major customer deserves our firm’s maximum effort to maintain.

## IT for customers

ITC1: The level of linkage with our major customer through the information network.

ITC2: The level of computerization for our major customer’s orders.

## Coordination with customers

CC1: The level of communication with our major customer.

CC2: Follow-up with our major customer for feedback

CC3: The frequency of periodical contacts with our major customer.

## Supply chain performance

SCP1: Our supply chain has the ability to quickly modify products to meet customers’ requirements.

SCP2: Our supply chain allows us to quickly introduce new products into the markets

SCP3: The length of the supply chain process is getting shorter. SCP4: We are satisfied with the speediness of the supply chain process.

SCP5: Based on our knowledge of the supply chain process, we think that it is efficient.

SCP6: Our supply chain has an outstanding on-time delivery record.

SCP7: Our supply chain provides a high level of customer service.

Xiande Zhao is professor of Operations and Supply Chain Management at China-Europe International Business School.
