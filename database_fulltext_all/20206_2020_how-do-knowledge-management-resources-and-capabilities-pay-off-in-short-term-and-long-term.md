## ABSTRACT

How sustainable are the financial payoffs obtained from knowledge management (KM) resources and capabilities? To illuminate this issue, we use multiyear, firm-level survey data, as basis in examining the extent to which KM resources and capabilities influence a firm’s financial performance is more pronounced in the long term than in the short term. By contrast, knowledge management systems (KMS) is associated only with immediate, short-term financial benefits with such gains prone to instability in the long term. Interestingly, knowledge stock (KS) in and of itself exerts neither short-term nor long-term impacts on a firm’s performance. However, both KMS and KS indirectly affect a firm’s long-term performance by virtue of KM capability.

Keywords: KM resources, KM capabilities, KMS, knowledge stock, firm performance, multiyear analyses

## 1. Introduction

The increasing instrumental function of knowledge in the development of new products and services, as well as the need to competently respond to turbulent business environments, has prompted the recognition of knowledge management (KM) as an important organizational initiative in pursuit of competitive advantages [1-3]. Serving as catalysts for KM initiatives, KM resources and capabilities facilitate the implementation of many KM processes, including the creation, sharing, and preservation of organizational knowledge. Although the intangible facets of KM initiatives, such as KM capabilities (e.g., organizational learning), are indispensable to firms competitive advantage, the tangible elements of KM initiatives, such as knowledge management systems (KMS)<sup>1</sup> and knowledge stock (KS)<sup>2</sup>, enable firms to unleash their latent capabilities and enhance their performance [4].

Recently, KMS has transmuted into various forms on account of emerging platforms, such as electronic repositories and forums, social media channels, and business intelligence tools. These decisions [5]. In addition to KM resources, KM capabilities have also been identified as key drivers of core competences and competitive advantages; KM capabilities denote a firm’s abilities to foster knowledge sharing among its employees, access internal and external knowledge sources, and create new knowledge to respond to market dynamics [6].

Although research on KM has proliferated in consonance with the process’ increasing strategic importance, the issues pertaining to the performance implications of KM resources and capabilities continue to challenge the ability of information systems (IS) scholars to disentangle the dynamic role of knowledge in this context [7,8]. Despite the development of numerous theoretical frameworks and the wealth of empirical manifestations, research findings specifically germane to KM performance at the firm level are infrequent, disparate, and somewhat equivocal. For example, Tanriverdi [6] demonstrates that information technology (IT) relatedness, i.e., the extent of using IT resources, positively affects a firm’s ability to create, transfer, integrate, and leverage knowledge across business units, thereby improving firm performance. By contrast, Haas and Hansen [9] argue, on the basis of a situated performance view, that heavy reliance on IT-based KM resources may backfire with respect to a firm’s competitive positioning and eventually deteriorate its performance. Moreover, extant studies have seldom investigated the sustainable value of IT-based KM resources and capabilities, thus failing to comprehend how they affect a firm’s performance over time.

#

In keeping with the call to argument in existing scholarship, this study aims to enhance our understanding of the impact of KM resources and capabilities on firm performance by answering the following key research questions: (1) What are the key IT-based KM resources and capabilities that are associated with firm performance? (2) How and to what extent does each of the KM resources and capabilities influence firm performance over time?

To answer these questions, first, we identify and elaborate the key IT-based KM resources and KM capabilities in a nomological model that demarcates the trajectory of KM-related valuecreation. We then assess the dynamic interplay among these prime KM constructs and their associations with firm performance. The specific questions to be addressed are as follows: Does a firm’s performance depend on the quality of KMS (e.g., availability, accessibility, stability, and response time)? Alternatively, is it the quality of KS (e.g., accuracy, relevance, and reliability) captured in KMS that actually affects firm performance? How do these KM resources determine a firm’s KM capability in terms of creating, finding, and sharing knowledge? Finally, to what extent does KM capability affect a firm’s financial performance?

Second, we consider the time factor by examining the sustainability of performance benefits accrued from the deployment of KM resources and capabilities. The majority of previous research on IT-based KM initiatives has focused on either case-based anecdotes or the cross-sectional value of KMS adoption, and little effort has been extended to a systematic probing of the upon the economic rent creation mechanisms [11], this study examines how KM resources and capabilities influence their respective levels of performance over time. To the best of our knowledge, no study has traced the intensity with which firms have infused investment into KM resources and capabilities, analyzed the dynamic connection between KM resources and capabilities, and explored their performance implications over time. The current research attempts to redress this oversight.

Third, a considerable research stream has explored the value of KM resources at the individual level, primarily inquiring as to the extent to which KM resources influence individuals task-related satisfaction and performance [12], as well as intention to share knowledge [13]. Although valid and useful, individual-centered investigations are silent about KM resources financial performance at the firm level. Such research thus fails to provide managers with a holistic and pragmatic understanding of the economic value of KM resources and capabilities. To fill this void, we employ financial measures (e.g., sales and ROI) as the principal yardsticks by which to gauge their performance.

The core findings from our research suggest that both KMS and KS are significant correlates of KM capability. Nonetheless, KMS is positively associated only with short-term financial performance, and the benefits derived tend to disappear over a long time horizon. Contrastingly, KM capability generates positive long-term payoffs. Interestingly, KS alone exerts no direct impact on either short-term or long-term value creation. However, it indirectly influences firm performance by virtue of KM capability. These results collectively suggest that firm performance does not hinge on how much firms know, but on how effectively they internalize and mobilize KM resources and how successfully they build and sustain KM capability over time.

## 2. Literature Review on Influencing Factors of KM

Many prior studies have focused on identifying influencing factors of successful KM in organizations [3,14-19]. While one stream of research in this area has proposed a more general perspective on the influencing factors, another stream has focused more on the role of IT-based initiatives in organizational KM.

## 2.1. General Influencing Factors of KM Success

Based on an in-depth review of existing KM literature, Lee and Choi [17] summarized common key factors of KM assimilation, including knowledge workers, technical knowledge

infrastructure, internal knowledge climate, knowledge management process, and external knowledge linkage. Frank et al. [16] further provided a systematic review of the extant literature on influencing factors of knowledge transfer among new product development teams and found four socio-technical subsystems consisting of personnel, technological, work design, and external environment. Ghobadi [18] also reviewed important knowledge-sharing drivers in software development context and proposed a classification framework. The framework categorizes the knowledge-sharing drivers into people-, task-, structure-, and technology-related drivers. These studies have provided useful, yet broader perspectives on the general influencing factors for various aspects of organizational KM activities and processes. However, the studies in this stream have a limitation in investigating the dynamic role of specific factors, like IT-based KM resources and capabilities, in firm-level performance implications.

## 2.2. Role of IT-based KM Resources and Capabilities

Unlike the studies focused on general influencing factors, other researchers have paid their attention specifically to the role of IT in KM as IT has been believed to play a critical role on organization’s ability to manage knowledge. Depending on how the role of IT is theorized and empirically substantiated, the studies surrounding the resource-based perspective of IT in relation to KM can be classified into three broad categories. The first category views IT as one of the Although this group of studies recognizes the importance of IT in KM, IT alone is deemed insufficient for maximizing firms’ KM performance [3,19-21]. For example, Gold et al. [3] examined the relationship between organizational effectiveness and knowledge infrastructures constructed on the basis of structure, culture, and technology. Identifying a wide range of antecedents that determine KM performance, such as knowledge workers, organizational culture, and IT support, research in this first category indicates that IT must coordinate with other strategic resources to influence KM and firm performance.

In contrast to the first category of studies in which IT was considered an important but auxiliary resource for KM, the second category studies consider IT an integral and central aspect of KM capability and firm performance [6,22-24]. For example, Mehta et al. [24] identified that the intensity of IT use has positive effect on knowledge exchange as well as combination. The studies in the second category indicate that IT resources (e.g., IT infrastructure, IT HR management process, and IT objects) alone can account for much of the variability in predicting KM capability and firm performance.

The studies in the third category concentrate on the factors responsible for KMS usage and the dynamics inherent in the interplay between KMS, KM capability, and firm performance [10,25,26]. For example, Bock et al. [25] developed and validated a KMS success model in which the extent of KMS usage could be predicted by the perceived KMS searchability, KMS output quality, KMS usefulness, and user satisfaction. The studies in this third category indicate that it is not the possession but the use of KMS that is critical to the success of KM initiatives.

This study follows the research streams of the second and third categories and examines the role of IT-based KM resources and capabilities in organizations. In particular, we examine the roles of KMS, KS accumulated in KMS, and KM capability as the key IT-based KM factors in inducing superior firm performance. Through this study, moreover, we theorize and test their relationships and dynamic values in short-term and long-term firm performance.

## 3.1. Knowledge-Based View and IT-Based KM Initiatives

The resource-based view (RBV) has spawned a wealth of research theorizing with regard to the value of organizational resources. RBV posits that organizational resources encompass both asset-type resources (e.g., physical assets) and capability-type resources (e.g., human capital) [11,27,28]. According to Wade and Hulland [29], resources can be defined as “anything tangible or intangible the firm can use in its processes for creating, producing, and/or offering its products (goods or services) to a market,” while capabilities denote “repeatable patterns of actions in the use of assets to create, produce, and/or offer products to a market” (p. 109). Researchers concur that various forms of IT resources (e.g., IT infrastructure, IT personnel, and IT-enabled knowledge) serve as strategic assets necessary for conceiving business value [30,31]. Drawing upon RBV, KM studies (e.g., [1-4]) have attempted to examine the organizational value of KM resources (e.g., KMS, KM teams, and codified knowledge) and KM capabilities (e.g., learning capability and dynamic capability) in diverse contexts. However, no comprehensive understanding has emerged as to the valuation and appropriation of KM resources and capabilities.

IT has long been touted as a boon to managing knowledge and enhancing KM capabilities and firm performance [6,22,23]. For example, Choi et al. [23] reveal that the extent to which IT reinforces KM practices in organizations determines the development of effective transactive memory systems (TMS). The authors also state that both IT and TMS make a positive difference in knowledge sharing and application. A similar perspective is espoused by Tanriverdi [6] in his assessment of how a repertoire of IT resources deployed within a firm boundary stimulates KM capability and firm performance. Tippins and Sohi [22] find that organizational learning mediates the effects of IT competency on firm performance. By and large, these studies suggest that IT resources (e.g., IT infrastructures, IT HR management processes, and IT objects) can account for the variability in predicting KM capability and firm performance. All in all, previous studies on IT-based KM initiatives propose to investigate the interplay among KM resources, KM capability, and firm performance.

In this study, we investigate the strategic value of two important IT-based KM resources— KMS and KS—which are also the two major quality dimensions of information systems. Our examination focuses specifically on the performance of the KMS implemented by a firm and the substance of the KS stored in KMS [32]. The organizational value of KMS has long been articulated and recognized as a positive influence on firm performance [1,10]. KMS empowers firms by enabling access to the organizational knowledge within and often across their corporate boundaries, thereby elevating efficiency through the reuse of accumulated knowledge and intellectual capital [12]. KMS improves firm value and performance [5], as well as solidifies a firm’s competitive position by stimulating organizational knowledge flow [33]. Knowledge codification through KMS renders organizational knowledge more concrete and agile, thus facilitating its dissemination, transformation, and application [34]. These KM components all translate to firm performance [35]. Nevertheless, they are also surrounded and threatened by uncertainties and vulnerabilities, such as the difficulty associated with KMS success, the multitude of factors that inhibit such success [25], and the time it needed for KMS benefits to materialize [7,8]. Studies on these issues foreground the cruciality of KMS quality to its successful implementation. We also examine the quality of the knowledge captured, codified, and stored in KMS as another essential IT-based KM resource. Although the knowledge accumulated by KMS is limited to the explicit characteristics of organizations’ intellectual assets, some scholars often argue that such knowledge nonetheless affects firm performance [33,36].

Whereas traditional RBV studies highlight the strategic merit of individual organizational resources, recent studies tend to underscore the high-level organizational capabilities (e.g., dynamic capabilities) that are driven by organizational resources (e.g., [22,37]). In particular, scholars maintain that a firm’s dynamic capabilities to manage organizational IT and other resources are the idiosyncratic sources of organizational value creation [37,38]. This perspective argue that a firm’s competitive advantage emanates from its internal processes, thereby enabling the firm to build high-level dynamic capabilities [11,37].

Consistent with this perspective, several KM researchers draw attention to the importance of KM-related high-level organizational capabilities, such as knowledge-production and knowledgeexchange [39], absorptive capacity [40], learning capabilities [41], and the capability to explore knowledge specific to a given environment [33]. We expand this viewpoint by inquiring into the strategic value of a firm’s KM capability as a high-level dynamic competency for designing and implementing organizational KM activities. The extant literature introduces a hierarchical view of the relationships among organizational resources, capabilities, and outcomes [27]. According to Grant [27], a firm’s low-level resources (e.g., knowledge assets and technology infrastructure) determine its functional (e.g., marketing and manufacturing) and cross-functional (e.g., new product development) capabilities. Numerous studies adopt this view of hierarchical relationships among resources, capabilities, and outcomes [19,42]. Similarly, Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [43] posit that a firm’s ability to create competitive advantage is a function of capability, which is influenced by human and technological resources.

Reflecting on this hierarchical standpoint, we can regard IT-based KM resources as a firm’s low-level assets that can also be thought to form the firm’s high-level capability to develop and carry out organizational KM activities, i.e., KM capability. In particular, KMS facilitates the realization of organizational KM activities while KS serves as the source of such events. These arguments form the basis of our hypotheses on the relationship between IT-based KM resources and organizational KM capability:

H1: High-quality KMS will lead to a high level of organizational KM capability.

H2: High-quality KS will lead to a high level of organizational KM capability.

## 3.2. Short-Term Value Creation by KM Resources

The strategic management literature has discussed two distinctive mechanisms with regard to economic rent creation: resource-picking and capability-building mechanisms [11]. The resourcepicking mechanism creates economic rent when firms build a competitive edge by acquiring strategically valuable resources more economically and effectively than do their rivals. Conversely, the capability-building mechanism creates rent when firms possess a more solid capability to enhance the productivity of their existing resources than do their competitors. Even as the two mechanisms are not necessarily independent within a firm and may complement each other [11], their effects on sustainable competitive advantage vary.

In congruence with this perspective, researchers have regarded organizational KM resources (e.g., KMS) as strategic sources for superior firm performance [2]. However, the value of organizational resources achieved through the resource-picking mechanism is transitory especially in today’s dynamic market environments. This short-term nature is ascribed to the progressive ease with which these environmental context resources are accessed their value, thus, cannot endure over time [46]. According to the resource-picking mechanism, therefore, value creation through IT-based KM resources is also transitory and short-term oriented.

Makadok [11] asserts that a firm’s competitive advantage derives from its selection and possession of strategically valuable resources, such as KMS. Accordingly, studies have indicated that the successful implementation of KMS is central to enterprise success because KMS can provide strategic value to a firm by enriching intellectual capital and enabling the reuse of attendant codification of knowledge thus improve firm performance [35]. Notwithstanding the foregoing, however, the sustainability of KMS value in today’s dynamic and fast-changing business environment is open to question [46]. Recent studies on IT resources contend that technologies per se do not provide sustainable value to a firm because competitors may easily duplicate individual innovations [30,47]. Using these arguments as bases, we develop our hypotheses for short-term (immediate) value creation by IT-based KM resources as follows:

H3: The quality of an adopted KMS will exert a positive short-term effect on firm performance.

H4: The quality of KS will exert a positive short-term effect on firm performance.

Dynamic capabilities under the capability-building mechanism can generate more sustained impacts on organizational performance by enabling firms to constantly renew, reconfigure, and recreate the requisite resources and capabilities for responding to environmental changes [46,48]. Thus, this capabilitybuilding mechanism helps firms continually develop new competitive activities and subsequently create a series of competitive advantages [49]. Moreover, dynamic capabilities are more idiosyncratic to a firm given that these are embedded in its business processes and developed through complex interactions with other resources within the firm. Such abilities are therefore also more causally ambiguous and inimitable [44,50]. Thus, through the capability-building mechanism, dynamic capabilities empower firms to achieve a greater competitive advantage over time, i.e., long-term advantage [46,49,51].

Organizational KM capability has been treated as a dynamic capability to convert existing organization’s KM initiatives and thus as a guarantee of sustainable competitiveness. A firm with strong KM capability can sustainably impede competitors’ efforts to replicate its business strategies and undertakings [53]. In other words, a firm’s idiosyncratic management of KM activities through KMS and KS can function as a valuable source of long-term competitiveness [1,8]. This outlook parallels the predominant view in the strategic management literature, which postulates that organizational capabilities are formed on the basis of firm-specific combinations of organizational assets and business routines [50], known as capability hierarchy [54]. Such high-level organizational capabilities are difficult to imitate [27,48], thus making a firm’s competitive advantage resist erosion by its competitors—long-term competitive advantage [55]. In congruence with this hierarchical view of organizational resources and capabilities, we espouse the view that organizational KM capability exerts a long-term effect on firm performance.

Our position is grounded on the fact that KM capability is an organizational high-level dynamic capability that empowers a firm to respond to market opportunities and threats through effective sharing of organizational knowledge and the creation of new, requisite knowledge by combining internal and external know-how [42]. Relating to this, a firm’s learning capability on its market has been discussed as a key source of organizational innovations to achieve long-term competitive advantage [56]. Moreover, a firm characterized by solid KM capability can reduce costs and eliminate ineffective business practices by repeatedly adjusting business processes [57]. Superior KM capability may, therefore, enable a firm to continue to outperform its competition by helping the firm timely and pertinently renew or adjust its business practices and products when new threats or opportunities arise. On the basis of these assertions, we develop our hypothesis on long-term value creation through KM capability as follows:

H5: Organizational KM capability will exert a positive long-term effect on firm performance.

## 4. Research Methods

## 4.1. Research Design

To validate our hypotheses, survey-based data with multiyear firm performances were collected from large- and medium-sized companies whose executives were enrolled in the “Chief Knowledge Officer Program” offered by Knowledge Management Research Center of a university in South Korea. At the time of this study, these firms implemented various stages of KM initiatives, which makes our research setting ideal for testing the proposed framework and hypotheses. To define target companies for our survey, we included diverse groups of firms and industries to maximize the diversity of the variables and, hence, increase the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, several criteria were applied to accurately select the target samples. First, the target samples were limited to companies that had started enterprise-wide KM initiatives for their business. Second, the target samples were limited to medium- to large-size companies because they can afford large investments on enterprise-wide KM activities, resources, and capabilities. After these initial framing procedures, target companies were invited to participate in the enterprise-wide surveys regarding their KM activities and outcomes.

The original survey questionnaires were sent to multiple respondents in various positions (including top executives, department managers and directors, middle managers, senior employees, and entry-level employees) who were directly involved in organizational KM activities mainly as users. In self-reported survey methods, common method variance has often been criticized as potentially reducing the validity of data [58]. To avoid this issue, our survey adopted a triangulation approach in which data were gathered from multiple respondents and secondary sources [59]. More specifically, survey questionnaires were sent to multiple respondents in the target firms who were involved in their KM activities. In addition, secondary multiyear financial data, such as sales revenues and profitability, were collected separately using one of the largest financial database (Yonhap Infomax) in South Korea, to evaluate sample firms multiyear financial performance in an objective fashion.

For this particular study, however, we considered only the survey responses from individuals holding managerial, not entry-level, positions to improve the validity of the perceived evaluations on enterprise-wide KM resources and capabilities. Through a large-scale multiyear survey, finally, we achieved a total of 96 complete firm-level samples from 50 companies. The responses from a total of 1,074 manager-level respondents in the sample firms were aggregated to develop our final firm-level data samples. Considering that the original surveys were large-field surveys involving multiple respondents through multiple years and that we also combined subjective survey data and objective financial data, the final sample size (n=96) is considered a respectable one.<sup>3</sup>

Regarding the main business areas, 47 of the 96 samples are categorized as manufacturing (e.g., consumer electronics, automotive, semiconductor, chemicals, biological products, etc.) and 49 samples as nonmanufacturing (e.g., telecom service, healthcare, banking, insurance, marketing, transportation, energy, and consulting). We used the partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling technique to test the proposed model. ## 5.1. Measurement Model Evaluation

To develop our firm-level measurement model, responses from multiple respondents holding managerial positions of the sample firms were aggregated for each item through the following procedures. Frist, among the multiple survey responses from various positions in each of the sample firms, we extracted only the responses from managerial, not entry-level, positions to improve the validity of the perceived evaluations on enterprise-wide KM resources and capabilities. Following Venkatraman and Grant [70], second, we averaged the multiple responses from a sample firm for each measurement item. Third, the level of agreement on each construct among all respondents of each sample, i.e., inter-rater reliability - r<sub>wg(j)</sub>, was tested to justify the threshold (0.70) recommended by Miles and Huberman [72]. After developing the firm-level aggregated data samples, psychometric properties of the reflective multiple-indicator constructs were tested in terms of convergent and discriminant validity [68,69,73].

## 6. Discussion

Although KM resources and capabilities have recently become entrenched concepts in business ecosystem, empirical evidence that support their longitudinal value has been sparse. In an endeavor to address this void, this study discerns the short-term and long-term effects of ITbased KM resources and capabilities on firm performance and uncovers the dynamic patterns of recursive relationships among KM resources, capabilities, and firm performance. To empirically evaluate the longitudinal performance effects of IT-based KM resources and capabilities, we analyze multiyear survey and financial data gathered from medium- and large-sized companies that implemented enterprise-level KM initiatives over time. Although previous studies have been performed to link KM resources and capabilities with firm performance (e.g., [20,21]), few empirical studies have been conducted to identify the effects of IT-based KM resources and KM resources and capability to support sustainable competitive advantage is critical to implement firm’s strategy formulation process [27], empirical research is of necessity to support their longitudinal value. By identifying longitudinal value of IT-based KM resources and capability, this study can contribute to the existing literature that have investigated only short-term effects of KM resources and capabilities on firm performance.

## 6.1. Research Implications

#

The empirical regularities we found offer several renewed avenues from which to comprehend the business value of KM resources and capabilities. As previously indicated, KM capability affects firm performance to a more marked extent in the long term than in the short term, suggesting that the benefits accrued from KM capability tend to materialize more strongly several years after investments in such capabilities are made. A reversal was observed in the case of KMS, which exhibited only short-term positive impacts on firm performance. The quality of KMS—referring to the degree to which KMS are available, accessible, ease to use, stable, and responsive—was associated with the immediate, short-term financial benefits, but such gains were found to be short lived and prone to instability in the long term. Interestingly, contrary to our expectations, the quality of KS in and of itself has neither short-term nor long-term effects on a firm’s financial performance. However, both KS and KMS indirectly influenced a firm’s longterm performance by virtue of KM capability.

One of the key findings intimates that as a high-level organizational capability, KM capability sustains superior performance, i.e., higher profitability. This finding thus empirically supports the argument for guaranteeing sustainable competitiveness through organizational dynamic capabilities [54,79,80]. The results also indicate that KM resources serve as organizational elements critical to developing and maintaining a firm’s enterprise-wide KM capability. In addition to its role in reinforcing KM capability, KMS as an IT-based KM resource produces short-term benefits in the form of increased sales, although such payoffs do not persist beyond the short-term time horizon. Controversy surrounds the sustainability of organizational KMS (e.g., [7,19]); our findings reconcile diverging perceptions in that they augment the strength of evidence for the distinction of such systems as contributory factors to enterprise sustainability. Specifically, the results delineate the particular trajectories through which KMS generates both short-term (through the direct path) and long-term (through high-level capability building) benefits at the firm level. Interestingly, the effects of both KMS and KM capability on a firm’s second-year performance outweigh the same effects in relation to the first-year performance. In line with the “lead-time effect” for IT value creation [81], this finding suggests that the value of KMS can increase in significance through both direct and indirect paths after some passage of time.

Surprisingly, however, KS alone has neither an immediate nor a long-term effect on firm association exists between codified knowledge and firm performance. Elucidating the implications of this negative relationship may necessitate an alternative perspective. Three potential counter-standpoints that are consistent with this unexpected finding are available despite an abiding espousal and appreciation of the merits of organizational knowledge in the strategic management literature [82]. First, KS stored in KMS is generally linked to codified or explicit knowledge [25]. The situated performance view postulated by Haas and Hansen [9] suggests that this type of knowledge may present insufficient value and often results in unfavorable consequences, particularly when used by highly experienced teams [83]. The authors go on to state that the use of KMS can drive firms to depend on decontextualized and encoded knowledge in accomplishing routine tasks that guide their behaviors, thereby resulting in the loss of casual ambiguity<sup>9</sup> of knowledge. Given that such ambiguity deters competitor duplication or imitation of organizational knowledge [84], its loss may erode their performance advantage [85]. Second, organizational KM activities for which KMS is used may conceivably precipitate focus on exploitation of existing knowledge instead of the exploration of new knowledge; this tunnel vision-like approach breeds knowledge inertia and barriers to improving performance [86]. Third, codified knowledge may have limited utility; beyond a certain point, its value and efficacy substantially diminish. Researchers in management have similarly voiced concerns over the obsolescence of knowledge in changing business environments [87]. Given today’s dynamic business realms [37], these alternative-explanations based on the bounded nature of codified knowledge seem plausible.

The current findings bring to light several important implications for researchers and managers. First, this study extends the existing literature through the nuanced perspective enabled regarding the effects of KM resources and capabilities on firm performance over time. Several studies have reported a positive outcome from investments in KM initiatives, but others present different claims that allude to the limits of KMS in enhancing firm performance [7,19]. Some researchers have concluded that KMS impedes rather than enhances firm performance in certain situations because of the inflexibility and obsolescence inherent in codified and explicit knowledge [9]. Our longitudinal approach reconciles these polarized views by propounding a systematic understanding of the resources and reasons for the inconsistencies in earlier research. This study extends the KM literature also by distinguishing the roles of diverse IT-based KM components (e.g., KM resources and capabilities) with respect to firm performance. This theoretical extension is expected to benefit both academics and practitioners by delivering a better conceptual basis for which to grasp the strategic values of organizational KM initiatives.

Second, another contribution of this study lies in its analysis of firm-level data to discern the multiyear value of KM resources and capabilities. Previous research (e.g., [8]) has assessed the working within a single firm. Therefore, a more holistic inquiry at the firm level would provide business executives with broader strategic insights into their overall KM performance. Our firmlevel analysis suggests that managers responsible for KM initiatives should formulate agile and sustainable strategic actions over time to fully experience the benefits of their KM investments. On the one hand, organizations should capitalize on KMS to secure “quick” and immediate financial returns that increase their liquidity and operational cash flows; on the other hand, they should optimize their KM capability to stabilize long-term financial performance. Moreover, managers should be constantly cognizant that their long-term financial performance is determined not by the quality of KMS (e.g., accessibility and responsiveness) but by the extent to which their employees are willing to create, share, and distribute knowledge within their corporate confines. In this respect, executives should create a virtuous value-circle by which KM resources and capabilities are continuously mobilized and consistently complement one another for the purpose of seamlessly generating both short-term and long-term benefits.

Third, despite the promising prospects apparent in the findings on the impact of KM resources on firm performance, these should be interpreted with caution. The results indicate that codified knowledge exerts no direct effect on performance, regardless of time orientation. Similarly, the positive effect of KMS on performance was limited only to the short term. An issue worth noting, however, is that such KM resources serve as a foundation for KM capability, which plays a pivotal role in garnering long-term benefits. These results collectively suggest that competitiveness but, at the same time, realize that mere possession of such resources does not guarantee stable economic gains over time. Aside from mobilizing and economizing these KM resources, firms should proactively retain the ability to adapt, integrate, and reconfigure organizational knowledge to keep pace with the requirements of changing environments and achieve sustainable competitiveness [48]. For example, managers are encouraged to establish strategic communities of practice that motivate employees from diverse departments to share and integrate their knowledge. Such organized forums foster the creation of new knowledge and know-how that are essential for survival in a turbulent environment.

Finally, given that KM resources and capabilities can function to generate a sustained competitive advantage, managers are advised to actively identify the contextual factors necessary for promoting and mobilizing such resources and capabilities. For example, organizational rewards can encourage employees to vigorously share and distribute knowledge, resulting in enhanced KM capability. As KM capability requires a relatively longer stretch of time to influence firm performance, managers need to formulate an effective long-term plan to secure sustainable support from team members and senior executives.

## 6.2. Post-Hoc Analysis: Controlling the Stage Effects

In this study, we used multiyear survey data (n=96) that represented various stages of KM and KMS implementation of 50 companies; after gathering initial data of the companies regarding their KM initiatives, additional samples from them (not all, but most) were gathered in the following years. The current findings from this multiyear survey data set can be challenged because of the heterogeneous stages of KM and KMS implementation in our original samples (n=96). Therefore, to further validate our findings, it seems worth to control a potential effect of different stages of KM and KMS implementation in our model tests. For this, we conducted a post-hoc analysis using a new data set that consists of only the initial-year data of the sample firms (n=50). This new data set represents sample firms’ early stage of KM and KMS implementation (see Appendix II for the demographics of the new samples and the additional test results).

Overall, the test results using the initial-year data set (n=50) exhibit similar patterns observed from the original samples (n=96), showing significant relationships among KMS, KS, and KM capability. In particular, both KMS and KS were found to be significant determinants of KM capability (at least $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , supporting H1 and H2, respectively. In addition, consistent with our findings using the original data set, the impacts of IT-based KM resources and capabilities on firm performance were found to change over time. That is, KMS exhibited short-term effects, i.e., a decreasing trend after the second year, while long-term effects were more pronounced for KM capability, i.e., an increasing trend throughout the three years, supporting H3 and H5, respectively. Interestingly, in the results using the initial-year data set (n=50), especially the path coefficients of KM capability for all three-years firm performance were slightly higher than those of the original data set. In line with this, the model accounted slightly higher variations of the first-, second-, and third-year firm performance (37.2%, 34.2%, and 30.2%, respectively). Consistent with the original findings, KS was not significantly associated with firm performance across all three years, which did not support H4. Therefore, the results of our post-hoc analysis using the initial-year only data set (n=50) further validated the robustness of our findings derived from the original data set (n=96).

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research Opportunities

This study has several limitations that should be addressed in future studies. First, a total of 96 firm-level, multiyear samples were analyzed to validate the proposed model. As pointed out, this relatively small size of the sample was mainly because of the adoption of the rigorous methodological approaches for data collection, such as the multiyear, large-scaled field survey from multiple respondents and the matching of primary survey data with secondary financial data in consecutive years. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the generalizability of the findings can be further enhanced with a larger sample size. Second, as we collected data from self-declared companies whose executives were enrolled in the “Chief Knowledge Officer Program” offered by a university, a self-selection issue may arise. If certain characteristics of unobserved firms, such as R&D investment, not only enhance KM capability but also are related to firm performance, this situation may overstate the effect of KM capability on firm performance. Thus, a study that collects data considering this self-section issue will enhance the robustness of the results and findings from the study [88]. Third, the study has not considered various environmental factors that can affect firm performance, such as global or local economy and industry competition. Identifying causality using any single factors, such as KMS or KM capability, may be risky [19]. Although many studies have reported causality between KM capability and firm performance [6,23], a better picture would be emerged by introducing various control or intermediate variables into research model. Next, this study does not investigate the impacts of KMS and KS on KM capability across time, while the main objective of this study is to examine the extent to which

KM resources and capabilities influence a firm’s performance over time. However, as the value of KM resources can be decayed with time [27], a study investigating the relationship between KM resources and capabilities over time would be of interest as a future research topic. Finally, in this study, we defined firm performance in a conservative manner to represent only the financial aspect of a firm’s operation. Clearly, a focus on the intangible side of firm performance as a result of KM resources and capabilities will provide managers with a more complete story regarding the evaluation of KM performance.

## 7. Conclusion

Previous studies on KM value evaluation have frequently been criticized for their exclusive prioritization of the static nature of KM performance with data collected from individual users. The present research attempted to fill this gap by examining how KM resources and capabilities influence firm performance over time based on the basis of multiyear, firm-level data. We also theorized and empirically validated the dynamic interplays between KM resources and capabilities, outlining the specific paths and mechanisms through which each of these key KM components influences firm performance.

The results from this study indicate that KM resources and capabilities have differential impacts on firm performance over time. KMS generates short-term payoffs only, but KM capabilities yield long-term returns. Interestingly, our data point to indirect, as opposed to direct influence, of knowledge stock; that is the quality of codified knowledge does not directly influence firm performance but indirectly mediates performance through KM capability. The combined findings suggest that firm performance is not determined by how much firms know, but how effectively firms use what they know through KMS. Conversely, the benefits arising from using KMS are unsustainable over time. An attendant recommendation, therefore, is for managers to develop and cultivate enterprise-wide KM capabilities to secure long-term financial stability by means of KM initiatives. As far as KM performance is concerned, organizations should learn “how to eat a fish,” i.e., using KMS, to advance their short-term success, but more importantly, they must gain knowledge of “how to fish,” i.e., promoting KM capabilities, in the pursuit of long-term, sustainable payoffs.

## Author Biography

One-Ki Daniel Lee is an Associate Professor in the Management Science and Information Systems Department at the University of Massachusetts Boston. He received his Ph.D. from the City University of Hong Kong. His research interests include organizational IT strategy and value, IT-enabled dynamic capabilities (e.g., agility and KM capability), and global IT project risk management. His research work has appeared in Information Systems Research, Information & Management, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Communications of the ACM, and others.

Byounggu Choi is a Professor at the College of Business Administration of the Kookmin University in Seoul, Korea. He was formerly on the faculty of the School of Information Technologies at the University of Sydney. He received his Ph.D. from the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). His research interests are knowledge management, social media analytics, and business value of IT. His papers have been published in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Information & Management, and others. He serves as an editorial board member of Information & Management.

Heeseok Lee is a Professor of Strategy and IT in the College of Business at Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). He received his Ph.D. from the University of Arizona. His research interests include knowledge innovation and IT strategy. He is a recipient of Herbert Simon Award from IAITQM. His research has been published in International Journal of Information Technology and Decision Making, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Journal of Knowledge Management, Information & Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly.
