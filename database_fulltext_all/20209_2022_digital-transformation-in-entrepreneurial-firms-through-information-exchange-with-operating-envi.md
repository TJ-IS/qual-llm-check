## Abstract

Changing digital technologies and innovation threaten established business models. Increasing uncertainty in the operating environment often drives the need to transform the core business model of firms through a process of digital business transformation (DBT). In this study, we conduct a longitudinal study of two digital startups in the crowdfunding domain and identify the core attributes driving such transformation in digital ventures. We build a framework that examines how DBT takes place in entrepreneurial firms through information exchange with the environment. This research will help entrepreneurs and managers of such firms design and develop reactive business models for market success.

Keywords: Case study; Crowdfunding; Digital business transformation; Digital ventures; Hack-a-Joe Labs; Operating environment; Wishberry.

Faced with a barrage of uncertain conditions due to new technologies, shorter product life cycles and tougher competition due to easy replicability leading to an increasingly hostile business environment, strategic business model transformation becomes a key to growth and often survival, especially for entrepreneurial firms. There are no permanent solutions to business problems. Correspondingly, coping strategies to business problems are emergent too. Hence, entrepreneurial firms need to continuously evolve to adapt to keep enhancing their capabilities for immediate survival and long-term growth.

Businesses, especially digital startups, realize the need to realign their business activities with the dynamic operating conditions in their respective business environments (Davison and Ou, 2017; Dutta et al., 2014; Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2004). Business transformation embracing digital

capabilities is no more a choice but a necessity that needs to be carefully implemented throughout the business value chain (Hensen and Dong, 2019; Leonhardt et al., 2017; Urbach et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018). However, this transformation and realignment are more complicated for young firms primarily due to their lack of experience about the market and limited access to market information. The “liabilities of newness” and “liabilities of smallness” and the enormous risks involved for such firms discourage the adoption of radical digital business transformation (DBT). However, despite such risks, entrepreneurial firms have started adopting a digital-first strategy. A recent International Data Group (IDG) report<sup>1</sup> has stated that 55% of startups have already adopted a digital business strategy compared to 38% of traditional enterprises. That report also highlights how startups can increase their revenue by up to 34% by relying on digital-first strategies. These developments in the domain digital business model are primarily stimulus–response strategies, with a focus on short-term growth rather than the development of sustainable competitive leadership. Although seminal studies from scholars like Weick (1991) have examined the stimulus–response model to explore the interaction of a business with its operating environment, the process of identification of an appropriate response strategy is still missing in extant literature. That coupled with the limited attention on entrepreneurial businesses with their unique structures and capabilities makes this context worthy of further investigation. With this focus, our study examines the following research questions:

RQ: What drives digital business transformation in digital entrepreneurial firms? How do these firms transform their business model to cope with changes in the operating environment?

The limited literature on this topic, combined with the need for in-depth examination and possibility of development of rich theoretical insights, guided our choice of a qualitative multiple case study approach for this research (Yin, 2014). We studied a crowdfunding startup (Wishberry) along with another technology startup (Hack-a-Joe Labs) which was funded through crowdfunding. Our purposive choice of these two diverse organizations was integral to representing both sides of a specific market (i.e., crowdfunding) with major differences in the broad-based focus initially to a very specific mode of revenue generation in Wishberry, a crowdfunding platform based in India. Simultaneously, we studied the change in business model at Hack-a-Joe Labs which evolved from a conventional lean technology startup to a mature and focused organization in the US, financed through crowdfunding. Our study identified the dominant factors (both environmental and organizational) impacting the business model transformation process for digital entrepreneurial ventures and examined their interactions with various components of a firm’s business model proposed by earlier studies (Osterwalder, 2004; Weill & Woerner, 2013). The insights from this study pave the path for developing a theory of DBT for entrepreneurial firms.

## 1. Conceptual Foundation

## 1.1. Digital Business Model and its Transformation

Scholars have recognized the possibility of “faulty thinking and self-delusion” (Porter, 2001, p. 73) owing to the use of the business model concept and the entanglement of its meaning with other management concepts like strategy and product marketing (Yip, 2004). Rigorous attempts have been made to define and describe the concept and its integral components. In its simplest definition, it is described as a plan for engaging in business activities (Applegate, 2001; Weill and Vitale, 2001) or as a model for the representation of a business (Osterwalder, Pigneur, & Tucci, 2005; George & Bock, 2011). We concur with the definition of the business model as the “content, structure, and governance of transactions” designed to create value through the exploitation of business opportunities (Amit and Zott, 2001; Zott and Amit, 2008). With an aim to structure the meaning of this complex concept, various scholars have described the business model in terms of its diverse components. Early scholarship considered revenue architecture in the form of monetization strategy of a business, its revenue model (Linder and Cantrell, 2000) or its cost and profit model (Chesbrough and Rosenbloom, 2002), as its core component. However, scholars like Chesbrough and Rosenbaum (2002), Petrovic et al. (2001) and Afuah and Tucci (2003), have asserted value as one of the most crucial components of business model. Consequently, the value proposition and value model are used to describe the concept. The role of partners and networks in helping develop the product or service is also identified to be fundamental to the business model (Chesbrough and Rosenbloom, 2002). Moreover, all of these components are assembled in an efficient formation to provide value to the customer. Consequently, customer-centric components like relationships are asserted to be integral to the business model (Zott et al., 2011). Putting all these components together, Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) proposed the business model canvas which defined the business model to be composed of the following nine components: value propositions, customer segments, customer relationships, key resources, key activities, key partners, channels, cost structure, and revenue structure. This classification of the business model concept has been considered to be one of the most complete descriptions and has since been adopted by both academia and industry successfully. In this study it is used to identify the changes in the business model of the firms.

Any change, both incremental and radical, in a firm’s business processes is accompanied by several smaller cascading changes. That makes it difficult to fully appreciate the scale and scope of change for a firm. However, the business model canvas has provided a common yardstick to compare differences in execution of DBT. Additionally, it has helped in separating dynamic ad hoc changes in a firm’s business from a planned DBT exercise. It has been useful in identifying samples for the study as well as for identifying the scale of change experienced by the firm.

However, this admission of the meaning of business model clearly lacks any attention to information technology (IT) which has become an indispensable and core component of businesses. The role of IT in businesses has transitioned from a focus on the design of information systems to the design of IT-enabled businesses and now to the design of business models for digitally provided services. Hence, any discussion on the business model concept is incomplete without evoking the role of digital technologies in business activities. Moreover, in this newly espoused relationship between business and IT, the aligning role of digital platforms in delivering value has been overlooked (El Sawy and Pereira, 2013). Of the limited studies with this consciousness, Hanelt et al. (2015) have highlighted the role of information systems in enabling innovation leading to the creation of new digital business models.

Digital services present a novel platform for the creation of new businesses while retaining the core business logic. For example, cloud services serve the conventional need for outsourced IT infrastructure but also present a whole new business model to service large customers, especially the enterprise ones. While scholars have suggested that all organizations have equitable access to digital resources, their potential to transform conventional business practices is starting to be explored (Sedera et al., 2016). Other IT artifacts like social networks, mobile devices, and open source software have been proven central to designing new business models too. With these

insights and with complete focus on the customer value, the term “digital business model” is coined. The three components of the digital business model, i.e., content, experience, and platform, work together to create a compelling customer value proposition (Weill and Woerner, 2013). In this description, while content describes what product is consumed (both physical product or information as well as meta information), experience explains how it is packaged, thus detailing the digitized business processes leading to a superior customer value proposition. Finally, the platform governs how the product is delivered to the customer (including both physical and digital delivery channels and consideration of the logistics involved).

However, the acceptance of the digital core in the business model indicates the need for a massive transformation of the business model. In alignment to this line of inquiry, a transformational view of the business model has been adopted in various studies (Schneider & Spieth, 2013). Factors such as radical technological changes, changes in rules and regulations, culture and the broader socioeconomic settings may threaten the value of existing business altering various components of their business model. Consequently, businesses are in a state of continuous flux in response to environmental changes (especially relevant to this study are radical technological shifts). The radicality of business model transformation is distinguished on the basis of the novel skills or resources required by the firm for such change and the resultant changes in the business model introduced therein. The greater the amount of resources that are changed from the initial business model, greater is the radicality of the change.

## 1.2. Operating Environment

Business activities are guided by the operating conditions in the respective business environment (Osterwalder, 2004). Firms need to adapt and conform to the dynamism of the business environment. Moreover, these continuous adaptations manifest as a change in the business model for the firm. However, the overtly competitive, rapidly changing, and increasingly uncertain economic environment makes business decisions regarding adaptation increasingly complex and challenging. Moreover, for young entrepreneurial firms with little to no experience of the market, coupled with the limited resources at their disposal, such decisions become all the more complex. Faced with a barrage of uncertain conditions due to new technologies and processes, shorter product life cycles and tougher competition leading to an increasingly hostile business environment, survival becomes the primary focus for such firms. This along with the need to ensure legitimacy due to “liabilities of newness” and “liabilities of smallness” (Stinchcombe, 1965), restricts risky strategic maneuvers for such firms. Hence, these firms try to carefully plan

While flexibility is a desirable capability for firms in fast-changing business environments, achieving and maintaining high levels of strategic flexibility are among “the most important yet difficult tasks” for firms operating in such environments (Shimizu and Hitt, 2004, p. 45). Moreover, it is all the more difficult for young entrepreneurial firms especially in rapidly changing technology environments and with limited organizational resources. Hence, they need to choose an appropriate strategic stance between strategic flexibility supporting agility (Sambamurthy et al., 2003) or strategic persistence (Audia et al., 2000). Both of these strategies are long-term and involve significant organizational investments. Hence, such ventures need to carefully plan their adaptation strategy with sufficient information about the environmental

conditions. In response to such needs, we can explain the environmental changes in the following two states: constant yet uniform changes and discontinuous environmental changes as environmental jolts (Kelly and Amburgey, 1991). While startups learn to adapt to continuous changes in the environment easily, it is the discontinuous changes that strain their existing business strategy and force them to choose a new strategic stance. Adaptation to such discontinuous changes requires careful strategic learning from the environment and thorough introspection of the firm’s inherent capabilities.

Strategic learning and implementation of that learning require reallocation of existing resources to new and often risky business processes. When implemented successfully, these learnings “foster knowledge asymmetries that help improve the firm's competitive performance in the market” (Gupta and Bose, 2018). While there is little potential for learning from a single occurrence of an environmental jolt, there is also the risk of wastage of organizational resources on an event which is not expected to occur again. Hence, strategic learning for competitive advantage requires identification of repeatable environmental jolts. This corresponds to the same-different model of stimulus–response as proposed by Weick (1991) in which he asserts that it may occur for reasons other than organizational learning. While there is a minimal scope of learning in the context of environmental jolts, primarily due to the rarity of such occurrences in traditional organizations, entrepreneurial strategies due to lack of rigid firm structures, promote learning in such instances (Meyer, 1982). The similarity of stimulus for repeatable environmental jolts adapts entrepreneurial firms to such changes while simultaneously helping them develop sources of competitive supremacy. This adaptation is a result of the permeability of the environment and active scanning by the firm to ensure information flow from the environment into the firm boundaries and vice versa.

## 1.3. Strategic Business Information Exchange

Ackoff (1989) has described information as useable data, inferences from data or data descriptions. This information can emanate from a variety of sources from both within and outside an organization and is also processed at multiple levels before being used for decision situations. However, the organization may lack access to some of the information that is critical for its strategic decision-making. As such, information behaves like any other resource for a business (Barney, 1991) and can form the basis for developing a competitive advantage.

Information is consumed both as a resource and as a commodity in various organizational functions at operational, tactical, and strategic levels. Information is a critical factor that determines growth and prosperity for all businesses. Likewise, information flow is considered to be the lifeblood of all critical entrepreneurial processes like new product planning and development (Eppinger, 2001). This movement or flow is ascribed to the logic of distributed systems that are made up of agents and the relationship between them. In simpler terms, information flows only through a predefined set of rules between separated systems which are connected or related in some way to each other (Barwise and Seligman, 1999). Information flows both within an organization and from outside its boundaries to inside it. However, this flow is governed by access to the various sources of information resources across the value chain (Choe, 2008). Hence, information acquisition is a crucial organizational activity.

Information acquisition involves environmental scanning, intelligence activities, and the importation of resulting information into the firm. As part of their active environment scanning and monitoring activities, firms look for information that can help them better meet the needs of their customers, manage their risk-taking, as well as challenge their competitors (Keh et al.,

2007). Structured information systems help firms balance their profit maximization objectives with end-user requirements of information systems (Marsden and Pingry, 1988). While larger firms have the required slack resources needed for such scanning and development of effective information systems, smaller entrepreneurial firms do not have them. Hence, entrepreneurs need to depend on both formal and informal sources of such information which may be useful for thei strategy development based on their understanding of the existing business conditions and forecast of future environmental changes. In this study, we focus on the information seeking behavior of the entrepreneurial organization in their adaptation to the operating environment. ## 2. Research Methodology

The case study methodology is used to examine a phenomenon in its natural setting by multiple data collection methods. Case studies offer an in-depth understanding of contemporary

phenomenon within their organizational context (Yin, 2014). This methodology is well suited to understand the nature and complexity of a phenomenon under such contexts. This strategy also offers a good starting point for theory building. With an exclusive focus on theory development, the qualitative case study methodology was chosen for this study. Further to ensure analytical generalization of the theory through replication, multi-sited, multiple case study approach was chosen for this study (Yin, 2014). The replication logic used in multiple case studies is analogous (Dibbern et al., 2008). Cross-case analysis across the case studies ensures analytical generalization which is necessary for rigorous theory building using the case study methodology (Eisenhardt, 1989).

Our purposive choice of the two diverse organizations helped us represent both sides of a specific market (i.e., crowdfunding) with major differences in the way business model change occurred in them. This choice was central to our aim of development of emergent theory from case study evidence (Eisenhardt, 1989). We analyzed the business model change from a very broad-based focus to a very specific mode of financing in Wishberry, a crowdfunding platform in India. It incrementally transformed from a wedding gift registry service to a rewards-based crowdfunding platform. Its pivot was guided by its learning from the operating environment and was reactionary in nature. Not all components of the business model witnessed major changes. Simultaneously, we studied the business model change at Hack-a-Joe Labs which transformed completely from a conventional lean technology startup in the US to a mature and focused organization financed through crowdfunding platforms like Indiegogo. Table 1 provides more details about the firms. Therefore, the transformation in Hack-a-Joe Labs was radical, as against Wishberry where it was incremental. The radicality of business model change was determined on the basis of the amount of reinvestment involved in the existing business model to ensure the change. Greater the reinvestment, greater was the radicality of the change. The choice of the two cases of business model transformation in conjunction with crowdfunding provided a holistic understanding of the phenomenon of change in a single domain. It also helped elicit the different perspectives from both sides of the entrepreneurial investment market with the introduction of a novel technology-backed strategy (i.e., crowdfunding) and thus helped untangle the closely intertwined phenomenon from its context. While choosing these case studies in two different markets with different levels of maturity of the entrepreneurial ecosystem, we were careful to also notice that there were no major systemic changes in the regulatory environment that spurred the transformation in both the firms. Both the firms operated in an environment lacking specific rules and regulations to ease this innovative form of entrepreneurial investment.

The data collection process and analysis of the same adhered to the essential elements of case study methodology as outlined by Yin (2014). The analytical framework presented earlier provided the guiding theory for data collection and the overall research design (Klein and Myers, 1999). Although it is ideal to start with a clean theoretical slate in theory building research such as ours, we chose to start the research design with some potentially important variables from extant literature. This approach of starting a priori with a theoretical framework is based on the “retroduction” strategy which describes the interplay of deductive and inductive research as can be seen in previous studies (Dibbern et al., 2008; Krishnamoorthi and Mathew, 2018).

As part of an ongoing project, one of the authors had access to the founders and the executive team of both firms chosen for this study. Beyond the core management team, other case study informants were recruited using the snowballing technique based on information potential and their role in the transformation process of the subject. Apart from interviewing the key participants of the initial and revised value chain for both the firms, we were given access to the firm’s mentors, customers and investors, on our request. Given the approval of the founding members for both the firms, we were able to conduct interviews for all internal participants. One of the external participants, i.e., a project owner on Wishberry’s platform declined to be interviewed owing to his engagement in an upcoming round of investment for his startup. Given his short engagement with Wishberry earlier, we did not consider this data to be crucial for the analysis. We conducted semi-structured interviews and collected documents (e.g., pitch decks and reports) relevant for data triangulation for both firms internally and from external public resources as well.

The data collection process and analysis were similar to those adopted in other multiple case study–based research articles (Ang et al., 2002). Although the interviews were semi-structured, some questions probing the key inflection points and their impacts were common across all respondents in order to increase the internal validity of the study (Gibbert et al., 2008; Yin, 2014) and minimize any retrospective bias of individual respondents (Huber & Power, 1985; Miller, Cardinal, & Glick, 1997). The interviews were started by a set of introductions guided by the interview protocol, followed by explaining the aims and objectives of this research in general and the present interview in particular. The participants were assured of full confidentiality before seeking their permission to audio record all the conversations for later transcription. Explicit acceptance was obtained from all the participants before recording the interview. The were also collected during the interviews to assist with data analysis. On an average, the interviews lasted for about an hour with the longest ones lasting for around 2 hours. For the first case study, 13 interviews were conducted with key informants over a period of 4 months in 2015. For the second case study, we conducted 15 interviews over 6 months in late 2014. ## 2.1. Data Analysis Procedure

The research design followed an inducto-deductive approach with constant iterations between data collection and analysis with a focus on theory development in line with suggestions provided by Eisenhardt (1989) and Patton (1990). The interview data from both case studies were transcribed and then cross-examined with archival data from internal documents like investor pitch decks, market research reports, various operational reports, and other marketing materials. These along with other publicly available documents like press kits and newspaper reports were used for data triangulation. The analysis of data across multiple sources helped build external validity and robustness in the study.

The analysis of the data was initially guided by the analytical framework proposed earlier. The role of the initial theory was of informing and validating the iterative theory development through categorization of observations. This process of analysis and categorization of data collected from both the primary and secondary sources was informed by the tenets of qualitative data analysis for the development of grounded theory (Strauss and Corbin, 1990) especially for data collection. This influence was aligned with constructivist grounded theory (Charmaz, 2014), wherein the theoretical sensitivity made up the core of data analysis. Evans (2013) summarized the differences in the role of literature between classic and constructivist approaches. He remarked that the starting point for classic grounded theory was a desire to explore a substantive area with no predetermined research questions prior to the study; it did not begin with a literature review. In contrast, a constructivist grounded theory began with a literature review as a means to establish what was known and studied in the substantive area. However, there were no significant differences between the two approaches for data collection.

For the analysis of data from the first case study, we did not use an a priori list of items and hence we followed an inductive approach to generate codes from the data. For the second case study, we reused the items generated from the first case study. But new codes were developed from the analysis of the data from the second case study. They were cross-checked with the data of the first case study. Initially the data was coded manually by one of the authors. To establish the reliability of the data analysis and to mitigate potential bias, another external researcher, not involved in data collection and research design, was used to read and code the transcripts independently. The two sets of codes were compared for all interviews across both case studies and the mismatched codes were examined and discussed extensively till both coders agreed about the codes. These codes were then grouped into concepts based on their relevance to the context of DBT and then included under the dimension of the refined model. With theory development at the core of this analysis, data from both interviews and various documents were categorized using various iterations of coding and classification as suggested by Miles and Huberman (1994), and this was continued until theoretical saturation was reached, as indicated by the lack of any new information or novel theoretical insights (Bowen, 2008). The crossexamination of data across the different data sources did not result in any deviant cases or codes, which further established the robustness of the data analysis. The analysis helped us develop a holistic view of the overall market conditions for the respective startups and the process of DBT during their corresponding reinvention phases. We were able to develop an appreciation of the drivers of change for the firms and the strategic stance adopted by them in response to these drivers. The quotations used in the case discussion below were chosen based on their relevance and were mutually agreed upon by both coders.

## 3. Case study

## 3.1. Case Study 1 – Wishberry

Up until about 2010, only traditional channels of funding like lending existed in India to support investment in innovative projects. Crowdfunding as a means to fund innovative initiatives was non-existent in India. Not only was there a lack of any ecosystem for such digital aggregations, but the market also lacked an understanding of the concept of crowdfunding. Although global platforms like Indiegogo or Kickstarter were accessible to both backers and project owners, it required payment using credit cards that were not widely used in India. Wishberry, a rewardsbased crowdfunding platform was launched in 2010 under such market conditions. It started as a wedding gift registry service to help prospective brides and grooms share their wish list of gifts that they needed with their guests. This helped curtail wastage of gifts which were not useful to the recipients and helped guests by suggesting appropriate gifts. The initial strategy of the firm was to serve the marriage market through a crowdfunding model designed to improve the practice of giving gifts. However, with the experience of the market conditions, it retained its crowdfunding model but pivoted it to a novel and creative crowdfunding category and became immensely successful.

## 3.1.1. Initial Digital Business Model

Wishberry’s business model was designed as a barebone charity-based (gifting) crowdfunding service. Every new project required them to develop a new catalog of gifts which the couple might find useful. For each successful project, it charged a single flat one-time commission at the time of launch on its platform. Its online platform was primarily used for sharing the wishlist with all prospective guests online. This entrepreneurial business was barely digital as the online platform was not critical to the business. Most of the activities, i.e., customer onboarding, wishlist development gift selection, etc. were conducted offline. The limited maturity of ecommerce in India at that time increased the involvement and complexity of many business processes, especially the wishlist creation process. The team would have to access various offline retailers and stores to identify unique gifts for their customers which they would share with all the guests. During that time, the business strategy was geared towards offline-first, and the product offered by them was customized entirely as requested. However, the existing sociocultural context in India at that time hindered its target customer segment from realizing its potential. Wastage on gifts was recognized as a problem, but according to the Indian culture, it was considered inappropriate for brides and grooms to request gifts for themselves from guests formally. Also, the complexities involved in Indian marriages made this a very low priority activity and there was limited adoption of this service. One of the founders remarked:

“With everything going about for marriage preparations, deciding on gifts that would be useful later was of least priority in most households. Also, sharing a wishlist of gifts implied a conscious expectation of gifts from the guests which was considered inappropriate in the society. However, if these gifts were in the form of donations to charity, people were more welcome to adopt it.”

This learning from the market impacted Wishberry’s growth strategy based on the realization that charity-based crowdfunding aligned better with the existing social system in India. One of the mentors highlighted some of the initial issues faced by Wishberry in introducing their product to the larger market:

“The idea of crowdfunding is new and they are competing directly with traditional funding options like VC funds. Hence, they needed to develop their niche.”

Also, due to the unstructured nature of business processes, the business model was quite complex. The mentor of Wishberry remarked:

“For every new project, they had to do a lot of groundwork before it was launched on their platform. Especially because no two projects were similar.”

## 3.1.2. Transformed Digital Business Model

The new model in Wishberry offered an online platform for collecting donations on behalf of various charities associated with the event. It adopted a flexible funding model which allowed projects to be funded even if they did not attain their target amount from backers. This model proved exceptionally successful for charitable projects which often did not attain their projected goal. The online medium provided greater reach for the charities and consequently improved the potential to collect more donations. This campaign with the newer business model proved to be very successful for the participating charities involved and consequently for the firm. One of the founders identified the need to develop new social media monitoring capabilities:

“We need to continuously monitor social media to build traction. We often have to seek our earlier customers across mails, social networks, etc. to engage them.”

Due to the success of charitable fundraising, the online platform started becoming pivotal to Wishberry’s overall business strategy. It became a critical component for the success of the business. Some activities like the survey of offline stores stopped being the key activities for the business and the business functions were aligned to serve a broader market for charities. After gathering strategic knowledge of the operating environment, Wishberry developed its business model as a mature charity-based crowdfunding platform. Reflecting on the early obstacles for the online business model, the founders remarked:

“Once you are online, you are no longer restricted to people within the country. People from outside India also wanted to back some of our projects. However, the existing laws were not very clear about such transactions. Hence, we pushed for some regulations in this domain.”

It was during these times that Wishberry realized that such a broad focus on both charity and creative crowdfunding was proving detrimental to its growth. Most charitable projects did not realize their initial goal, and the rewards-based projects were also failing to attain their project goals. One of the founders reflected:

“Charitable projects could still continue without reaching the project funding goal completely. For example, if it was for feeding some poor people, lesser people could be fed, but it can continue. However, in the case of a creative project, the quality of the project and its associated rewards would dwindle if it failed to collect the projected funds required for the project initially. This was not acceptable to both of our customers, i.e., the project owners and the backers.”

Therefore, in early 2013, charity-based crowdfunding was annulled from Wishberry’s platform them. Further, it allowed funding of projects which were successful in attaining its initial funding target entirely only. Wishberry learned that this form of funding was more successful than the flexible mode as it built backers' trust and campaigners' accountability. The founders also learned that certain sectors were more amenable to rewards-based crowdfunding. This included projects like films and music that could not turn to conventional modes of funding like a venture capital. Rewards-based crowdfunding was the best option for these kinds of projects. The founder of Wishberry commented on the refinements of their business model as a response to changes in the operating environment:

“We need to keep refining our product categories to maintain our niche. Also, we need to provide other services to improve the success rate of campaigns.”

Along with these changes to the business model, the crowdfunding startup became a completely digital entity. Accordingly, it was expected to be available to its customers in their platform of choice. One of the founders recollected:

“Ours was a web based platform but now we have to take the mobile-first route. Who knows what’s next?”

The ease of doing business on the digital platform became a critical success factor for it. All of its primary activities including advertisement, customer onboarding (both project owners and backers), project development, project advertisement, etc. were performed on the online medium only. So, the reach of the platform increased for project owners too. Although backers from diverse geographies could already fund projects on the platform, project owners were required to visit the startup physically before launching a project. Projects were curated, and only projects with a huge potential for success or having novelty were allowed to launch on the platform. A new source of revenue was also developed in the form of consultancy fees for projects which needed some handholding to succeed on the crowdfunding platform. One of the founders remarked:

“Earlier our revenue was intrinsically tied to the success of the projects. Many times the project owners did not put in too much effort to make it successful as they were shy to request funds. But then we also lost time and revenue because of it. So, we started curating and then launching projects on the platform.”

But with its move to an entirely digital form, Wishberry expanded the market boundaries beyond the physical to the digital space. With these changes in the digital platform, it gained immense success and the percentage of projects successfully funded became more than that of global players like Kickstarter and Indiegogo. Also, Wishberry’s focused business model curtailed most other competitors that competed with a broader focus in the same product-market domain. ## 3.2. Case Study 2 – Hack-a-Joe Labs

It is a US-based early-stage startup in the home automation sector. Their first product Hook was an affordable home automation hub that enabled existing electronic devices in the home, such as coffee makers, lamps, light bulbs, heaters, or anything that is powered by electricity, to be controlled through the Internet. Conventional smart home setups required all devices to be capable of communicating with each other which generally required replacement of existing home appliances with new “smart” appliances. Cheap and readily available radio frequency (RF) remotes, which allowed basic control of conventional electronic appliances wirelessly, provided a partial solution to this problem. It allowed a common and single RF remote to control the state of multiple devices simultaneously. While common RF remotes had become very affordable, they could only be used to control a limited set of devices. Simultaneously, they did not bridge with other smart home devices easily. The Hook hub enabled control of such conventional RFbased remotes through a smartphone over a Wi-Fi network thus allowing seamless integration of legacy electronic appliances in the smart home ecosystem with minimal changes. The low maturity of the existing smart home ecosystem presented a lucrative market opportunity for such products.

## 3.2.1. Initial Digital Business Model

The initial business concept was developed in line with the conventional technology startup roadmap. With initial feedback from a close group of testers, the initial product idea and the overall business concept was developed. The business model was designed around need-based implementation. Initially, the founders attempted funding through conventional venture funding channels. In this model, the organization was planned for organic growth, and roles and responsibilities were allocated in an ad hoc manner. The startup form was understandably lean and skills and resources were added after consideration of costs associated with them. The initial plan was to first secure funding and then develop the product. Till then a rough prototype developed by the team was considered adequate for pitching to investors. With this plan, the early business model was focused on product innovation and exploitation of market opportunity. One of the founders shared his early idea as:

“Our focus was on developing the product features to ensure its technical supremacy in the market. We had not planned for it as a sustainable business model. Hence, it should be capable of thwarting competition from substitutes for some time.”

Like conventional technology startups, securing investment and product development was the focus of the firm. Developing product characteristics was considered secondary as was the development of a structured business model. Consequently, all available skills and resources were allocated to the development of a prototype in order to secure venture funding. With full focus on product development, the need to scan and identify relevant market signals was ignored. Expertise in the form of marketing and sales were considered redundant at this stage. This monolithic focus in the initial stage improved the product quality but did not appropriately reflect on the value proposition for the target market as assessed by experts and venture capitalists. The founder remarked:

“We had not planned our business functions then. We did not have a CEO, CFO, and other CXOs. We were all doing a mix of things. What we lacked most was someone to help us with marketing, especially in investor pitch meetings.”

## 3.2.2. Transformed Digital Business Model

Subsequently, with feedback from investors and mentors coupled with the lack of confidence on the business potential of the product, the team went back to the drawing board to devise a new strategy for the business. The conventional startup growth cycle was risky due to the transient business potential of the product. The only value proposition of the product was affordability and limited changes required for the adoption of smart home technology in conventional settings. The founders realized that they would need to assess the market potential of their product in order to plan a relevant business roadmap and accompanying business strategy. This started a phase of active environmental scanning. The team actively engaged with industry experts and mentors in this domain to help develop the business model around the product. During these discussions, they were introduced to the concept of crowdfunding. The firm realized the potential of crowdfunding platforms like Indiegogo and Kickstarter to reach customers directly and secure funding for their product even before it was manufactured. This reversal of the value chain sequence implied reorganization of their current activities and required them to revamp their existing business model entirely. A crowdfunding campaign compelled them to update their business model to integrate the newer requirements of the platform like early prototyping and

product demonstration, frequent updates, etc. It also involved new investment in social media publicity and other similar activities before production. As one of the founders puts it:

“We were required to hire a lot of different people. We hired an external expert to be the CEO. We also got some external help for social media publicity etc. All of these positions were hired on the basis of equity and hence didn't require much immediate investment. But then our business model had changed substantially. And not before long, we had started working like established, mature technology firms, with clearly defined roles and tasks.”

The business model configuration and the planned organizational growth trajectory were also updated. For example, the team had to divert their marketing plan from post-funding to prefunding. They also needed to secure much of their customer base before manufacturing the product and needed to hire relevant skills for social media publicity and marketing. While doing so, they also had to ensure their cost competence in both the structure and form of the organization. While doing so, they had to ensure their cost competence in both the structure and form of the organization. One of the founders of Hack-a-Joe Labs remarked:

“Right from the start we knew that we are a low cost home automation startup. We had to maintain a lean organisational profile.”

Furthermore, securing a contract manufacturer with acceptable levels of consistent product quality became a high priority business activity. Due to their small size, they could not find a single manufacturer who could provide all components of their overall product. They had to make multiple contracts with different manufacturers. As the manufacturing phase started, logistics became a major cost center and a high priority business activity. Ensuring efficiency in the supply chain became crucial. One of the founders remarked:

“Any change in the scale of our order, would have to be followed by strict quality check. Many times the manufacturing was done across multiple factories and sub-vendors.”

Simultaneously, allocation of roles and responsibilities needed to be clearly defined as well. With the new business model, the firm managed to garner immense success in its crowdfunding campaign and was able to achieve 646% of the originally planned funding goal. The product was shipped as per schedule to backers and was later launched for general availability on various ecommerce platforms. With the outcome from the case analysis highlighting the role of strategic business information exchange with the external environment and its impact on the digital business model of entrepreneurial firms, we gained new insights into the DBT process of entrepreneurial firms. While the DBT in Wishberry was incremental and evolved slowly with increasing access to strategic business information from the operating environment, the DBT at Hack-a-Joe Labs was radical owing to the significant reversal of its value chain but was evolutionary in terms of the pace of change. Owing to its limited access to strategic information and its relatively small size, it lacked the necessary resources to execute major changes to its business model rapidly. However, core business attributes like customer relationships, and strategic partnerships were reevaluated and reestablished as part of the DBT exercise. Wishberry curtailed its broad customer segment to a very focused group interested about creative content. Simultaneously, the strategic information from the operating environment helped them identify the need to develop an immensely involved relationship with customers by switching from a commission-based revenue model to a revenue-sharing model. In case of Hack-a-Joe Labs, its relationships and partnerships changed and customer interaction became a significant business activity. Adoption of a crowdfunding strategy also dispersed initial business risks due to limited working capital and other resources. Its relationship with the crowdfunding platform and social media channels gained significance as it contributed directly to the business success. The DBT for firms, especially startups, involves development of novel value proposition for the end customer as a result of active environmental scanning and incremental improvement of market intelligence. Access to information, along with a carefully planned digital business strategy, allows such firms to effectively implement a core digital transformation. We discuss these insights from the analysis of the two case studies under the following headings.

Together they defined the nature of the business environment for the business. Innovation, change of taste, and technological advancements were the core concepts that emerged under the dimension of environmental dynamism, which represented the degree and instability of changes in the business environment (Child, 1972). Digital startups are impacted by dynamism due to innovation (includes both product and process innovation) which has the potential to obliterate their competitive advantage in their core market. In the case of Wishberry, product innovation did not drive the business transformation. But it was the introduction of process innovation like online onboarding and curation due to digital platform affordances that compelled it to adopt this transformation. But in the case of Hack-a-Joe Labs, both product and process innovation drove transformation. Product innovation in the form of integrated home automation products and process innovation in the form of introduction of crowdfunding as a viable funding model and contract-manufacturing drove it. Change of taste has a similar effect on the rent-earning capability of the products and services offered by a business. Hunt and Morgan (1995) have attributed competition from other players in the same or related industry as the primary cause for change of customer. Furthermore, they have added governmental actions as another major source of change in the value-creating potential of a firm’s resources. Together these changes have the capability to diminish the advantage from a particular existing resource of a firm and turn into a non-resource or even a contra-resource. In the case of Wishberry, preference for a focused reward-based crowdfunding model drove its transformation, but correspondingly customer preferences did not drive digital transformation at Hack-a-Joe Labs. Interestingly, undiscovered customer expectations drove its adoption of crowdfunding as the basis of digital transformation. Technological advancements disrupt the existing sources of competitive advantage and create novel sources of value in the same product-market domain. But unlike innovation, such advancements in other domains have the ability to neutralize the value

proposition of the current offering. Interestingly, they played a crucial role in driving digital transformation in both the case studies. For Wishberry, technological advancements like smartphones and online payment platforms were the core drivers for transformation. Similarly, technological advancements like the introduction of low-cost integrated smart home solutions drove the urgency for quick product launch. In summary, technological advancements emerged as a crucial element of operating environment dynamism that drove DBT in both entrepreneurial firms.

Socioeconomic conditions and competitive hostility emerged as the core concepts that illustrated the uncertainty in the operating environment for digital entrepreneurial firms. Scholars have suggested that the primary task of an organization is to cope with task environment uncertainties (Thompson, 1967) and it is a key variable in explaining organizational behavior (March & Simon, 1958). Socioeconomic conditions were found to be an integral aspect of uncertainty for such firms. It corresponded to the effect uncertainty according to Milliken’s typology (1987), i.e., “a lack of knowledge about cause-effect relationships, in particular about how states of nature will affect the organization” (Sutcliffe and Zaheer, 1998). While Hack-a-Joe Labs operated in a relatively socially synchronous market, Wishberry’s transformation was driven by the existing social system which hindered adoption of its gift registry platform. Economic attributes were a key driver for their transformations too. For Wishberry, the realization of the lack of any source of funding for creative people led its transformation. Similarly, identification of micro-funding as a preferred funding method as opposed to bulk financing drove Hack-a-Joe Labs’ transformation. The nature of competitive hostility is volatile and unpredictable and creates immense pressure on the firm-level strategy. It is a result of multifaceted, intense, and variable competition in the industry (Miller & Friesen, 1978). Given the unproven nature of crowdfunding in India,

Wishberry faced little competition. But the abundance of diverse products with different value propositions created a competitive market for Hack-a-Joe Labs. The risk of intense competitive hostility especially from established firms drove its digital transformation.

Finally, heterogeneity and interactions emerged as core concepts from our analysis of the dimensions of the operating environment. Heterogeneity of the production environment requires firms to make corresponding changes to their value chain components to maintain consistency of the value proposition of the product or service offering. In case of Wishberry, downstream changes in the value chain like inconsistent marketing of charity-based crowdfunding and hence their lack of success led to increased heterogeneity of the operating environment. That along with the introduction of new competitors in the market drove transformation. In the case of Hack-a-Joe Labs, upstream changes like inconsistent supply commitments led to newer supply and production contracts as a result of the adoption of crowdfunding. Greater the interactions with the environment, greater the variations leading to increased complexity. Organizations of all sizes engage in continuous interactions with their partners to append value at each stage of production or service delivery (Håkansson and Snehota, 2006). For both the case studies, variation in the interactions with their value chain partners as the joint marketing of the projects on Wishberry’s platform and multi-party manufacturing for Hack-a-Joe Lab's product drove the digital transformation in them respectively. In summary, interactions emerged as a crucial element leading to the complexity of the operating environment that drove DBT.

The concepts that have emerged out of the case studies also resonate with Miller and Friesen’s (1983) and Newkirk and Lederer’s (2006) conceptualization of the role of operating environment characteristics in driving the business strategy of incumbent firms. These studies have asserted the role of operating environment characteristics like uncertainty, complexity, and dynamism in strategy making. Organizations develop their strategy based on their interactions with the operating environment dynamically. Our findings too reconciled with these observations and we found these concepts to be strong drivers of DBT in entrepreneurial firms. The environmental characteristics like dynamism, uncertainty, and complexity exerted pressure to change the extant business strategy through the development of new business concepts.

## 4.2. Strategic Business Information Exchange

Information permeability and information flow emerged as the dominant concepts from our analysis of the dimension of information exchange capability. Information permeability refers to both the environment and the boundary agents governing the transfer of information. It was a core element that drove the business model transformation for both firms. With greater access to market information and their active scanning behavior, information permeability improved steadily over time for both firms. The firms were able to capture insights from experts and mentors in this domain which helped them realize the need to transform their current business model. Information flow refers to the actual movement of information for consumption during the value creation processes. Both firms asserted that their transformation journey was a result of the development of continuously evolving insights of their respective domains. This steadily improving information flow with the environment helped develop an urgency for change from extant business models. In summary, both information permeability and information flow are crucial elements driving the DBT of digital entrepreneurial firms.

The concepts that have emerged from the case studies also resonate with Teece et al. (1997) in establishing the crucial role of information in decision-making (specifically in the context of DBT for this study). While proposing the dynamic capabilities framework, they have emphasized the role of information in different elements embodying it as resources, core competences, and organizational routines. Firms transforming their digital business model need to develop various capabilities dynamically to cope with both internal and external changes. Also, information transmission was asserted to be at the core of such capabilities. Our findings reiterated the need to develop structures and probes to capture relevant business intelligence from the transacting entrepreneurial firms. ## 4.3. Digital Business Model and its Transformation

Digital business model components, digital strategy, and digital model transformation impact emerged as the core dimensions affecting the digital business model transformation category of digital entrepreneurial firms from our analysis of the two case studies. While the first two constructs represent the drivers of change, the third construct illustrates the specific impact of this change on the firm’s business model. The first construct is already discussed in detail in section 1.1 of this paper.

Mission, vision, and strategic priorities were the core concepts derived from the analysis of case studies in the dimension of digital strategy. Mission refers to the business goals of the firm. Organizational mission drove the business model transformation for both firms. Wishberry was driven by the purpose of providing a source of funding for the unserved population, and hence it pivoted to uphold that goal. Hack-a-Joe Labs upheld affordability in home automation as its primary mission and accordingly transformed to maintain affordability through crowdfunding based financing as against venture capital based funding which would have resulted in increased pricing due to the compulsions of innovation. Vision refers to the desired future position for the entrepreneurial firm. While Wishberry’s vision adhered to developing a niche in the crowdfunding domain, its product vision was to enable ease-of-use for the platform and both drove the transformation of its business model. Hack-a-Joe Labs realized the transient nature of their value proposition and hence swift market capture drove its organizational vision. Easy abridgment with newer technologies was the guiding product vision during its business model transformation. Strategic priorities refer to the list of strategic objectives ranked according to their importance for the firm. With low barriers to entry in the crowdfunding domain, Wishberry was compelled to reconsider its priorities from an all-inclusive financing model that represented a broad focus to a specific, engagement-oriented all-or-nothing financing model. But Hack-a-Joe labs did not need to reconsider its priorities owing to the transient and established nature of the domain. In summary, mission and vision are essential drivers for the DBT of digital entrepreneurial firms.

Value proposition, segmentation, customer relationships, revenue architecture, delivery channels, and strategic partnerships evolved as the core concepts that were affected in the dimension of DBT. Value proposition refers to the overall value offered by the product or service offering. While Wishberry’s value proposition changed gradually from a funding platform to a focused engagement platform for creators, Hack-a-Joe Lab’s value proposition continued to be affordable smart home platform even after the transformation. Segmentation refers to positioning of the product in the market. Unlike Hack-a-Joe Labs, Wishberry’s campaigner segment changed to creative category after the transformation. Customer relationships refer to the type of association with the customer. Both the firms benefited from the affordances of digital platforms and reenacted their corresponding relationships with their customer base with greater engagement and rich feedback from them. Revenue architecture refers to the revenue strategies and systems which capture value of the market offering. It was revised in both firms. While Wishberry introduced novel revenue systems in the form of consultation fees and new financing model, Hack-a-Joe Labs switched from a conventional financing model to a pay-to-produce model for financing the initial manufacturing costs. Delivery channels refer to the intermediary agencies that deliver the potential value to the end customer. Although Wishberry offered novel delivery channels through mobile devices for its service offering, Hack-a-Joe Labs’ offering being a physical product, continued to be offered without change after the transformation. Strategic partnerships refer to the relationships with external entities that are required for delivering value through the product or service. Both firms developed novel strategic partnerships after the transformation. While Wishberry onboarded new in-house software development and developed new partnerships with various payment processing teams, Hack-a-Joe Labs hired a new media agency to help them in their crowdfunding campaign. In summary, novel customer relationships, revised revenue architecture, and new strategic partnerships are essential elements of the DBT of digital entrepreneurial firms.

## 5. Cross-case Analysis

We performed a cross-case analysis, as part of identifying dimensions and following Eisenhardt (1989) we looked for within-group similarities coupled with inter-group differences.

## 5.1. The Role of Operating Environment in DBT

The impact of the operating environment emerged as a common attribute governing DBT in both case studies. Dynamism, uncertainty, and complexity of the operating environment were core drivers of DBT in both firms. In the case of Wishberry, its operating environment changed significantly as crowdfunding gained prominence in the domestic market and supporting infrastructure in the form of regulations were introduced. But new regulations introduced challenges for the digital startup (e.g., ambiguity of rules for accepting foreign funding). Adherence to these new set of rules increased the complexity of doing business in this domain. It created a pressure on Wishberry to manage its relationships with the new set of market realities. Hack-a-Joe Labs too faced a similar set of compulsions from the operating environment to conform to changes and associated uncertainties, primarily in its effort at conventional funding activities. It was exposed to greater scrutiny once the transient advantage of the product’s value proposition was revealed to the investors. Economic conditions of the market led to greater uncertainty and drove DBT for both firms. While Wishberry experienced economic uncertainty owing to lack of familiarity with its business model, Hack-a-Joe Labs experienced economic uncertainty in terms of volatility of pricing for smart home technology. Lastly, the complexity of the environment was increased significantly as a result of multifaceted interactions of the studied firms with their environment. For both firms, their need to liaison with multiple partners including manufacturers, platform providers, marketers, etc., to deliver their products and

services resulted in higher levels of complexity in their relationships with other stakeholders in the ecosystem.

Studies by scholars like Ismail et al. (2017) and Berghaus and Back (2016) have highlighted the role of innovation, culture, and socioeconomic conditions, changing customer preferences as crucial to DBT. Specifically, although innovation is generally driven by internal aspirations, it can also be a response to external triggers in the competitive environment. It is thus a core driver of such transformation. But contrary to previous studies, we did not find evidence that innovation, change of taste, socioeconomic conditions, competitive hostility, and heterogeneity were core drivers for DBT. One reason for this could be our specific focus on young entrepreneurial firms instead of established firms in previous studies. However, between the two case studies, dynamism due to technological advancements in the market along with interactions resulting in increased complexity was found to be common for both.

Our results further supported the same-different stimulus response model of organizational learning as proposed by Weick (1991). Although the same stimulus leading to different response is an ample indication of learning by the organization, it may not necessarily be a result of learning for established firms. But we found ample evidence to suggest that variable response of entrepreneurial firms to external stimuli, is a pronounced outcome of learning by the firm. Such firms actively scan for information in the operating environment that has the potential to alter their existing business processes. In a dynamic operating environment, such firms experience various triggers to change their current business model. They may then choose to adopt a dominantly agile or a persistent strategic stance, based on their learning from previous experiences and through self-evaluation of their present dynamic capabilities. Weick has further suggested that when strong cultures are embedded in an organization, learning occurs but varies according to the organizational routines and not the degree of stimulus. Entrepreneurial firms lack strong structuration and rigid processes, and hence, their responses to environmental changes are based on their learning from other previous instances. Our analysis of the two case studies did not reveal any dimension of organizational response that suggested its dependency on the scale of the stimulus and, hence, provided support to Weick’s model of organizational learning.

## 5.2. Changes in the Core Components of the Digital Business Model

Both firms adopted DBT in various components of their existing business model. In case of Wishberry, the core content of their crowdfunding platform was adapted by switching to an allor-nothing funding model combined with a focus on a narrow set of categories. In the case of Hack-a-Joe Labs, the informational content of their product was altered by significantly enhancing the services provided along with the product. Initially the plan was to provide a webbased interface to interact with the hardware. However, it was iterated to provide an interface for voice platforms like Alexa. This increased the value proposition of the product primarily by extending the useful lifetime value of the product. Similarly, the customer experience was altered in both firms. Assistance in running crowdfunding campaigns by providing support in the development of pitch content was one way in which the experience of one of its customers (i.e., project owners) was enhanced. The experience of backers on its platform was also improved by providing online payment options if available and supplementing that with cash pickup services. For Hack-a-Joe Labs’ customers, regular updates and enhancement of features based on feedback from potential customers on the crowdfunding platform improved the customer experience through greater engagement. Finally, the platform characteristics were altered by Hack-a-Joe

Labs in its quest for DBT. This change was reflected in its adoption of crowdfunding to improve the delivery of information of their products across new platforms.

In conclusion, our study revealed that DBT is mandatorily reflected in alterations of the customer experience of the product or service offering for digital entrepreneurial firms. It was the dominant component of the business model that was altered during a DBT exercise.

## 5.3. Transformation of Core Business Attributes

While the value proposition of the core offering was not altered for Hack-a-Joe Labs, Wishberry’s adoption of a focused (i.e., creative rewards-based crowdfunding model) product altered its core value proposition. This renewed focus also led to re-segmentation of its productmarket domain. Simultaneously, the delivery channel of its service was also extended to include mobile platforms. But unsurprisingly, changes to customer relationships, revenue architecture, and strategic partnerships were noticed in both the case studies. As discussed earlier, Wishberry’s changes to the core business attribute reflected in more inclusive customer relationships that included new revenue streams (e.g., consultancy fee) and development of new strategic partnerships (e.g., with online payment processors). For Hack-a-Joe Labs, these changes were represented by greater engagement of customers in the product development, change of revenue architecture from post-sales to pre-sales, and development of new strategic partnerships between developers of marketing campaigns and social media influencers.

## 6. Conclusion

DBT is an inclusive and expensive activity and is risky for both entrepreneurial and established firms. Established firms operate without the fear of insolvency in the near future whereas young entrepreneurial firms generally lack sufficient financial investments in their early stages and thus primarily focus on survival in the market. However, established firms need to be aware of the risks of loss of its existing core customer base as well as how to maintain their corporate image. In contrast, entrepreneurial firms have relatively more freedom to change their existing market image for DBT. This along with the limited organizational structuration (replete with issues like hierarchy and bureaucracy), allows more flexibility to entrepreneurial firms. Despite the liabilities of newness and smallness, the limited scale allows entrepreneurial firms to have the opportunity to amend their digital business model with newer insights from the transactional environment if they are able to curtail the risks involved in it. This agility, usually distinct to entrepreneurial firms, is key to their survival and growth, especially after unprecedented environmental jolts. The key attributes of the environment and strategic business information flow, identified in this cross-examinational study, provide insights for such firms to endure both incremental and transformational changes. This in combination with the digital business strategy of the firm and its business model are key to digital transformation of entrepreneurial firms. Our research contributes to the extant understanding of DBT in entrepreneurial firms and provides key insights for practice by entrepreneurs of digital startups.

## 6.1. Theoretical Contributions

This research is an early attempt to capture the dynamics of DBT in entrepreneurial firms. Using case studies, we identified the antecedents, moderators, and outcomes of this process. The focus of this study was to identify the key attributes that affected the transformation process in such firms. While doing so, we looked beyond the organization-level and individual-level variables. We focused on the role of the operating environment in driving such a change. The primary contribution of this study was the identification of the role of specific operating environment characteristics in driving DBT. Although various studies have highlighted the role of environment in strategy making (Miller & Friesen, 1983; Sutcliffe & Zaheer, 1998) and transformation processes of firms (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000; Gupta and Bose, 2018), our identification of the role of innovation, change of taste, and heterogeneity in the firm’s specific operating environment could generate a new debate on key environmental attributes affecting such a change. From the constructs that have emerged from the case studies, we found that dynamism, uncertainty, and complexity determined the need for transformation of a firm’s digita business model. Although it was difficult to identify external triggers to DBT for any specific entrepreneurial business especially due to the unique operating conditions enacted by the environment, any significant changes to the above mentioned dimensions of the operating environment were a potential trigger to ensure business success through DBT.

A second important contribution of this study was in highlighting the role of business information for DBT. We discussed three primary attributes of the information exchange process, i.e., the permeability of the environment in allowing access to business intelligence, the inclination of the firm in actively scanning the environment, and the information itself. This multi-faceted analysis of the information construct provided a holistic understanding of the process of strategic information exchange with specific examination of factors enabling and also obstructing this process. Future studies can take cues from this examination and extend such analysis to other domains in which the role of information is central.

## 6.2. Contributions to Practice

The insights from this study have useful implications for practicing managers (especially entrepreneurs of digital ventures). Contrary to general suggestions like the need for awareness of the operating environment, specific observations from this research such as the need to be aware of technological advancements in the domain and the need for active environmental scanning can be more readily adopted by managers of entrepreneurial firms. With the constraint of limited resources for entrepreneurial firms, they would benefit more with such specific advice. They should be able to divert their resources to tasks with higher priorities which have a higher potential for improving the firm's performance. From the case studies, we also found little conclusive evidence of the direct role of interaction between complexity and innovation in driving DBT in digital ventures. Hence, businesses should carefully consider and assess before committing to DBT exercises. Our findings suggest that not all innovations (both radical and incremental) have a direct impact on the decision for DBT in entrepreneurial firms. In response to environmental turbulence, such firms choose between an agile or a persistent strategic stance. Decision-makers of such firms assess the transformative potential of the external stimuli on the basis of dynamism, uncertainty, and complexity of its specific operating environment and then its own capability to successfully sustain the corresponding change in its business model. Based on this evaluation, they choose predominantly one of the two strategic stances. Young firms cannot react to all observable changes in the operating environment as business model change is an expensive process. Also, not all transformations lead to any significant improvement in performance but instead sometimes even lead to business failure and wastage. Hence, entrepreneurs can use the insights from this case study to assess specific attributes which they should consider for DBT. While doing so, they must also consider the potential impact of this

transformation on specific customer experiences, its existing revenue architecture and alterations to the existing set of strategic partnerships with its key partners and suppliers. For example, Wishberry’s realization about the mismatch of its existing product-market mix and selfassessment of its business capabilities as a crowdfunding platform, helped it to contain the market dissonance by creating a niche for itself in the unexplored creative rewards-based crowdfunding domain. Further, we believe that the case description in itself will serve as a handy source of insights into the functioning of firms in this domain. Young entrepreneurs will significantly benefit from this experience and can use the learning to design an agile business model to be reactive to changes in the operating environment.
