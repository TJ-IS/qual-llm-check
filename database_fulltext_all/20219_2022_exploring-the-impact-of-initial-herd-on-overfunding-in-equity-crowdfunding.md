# Exploring the impact of initial herd on overfunding in equity crowdfunding

Yijing Li<sup>a</sup>, Fei Liu<sup>b</sup>, Wenjie Fan<sup>c,</sup>\*, Eric T.K. Lim<sup>a</sup>, Yong Liu<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Information Systems and Technology Management, UNSW Business School, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia

<sup>b</sup> Department of Digitization, Copenhagen Business School, Copenhagen, Denmark

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information and Service Management, Aalto University, Helsinki, Finland

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Crowdfunding Herd Herding behavior Overfunding

## A B S T R A C T

Outcome of equity crowdfunding campaigns often exceeds original fundraising goals, leading to market ineficiency. This undesirable phenomenon of overfunding garnered little attention in past studies. Synthesizing extant literature on crowdfunding and herding, we identified the initial herd made visible by funding progress indicator as the main cause of overfunding. The impact of the initial herd can be quantified by three dimensions: maturity, intensity, and persistency. We then advance and validate a research model for examining how dimensions of the initial herd afect overfunding in equity crowdfunding. Findings from this study can shed light on plausible remedies for the overfunding issue.

## 1. Introduction

Raising initial capital is an inevitable hurdle confronting entrepreneurs at the seeding stage of their business venture [1]. Although multiple sources of financing like banking institutions, private foundations, governmental agencies, business angels (BAs), and venture capitalists (VCs) are available for fund raising [2], they are less accessible to budding entrepreneurs who lack the evidence of innovative capability and venture profitability. Because bankers and professional investors are generally risk averse, they are more inclined to disregard business ventures with high return variability [3] and unproven profitability [4]. Furthermore, it is dificult to assess the value of seeding these nascent initiatives due to moral hazard [3], opacity [5], and the intangibility of new innovations [6]. Statistics have indicated that 82 % of start-ups are self-funded or supported by the entrepreneurs’ friends and relatives [7], whereas less than 1 % of these entrepreneurs managed to raise initial capital from VCs [8]. Fortunately, emerging financial alternatives, like crowdfunding, have dramatically expanded funding opportunities for start-ups in capital markets [9].

Crowdfunding has emerged as the de facto online avenue for entrepreneurs to broadcast their financial needs and solicit capital from a vast number of micro investors. Particularly, equity crowdfunding, which emulates the payof structure of conventional equity financing market [13], has emerged as the default venture fundraising model for entrepreneurs online. In equity crowdfunding, fundraisers (i.e., capital seekers or entrepreneurs) enact campaigns to narrate their business venture, outline their fundraising strategy, as well as interact with and sell equity to a large group of micro investors (i.e., funders) in exchange for capital [12]. Powered by the platformized micro-investment mechanism, equity crowdfunding bestows entrepreneurs, who are starved of resources to generate publicity for their business ventures, with better odds of financing success by reaching out to an expanding base of unsophisticated online investors [10].

In spite of the merits of crowdfunding, it is plagued by problems of overfunding. Overfunding is a distinct feature of crowdfunding, whereby funders continue to invest in “overly successful” campaigns even beyond the fundraising goal stipulated by fundraisers. Statistics have shown that successful campaigns in leading equity crowdfunding platforms were overfunded by more than 50 % on average [14], with blockbuster campaigns tripling the amount of raised capital as compared to that being solicited [15]. On the flip side, over 30 % of campaigns on these same platforms struggle to raise more than 1/3 of the amount being sought, with many of these campaigns receiving little to no investment [16]. As a consequence of overfunding, a huge number of promising start-ups may fail to attract the financial support they desperately need because they are eclipsed by their overly successful counterparts [17]. Besides, overfunding can be equally detrimental for those overly successful fundraisers because the cash surplus from their overfunded campaigns is unlikely to be efectively managed, as it has not been budgeted in the original business plans [13]. Overfunded campaigns tend to become overly ambitious, culminating in unrealistic claims, unfulfilled obligations, and ultimately, disappointed funders [13]. Overfunding, in promoting the suboptimal allocation of scarce monetary resources, hence stands out as one of the primary causes of market ineficiency for crowdfunding platforms. The skewed allocation of capital not only diminishes the benefits of participation for both fundraisers and funders alike, but more importantly, it culminates in a “market for lemons” that constrains the growth of crowdfunding platforms [6]. For this reason, it is not surprising that a majority of equity crowdfunding platforms are aware of and have taken action to contain overfunding by empowering fundraisers to either decide whether to persist with the financing process after having received suficient capital or select appropriate funders from all pledges. Yet, despite the preceding measures, the pervasiveness of overfunding implies a lack of due diligence on the part of crowdfunding platforms and fundraisers. Concentrating on equity crowdfunding platforms where overfunding is not strictly prohibited, this study attempts to unveil the drivers of overfunding on such platforms.

Even though past studies have alluded to the predominance of overfunding on crowdfunding platforms, few have scrutinized the causes behind this trend [19]. Unlike conventional fundraising, the prevalence of information asymmetry on crowdfunding platforms compels funders to turn to alternate signals for ascertaining campaign desirability. On crowdfunding platforms, the digital visibility of fundraising progress yields quality signals that entice funders to gravitate toward campaigns characterized by a strong start to their funding process [25,26]. These campaigns are often capable of garnering a herd of investors in the early stages of the funding process, which in turn appears as an initial surge in fundraising progress that continue to draw in other funders at an expedited rate. Because crowdfunding platforms constitute fertile breeding grounds for the emergence of such herd, we advance herding as a salient driver of overfunding. Like Raafat et al. [22], we define herding as the alignment of the thoughts or behaviors of individuals in a group through local interaction and without centralized coordination.

While prior research has acknowledged the existence of herding beha vior on crowdfunding platforms [20,21,23], there is a paucity of studies that has examined the impact of herding on overfunding. To this end, we depart from extant literature, which treats herding as an amorphous clustering phenomenon [25], by conceiving the formation of an initial herd as the first visible sign of explosive investment interest toward a given campaign, and probing the bandwagon efect of such early momentum on enticing funders to the campaign, thereby leading to its overfunding. Moreover, because the formation of a herd adheres to a conspicuous pattern that embodies formation, growth, and dissolution [24], we conceptualize initial herds on crowdfunding platforms as a multifaceted phenomenon characterized by their maturity, intensity, and persistency. As alleged by Banerjee [26], people benefit from second mover advantage by mimicking early successors. This in turn accentuates the criticality of taking into account the timing when initial herds begin to form, which we termed as maturity. Additionally, herds were found to be volatile in that their population quickly peaks and dissipates [27], which we labeled as the intensity and persistency of herd volatility in the context of crowdfunding. By analyzing 250 campaigns on a leading equity crowdfunding platform where overfunding is permitted, we endeavor to disentangle the efects of herding on overfunding by: (1) eliciting three focal dimensions constituting initial herds on equity crowdfunding platforms; and (2) explicating the impact of these initial herd dimensions on overfunding.

## 2. Theoretical Foundation and hypotheses formulation

## 2.1. Entrepreneurial finance

Despite the instrumental role played by start-ups in energizing the economy [28,29], they often encounter hurdles in securing initial capital through debt financing from banking institutions [2,6,28,30]. This is because start-ups not only struggle to ofer collaterals in exchange for bank loans [5], but they also find it hard to put forth a convincing business proposal with sustainable revenue streams to guarantee profitability [4,30]. Consequently, entrepreneurs are often forced to either approach private equity financiers, namely corporate investors, BAs, and VCs [28] or turn to alternative funding sources, which may involve tapping on their own personal savings or negotiating loans from friends and families [2,5,31].

BAs and VCs are two dominant sources of seed funding for entrepreneurs [29,32] because these specialized financing agents are equipped with the necessary expertise to assess the profitability of start-ups [4,6,28]. Nevertheless, past studies have illustrated that only a handful of start-ups managed to secure investments from these sources because of their limited supply and intense scrutinizing procedure [3,6,33]. Particularly, assessing innovative business ventures demands highly specialized knowledge so much so that it is an uphill battle for entrepreneurs to appeal to investors with matching expertise and interest to kick-start fundraising [6]. This dificulty in fundraising is further amplified by the skewed distribution of capital. Increasingly, non-technological business ventures are facing a draught of investments as BAs and VCs start to chase after technological innovations, hoping to catch the next unicorn [4]. Furthermore, entrepreneurs in less developed regions are constrained in their funding opportunities because BAs and VCs tend to invest in business ventures that are situated in mature markets with greater consumption power [4,6]. In this sense, conventional investors with a profit-oriented mindset are ill-suited for start-ups characterized by atypicality and uncertain market responses [2,3].

## 2.2. Equity Crowdfunding

Blending the concept of microfinance [34] and the maturation of digital platforms, crowdfunding has emerged as a natural progression toward a more inclusive and versatile funding option for individuals and enterprises [2,35]. Compared to reward-based crowdfunding in which non-entrepreneurial campaigns could solicit funds by ofering nonpecuniary rewards in exchange, equity crowdfunding serves as the main avenue for small enterprises to raise capital and generate publicity [2,9]. Unlike its conventional counterparts, equity crowdfunding cam paigns enable independent investors to funnel resources toward promising business ventures by sharing equity and at the same time diffusing risks among fellow funders [12]. In a way, the equity crowdfunding platform ensures a more fluid and granular distribution of resources guided by the wisdom of crowd. Given the global outreach of digital platforms, crowdfunding, which complements rather than supplant conventional funding means, is shaping up to be a vital financing source tailored for start-ups that are experimenting with pioneering business models and/or revolutionary revenue streams [2,35].

Espousing a platform-mediated fundraising mechanism, crowdfunding facilitates the exchange of monetary resources between capital seekers and investors [36]. Equity crowdfunding platforms serve as a medium with low entry barrier for fundraisers to advertise their business ideas, and publicly issue equity shares to funders beyond their immediate geographical vicinity to secure investments [33,37,38]. Although the micro-investment me chanism of crowdfunding disperses the risk borne by individual funder [37], its highly asymmetric decision-making environment renders it difi cult for funders to pinpoint profitable campaigns [33]. This asymmetry is further exacerbated by the lack of knowledge and sophistication of funders as compared to professional investors such as BAs or VCs [12]. These amateur funders are often unqualified to assess the merits of each campaigned business venture, especially in a digital environment where due diligence cannot be efectively enforced [39]. Consequently, prior research has borne witness to how unsophisticated funders, when making investment decisions in equity crowdfunding, rely on diverse information cues like elements of business narratives and fundraisers’ financing strategies [41,46,47]. To name a few, scholars have discovered that the comprehensiveness of business narratives, as denoted through literal elaboration [46,47] as well as the provision of pictures, videos, and external links [48,49], constitute salient drivers of funders’ pledges. This is because an inclusive and versatile business proposal can not only aid prospective investors to comprehend the merits and profitability of the venture [17], but it can also shape prospective investors’ perceptions of campaign quality [47]. Likewise, Hakenes and Schlegel [42] revealed that funders often deem equity crowdfunding campaigns with high fundraising goals as safe bets because they demand corroboration from a larger number of peer investors before the invested funds can be dispensed, whereas Courtney, Dutta and Li [45], Mollick [10], as well as Cumming, Leboeuf and Schwienbacher [41] have countered that a protracted fundraising period could be interpreted as a sign of hesitation on the part of fundraisers, which in turn jeopardizes campaign success.

Because of dispersed risk and information asymmetry, inexperienced funders have also shown their susceptibility to social drivers, such as fundraisers’ responsiveness to funders’ feedback [48] and their frequency of posting updates [50], when forming pledges. Indeed, social capital has been touted to be indispensable in mobilizing funders to pledge for equity crowdfunding campaigns [51]. For example, early investment momentum generated by internal private contacts [52] and fundraisers’ external social media activities were found to attract financial commitments because of higher trust [29,44]. Likewise, other socio-psychological factors like contributor’s anonymity [55], pro-social propensities [54], and reputation effect [53] have been reported to be deterministic of fundraising success by inducing funders’ pledging intentions. From the above statement, it is clear that past studies in equity crowdfunding have largely centered on investigating the key drivers of fundraising success, while the potential issue of overfunding, a distinct phenomenon in which funders continue to invest in a crowdfunding campaign when the pledged amount has already surpassed its predetermined goal [20,24,40], has been overlooked.

## 2.3. Overfunding in Equity Crowdfunding

Overfunding is a major challenge accompanying the visibility of crowdfunding campaigns. Even though overfunding can allow entrepreneurs to expand on their business ventures through the launch of stretch goals, its detrimental impact on fundraisers, funders, and platform providers have received broad consensus among researchers $[ 1 0 , 1 8 , 2 3 , 4 0 , 6 4 - 6 6 ]$ . First and foremost, by channeling scarce monetary resources toward highly visible campaigns, overfunding deprives under exposed fundraisers of an opportunity to showcase their untapped talents. As most crowdfunding platforms espouse an “all-or-nothing” policy, campaigns are obliged to return all elicited funds whenever they fail to achieve their funding goals. Indeed, the “cannibalization efect” of overfunded campaigns has been confirmed by Kim, Lee, Cho and Lee [65] to hinder nascent fundraisers’ campaigning eforts in crowdfunding [64]: many promising start-ups in equity crowdfunding cannot attain their fundraising target because they are overshadowed by their overly successful counter. parts [22,67]. Apart from depriving underexposed fundraisers of much needed monetary resources, overfunding could also pose a threat to overly successful fundraisers by inflating their egos and spurring them to take on unnecessary risks. Overly successful fundraisers, who are bestowed with windfall-like capital surplus, may prove incapable of absorbing the extra resources because these resources have not been budgeted in the original business plans [18]. It is not uncommon for overfunded campaigns to be come overly ambitious, leading to unrealistic claims, unfulfilled obligations, and disappointed funders [10,18]. Mollick [10] witnessed that over 75 % of reward-based overfunded campaigns significantly fell behind on their pro duction schedule, culminating in protracted delays in product delivery. In this sense, overfunding imposes unwarranted pressure on fundraisers by forcing them to guarantee greater returns in exchange for extra resources. This in turn often leads to the violation of promised obligations, damaging entrepreneurs’ reputation, and harming their future financing round [13]. Hence, it is unsurprising that overfunding has been criticized by Turan [68] by constraining exit and future fundraising opportunities for overfunded campaigns because of the latter’s abrupt expansion on post-money valua tion.

Second, as a consequence of overfunding, funders may be coerced into accepting undesired alterations in the business plan or even resource misallocation because they are unable to audit fundraisers’ cash flows and operations once investments have been pledged [69]. Besides, funders shareholding might be diluted by the additional shares issued by the entrepreneur in exchange for extra monetary resources. This not only shrink the amount of dividends paid to funders, but it could also erode the voting rights of funders given an increased number of shareholders [68]. Beyond the compromising gains from overfunded campaigns, pledging for overexposed ventures could also blind funders to other promising business ventures that are dwarfed by their more successful counterparts [18,24].

Last but not the least, while equity crowdfunding platforms could generate trafic by promoting overfunded campaigns, the skewed allocation of capital could cause adverse selection among fundraisers [24,60]. This is because the centralization of monetary resources may deter aspiring capitalseekers from fundraising on crowdfunding platforms because of the fear of being overshadowed, which in turn might reduce these platforms to a “market for lemons” [60,65,66]. Though overfunding has been prohibited by most peer-to-peer lending and philanthropic platforms, this phenomenon remains largely unregulated in venture crowdfunding platforms (i.e., equity- and reward-based platforms) as long as additional incentives are introduced (e.g., issuance of new shares) [19]. Conceivably, overfunding can be deemed to be one of the primary causes of market ineficiency for crowdfunding platforms because of its detrimental efects on the long-term benefits of fundraisers, funders, and platform providers [64].

Yet, in spite of the challenges associated with overfunding, only a handful of studies have touched upon its drivers. Koch [19] unveiled several antecedents of overfunding, including campaign characteristics, fundraiser attributes, funder’s philanthropic inclination, and quality endorsement from the platform, which double as predictors of crowdfunding success. Similarly, Cordova, Dolci, and Gianfrate [43], in comparing the predictors of campaign success with those of overfunding, exposed considerable overlap between the two. Though Cordova, Dolci and Gianfrate [43], and Koch [19] have elevated our awareness of overfunding as a notable trend in crowd funding, how social influence, which has been afirmed to be influential in dictating funders’ investment decisions, could entice overfunding remains largely unexplored.

## 2.4. Herding in Equity Crowdfunding and the role of digital visibility

The digitalized nature of crowdfunding platforms has converted con ventional private equity financing to an open and transparent process, whereby prospective investors could observe the financing progress of campaigns and peer investors’ pledging decisions on a real-time basis [53]. But at the same time, the digital visibility of fundraising progress on equity crowdfunding platforms has substituted due diligence [55]. Because of the transparency of fundraising progress, crowdfunding platforms, through fostering observational learning [57], have become fertile breeding grounds for herding behavior [21,53]. Specifically, the initial herd, which encapsulates an early momentum of synchronized pledges for a given campaign, has been accentuated as a visible bandwagon that directs investors’ subsequent funding choices in crowdfunding [56]. Indeed, ample studie have testified to the decisive role of inchoate financing progress on the outcome of crowdfunding campaigns. Agrawal, Catalini, and Goldfarb [58] contended that fundraisers’ initial accomplishments could shape prospective investors’ beliefs about the venture, which in turn serve to galvanize tides of funders. This is because more pledges in early stages of fundraising could be interpreted as being in possession of private information, which invigorates subsequent investments due to the occurrence of information cascades [51,59]. Consistent with Astebro, Lovo, Fernandez Sierra, and Vulkan [56], we stress the pivotal role played by the initial herd in steering the outcome of fundraising for a given campaign by not only fostering subsequent herding behavior in investments [51], but also by perpetuating a chronic yet stable funding process [56] despite the absence of direct evidence attesting to the true merits of the campaign.

To summarize, though crowdfunding has been popularized as an efi cacious financing avenue, a fulcrum to pivot away from professional capital injection [61], and an invaluable promotional tool for innovative business ventures [11], it should not be hailed as the panacea for resolving the financing predicament confronting entrepreneurial ventures. Particularly, overfunding constitutes a major problem that undermines the ability of entrepreneurs to raise capital on these platforms. In light of funders’ susceptibility of herding, this study is among the earliest attempts to disen tangle the relationship between herding and overfunding on equity crowdfunding platforms. Embracing extant literature that attests to the bandwagon efects of initial investment trends [20,51], we strive to generate novel insights into overfunding on equity crowdfunding platforms by recognizing the role of the initial herd in mobilizing subsequent investments. By comprehending how initial herds, evoked by campaigns with exceptional progress in the early stages of their funding process, exacerbate overfunding, we can move one step closer toward realizing the equality of fundraising opportunity envisioned in crowdfunding [58,62,63].

In the next sections, we first decompose the initial herd into a multifaceted concept comprising the three constituent dimensions of maturity, intensity, and persistency before articulating how each of these dimensions contributes to overfunding. Moreover, during the formulation of our hypotheses, we also explore the interplay among the three initial herd dimensions to dissect its dynamic nature. ## 2.5. Multidimensional view of initial herd and its impact on overfunding

Herding describes a discernible collective behavior in a group of individuals [70]. This collective behavior can be invoked by multiple factors ranging from information cascades to shared beliefs to social ideology [25,71]. For instance, Sun [72] asserted that information cascades may exist when people jump onto the bandwagon created by others and discount their own information, especially given the inaccessibility of private information and the visibility of peers’ decisions. Zhang and Liu [21] revealed that lenders in microloan markets tend to join a herd from which they can infer borrowers’ credibility. However, Burtch [20] countered that mere conformance to herds by discounting private information may not be an optimal strategy, and conceived information cascades as a negative form of network externality. In the same vein, herding can be induced when people share the same information and rely on identical benchmarks to evaluate alternatives and make decisions [25,73,74]. This line of thinking aligns with the convergence theory, which holds that the observing crowd is generally composed of people with comparable preferences [75]. Indeed, past studies have borne witness to the occurrence of unintentional herding in a con ventional funding situation because of the inclination of professional investors to evaluate information identically as a consequence of homogeneity in education and training background [71,73].

Extant literature on herding behavior has primarily revolved around the magnitude of the herd by theorizing it as a monolithic construct. Consistent with Crane and Sornette [76]’s postulation of herding behavior as a dynamic process embodying formation, growth, and dissolution, we model herding behavior as a multifaceted concept con sisting of maturity, intensity, and persistency. Echoing extant literature on crowdfunding, which accentuates the visibility of early pledging momentum and its decisive role in dictating fundraising outcome [25,26,53], we posit that the initial herd, formed during the fundraising of a crowdfunding campaign, is a formidable force that bolsters the probability of overfunding for the campaign.

According to Banerjee [26], people often benefit from being late movers, because more information can be extracted from observing early movers’ performance. Gul and Lundholm [77] confirmed that there is a trade-of between strategic delay and immediate decision such that a ten dency toward the latter acts as an impetus for herding even in the absence of information cascade. These studies stress the criticality of considering the timing when an initial herd picks up momentum, what we labeled as maturity. Specifically, the maturity of the initial herd is an indication of the time it takes for this herd to form. According to Vismara [51], information asymmetry is more severe in the early stages of the funding process because potential funders have less time to assess the campaign and as a consequence, would be making investment decisions based on their observations of previous funding records. Arguably, the earlier the initial herd forms, the more likely it will serve as a salient signal of desirability for potential funders. It has also been established that less mature herds (i.e., ones that formed earlier) tend to self-perpetuate, thereby amplifying their impact [21]. Moreover, less mature initial herds translate into a lengthier time period for subsequent funders to pitch in. Taken together, we antici pate that a less mature initial herd, which manifests as a surge in fundraising progress shortly after the launch of a campaign, heightens the chances of overfunding for the campaign. We therefore hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. The maturity of an initial herd in a campaign is negatively associated with its degree of overfunding.

The instability of herding behavior has been highlighted in past studies [72]. Bikhchandani and Sharma [25] pointed out that herds are usually fueled by intuitive hunches based on limited information, and can become less prevalent when more information is accessible for newcomers to de liberate on. Dictated by their lack of sustainability, herds conform to a typical convex pattern that dissipates after reaching a saturation point [27]. Accordingly, we conceptualize the intensity of an initial herd as the number of funders it galvanized at its peak. On crowdfunding platforms, a more intense initial herd should lead to the fulfillment of a larger portion of the fundraising goal in the early stages of the funding process for a given campaign. The intensity of initial herd has been found to entice subsequent funders’ pledging decisions in two fronts. First, the bandwagon generated in the initial herd could signal the availability of private information [56] and serves as a sign of credibility [78], which in turn boost funders' confidence on investments. Second, funders may prefer well-funded campaigns merely because the chances of those campaigns to attain the fundraising goal could secure the execution of their pledges [51]. Apart from accumulating later pledges, the magnitude of initial herd directly contributes to the overall fundraising process. In line with previous work, we expect the intensity of the initial herd to accelerate funders’ pledging progress [78], thereby culminating in overfunding. We therefore hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. The intensity of an initial herd in a campaign is positively associated with its degree of overfunding.

The volatile nature of herds also means that the number of followers often quickly peaks and dissipates [22]. While the intensity of the initial herd reflects its magnitude, the duration for which this initial herd can persist is representative of its momentum [79]. For this reason, we argue for persistency as a predictor of the impact of the initial herd on overfunding. Persistency of an initial herd can be construed as the extent to which one can observe constant progress toward the fundraising goal of a campaign since its inception. Because persistency of the initial herd validates the ro bustness of a campaign, the bandwagon efect is likely to be inflated [80]. Because of its reassuring quality, observing a more persistent initial herd can entice potential funders to support and push a said campaign beyond it original fundraising goal. We therefore hypothesize that:

## 2.6. Interplay among dimensions of initial herd

Prior research has testified to the decisive role of early funding perfor mance in determining fundraising outcome [23,53]. The strategy for timing the investment has been investigated extensively within the finance dis cipline. As noted by Gale [81], the timing of investment can be either endogenous, whereby agents rely on their own assessment to make investment decisions, or exogenous, whereby investors would wait to acquire more information from peer decisions before deciding whether to invest. Re gardless, it is our contention that the early formation of initial herds can leave more room for both types of funders to make up their mind. There is an abundance of research that has emphasized the pivotal role of early funding performance in shaping subsequent investments [23,52,53]. First, because an immature initial herd, which emerges in the beginning stages of a campaign, is usually seen as indicative of the latter’s superior quality and its receptivity in the market [23,53], it is more likely for funders to exercise less caution in pledging for the campaign due to their heightened desire to capitalize on the opportunity, thereby intensifying the magnitude of the initial herd [82]. Second, in light of the reinforcing efect of early funding performance, the early formation of the initial herd can induce subsequent funders to make hasty decisions and propagate this momentum [21,43]. Taken together, an earlier, less mature, burst of collective investment be havior can mobilize subsequent funders and generate lasting momentum, which in turn culminates in a more intense and persistent initial herd. We therefore hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 4. The maturity of an initial herd for a campaign is negatively associated with its intensity.

Hypothesis 5. The maturity of an initial herd for a campaign is negatively associated with its persistency.

According to Zhang and Liu [21], herds in crowdfunding can be self reinforcing through their own momentum. Past studies have exposed that funders tend to imitate former funders’ decisions and enable information cascades [23]. This cascade of behavior can be intensified if the same type of behavior were to be noticed repeatedly. In this sense, the intensity of an initial herd may augment its persistency. Moreover, it has been found that the longevity of online herds in online environments depends on their scale, meaning that a large-scale outburst of herding behavior tends to decay more slowly [83]. We, therefore, anticipate that the intensity of initial herd in crowdfunding context may extend the time period for which it can be sustained:

Hypothesis 6. The intensity of an initial herd for a campaign is positively associated with its persistency.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Construct operationalization

Although herding behavior has been covered extensively within extant literature on crowdfunding, there is a dearth of research that has attempted to operationalize herds. To this end, we espouse the measures advocated by Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny (hereafter LSV) [84] in the stock market to capture initial herds in the crowdfunding process. LSV is one of the most recognized measures of herding behavior. The idea behind LSV is to compare the actual trend of trading behavior in the stock market with a hypothetical situation where traders execute random and independent trades without the interference of herding. According to Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny [84], LSV denotes unexpected variations in the trading trend that exceeds the threshold permitted by a null hypothesis of no herding. Consequently, LSV accounts for herding behavior generated not only by buyers who purchase shares of a stock, but also by sellers who ofload the shares they hold. Particularly, LSV can be defined as:

The first term portrays the exact discrepancy between the proportion of buyers from the expected proportion of buyers in the stock market. The second term represents the expected value of the first term with herding efect excluded [79].

The LSV in crowdfunding can hence be formulated as:

$$
L S V _ {i, t} = (R _ {i, t} - \lambda_ {i}) - E ^ {N H} [ R _ {i, t} - E [ R _ {i, t} ] ] = (R _ {i, t} - \lambda_ {i}) - 2 \mathrm{exp} (- \lambda_ {i}) \frac {\lambda_ {i} ^ {| \lambda_ {i} | + 1}}{| \lambda_ {i} | !}\tag{2}
$$

where the first term is the observed deviation in the daily transaction amount for campaign i, and the second term is the expected value of the first term in the scenario where there is no herd formation. Consistent with Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny [84], the initial herd for campaign i appears in time t when $L S V _ { i , t }$ exceeds 0 for the first time and disappears when $L S V _ { i , t }$ falls below 0.

## 3.2. Data collection and descriptive statistics

To validate our hypothesized relationships, we tracked daily investments of all campaigns (i.e., 250) that were launched on a leading equity-based crowdfunding platform in the UK between July 2011 and May 2015. The platform ofers a liberated financing environment where fundraisers can actively decide whether to accept extra monetary resources once their campaign has attained the predetermined fundraising goal within a given period. To pocket the excess resources, fundraisers are required to issue additional equity in exchange. Our statistics show that 192 campaigns managed to attain their fundraising goals, with 132 being overfunded by 20.1 % on average. Because of the emphasis on overfunding in this study, we exclude underfunded campaigns in validating our research model. Hence, our dataset comprises 192 successfully funded campaigns with 39,303 investment traces.

For each campaign, we calculate the degree of overfunding as the percentage ratio between the actual investment it received and its fundraising goal. We extract the daily number of investments during the entire fundraising period, which enables us to compute the LSV score. Based on the daily LSV score, we can arrive at calculations of the focal dimensions of initial herds as follows: Initial herd maturity is conceived as the timing when the first positive $L S V$ is detected for a given campaign. Initial herd persistency, defined in this study as the duration for which the initial herd perseveres, is computed by the number of days from its formation (i.e., when $L S V _ { i , t }$ exceeds 0 for the first time) to its dissolution $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ when $L S V _ { i , t }$ falls below 0). Lastly, we operationalize initial herd intensity as the peak in the daily number of investments during the herding period. It is measured by the logarithmic form of the peak amplitude of the initial herd. Definitions and descriptive statistics of all focal variables are detailed in Table 1 below.

To better illustrate the initial herd being captured through our for mulation, we present two exemplary campaigns with distinguishable forms of initial herds (i.e., rectangles highlighted in dark grey) in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2, the campaign named project1 at the top was overfunded by 71.54 %. Its initial herd (i.e., 0.01 maturity, 2.62 intensity, and 0.85 persistency), which formed at the very beginning of the fundraising process, is likely to drive such an astonishing rate of overfunding. In contrast, the campaign named project2 at the bottom was overfunded to a considerably lower extent (i.e., 8.83 %), demonstrating how variations in the dimensions of its initial herd (i.e., 0.42 maturity, 1.11 intensity, and 0.90 persistency) can lead to a separate fundraising outcome.

## 4. Data analysis

Our structural model was estimated by using SmartPLS 3.0 [86]. PLS, an SEM-based technique, has been widely adopted in information systems studies to estimate both structural and measurement models by maximizing the overall variance explained in endogenous variables [87,88]. PLS is an appropriate analytical technique for our research because of its advantages in complex exploratory [89] and theorydriven [90] studies. Additionally, PLS supports single-item measures in structural equation modeling, which coincides with our circumstances where secondary data are utilized [88].

## 5. Discussion

Equity crowdfunding ofers nascent entrepreneurs an avenue for resolving their financing predicaments. Yet, the preponderance of overfunding in equity crowdfunding has skewed capital allocation, thereby restricting the ability of promising business ventures to elicit investments on crowdfunding platforms [17]. In the meantime, overfunded campaigns, which manage to solicit extra funds beyond their original fundraising goal, may run the risk of being overly ambitious and ultimately fail to kickstart their business [6]. We, therefore, advance a research model to investigate the causes of overfunding in equity crowdfunding. Viewing crowdfunding as a breeding ground for herding, we posit the formation of the initial herd to be a key driver behind overfunding. In so doing, we further conceptualize the initial herd as a more nuanced construct consisting of the three dimensions of maturity, intensity, and persistency.

Results indicate that the persistency of the initial herd is positively associated with campaign overfunding. This lends credence to Macht and Weatherston [80]’s postulation that long-lasting initial herds may be perceived as a robust signal of campaign quality, which in turn

First, an initial herd, which exhibits a peak of intense magnitude, points to a concentration of synchronized investment decisions. By projecting a surge in fundraising progress, an initial herd can tempt subsequent funders to follow suit [43]. Second, in line with Crane and Sornette [76]’s observation of the durability of intense bursts of collective responses in social network, the positive linkage between the intensity and persistency of the initial herd implies that the magnitude of the initial herd can generate more momentum and render it more resilient to volatility. Consequently, the intensity of the initial herd in a given campaign can also give rise to overfunding indirectly through its augmented persistency.

While past studies on funders’ herding behavior in crowdfunding have exposed the detrimental impact of herds on fundraising outcomes [23,51,56,64], our findings suggest that even when a prominent initial herd could attract prospective investors to contribute excess capital, this undesirable efect can be contained if its formation is delayed. Countering the positive impact exerted by the intensity and persistency of the initial herd on campaign overfunding, increasing its maturity, which is represented by the amount of time it takes for the initial herd to form, can curb over funding. Mature initial herds, which form later in the funding process, are less likely to induce overfunding. Likewise, the maturity of the initial herd was also discovered to be a salient driver in diminishing its intensity. This inhibiting efect of maturity on overfunding stems from the digital visibility of the initial herd as a quality signal [21,97]. It also adheres to Astebro, Lovo, Fernandez Sierra, and Vulkan [56] and Liu, Brass, Lu and Chen [64]’s assertations on the imperceptibility of delayed formation of initial herd. A campaign, which registered a surge in fundraising progress right from the start, would grab funders’ attention in comparison to one that achieved the same level of fundraising progress much later in the funding process [56]. In other words, delayed formation of initial herds does not punctuate fundraising progress, which in turn obscures its visibility as a quality signal. Besides, mature initial herds also grant prospective funders more time to source for alternative quality signals. Deviating from our speculation, the timing of the formation of initial herd seems to have little efect on its persistency. One plausible explanation for this contradictory finding is that herds, which are formed much earlier in the funding process, tend to be fragile because of the absence of informativity and trustworthiness [76]. For example, the campaign’s initial investment momentum can be easily generated by self-pledging [52], whereby fundraisers elicit the help of their friends and families to invest regardless of project quality. Early formation of initial herd as generated through social endorsement has been uncovered to be a prime deterrence in the investment decisions of subsequent funders because it could be interpreted as initial funders’ deference to the fundraiser rather than the economic merits of the campaign [64].

Specifically, postponing the formation of the initial herd is not only efective in constraining its intensity, but it also reduces the likelihood of overfunding. ## 5.1. Implications for theory

Our study contributes to the extant literature on crowdfunding and entrepreneurial financing in three ways. First, while entrepreneurship and information systems scholars have accentuated crowdfunding as a revolutionary financing channel for entrepreneurs [2,35], we add to this pool of burgeoning work by unveiling how the potential of crowdfunding can be restrained by adverse consequences caused by overfunding. Particularly, we postulate the formation of the initial herd as a conspicuous signal of campaign desirability in the funding process that is visible through crowdfunding platforms. The initial herd is expected to engender a surge in fundraising progress, which can be easily picked up by potential funders as a sign of quality founded on peer endorsement. The initial herd not only afords casual investors the illusion of the redundancy of carrying out sophisticated value assessment of start-ups [21,51], it also tends to encourage overfunding, thereby leading to ineficient resource allocation in capital markets.

Second, we delineate characteristics of the initial herd into three constituent dimensions, namely maturity, intensity, and persistency. We derive these three dimensions from synthesizing prior research on herding behavior $[ 5 6 , 7 6 , 7 9 , 9 9 ]$ to capture the timing of the formation of the initial herd, the size of the crowd it galvanizes, as well as the duration until its dissolution. Through espousing a well-grounded statistical technique [84], we demonstrated how we can identify an initial herd in the funding process and elicit its focal dimensions. In so doing, this study ofers multidimensional theorization and operationalization of initial herding behavior to assist future studies in scrutinizing the efects of herd formation on crowdfunding success.

Finally, our findings shed light on the mechanisms through which initial herds afect overfunding. We not only attest to the negative influence induced by the maturity of the initial herd on overfunding, but also confirm the positive impact on overfunding that is imposed by the intensity and persistency of the initial herd. Furthermore, this study bears witness to the self-perpetuating property of initial herds [76] by confirming the existence of a positive relationship between intensity and persistency. In other words, initial herds can gain momentum to sustain itself much longer than is probable through its mass alone. Notably, our findings unveil maturity as a salient inhibitor of the untamed growth, and the ensuing adverse consequence of initial herd. The absence of dominating visual cues, caused by delaying the formation of the initial herd in early stages of funding process [21], encourages prospective funders to source for a greater variety of quality signals and deliberate more on their decisions. In this sense, greater maturation of the initial herd functions as a counterweight to impulsive and irrational decision-making on crowdfunding platforms [51].

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Our study reminds practitioners in entrepreneurial financing about how the democratization of capital allocation in crowdfunding can be compromised through overfunding [64,65]. Particularly, we yield invaluable insights that contribute to the containment of overfunding, which can be detrimental to crowdfunding platforms if left unchecked. Prior research has documented that overfunded campaigns often fail to fulfill their promised returns due to inflated ambitions [10], whereas a large number of promising start-ups struggle to raise capital [37]. We, thus, address this practical conundrum by highlighting the formation of the initial herd as a primary cause of overfunding.

Beyond drawing practitioners’ attention to the threat posed by the initial herd to the eficiency of crowdfunding markets, we can prescribe actionable guidelines to tackle this issue. While most equity crowdfunding platforms allow fundraisers to decide whether to accept excessive funds after they reach their financing goal or inversely select funders at the end of the fundraising timeframe, fundraisers appear to relish unneeded funds given the pervasion of overfunding in existing platforms where such phenomena can be avoided [66]. Moreover, such ungirt regulation, though retains the platform as a free market of equity financing, is unable to restrain funders’ impulse to overfund by herding with the crowd. Our finding points to the possibility of taming the initial herd without constraining the funding process. For instance, crowdfunding platforms can temporally hide the fundraising progress for a short period of time during the launch of each campaign. This approach can assist in eradicating the initial herd as a dominating quality signal that distracts potential funders from objectively assessing the merits of each newly launched campaign. By rendering the debut for each campaign to be equally visible on a crowdfunding platform, it reduces the likelihood for the campaign to be overshadowed by its more attention-grabbing counterparts. Through this approach, crowdfunding platforms can postpone the formation of the initial herd and alleviate overfunding problems. Concurrent with preserving its benefit in expediting the funding process, the domestication of initial herds through digital visibility management can help crowdfunding platforms move one step closer toward realizing equality in fundraising.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

The study has several limitations that can translate into avenues for future research. First, to accurately identify collective investment behavior, the study concentrates on the daily number of funders for each campaign without accounting for the interplay among competing campaigns and the heterogeneity of funders’ contributions. Future research may extend this work by considering disparities in contribution among individual funders and cross-campaign interactions to unravel the efects of herding behavior on crowdfunding platforms as a whole. Second, as a pioneering attempt to unravel the linkage between herding and overfunding, this study embraces previous assertions on the early momentum of investments as a powerful signal to prospective investors. Because herding has been conceived as herders’ collective responses to stimuli [22,70], probing the impact of such visible outbreak of investments on the entire funding process can aid us in isolating initial herds as the source of such a bandwagon efect. Doing so also allows us to exclude the impact of coexisting social mechanisms (e.g., bystander efect), thereby increasing the internal validity of our findings. Future studies may complement our study by examining how initial herds can potentially give rise to aftershocks in the form of successive herds.

## 5.4. Conclusion

Equity crowdfunding significantly converts the conventional equity financing market into an open and transparent arena, where funders may easily join herds to evade due diligence and cause overfunding [20,26]. Leverage on a unique dataset on the daily transaction records of a leading equity crowdfunding platform, we witnessed the ubiquitousness of overfunding even when fundraisers have the power to refuse excessive funds. We then attested to the role of the formation and evolvement of initial herd on generating overfunds. While our results condemned the intensity and persistency of initial herds that would stimulate overfunding, delaying the outbreak of such synchronized investment trends could significantly diminish funders’ proneness of committing unneeded capitals to already successful campaigns. Our findings on the double-edged impacts of initial herd not only advance our comprehending on herding and overfunding, but yield new insights for crowdfunding practitioners to optimize market eficiency by creating a self-regulated environment where overfunding can be suppressed.
