# Why do citizens participate on government social media accounts during crises? A civic voluntarism perspective

Junpeng Guo<sup>a</sup>, Na Liu<sup>b</sup>, Yi Wu<sup>a,</sup>\*, Chunxin Zhang a

<sup>a</sup> Building 25-A, No.92 Weijin Road, Nankai District, Tianjin, 300072, China <sup>b</sup> Building J12, The University of Sydney Business School, 2006, NSW, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Citizens’ participatory behavior Government social media accounts Civic voluntarism model Perceived connectivity

## A B S T R A C T

Government social media accounts (GSMAs) are increasingly used by government agencies for crisis management. The success of crisis management through GSMAs highly depends on citizens’ participation by commenting on and sharing posts. Drawing on the Civic Voluntarism Model (CVM), this study proposes a research model to explore the key antecedents of citizens’ commenting and sharing behaviors during crises. Through a field survey conducted after the 2015 Tianjin explosions in China, this study demonstrates that emotional support, external political eficacy, rumor control, civic skills, and mobilization are significant antecedents of commenting and sharing, whereas perceived reciprocity only matters for sharing. Moreover, the perceived connectivity of GSMAs moderates several of those relationships. The theoretical contributions and practica implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Various natural and man-made disasters, such as earthquakes, tsunamis, and chemical explosions, pose an ever-present challenge of crisis management. To cope with these crises, the dissemination of relevant information to all crisis response organizations and individuals (e.g., medical assistance, government agencies, public organizations, victims, and volunteers) is crucial to saving lives and minimizing damage. Under such urgency, stress, and uncertainties [1], reliable and efective communication become a key component of crisis management [2]. However, this type of communication during a crisis is dificult to achieve, because it must be established between organizations, communities, and the public. Fortunately, social media (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, and Weibo) is increasingly being used by government agencies to facilitate reliable and efective communication because of its costefectiveness and unique afordances. For example, Facebook has developed the “Safety Check” feature that allows people in a crisis area to confirm that they are safe

Social media has been increasingly used by both the public and crisis management agencies (e.g., [3–6]). Specifically, government agencies use social media to reach to citizens quickly, to keep citizens informed about crisis status, to avoid the spread of false information, to answer questions from victims’ families, to monitor the situation, and to direct donations/assistance to the afected victims [7]. To achieve these goals, government agencies established oficial government social media accounts (GSMAs) to build public profiles on various social media platforms, to disseminate information, and to gather feedback from citizens [8]. Typically, GSMAs play a critical role in crisis management. For example, during the devastating wildfire that happened at Fort McMurray (a Canadian city) in May 2016, diferent levels of government oficials efectively used Twitter and Facebook to disseminate information and updates on fire locations, safety information, and resource status. The victims of the wildfire mentioned that GSMAs were a vital source of information, and they replied or retweeted the posts created by GSMAs. They also participated by engaging government agencies with questions and comments.

For crisis management, GSMAs provide an open channel for crisis information exchange [4] and disseminate timely local updates on the crisis [9] to reduce citizens’ anxieties [5]. GSMAs can gather timely feedback from the citizens to improve the quality of informational and emotional support given to the citizens during crises. Thus, increased access to and the use of GSMAs have increased citizens’ participation in crisis management [3], because citizens’ interaction with GSMAs is critical to the success of digital crisis management [10]. In particular, commenting on GSMA posts are valuable sources of public feedback and suggestions on the activities of government agencies. Sharing GSMA posts will broaden the situational awareness of a crisis. Hence, the citizen’s participatory behaviors of commenting on and sharing posts is important to achieve the goal of GSMAs, which is to convey the right information to the right target through social networks [11].

Previous research on social media during crises mainly focused on public’s use for information dissemination [4,5] and communities’ use for responding to crises [3,7]. The few existing studies on government use of social media concentrated on government’s disseminating crisisrelated information to the public [9] and performing their dominant role during a crisis [11]. Research on citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs during crises is limited, as this is still an under-researched phenomenon. The characteristics of GSMAs influence citizens’ participatory behaviors, when compared with traditional e-government platforms, such as governments’ web portals. Specifically, empowered by various interactive functions of social media, citizens can easily provide their feedbacks and suggestions to government agencies and freely disseminate the posts from a government agency to a wider audience [8]. On the basis of the diferences between interactive features of GSMAs and other web portals of governments, investigating the antecedents of citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs during crises is important. Thus, this current study aims to answer the following research question: “What are the factors that influence citizens’ participatory behaviors of commenting on and sharing posts at GSMAs during crises?”

Citizens’ participation is a public political activity [12,13]. Thus, we will be using the Civic Voluntarism Model (CVM) derived from public administration literature as the overarching framework for the study. The CVM has been widely used to predict and explain citizens’ participation in ofline political events (e.g., presidential elections and governmental policy-making) [13] and online policy deliberation activities [14,15]. Similarly, citizens’ participation in GSMAs provides feedback to the government regarding their efectiveness in crisis management, thereby contributing to the development of crisis policies. Therefore, the CVM is an appropriate theoretical foundation for our study. On the basis of the CVM’s perspective, three groups of factors influence citizens’ participatory behaviors, namely, motivations, resources, and mobilization [13]. Hence, the first objective of this study is to identify the specific key antecedents of citizens’ participatory behaviors on GSMAs in the motivations–resources–mobilization perspective.

Although the CVM has been widely used to explain citizens’ participation in various political campaigns and social events, the role of social media in influencing the explanatory power of the motivations, resources, and mobilization factors has not been considered. Thus, informed by previous research on social media, we want to examine the role of social media in assisting GSMAs to shape citizens’ participatory behavior [16]. GSMAs rely on citizens’ online social networks for in formation dissemination and feedback collection [3]. In this study, we determine citizens' perceived connectivity, which is defined as the ease of reaching others who share similar interests or concerns through GSMAs [15]. This is a crucial factor in elucidating the role of social media, which enables GSMAs to shape citizens’ participatory behaviors. The goal of citizens’ participation in GSMAs is to convey the right in formation to the right people through social networks [11]. Hence, the importance of the key antecedents of citizens’ participation may depend on the extent to which GSMAs are perceived to provide connectivity for citizens. Therefore, the second objective is to investigate the moder ating efects of perceived connectivity on the relationship between antecedents from the CVM and citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs.

The study provides several theoretical contributions. First, although most of the past studies concentrated on either the public’s or communities’ use of social media, this current study enriches the crisis management literature by focusing on and identifying key antecedents of citizens’ participatory behaviors, i.e., commenting on and sharing posts on GSMAs that reflect governments’ use. Second, this paper applies the CVM to social media contexts and further extends the theory by incorporating rumor control as a social benefit under the motivation dimension. Third, this study contributes to an increasingly holistic theory of citizens’ participatory behavior by developing a contingency model of perceived connectivity on GSMAs. The results provide practical guidance to government agencies for developing practices to engage citizens in crisis management during crises. This study ofers insights to providers/operators of GSMAs in designing and improving connectivity performance, thereby playing an important role in crisis management.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Crisis management and social media

Crises are events, such as hurricanes or terror attacks, that are observable in time and space; during crises, societies, or larger subunits (e.g., communities and regions) incur physical damages and losses, leading to disruption of routine functioning [17]. The volatility and unpredictable nature of crises pose unique challenges and requirements for crisis management [6]. Such contexts demand that technologies have a high-level of agility and responsiveness to uncertain situations [18] and are suficiently flexible to facilitate emergent activities and to enable improvization [19]. Given its eficiency in facilitating communication and coordination, social media is among the efective communicating technologies that can potentially support and enhance effective crisis management [6].

The advancement of social media ofers government agencies a better way to deliver public value to people in an eficient manner and to rapidly and efectively communicate with many citizens, especially during crises [20]. Social media enables information to flow from government to citizens and from citizens to governments and to other citizens [21]. Moreover, citizens can share important messages without losing their credibility by simply retweeting or forwarding the messages, because social media is often perceived to be a more credible source of information than traditional mass media [22]. On such platforms, citizens act as a partner rather than a customer in the information delivery process [23]. Moreover, social media platform engages interested citizens to further leverage the collective power of information dissemination and feedback collection. Compared with traditional government online portals, social media is more efective in disseminating information and collecting feedback. Social media helps connect governments with citizens.

On the basis of the urgency of real-time information and crowdsourcing situational information dissemination, social media is becoming the most preferred platform to manage crises [5]. The goal of crisis management is to convey the right information to the right people, and social media is an efective tool for rapid information exchange [11]. Specifically, the public use of social media can improve their crisis situational awareness, facilitate emotional relief [4], and control crisis rumor [5]. Results of the survey presented by the American Red Cross, Americans are increasingly reliant on social media to learn about ongoing crises, seek help, and share information after emergencies<sup>1</sup> . Additionally, social media allows the public to initiate a collective action, share identification, and implement a collaborative control [3] and exchange information [7] to respond to crises in their communities. Furthermore, government agencies can leverage social media to disseminate crisis-related information to the public [9] and reinforce their domination during crisis [11]. However, citizens’ par ticipation in GSMAs, in which governments intend to disseminate authoritative crisis information to and collect suggestions and feedbacks from citizens, is unclear. Social media provides an efective channel for governments to speak with the public [24].

## 2.2. Citizens’ participatory behaviors

Citizens’ participation in GSMAs during crisis is facilitated by the many functions of a social media platform. In a common social media platform, a user can comment on a post, share (repost) a post, click the like button of a post, or just simply save (bookmark) the post for future reference. Although all the actions indicate that citizens are engaged with the posts, not all behaviors contribute to crisis management directly.

The main goal of GSMAs is to convey the right information to the right people through their social networks [11]. Thus, commenting and sharing play a more active role in achieving this goal compared with liking and bookmarking. Citizens’ commenting on a crisis-related post can help attract attention to the crisis-related information and can improve the completeness of the information by adding personal views and insights. Comments from citizens in the crisis-afected areas may also provide useful suggestions or feedback to government agencies. These agencies become increasingly aware of the ongoing situations [4,5]. Similarly, sharing posts also plays an active role by enabling the dissemination of crisis-related information at an exponential rate to reach more audiences and increase awareness [4]. Sharing information has long been recognized as critical for efective crisis responses [6,25]. Bookmarking and liking, although observed on GSMAs, are less critical for efective and timely crisis management. These two behaviors do not contribute to information creation or information dissemination during a crisis [26]. Thus, in this study, we focus on citizens’ commenting and sharing behavior on GSMAs to indicate citizens’ participatory behavior. This study is consistent with prior literature, which stated that commenting and sharing are significant proxies that represent the active usage of Facebook users and are given equal weightage by users in their daily use (Kim and Yang [27].

## 2.3. Civic Voluntarism Model (CVM)

Citizens’ participatory behavior has been extensively studied in the field of public administration [28], and a few theories have been proposed to identify the antecedents of participatory behavior. Among them, the CVM is comprehensive in identifying the major factors related to citizens’ participation; this model has also been empirically validated in many contexts, such as citizen’s participation at political campaigns [29]. The CVM suggests three groups of factors, namely, motivations, resources, and mobilization, as major influencers on citizens participation in civic and political activities [13]. This model has been recently applied to explain citizens’ participatory behavior in online contexts, such as online policy deliberation forums [14,15].

With the penetration of social media in citizens’ daily lives, government agencies start to use GSMAs as a new channel to disseminate information, solicit feedback, and maximize the impact for all messages by leveraging social networks among all citizens. Citizens, who are empowered with various interactive functions of social media platforms, can provide their suggestions/feedback toward governments policies easily. Given the openness, user friendliness, and information visibility of GSMAs [30], citizens are free to comment on and share any post with little technical cost. The distinctive nature required for online participation (commitments for commenting and sharing) in GSMAs and ofline participation in a political campaign makes it imperative to identify the motivation, resource, and mobilization factors in the con text of our study.

According to the CVM, the motivation perspective includes benefits for individuals and groups and a sense of political efficacy [13]. GSMAs enable citizens to form a community based on a crisis and attain col lective actions in helping victims [3]. Citizens participating on GSMAs also hope that this platform will provide them with the same help when they experience a crisis in the future. Thus, perceived reciprocity is an individual benefit for citizens to participate on GSMAs. It refers to the degree in which individuals help others in a crisis because they expect to be helped in other crisis in the future [31–33]. The CVM also suggests that group benefits are a source of motivation, which mainly relate to care for victims, and refer to the positive outcomes that can be enjoyed by all members regardless of their participation [34]. During crises, citizens intend to show their emotional support in releasing negative emotions of the group that is afected by crises, especially the victims [1,4,6]. Thus, emotional support, which is defined as the extent to which a citizen expresses care or concern for others [35], is a critical motivation that reflects the group benefit that can be derived from citizens’ participatory behavior in GSMAs. According to the CVM, another type of motivation is external political eficacy, which refers to a citizen’s belief that the political change is feasible, that is, the government is responsive [36]. Its use is believed to benefit the whole society, which is known as a social benefit, due to the openness of social media [37]. The primary goal of GSMAs is to convey the right information to the right people [11], and participation from voluntary online citizens leads to successful rumor control by spreading timely and credible crisis information [5]. Thus, rumor control, which refers to an individual’s efforts to distribute reliable information and control collective anxiety to suppress rumors spread during a crisis, is an important antecedent of citizens’ participatory behavior.

From the resource perspective, civic skills have been regarded as a key antecedent in the CVM [13]. GSMAs require citizens to articulate their suggestions/feedback and communicate with other citizens during a crisis. Therefore, individuals with great civic skills should be able to present their views/insights well during the commenting process for facilitating their participation in GSMAs [13,15]. According to the CVM, the mobilization perspective includes factors that encourage citizens to invite others to participate. Mobilization involves requests that citizens introduce others to participate in GSMAs [13]. Mobilization for citizens typically occurs in the form of requests to participate from an individual’s relatives, friends, acquaintances, and sometimes even strangers, especially during a crisis. Citizens are willing to respond to this request and participate because of the social influence of the inviter. Thus, mobilization is identified as a crucial factor of citizens’ participatory behavior.

## 2.4. Perceived connectivity in GSMAs

Although the CVM and participation literature have informed the identification of the key influencing factors of citizens’ participation in GSMAs, the features of GSMAs as an interaction platform should be considered as well. In the context of GSMAs, connectivity is an IT facilitating condition that makes it easy for an individual to reach and communicate with others who share similar interests or concerns [16]. GSMAs remove the hindrance of participation by afording an avenue for citizens to involve themselves proactively in a crisis [3]. Citizens increase connections through their initial participation in GSMAs, and these connections engage more citizens to participate in activities. Their participation in diferent forms (e.g., commenting on a post and sharing a post) leads to an accumulation of resources (e.g., crisis updates, safety information, and recovery interventions). From the connectivity perspective, GSMAs overcome distance and alienation by bridging gaps between displaced citizens. Citizens are further encouraged to connect to those whom they would otherwise be unable to reach out to without GSMAs. Thus, GSMAs’ connectivity enables citizens to spread information fast to a wide population in a short time, but it also allows them to establish new relations and expand network at social media platforms.

In the context of GSMAs and other interactive platforms [15,38], participants should communicate and exchange information regarding a focal issue with each other. Past studies examining the use of IT for public participation have suggested that IT should ofer related capabilities (e.g., connectivity). For instance, Shah et al. [39] emphasized the role of IT as a sphere of communicative action for sharing political perspectives or concerns. Vedel [40] proposed that IT-enabled discussion among the public is the key support for online decisionmaking and participation in achieving e-democracy. Similarly, Phang et al. [14] and Phang et al. [15] demonstrated that IT-enabled con nectivity assists individuals to reach to people with similar interests who tend to respond; this situation leads to individuals’ participation in online policy deliberation forums. Furthermore, Leong et al. [3] qualitatively presented that social media connectivity can facilitate the afected community to achieve collective participation and collaborative control in a crisis. Similarly, Tim et al. [6] presented that social media connectivity allows social media to serve not only as an in formation-sharing platform between local eyewitnesses but also as an information source for traditional linear media. In summary, the literature has suggested that connectivity of social media is another crucial influencing factor of citizens’ participation. Therefore, we adopt the concept of perceived connectivity, which is defined as the ease of reaching others who share similar interests or concerns through GSMAs [15], to explain the role of social media that enables GSMAs to shape citizens’ participatory behavior.

## 3.1. Motivations for citizens’ participatory behavior

Perceived reciprocity is an important predictor of participation behavior in online communities [31–33]. It increases users’ expectation of getting efective feedback and increasing the likelihood of recontributing in a group [41]. According to the CVM, perceived reciprocity is also an important predictor of citizens’ participation in a political event [13]. During a crisis, where citizens’ participation in a GSMA may not provide apparent or direct benefits to themselves, they may believe that they will get similar help when they experience similar things in the future, if they do the same thing to help others now. If they believe that their participation would bring them similar support, if they are in a crisis in the future, then they are likely to show concerns and attention through commenting on the GSMAs’ post or simply help passing around the critical information to a wide audience through sharing. Prior research has also confirmed that perceived reciprocity is a significant antecedent of sharing crisis information on WeChat [42]. The partici pation in GSMAs also enables citizens to understand the crisis management principles and process better. Thus, they can be more prepared than before for such things in the future. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H1. Perceived reciprocity is positively related to citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing.

The CVM states that citizens are likely to participate actively in an event if they believe their participation brings some benefits to others (e.g., victim communities). Most people care about the victims of a crisis and are willing to work together to deal with the crisis [4]. A crisis could be devastating, and people afected by the crisis during its occurrence do not only need information and help but also need empathy, comfort, or other forms of emotional support. Emotional support, which is identified as the paramount collective benefit, can be delivered in the form of sharing sadness or expressing mental encouragement through GSMAs [1]. Prior study has also found that citizens are keen to show their concerns to relieve the victims’ emotional sufering in the postcrisis stage [2]. The sharing of sadness and the expression of encouragement become easy with the use of GSMAs. Thus, citizens are likely to use GSMAs to provide encouragements, show sympathy, and empower the victim community if they believe their participation would bring emotional support to the people afected by the crisis [3]. Therefore, we propose that

H2. Emotional support is positively related to citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing.

According to the CVM, external political eficacy, which makes citizens believe that the government is responsive to their inputs, is another essential motivation for citizens’ participation in political activities [13]. In an online context, external political eficacy is also a significant antecedent of public’s contributory behavior in online policy deliberation forums [14,15]. Given the urgency and unpredictability nature of a crisis, citizens are likely to assist governments during the crisis by spreading the latest crisis information and providing suggestions, feedback, and resources on GSMAs if they believe and experience the responsiveness from governments on their inputs. Therefore, we posit that

H3. External political eficacy is positively related to citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing.

Rumor control refers to an individual’s efort to distribute reliable information and control collective anxiety to suppress the spread of rumors during a crisis [5]. Rumor control identifies, investigates, and mitigates disruptive rumors; it is considered to be a main motivating factor of citizens’ participation. The posts on GSMAs are usually perceived as being reliable in its source and contents [22] because most of them are from government agencies. Therefore, citizens are willing to share this type of authoritative information because it helps other citizens to collect reliable information about the crisis; this way it reduces the spread of potential rumors. Citizens are also willing to provide right information (e.g., latest updates on crises) and personal insights to complete the crisis-related posts for eliminating any potential content ambiguity. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H4. Rumor control is positively related to citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing.

## 3.2. Individual resources for citizens’ participatory behavior

In the CVM [13], resources refer to the money, time, and civic skills possessed by an individual. Social media platforms, such as GSMAs, are freely accessible to citizens having little technical skills. Thus, revealing neither money nor time is a barrier to participating in GSMAs during a crisis. Therefore, we contend that civic skills, which refer to citizens’ organizational and communication abilities that can facilitate their participation in GSMAs, are important personal resources needed for the desired behavior [43]. Citizens may develop their civic skills by performing various tasks or assuming roles in institutional settings, such as the workplace, voluntary organizations, and religious institu tions [13]. In the crisis event, citizens’ participatory behavior requires them to articulate their suggestions/feedback and communicate with others about certain crisis situations. Accurate information dissemination is critical for efective crisis management [3]. For instance, sharing posts about a crisis may require individuals to possess the skills that can allow them to clearly understand the information on GSMAs and comments on posts asks for citizens’ ability in accurately expressing the professional knowledge of crisis. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H5. Civic skills are positively related to citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing.

## 3.3. Mobilization of citizens’ participatory behavior

According to the CVM, mobilization refers to the request by others for individuals to participate in activities and is a crucial factor in en couraging the public to participate in political activities [13]. Mobilization is distinct but related to the concept of subjective norms, which is a significant determinant of individual behavior in IT. Specifically, mobilization works because people are influenced by subjective norms [34]; in this case, this mobilization is done to conform to others’ request in participating in crisis-related activities. Mobilization of citizens participatory behavior typically occurs in the form of requests by an individual’s relatives, friends, acquaintances, or sometimes even from strangers [13,15]. For instance, if someone requests you to share crisisrelated posts during a crisis, then she or he may simply tag you in the posts on GSMAs. Thus, this form of mobilization could drive you to share the posts or provide additional information to a crisis post. Accordingly, we propose that

H6. Mobilization is positively related to citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing.

## 3.4. Moderating efects of perceived connectivity

Perceived connectivity enables citizens to reach to other people with similar interests or concerns [15]. Citizens with high connectivity allow their participation in GSMAs appreciated by a wide group of users. Their eforts of commenting and sharing in GSMAs are easily and well recognized by people with similar interests or concerns. Thus, their eforts in helping others and engaging in crisis management as a citizen are likely to be reciprocated in the future. As a result, citizens with high connectivity will be likely to participate in GSMAs through commenting and sharing as they are likely to expect the same help in a similar situation. Therefore, we propose that

H7. The relationship between perceived reciprocity and citizens participatory behavior in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing is strengthened by perceived connectivity.

Citizens from diferent locations during a crisis may experience the same trauma and are eager to provide relief to victims. GSMAs are an efective tool that can expand emotional support through citizens participation. In a high perceived connectivity condition, the expressed emotional support from citizens can be quickly disseminated to others who are with similar interests or concerns by providing suggestions and feedback on posts. Thus, the collective benefit of emotional support is believed to be enjoyed by a broad range of potential victims in a crisis, and the impact of emotional support in relieving victims is perceived to be amplified. This mechanism serves as reinforcement for corresponding citizens to increase their participation in the GSMAs [41]. For example, the suggestions/feedback on mental health support that aims to relieve victims’ anxiety could be provided by commenting on a crisis post or sharing a relevant post on GSMAs. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H8. The relationship between emotional support and citizens participatory behavior in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing is strengthened by perceived connectivity.

Similarly, compared with the low connectivity condition, the in fluence of confidence in government responsiveness (i.e., external political eficacy) on two participation behaviors (i.e., commenting and sharing) become great when perceived connectivity is high. Government responsiveness, which is empowered by external political eficacy, makes citizens feel that their inputs will not be left unnoticed. Thus, they feel that their inputs become worthwhile. In this sense, the connectivity feature of GSMAs allows people with similar interests/ concerns to support each other’s inputs (e.g., by commenting on or sharing each other’s inputs). This situation strengthens the visibility of their inputs to governments. Accordingly, given the same level of external political eficacy, the likelihood to comment on and share posts on GSMAs that citizens will be motivated by government responsiveness becomes high with great perceived connectivity. Therefore, we propose that

H9. The relationship between external political eficacy and citizens’ participatory behavior in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing is strengthened by perceived connectivity.

High perceived connectivity allows like-minded citizens (i.e., with similar interests or concerns) to contact each other easily during a crisis [15]. Similarly, high perceived connectivity breaks the barriers to information access; this situation eases the access of citizens to commonly recognized information, which efectively reduces information asymmetry in managing a crisis [3]. Thus, citizens will obtain credible information when efective rumor control and citizens’ participatory behavior are in place. The efectiveness of rumor control eforts on GSMAs is guaranteed and is further enhanced by the quick dissemination of accurate information in the highly connected network. Thus, the likelihood of citizens to share a large number of posts or provide considerable feedback that is motivated by rumor control will be improved in high connectivity condition in GSMAs. Thus, we predict that

H10. The relationship between rumor control and citizens participatory behavior in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing is strengthened by perceived connectivity.

Perceived connectivity in the social media environment will influence the relationship between civic skills and citizens’ participatory behavior. Citizens in the high connectivity condition have more opportunities to communicate and exchange ideas with more people of similar interests or concerns than those in the low connectivity condition. Thus, their participatory behavior on GSMAs would impact a large group of people. This situation will make them careful and accurate in elaborating their suggestions/feedback. This way assists in efectively communicating with their contacts. Information is as crucial as food and water in a crisis situation; thus, information sharing/provision is critical during crises [25]. On the basis of the benefits derived from information sharing/provision during crises, citizens would be willing to communicate efectively and will value the importance of civic skill when conducting participatory behavior on GSMAs. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H11. The relationship between civic skills and citizens’ participatory behavior in terms of (a) commenting and (b) sharing is strengthened by perceived connectivity.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Research setting

The crisis identified for this study is the 2015 Tianjin explosions. These explosions killed over 100 citizens and injured hundreds of others. The crisis happened at a container storage station at the Port of Tianjin, China on Wednesday, August 12, 2015. During this crisis, various government agencies used their social media platforms on Sina Weibo to disseminate crisis-related information to the public. Since that day, around 330,000 Sina Weibo users have been engaged in discussions about the event; this engagement resulted in more than 4.7 mil lion relevant posts.<sup>2</sup> This context provides an appropriate research setting in which we can focus on citizens’ participatory behavior on GSMAs during a crisis.

## 4.2. Variable measurements and data collection

We adapted and used survey instruments from previous validated scales to test our hypotheses. To measure perceived reciprocity, we adapted three items from Yu et al. [33] and Wasko and Faraj [44], including the sample item “Participating in GSMAs benefits for me because I expect future help from others.” We also adapted three items from Eriksson et al. [35] to measure emotional support, including the sample item “During a social crisis, I participate in GSMAs because I hope to make victims feel be understood through participating.” We adapted three items from Oh et al. [5] to measure rumor control. To measure external political eficacy, civic skills, mobilization, and perceived connectivity, respective items were adapted from Verba et al. [13] and Phang et al. [15]. We assessed most items based on a 7-point Likert scale, except for mobilization, which was measured on a binary scale. All survey items are summarized in Appendix A. Furthermore, we controlled personal relevance, which is defined as the degree to which a crisis is relevant to an individual, on the basis of the scale of directly relevant (i.e., the crisis directly afected the individual), indirectly re levant (i.e., acquaintances of the individual were directly afected), and irrelevant (i.e., the individual was essentially unafected by the crisis).

Citizens’ participatory behavior was operationalized as a formative construct with two indicators (i.e., number of commented posts and number of shared posts). The two indicators were measured using archival data. With the Weibo IDs obtained from survey respondents, we manually collected their personal profiles and activities on Weibo within the first month after the Tianjin explosions. The posts and activities that were not related to the crisis or involving GSMAs were excluded. We counted the number of posts from GSMAs that each respondent commented on and shared. Table 1 shows descriptive statistics of citizens’ participatory behavior in the final sample.

We conducted a pilot study with 87 MBA students residing in the city of Tianjin regarding their participatory behavior on Sina Weibo during the 2015 Tianjin explosions to validate the measurements.

Our study sample consisted of citizens residing in the city of ex plosions (Tianjin) who had all participated in the GSMAs during the 2015 Tianjin explosions. After the explosions happened, various GSMAs published many crisis-related posts through the most popular microblogging platform in China (Sina Weibo), and citizens actively participated by commenting on and sharing these posts. We first identified the GSMAs’ posts related to the event and then sent survey invitations through private messages to users who had reacted to these posts. The data collection period lasted for about 3 weeks from September 27, 2016 to October 20, 2016, and we received 205 valid responses from the 437 invitations sent. Table 2 shows details of the sample population.

## 6. Conclusions and limitations

## 6.1. Discussion on results

The data analysis demonstrates that most of the hypotheses are empirically supported, except for H1a, H7a, H10a, and H10b. Perceived reciprocity does not predict citizen’s commenting on posts on GSMAs in this study. When commenting on a post on GSMAs, the post and at tached comment appear on the News Feed, but other posts push them out of News Feeds before long, especially during a crisis where many posts are delivered. Thus, most of the comments provided by citizens under the posts are likely not viewed by others. Even though citizen are aware of this situation, they are still active in commenting on posts. Thus, this finding indicates that commenting on posts on GSMAs is likely to be intrinsically driven rather than extrinsically driven (e.g., perceived reciprocity) in the event of a crisis. Therefore, perceived reciprocity is not a significant factor when citizens consider commenting on a post on GSMAs. Furthermore, the moderation role of perceived connectivity on the relationship between perceived reciprocity and commenting on GSMAs can be ignored.

Our research shows that perceived connectivity does not moderate impacts of rumor control on commenting or sharing on GSMAs. Oh et al. [5] found that information source and content ambiguities are the two critical drivers of rumor mongering. Thus, a plausible explanation is that the expected ability of rumor control through the use of GSMAs is mainly based on the information source reliability and information content credibility instead of the scale of one’s social network. The context of our study is GSMAs, where all the information posted is mostly from authoritative government agencies. Accordingly, the main efect of rumor control is significant regardless of a participating citizen’s perceived connectivity.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

This study provides several contributions. First, although past studies have mainly focused on either public’s or community’s use of social media tools during a crisis, this study is one of the few works that contribute to the existing literature on crisis management by focusing on citizens’ commenting on and sharing posts on GSMAs that reflect governments’ use. Drawing on the CVM, we extend understanding on the driving factors of commenting and sharing behaviors in GSMAs. Specifically, we identify and empirically verify emotional support, external political eficacy, rumor control, civic skills, and mobilization as significant key antecedents for commenting and sharing behaviors from dimensions of motivations, resources, and mobilization. However, in dividual benefit, in terms of perceived reciprocity, only matters for sharing behavior. This result brings a new insight that sharing is considerably extrinsically driven by the expectation to receive help in the future, whereas commenting is considerably intrinsically driven.

Second, this study further extends the CVM by including rumor control as a social benefit within the motivation dimension in the consideration of the openness of GSMAs [30]. In addition to individual and group benefits, we find social benefit to be an important subdimension of motivation in the CVM. The CVM research scope has been applied in closed contexts of fixed groups of qualified citizens [13] and online communities of registered users [15]. In the open context of social media platforms, anyone with little technical cost can freely access GSMAs and benefit from the information/posts regardless of registration. For example, GSMAs can be accessible through search engines, such as Google. Thus, the participation of a single citizen could benefit the entire open network on social media platforms, which includes registered users and other online bystanders. Accordingly, the inclusion of social benefit into the CVM motivation subdimension further enriches the existing knowledge of CVM and expands the applications of CVM from the context of closed groups to the settings of open communities.

Finally, this study contributes to a holistic theory of citizens’ par ticipatory behavior on GSMAs by developing a contingency model of the CVM factors and perceived connectivity of GSMAs, which is a critical feature of social media. The connectivity of GSMAs empowers a citizen to easily communicate with other people sharing similar interests or concerns during a crisis [16]. Such a feature is typically important for crisis management because the goal of crisis management is to convey the right information to the right people [11]. Thus, the IT factor, that is, perceived connectivity, provides the condition that may alleviate constraints of the individual citizen from participating in crisis management and thus promotes citizens’ participatory behavior. To this end, we find the efect of perceived connectivity on the positive relationships between the CVM factors in terms of motivations and resources and citizens’ participatory behavior and further elucidate the diferences in commenting and sharing. The findings, which are consistent with those in previous studies (e.g. [3,6],), once again demonstrate the importance of GSMAs’ connectivity function in crisis management.

## 6.3. Implications for practice

The findings provide practical guidance for the efective use of social media by crisis management agencies. The study ofers a greater understanding of the methods of engaging citizens in participatory behavior in terms of commenting on and sharing posts on GSMAs during crises. Typically, crisis management agencies need to improve citizens’ confidence that their participatory behavior can indeed help agencies’ in crisis planning, response, or recovery, that is, external political eficacy. This task may be achieved by convincing citizens that their participation matters for crisis management. For instance, the dashboard of valuable posts, the list of top information providers, and other materials that detail how citizens’ shared and commented posts have been incorporated in victim identification, crisis recovery in structions, charity donation, and other crisis management operations can be included on GSMAs for this purpose. Crisis management agencies also need to recognize the importance of improving the public’s civic skills to facilitate their participatory behavior in case of crisis events. The agencies should provide crisis-related knowledge and materials (e.g., self-rescue skills, recovery tips, and crisis-conversation skills) to the public through their GSMAs. Requests to participate can also be sent to mobilize citizens for increasing citizen’s participation. One way to achieve this goal is to use mobile location tracking technologies to identify those who are within the crisis-afected area and urge them to share or provide any real time and credible crisis information. Crisis management agencies should also proactively highlight the individual benefit (i.e., perceived reciprocity), group benefit (i.e., emotional support), and social support (i.e., rumor control) to be obtained by participating in crisis management on GSMAs. For group and social support, the agencies should apparently show their appreciation to the public for their participatory behavior in helping victim or other afected individuals.

Our study also ofers important insights into GSMAs’ platform operators. Perceived connectivity is an important feature of GSMAs because it enables citizens to reach to others who have similar interests or concerns during the crisis. Thus, operators should pay close attention to improving connectivity performance of their platforms. They should design and use certain connection technologies to assist citizens in building connections with others who share similar interests for achieving the aforementioned goal. For instance, the operators of GSMAs should recommend the crisis management professionals to reach out to crisis-afected citizens automatically after a crisis happens. Liu et al. [48] argued that no preferred single social media form is available for disseminating information about the crisis. Thus, a continued refinement in communication technologies is required for ef fective dissemination of information to the afected citizens during crises.

## 6.4. Limitations and future work

As we interpret the findings of our study, its limitations must be acknowledged. First, we conduct our study on a single social media platform with respect to only one crisis event. As institutional contexts vary across nations, this way may restrict the generalizability of our findings. However, this limitation may be less critical with the inclusion of external political eficacy in our model, which is closely related to the institutional context [13]. Nonetheless, future research of GSMAs in other countries with diferent institutional contexts may further validate our results. Moreover, this study focuses on GSMAs (i.e., Sina Weibo) for citizens’ participatory behavior. With the prevalence of various IT platforms, such as government online portals and online forums, future research may address how the characteristics of these platforms can facilitate other social media participatory behavior. Although a cut-of threshold of 0.10 as an acceptable R-square is considered [49], the values of R-square in this study are not that high (e.g., 0.131 and 0.214). This result may be due to the fact that self-report and archival data are used to measure independent and dependent variables, respectively. Past studies have demonstrated that variance explained by key determinants, which are measured by survey, for actual use is usually considerably lower than that for use outcome measured by the survey method [50]. One of the potential ways to increase the Rsquare value is to include additional factors. For instance, information quality and social influence are also strong predictors for individuals sharing or commenting on posts in online communities.

## 6.5. Concluding remarks

With the increasing prevalence of GSMAs, understanding how to engage citizens’ participatory behavior in terms of commenting on and sharing crisis-related posts for crisis management and other government activities is imperative for citizens, government agencies, and the society. Our model, which is theoretically driven and empirically supported, could provide a holistic understanding regarding the role of social media in crisis management.
