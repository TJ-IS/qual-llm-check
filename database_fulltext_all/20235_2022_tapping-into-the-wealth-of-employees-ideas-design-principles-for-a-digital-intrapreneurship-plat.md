# Tapping into the wealth of employees’ ideas: Design principles for a digital intrapreneurship platform

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Action design research Intrapreneurial behavio Emplovee-driven innovation Digital intrapreneurship platform Socio-technical systems Design knowledge

## A B S T R A C T

Digital transformation ofers organizations opportunities to involve intrapreneurs, i.e., employees sharing the zeal of entrepreneurs, but innovating within organizational boundaries through the generation of new ideas. Despite organizations’ interest in exploiting their employees’ innovation potential, tools as digital intrapreneurship platforms guiding and hosting this innovation process are limited and their design is challenging. Thus, we provide the results of an action design research project with an IT service provider and describe the iterative design process. The designed intrapreneurship platform is ingrained in the socio-technical system theory. Its evaluation derives design principles, guiding organizations to design viable platforms facilitating intrapreneurial behavior.

## 1. Introduction

To succeed in the everlasting hunt for more innovation, idea-driven organizations with a novel understanding of the employee emerge as an increasingly important phenomenon [1]. Today’s leading tech organi zations, such as Google or Apple, form environments for so‐called intrapreneurs, i.e., employees who may be distributed among all departments who share the drive and zeal of entrepreneurs, but innovate within the boundaries of their organization depending on its monetary, technical, and human resources [2–4]. They are characterized by an anticipatory and agentic behavior aimed at creating new ideas for the organization, thereby increasing an organization's capability to respond to internal and external developments [2,4,5].

To assemble these intrapreneurial employees, provide them with the possibility to work collaboratively with a large number of colleagues, and use their collective innovation potential, organizations need con nectivity and collaboration tools [4,6,7]. While employee‐driven innovation has only been a side note for decades, just characterized by the existence of simple suggestion boxes, the digital transformation now ofers organizations the opportunity to utilize sophisticated platform‐- driven processes. The use of digital intrapreneurship platforms<sup>1</sup> may not only help to increase employees’ motivation and commitment to create innovation, but may also cost considerably less than nondigital initiatives [8–10]. Moreover, through the use of these platforms, some organizations have already gained significant benefits in terms of financial savings due to an increasing number of employee-generated ideas. For example, Allianz UK has achieved an estimated annualized benefit of more than \$28.3 million based on more than 41,000 newly generated ideas, since the introduction of such a platform [10]. Consequently, as these platforms could be accelerators and catalyzers for employee-driven innovation, it is important to answer the question of how to design digital intrapreneurship platforms that address issues, such as how to incentivize participation, how to keep idea generation up and running, as well as how to measure an idea’s value [11].

A platform’s digital nature, the interrelationship with a diverse set of stakeholders, and an overall increasing complexity of platform architecture present challenges to its design [12–16]. Existing design and intrapreneurship platform research predominantly lack the theoretical basis and do not evaluate the developed artifact (e.g., [7,8,17]). Literature is also silent when it comes to providing explicit design principles or blue prints for digital intrapreneurship platforms. Consequently, theoretically grounded and rigorously evaluated design principles for such platforms represent a significant research gap [11].

Therefore, we formulate the following research question: Which design principles guide the design of a digital intrapreneurship platform fostering employee-driven ideas?

To answer our research question, we follow a design science re search (DSR) approach as recommended by de Reuver et al. [11]. Specifically, we apply the action design research (ADR) methodology developed by Sein et al. [18], which is regarded as a promising approach to tackle multi-actor design settings, such as digital intrapreneurship platforms [11]. The theoretical foundation of the designed platform is drawn upon the components of socio-technical system (STS) theory [19–22], which we operationalize into design principles for the platform design. By following the Framework for Evaluation in Design Science (FEDS), we conduct an extensive and rigorous evaluation of the platform’s design [23].

We apply ADR to a real world problem of a large information technology (IT) organization, which provides services for a conglomerate of institutions in the financial industry. This organization was sticking to the existing practice of submitting employee-driven ideas through physical suggestion boxes. Overall, this practice was characterized by a low acceptance rate as well as a low number and quality of submitted ideas; thus giving away valuable innovation potential of employees personifying intrapreneurs. More specifically over a period of 4 years, in our case, an organization with almost 5000 employees across 3 locations, only 18 ideas were submitted to suggestion boxes, and 4 of these ideas alone were actually implemented. The mentioned challenges demonstrate the weaknesses of the existing procedure and call for a new form, which makes use of a platform to facilitate em ployees’ intrapreneurial behavior in the researched organization.

Our research contributes to the topic of digital intrapreneurship in Information Systems (IS) research in 2 ways. First, based on insights gained from an ADR project with an IT service provider, we contribute to IS literature by deriving theoretically grounded and rigorously evaluated design principles for digital intrapreneurship platforms fostering employee-driven ideas. Second, we contribute to design research by highlighting the importance of involving stakeholders in the platform design process, who are not directly involved in the use of the platform (e.g., department head). Building on these findings, we provide implications for organizations’ innovation capability by describing the management’s role in deriving value from these platforms, such as identifying new intrapreneurs.

## 2. Related work and theoretical foundation

In this section, we present related work and discuss the theoretical foundation of the digital intrapreneurship platform design. As research on the design of such platforms is scarce, we build on related literature focused on digital innovation platforms, idea management systems, and internal crowdsourcing platforms. Next, we explore existing ADR studies in the context of innovation to derive general design insights for the platform. Additionally, we outline on the STS theory [19–22], which represents the theoretical foundation of our work, and relate thi theoretical framework to the digital intrapreneurship platform.

## 2.1. Digital innovation and intrapreneurship platforms

Literature on digital innovation and intrapreneurship platforms design and the factors of success is still scarce. According to de Reuver et al. [11], prior research has predominately focused on anecdotal ex post studies with success stories of organizations, such as Google [24] and Apple [25,26]. Furthermore, research on digital innovation plat forms has not discovered much design knowledge so far [11]. With respect to the design of digital intrapreneurship platforms, a review of existing literature revealed an even more pronounced lack of research. Nevertheless, a preceding literature search for concepts of digital in trapreneurship platforms had shown that a variety of similar or partly overlapping systems and platforms. in prior literature. could be used to derive important design components as well as inspire and guide the platform design in the context of our work. For example, existing research on idea management systems and internal crowdsourcing platforms, can ofer meaningful insights into various design aspects of digital intrapreneurship platforms. As these systems were developed to reach similar objectives or solve similar problems and may comprise components, which could be important for digital intrapreneurship platforms as well (e.g., crowd building possibilities or comment functionality), considering these literature streams is relevant to address the design problem. A broad and iterative literature search that incorporated various search terms that are related to a digital intrapreneurship platform<sup>2</sup> ensured a systematic search of existing research without making strict assumptions.

Prior literature, summarized in Table 1, informs about design insights from research on digital innovation and intrapreneurship platforms. For example, Sandström and Björk [8] ofer a description of an idea management system for a specific setting without providing a theoretical basis or evaluating the developed artifact. They emphasize the need for well-defined criteria to evaluate innovative ideas and the requirement for the installation of a database collecting submitted ideas.

While Bansemir and Neyer [17] solely portray shortcomings and requirements of 3 existing platforms, their research ofers some insights on the design of idea management platforms. More specifically, the authors point out the importance of cross-departmental collaboration and consider motivational aspects when designing their systems (e.g., incentive structure). The literature review conducted by Flynn et al. [29] aims to analyze the process of idea generation, and barely touches upon the conceptualization of an innovation platform. The paper provides design insights regarding the inclusion of multiple users across diferent departments as well. Another strand of literature focuses on specific, but minor aspects of these platforms. For instance, Westerski et al. [30] provide design insights in the form of possibilities for transparent information presentation on a platform. Benbya and Leidner [7,10] do not evaluate an idea management platform but distil recommendations and lessons learned from the introduction of one. Moreover, Ciriello et al. [28] create an IT artifact to provide a proof of concept, without deriving concrete design principles for their ideascreening framework. Abu El-Ella et al. [27] take a solely technologyfocused perspective by exploring the integration of diferent technologies for employee participation in innovation, and thereby, emphasize the role of incentive systems to include more employees.

## 2.3. Socio-technical system theory

Digital intrapreneurship platforms form an IS-enabled activity of multiple individuals that is triggered through a call for action within the organization. First, these platforms are characterized by activities that occur in a competitive or collaborative setting among a group or network of individuals who perform specific tasks and who are part of a certain structure [41]. Second, digital intrapreneurship represents a phenomenon that is shaped by technology, such as dedicated specialist tools [42] and generic social media (e.g., blogs or wikis) [43]. Third, digital intrapreneurship occurs in an organizational setting that forms a distinct environment [44]. Researchers have applied the STS theory to examine IS that comprise complex interactions between individuals, technology, and the corresponding environment [45,46]. Therefore, based on prior research [22], a digital intrapreneurship platform can be seen as an instantiation of a complex work system [47], where the STS theory serves as a helpful theoretical foundation that has been widely used as a theoretical lens in empirical and conceptual literature for successfully designing work applications [45,48].

The theoretical foundation of the designed digital intrapreneurship platform is drawn upon the components of STS theory [19–22]. The fundamental assumption of STS theory is that the design of systems should be a process that considers both technical and social factors afecting the usage and functionality of IT-based systems [46].

According to Lyytinen and Newman [22], within a work system an interaction between task, actors, structure, and technology occurs. These components of work systems (see Fig. 1) are all embedded in the organizational environment, and are major drivers and influencing factors of change [22,49,50]. Within our work, we consider the following 5 components of digital intrapreneurship platforms from the theoretical perspective of STS (e.g., [22]).

The actors component involves the modeling and design of all pertinent individuals of and in the work system [22,50–52]. In our context, this component contains the main stakeholders, such as the organization’s managers and intrapreneurial employees, who carry out innovation projects.

The structure component covers the design of communication and workflow [22]. Thus, this component encompasses the organization’s systems of authority, and the behavioral dimension (i.e., behavioral patterns, such as how actors execute authority, communicate, or work within the case organization’s boundaries), as well as the normative dimension (i.e., norms, values, and overall role expectations).

The technology component incorporates all modeled tools, such as hardware and software [22]. In the context of our work, it represents all IT/IS systems that are part of the digital intrapreneurship platform or enable accompanying processes.

The task component comprises the design of purposes, goals, and deliverables [22]. Within the work at hand, the component describes the digital intrapreneurship platform’s purpose, objectives, and processes, which enable the preparation of intrapreneurship projects within the organization.

Finally, the environment component accounts for external aspects influencing the work system itself [22]. So, it outlines to what extent the digital intrapreneurship platform takes place in a certain organizational setting. Thus, a work system can be defined as a system in which people and technology perform processes and activities to develop products/services for internal and/or external customers [47].

## 3. Action design research project

In our research project, we cooperated with an IT service provider in the financial industry to solve the challenges within their existing system to generate innovation ideas (i.e., suggestion box). By designing a digital intrapreneurship platform to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior (i.e., employee-driven innovation), we address the low acceptance and usage rate prevailing in the existing system.

## 3.1. Methodology selection

Given the interdependence of technical and social aspects related to work systems, we decided to design a socio-technical intrapreneurship platform to increase intrapreneurial behavior. According to Leavitt [19] and Lyytinen and Newman [22], successful work systems are characterized by a balance between all STS components. Therefore, we needed to understand technical and social requirements/challenges of individuals, culture and organizational structures. Using the STS theory [19–22] as the theoretical lens enabled us to counteract potential imbalances and ensure that all components for a successful work system are considered in the new design of the digital intrapreneurship platform. Given our overall research goal of deriving design principles for digital intrapreneurship platforms, we therefore, choose an approach that provides an opportunity to build an innovative IT artifact em bedded in the social environment of the organization.

As DSR considers technical design aspects [53] and action research (AR) is strongly oriented toward social design aspects [54,55], in particular the collaboration of stakeholders, we decided to apply a combination of DSR and AR in our study. Therefore, we follow an ADR approach, as recommended by de Reuver et al. [11], which balances the rigor of DSR and the relevance of AR [56,57]. Specifically, we follow the methodology developed by Sein et al. [18]. Hence, we use ADR as a “research method for generating prescriptive design knowledge through building and evaluating ensemble IT artifacts in an organizational setting” (Sein et al. [18], p. 40), while addressing an underlying practical problem (Sein et al. [18]).

In the context of this project, one substantial argument for choosing ADR is that the research method specifically acknowledges IT artifacts (e.g., intrapreneurship platforms) shaped by both researchers and practitioners. This is in line with the project in this work, where re searchers, the main developer and employees covered the theoretical, technical, and practical perspective, and contributed their expertise to the platform design process. Moreover, the method is directed by realworld problems employees face in their daily work (Sein et al. [18]). In this research project, we address the low acceptance and usage rate of the existing system (i.e., suggestion box) that was used for facilitating intrapreneurial behavior (i.e., employee-driven ideas for innovation). Another argument for using ADR is that the organization’s objective to efectuate internal digital change fits well with the interventionist nature of ADR. Thus, changing digital practices through interventions suits the idea of the artifact in ADR, which needs to include both the IT artifact (e.g., digital intrapreneurship platform) and the related social and work environment.

## 3.2. The case organization

To develop a digital intrapreneurship platform and to demonstrate its efects, we cooperated with an industry partner. This organization asked us to help them to solve the challenges within their existing approach to generate innovation ideas (i.e., suggestion box). Applying the ADR method, we implemented a digital intrapreneurship platform artifact in the case organization together with the organization’s project team. The case organization is a large IT service provider for a conglomerate of financial institutions. It ofers a whole range of IT services, containing development and implementation of IT solutions, networks and technical infrastructure, as well as training, consulting, and support. The organization employs almost 5000 people across 3 locations, handles nearly 141 billion technical transactions and is responsible for servicing 118 million accounts per year on average.

We gained first-hand insights into issues related to the transformation of traditional idea management (i.e., suggestion box) (see Appendix F, Fig. F1) into a new form of system, which emphasizes employee-driven ideas based on a digital intrapreneurship platform. We not only learned about experiences with the existing (and less successful) system, but were also able to implement and test the newly designed digital intrapreneurship platform and discuss its impact with practitioners and end-users.

The ADR project started in October 2015 and lasted two and a half years. After 2 cycles (alpha and beta cycles) within the ADR process, a digital intrapreneurship platform artifact evolved. The project was completed in April 2018, when we used the findings from the designed digital intrapreneurship platform (IT artifact) to formalize design knowledge [58].

At the start of the project, we identified 3 internal stakeholders: the project team (consisting of 5 employees), the in-house consulting department, which has a service and advisory function across the organization (in the following referred to as department), as well as the remaining employees of the organization. Because of its cross-divisional nature, the department (with 88 employees) was chosen as the initial location to develop a new artifact. This artifact, for enhancing intrapreneurial behavior, needed to be tested by the department before rolling it out in the entire organization.

Four researchers, representatives of the department (including the head of the department and one finance representative), and the project team (including the innovation manager and a main developer) formed the ADR team. Within the ADR team, an information scientist from the organization took the main developer’s role. He was also a university afiliate who has taken part in a part-time study program. In the beginning, the ADR team conducted several meetings to discuss the project’s conditions and goals. During the discussions, the case organization’s representatives emphasized that they perceive designing a new IS-enabled system as appropriate to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior in the form of employee-driven ideas.

## 3.3. Insights into the design research process

In line with the ADR process that Sein et al. [18] present, we executed the ADR project in 4 stages: 1) problem formulation, 2) building, intervention and evaluation, 3) reflection and learning, and 4) for malization of learning.

In the first iteration (alpha cycle), the ADR team designed and evaluated the conceptual design of the digital intrapreneurship platform. Based on the alpha cycle’s results, we refined the artifact’s design in a beta cycle that was then implemented and evaluated by various employees at the IT service provider. In the following, we outline the methodology of the 4 diferent stages. We then describe the results and accurate implementation in more detail, in Section 4, following the same structure. For a summary of the data collection and analysis methodologies for the extensive and iterative design process see, Ap pendix A, Table A1.

## 3.3.1. Problem formulation

In the first stage, we identified the issues relevant for both practi tioners and researchers to specify the challenge to be addressed. To obtain a deeper understanding of obstacles in the existing system (i.e., the use of a suggestion box) for developing innovation ideas among the employees of the case organization, we conducted exploratory interviews. By examining practitioners’ perspectives on intrapreneurial behavior at an IT service provider in the financial industry, the ADR project followed Sein et al. [18] first principle (i.e., the principle of research inspired by practice). As a groundwork and preparation as well as to triangulate gathered data, we complemented investigations by studying documents regarding the boundary conditions of our project in the researched organization. In total, we scanned 18 documents, including innovation management objectives, management notifications regarding organization’s strategic orientation as well as process documentations of the existing work system.

The exploratory interviews were broadly structured in accordance with the 5 STS components to ensure that comprehensive insights could be generated for a successful design. Additionally, the findings of a document analysis delivered working assumptions, which built the foundation of the interviews. While the ADR researchers prepared the explorative interviews, the practitioners coordinated the sampling of the interviewees. To prevent selection bias, we informed the practi tioners on how to deploy a representative sample. In total, we selected 12 interviewees (1 department head, 2 project managers, 3 technical specialists, 1 innovation manager and 5 regular employees working in the project management ofice), which approximately reflected the distribution of roles in the department. At the beginning of each in terview, we provided basic information regarding the study’s purpose, anonymity, and the use of gathered data to the interviewees [59]. The interviews lasted between 50 and 80 min each and were conducted by 2 researchers. The interview questions addressed a wide range of innovation management topics among which a number of questions focused specifically on problems and issues in handling and using the suggestion box for employee-driven ideas both from a social and technical perspective. We recorded all interviews, transcribed them as text files, and then stored all data sources anonymously, so that they could not be traced back to the participants.

To analyze the collected data, we followed an inductive coding approach [60], which helped us to extract frequently mentioned themes that are dominating across interviews. To enhance internal validity [61], 2 researchers coded all transcripts. Intercoder agreement between the 2 coding researchers was on average at 92 percent. In the first step of the qualitative data analysis using NVivo, both researchers identified, marked, and labeled text segments related to the project that the interviewees mentioned frequently. Based on these 743 text segments, 20 categories were derived. After this step, the 20 categories were assigned to the STS components to better deduce design requirements for the planned platform. Thus, in discussions with all 4 researchers, the number of categories was iteratively reduced to 5 to create an alignment with the STS components. Based on the final categories, we translated the identified main issues into a set of meta-requirements [62] formulated as challenges. These challenges needed to be met and solved by the newly evolving IT artifact.

Next, we synthesized design insights from existing research by re viewing literature on digital platforms in related contexts (cf. Section 2). These results were also structured according to the STS components (see Appendix F, Table F1) following Sein et al. [18] second principle, to guarantee a theory-ingrained artifact. Finally, we matched the challenges derived from the interview sessions with the design insights gathered from existing research to collectively inspire the initial design principles (see Appendix F, Fig. F2). Therefore, we ensured that the platform design was informed both by practice and research, and that all components for a successful STS were considered.

Furthermore, we analyzed existing IS in the organization (e.g., organization’s wiki) to identify opportunities and/or restrictions for establishing a new digital platform within the existing technological infrastructure.

## 3.3.2. Building, intervention, and evaluation

The second stage interweaves the building of the IT artifact, the intervention in the organization, and the evaluation (BIE) (Sein et al. [18]). Accordingly, practitioners and researchers jointly designed, reflected, and evaluated on the digital intrapreneurship platform in an interactive and overlapping process. Our ADR study followed an ITdominant BIE scheme (see Fig. 2) to generate design knowledge on the design artifact that was continuously instantiated and repeatedly tested through organizational intervention (Sein et al. [18]). The IT-dominant BIE form was chosen as the guiding concept, as the anticipated design of the digital intrapreneurship platform deviated considerably from the existing system.

The initial design of the digital intrapreneurship platform was generated based on the challenges of the previous stage. Using the STS theory as a basis (see Section 2.3), we transformed the identified challenges with the help of design insights from literature into a conceptual design (1) that described the functionalities of the platform, and (2) that fulfilled social requirements relevant for a better integration of the digital intrapreneurship platform in the organization and greater employee acceptance of it (for a detailed description see Section 4.2).

Based on this conceptual design that was visualized in the form of mock-ups, a first set of preliminary design principles emerged. We formulated the design principles in a way that enables practitioners and researchers to create further artifacts that belong to the same class of systems in the same or similar context [62–64]. The design principles are not only based on the principle of practically inspired research in the context of IT service provision in the financial industry but also theoretically ingrained research, which Sein et al. [18] suggest. We also relied on insights from prior research to formulate the identified design principles as well as select functionalities whose efects have been evaluated in existing literature, and match with the challenges raised within the interviews (see Appendix F, Table F1).

## Evaluation strategy

A systematic and rigorous evaluation of the artifact is an essential part of each DSR project [65]. The same is true for ADR projects. Therefore, the interventions were evaluated with 3 primary goals: (1) to test whether the design principles’ intended efects are realized, (2) to assess whether there is a need for additional cycles, and (3) to evaluate the artifact in a real setting by considering the environment of an organization rather than synthetic situations (Sein et al. [18]). More specifically, we followed the FEDS strategy (a framework for evaluating DSR artifacts), which proposes conducting an evaluation in 4 steps [23].

The evaluation’s objective was to provide evidence that the sug gested design could be instantiated in the form of an artifact that addresses the described problems, and attains the expected environmental utility, e.g., facilitating employees’ intrapreneurial behavior. Within the strategy, we decided to rely on the human risk and efectiveness strategy, which should be adapted, if considering a “socio-technical ar tifact with major uncertainties about social and use issues” ([23], p. 83) and when “a critical goal of the evaluation is to rigorously establish that the utility/benefit will continue in real situations” ([23], p. 82). Thus, we iteratively evaluated the artifact’s conceptual design in the alpha cycle within the ADR team to obtain first insights. The evaluation approach assesses the functionality outlined in the design principles and the impacts of their instantiation in separation from each other. Thus, this episode can be classified as a formative evaluation [23]. Equipped with a more in-depth understanding of the impacts, in the beta cycle we evaluated the individual design principles in focus groups and a pilot study. Subsequently, in a field study, a summative evaluation was conducted to validate the design and the efects of the platform’s in stantiation (all 5 design principles together in an artifact) to increase the generalizability [57]. These naturalistic evaluation episodes, in the form of field studies, were conducted at the IT service provider with real users in the in-house consulting department who have real problems [23].

With regard to the evaluation properties, we focused on the validity of the overall design and the efects of its instantiation on employees intrapreneurial behavior (i.e., employee-driven innovation). Fig. E1 (see Appendix E) depicts the 3 complementary evaluation episodes.

## Alpha cycle

In the alpha cycle, the ADR team designed and iteratively evaluated the artifact’s conceptual design (e.g., with mock ups) to obtain first insights and to prevent resource-consuming expenditures. Therefore, the experience of practitioners (i.e., employees at an IT service provider in the financial industry) and researchers shaped the design of the di gital intrapreneurship platform. Based on the alpha cycle’s results, the artifact’s design was further refined in a beta cycle that included the intervention and evaluation among employees. During both cycles, the researchers ensured that the design alterations were in accordance with existing literature in IS research Sein et al. [18].

## Beta cycle

In the beta cycle, the evaluation took place among employees at the IT service provider using a structured approach. We divided this evaluation into 2 parts: in the first part, we conducted 4 workshops. The workshop series was designed as a set of exploratory focus groups (EFG) [66] that facilitated open discussions and, thereby, enabled the collection of qualitative data on participants’ opinions, attitudes, and be liefs [67] as well as people’s understanding and the use of the artifact [66]. The results of the EFG evaluation were used to propose and achieve incremental improvements in the artifact’s design [66]. During the workshops, which lasted between 60 and 90 min, we presented the beta design to 20 employees and asked them to provide their feedback [66]. To receive comprehensive feedback from multiple perspectives, we purposefully chose employees who hold diferent roles in the inhouse consulting department. One participant was the department head, 2 participants provided internal IT support for the department, 1 was an innovation manager, 1 a team assistant, 4 were in-house con sultants, and the remaining 11 were employees responsible for project management duties all around the in-house consulting projects.

Based on the workshops’ feedback, we conducted a 3-month pilot study with the beta prototype, which formed the second part of the evaluation. With the pilot study, we simulated and tested the developed artifact to obtain deeper insights on the intervention’s efects. To this end, we conducted semi-structured interviews with participants who took part in the pilot study. The interviews gave indication of further issues in the prototype’s functionality and features. More specifically, the interview questions addressed the first set of design principles and covered all aspects regarding prototype use in the pilot study. We asked the interviewees about anticipated and unanticipated consequences of the design principles and the underlying separate design features, which represent the design principles’ implementation in the platform.

Although all design principles seemed to be valid in general, we had to refine the first set of design principles (see next section) based on the anticipated and unanticipated consequences gained in the intervention and evaluation phase.

## 3.3.3. Reflection and learning

Following the 2 design cycles, we carried out reflection and learning sequences. Hereby, we used the anticipated and unanticipated con sequences (see section 4.2.3, Table 5) detected in the building, intervention and evaluation stage as input for revising the design principles in this stage. To transform situated learning into results that can be applied to a broader class of problems, we consulted all the materials collected in previous stages, such as the transcripts and data from the 4 workshops, the findings of the pilot study, the opinions expressed in the 20 interviews, reports, and evaluation results. During this stage, we used data triangulation of the artifact’s prototypes (and the corresponding evaluation). We also compared our results with the findings of a literature review on digital intrapreneurship platforms and idea management research to cross-validate and verify the results. Based on this data consolidation, we identified that some important aspects were not suficiently considered in the design principles. For instance, these were not covered by existing design knowledge that we had extracted from literature to inform the initial design (see Section 2.1 and 2.2). Therefore, we concluded that another evolution of the design artifact was required to integrate the currently lacking aspects (represented by the unanticipated consequences) in the revised design. Thus, after consolidation and exploitation of all data, the second set of theoretically and practically grounded design principles (called revised design principles) emerged.

Subsequently, we systematically evaluated the revised design principles and the emerged platform, to confirm our design knowledge and ensure that the design principles address the challenges identified in the previous system. Here, we performed a naturalistic, ex post evaluation of the design based on a field study (real-world context) to validate our design knowledge in interrelated and comprehensive evaluation episodes [23]. In discussions, key decision makers had to provide feedback on the design principles’ function and form. Furthermore, we evaluated the results of the field study, which lasted 12 weeks, in the form of semi-structured interviews with end users. Employees in diferent roles and positions (e.g., innovation managers, IT experts, in-house consultants, information scientists, and software developers) in the in-house consulting department were interviewed to evaluate our design knowledge’s viability from their perspective. After having analyzed 22 interviews, we jointly posited that data saturation had occurred. Upon noticing homogeneity in replies, we rejected the necessity of additional interviews [68].

## 3.3.4. Formalization of learning

Lastly, in line with Gregor and Hevner [58], we formalized design knowledge in the form of validated design principles derived from theory and practice. More specifically, we formulated a set of 5 design principles (see Section 4.3, Table 6) as well as their corresponding design features for digital platforms fostering intrapreneurial behavior. The continuous reflection of the problem, the underlying theoretical foundation, and the evolving artifact were essential for identifying a contribution to the existing knowledge (contribution type level 1 according to Gregor and Hevner [58]).

## 4. Designing a digital intrapreneurship platform: the ADR project in detail

In this section, we describe the design process of the ADR project structured according to the 4 stages.

## 4.1. Problem formulation: results of evaluation

In our project, we applied ADR to a real-world problem and challenge for a large IT service provider in the financial industry, who is squandering valuable innovation potential. This organization had been sticking to the existing practice of submitting employee-driven ideas through a suggestion box, which fell short of expectations. For example, one manager described, “due to a lack of transparency the acceptance rate of the suggestion box and subsequently the numbers and quality of submitted ideas was quite low.” Our research addresses these issues by fully capitalizing on the organization’s innovation potential.

To obtain detailed insights into the organization’s issues and to set the boundary conditions of our research, we analyzed the collected 18 documents, as described in Section 3.3.1, to prepare the exploratory interview study and to triangulate the gathered data [69]. During preliminary meetings between the ADR project parties, the practitioners emphasized that they perceive designing a new IS-enabled system as appropriate to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior in the form of employee-driven ideas in the organization. Therefore, the ADR team conducted exploratory interviews to determine employees’ experience in developing ideas, and to collect clear-cut requirements for designing a new digital intrapreneurship platform. The interview questions were broadly structured by taking the preliminary findings of the document analysis into account and by considering the 5 components of STS.

The analysis of 12 interview transcripts and 18 internal documents resulted in the extraction of 5 challenges for the design of a new platform. These challenges were related either to the social or technical components of platform design and are as follows: (1) idea management as black box, (2) long throughput times, (3) exclusion of contributors’ peers, (4) low employee commitment to employee-driven innovation in general, and (5) dificulty to identify intrapreneurs in the case organization. After highlighting the 5 challenges and their underlying shortcomings in Table 3, we exemplify some of these challenges in more detail in the following paragraphs.

A particular challenge of the existing system was that the idea management process “is like a black box, owing to the lack of transparency in this process,” as stated by one employee. Especially the idea submission, selection, and evaluation processes through a suggestion box were neither visible nor comprehensible for the contributing employees. For example, contributors did not know in which stage of the evaluation process their idea was, or why their idea had been rejected. The innovation manager illustrated another problem, “repeated submissions of similar innovation ideas in the suggestion box [is] not rare.” Additionally, the document analysis showed that no standardized form or interface existed to describe complex innovation ideas in a comprehensible and structured way. Overall, 11 out of 12 interviewees mentioned aspects of this first challenge.

Long throughput times were also particularly challenging as brought up by 8 interviewed employees. “As you do not receive immediate feedback, and throughput times are rather slow, it takes a while until you know what happened to your idea,” described one interviewee. After contributors had submitted an idea, they had to wait for several months until they received positive or negative feedback toward their idea. One employee exemplified, “Honestly, I think these ideas are just fizzling out somewhere, I haven’t heard from them in ages.” Apart from resource constraints, ideas often got stuck during the evaluation process. The long throughput times were also caused by high idea documentation requirements, and the lack of bundled data collection as internal documents demonstrated.

Because of the long lead times from idea submission to idea im plementation, an inclusion of all employees was not possible and no coordination of innovation projects across diferent departments and organizational subsidiaries took place. Additionally, contributors nei ther got individual nor collective feedback from other employees, nor did they have the option to integrate their fellow coworkers in the idea enrichment and evaluation process. This was highlighted by 10 em ployees, who had wished for such a possibility. For instance, one employee stated, “Usually you don't get any feedback and no resonance at all. That's a bit of a pity. I'm sure there are a few colleagues who could contribute something helpful to my idea.”

Eight interviewees also mentioned another challenge of the existing work system, as they did not regard the development of innovation ideas as one of their responsibilities and core tasks. For instance, one employee said, “Oh, is there such a box? I didn't know that. I get the feeling that I should do my normal tasks first. As far as I know, the innovations have been outsourced.” As a consequence, most employees were not committed to employee-driven innovation, but instead regarded the generation of new ideas and innovations as the core duty of the IT service provider’s R&D department. Hence, the employee acceptance rate of the suggestion box and subsequently the numbers of submitted valuable ideas was quite low. Additionally, no incentives existed to stimulate employees’ participation in employee-driven innovation projects, exemplified by one quote, “why should I do this? No one will see or ap preciate it anyway.”

Last but not least, the “dificulty to identify intrapreneurs, who con tribute extraordinarily to [employee-driven] innovation” in the organiza tion, as well as intrapreneurship development and enhancement were significant challenges according to 4 managers. In the course of a new performance evaluation initiative, the management board was keen to identify intrapreneurs. “Since no statistics existed on the quantity or quality of ideas,” contributed by employees most active in employee driven idea generation, “no information on the most innovative employees could be extracted,” summarized an innovation manager.

The described challenges demonstrate the weaknesses of the existing system at the IT service provider. Overall the suggestion box was not suficient to handle the emerging ideas; thus, giving away valuable innovation potential of intrapreneurs. This situation calls for a new form to handle and facilitate intrapreneurial behavior by means of a digital intrapreneurship platform; therefore, conceptualizing a research opportunity.

Additionally, we were able to deduce from an examination of related literature [6,46] and STS theory [19–22] that the IT service provider lacked the dynamic force (social component) and structural functioning (technical component) necessary for an efective employeedriven management initiative across organizational locations. This backwardness made the existing system counterproductive to the aspired innovation generation in the organization.

Furthermore, empirical literature [19–22] has highlighted that all components of the STS model have to be in balance for designing successful work applications. However, we conclude from the issues and shortcomings that we analyzed and synthesized above that the existing system was characterized by strong imbalances according to Leavitt’s diamond [19]. For instance, the existing technology (i.e., suggestion box) fell short of enabling intrapreneurial behavior among employees, because it was a black box that lacked transparency. This led to an imbalance in the corresponding components (technology and actors). Thus, our research in the next stage was driven by the organization’s practical necessity to provide an eficient and efective system for the activation and preservation of intrapreneurial behavior in the form of employee-driven ideas.

## 4.2. Building, intervention, and evaluation

As Sein et al. [18] propose, an ADR project should not only be inspired by practice but also rooted in theory. Thus, we justified the identified preliminary design principles’ validity by studying the existing literature on STS theory [19–22], and by employing the design insights from prior research (see Appendix F, Fig. F2). The following sections summarize the key insights from existing research and practice, and serve as the baseline for the preliminary set of design principles (alpha cycle) and revised design principles (beta cycle).

## 4.2.1. Theoretical foundation

Rather than solely focusing on the submission of innovative ideas, this new platform’s design presents a more comprehensive approach by taking all STS components into account. Synthesizing existing literature on digital innovation and intrapreneurship platforms revealed specific design insights, which we structured according to the STS components (i.e., actors, structure, technology, task, and environment). Similarly, we categorized the results of the problem formulation stage (i.e., 5 challenges) based on the 5 components of STS theory (see Appendix F, Fig. F2). Therefore, the ADR team could match challenges derived in the interviews sessions with the design insights found in literature, because both data sources were structured in accordance with the 5 STS components, to derive design instructions and inform the design of adequate features for the digital platform (see Appendix F, Fig. F2).

Each derived design principle is created to solve one challenge, which was informed by design insights from literature (see Table 4). In regard to the structure component, the design of a transparent workflow structure (i.e., process of idea evaluation) was required. ## 4.2.2. Design principles

As the contextual conditions under which the ADR project was executed can play an instrumental role in designing the digital in trapreneurship platform, we considered the context of the IT service provider operating in the financial industry in the following design principles’ derivations.

## Design principle 1: Transparency

Design principle 1 (DP1) – Transparency postulates the visibility of all stages and stakeholders of the idea evaluation process, and addresses the lack of transparency as well as redundant and low quality of idea submissions in the previous work system of the case organization. This design principle builds on Mettler et al.’s [39] ADR project; one of their requirements calls for a clean and understandable presentation of relevant information and access to this information for all employees. Moreover, several papers in the literature analysis highlight the importance of transparent handling of idea management, and the distinct steps of idea submission, selection, and evaluation (i.e., [8,30]). This was also confirmed by the interviewees, “I think transparency is very important. As a contributor of ideas, I find it central to know what is the status of my idea, what is happening to it and where is it going. That's the bottom line." Thus, we formulated the first design principle³ as follows;

DP1: Provide transparent information on the innovation idea process and progress to aford employees to gain an overview over past ideas and workflow, given that organizations aspire to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior among their employees.

Therefore, we derived distinct platform features for DP1 – Transparency, such as the “idea workflow” that makes the diferent stages of the idea management process visible and transparent to everyone at the IT service provider (see Fig. 3; for detailed feature description see Appendix C).

## Design principle 2: Real-time capture and alert function

Design principle 2 (DP2) – Real-time capture and alert function relates to real-time capture and corresponding functions that help to reduce the long throughput time of ideas at the case organization. In sum, literature rarely discusses this topic. Only some papers describe that instant e-mails functioning as alerts (e.g., [8]) could be helpful to speed up the feedback loops through immediate information about newly submitted ideas. The exploratory interviews further demonstrated the importance of this principle. For instance, one employee explained, “If everything is collected in real time and I get an [alert] email, it won’t happen that I look into the portal only once a month and there are ten new comments, but already three weeks old. Because sooner or later no one will care then.” In addition, the principle also facilitates data collection, which can then be used to analyze information and reduce bottlenecks in the innovation process flow by notifying the responsible individuals at the IT service provider. Therefore, we address this in the second design principle:

DP2: Track the idea management process in real-time and provide alert functions to aford employees to react quickly to newly submitted innovation ideas, given that organizations aspire to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior among their employees.

For the realization of the second principle, we derived various design features. For instance, if a newly submitted idea matched employees’ expertise and interest, they were instantly informed by an alert e-mail inviting them to collaborate (for detailed feature description, see Appendix C). Overall, the second principle allows for real-time tracking of progress and reduced lead times from idea submission till implementation.

## Design principle 3: Community integration

Design principle 3 (DP3) – Community integration relates to the insight that facilitating community integration is important to improve ideas and increase the organization’s overall innovation success. According to Bansemir and Neyer [17], collaboration is an important part of an idea management system. In their in-depth interviews, they detect a value in multidisciplinarity of idea submission by involving multiple business units and employees across diferent departments. Further studies shared this view and outline the necessity of design for co-problematization [37] to enable the possibility to solve problems with the feedback of colleagues. Mettler et al. [39] outline the importance of integrative and open software design, so that the platform encourages far-reaching participation [27] among all employees in the organization. Highlighting the importance of community integration in the case organization one employee explained, “I think it is important to integrate everyone, because it not only promotes creativity, but also, of course, the exchange and the networking of the entire workforce. Also, I know that we have many colleagues who are quite creative, who have ideas, who also like to express their ideas, be it downstairs during a cofee break or in any other way and they would then use such a platform.” So far, employees in the case organization had no possibility to integrate peers in an eficient way, which means their ideas did not receive collective feedback. Consequently, we derive the third design principle:

DP3: Provide features to integrate the organizational community to aford employees to evaluate and refine submitted innovation ideas with the employee crowd, given that organizations aspire to facil itate intrapreneurial behavior among their employees.

Therefore, we incorporated distinct platform features, such as “idea commenting” or “idea voting” [7,38] to integrate the whole employee crowd into the platform (see Fig. 4; for detailed feature description see Appendix C). The necessity of these elements is explained by another interviewee, “Ultimately, the platform also collectively reflects how the employees rate the idea. And that's what keeps us motivated.” In total, the third principle facilitates community integration, and thereby, the in clusion of colleagues by accommodating employees’ interests to submit, comment, or rate an idea and to provide idea contributors with the collective feedback.

## Design principle 4: Infusion of innovation culture

Design principle 4 (DP4) – Infusion of innovation culture addresses the lack of awareness regarding the organization’s innovation orientation and deals with the lack of commitment toward intrapreneurial behavior among employees at the IT service provider. The analysis of existing literature revealed the importance of this fourth principle. Thus, in centivizing employees’ idea contribution is one way to influence in dividuals’ willingness to exchange their knowledge [71]. Moreover, Bansemir and Neyer [17] mention in their analysis of idea management systems, the absence of an integration mechanism of motivational aspects in system design as a major shortcoming. As a solution, Benbya and Leidner [7] recommend organizations to foster a culture of bottomup innovation using an appropriate mechanism, such as platform areas with innovation award announcements. Moreover, the literature also proposes other management actions such as innovation time or rewards to promote an innovation culture [27,72]. Therefore, we address this in the fourth design principle:

DP4: Provide platform configurations to infuse an innovation cul ture to aford managers to increase employees’ awareness of organizational innovation objectives, given that organizations aspire to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior among their employees.

We translated insights from literature into various distinct platform features representing this design principle. For instance, a “reward system” (see Fig. 5) or “employee of the month” election aim to incentivize and motivate employees at the IT service provider to innovate, and can therefore help to infuse an innovation culture. One employee confirms this with an example like this, “Rewards are pretty cool and of course an incentive to post ideas. It simply requires some sort of appreciation. In any case, it would be productive and would push the whole thing forward in my eyes.” In total, the fourth principle should lead to an infusion of innovation culture, an increase of the awareness, and a higher employee commitment toward employee-driven innovation.

## Design principle 5: Identification of informal roles

Design principle 5 (DP5) – Identification of informal roles addresses the dificulty in the case organization to identify key contributors to employee-driven innovation, thereby outlining the high importance of identifying individuals who are contributing the most to the organization’s innovation success as IT service provider in the financial industry. Ciriello et al.’s [28] DSR reveals the importance of certain key individuals in the innovation process. Likewise innovation management literature mentions key actors, such as intrapreneurs, who are characterized as individuals willing to take risks, activate their personal networks, and promote innovations through the various stages of the development process enthusiastically [4,73,74]. Moreover, the literature highlights the essential role and necessity of identifying such individuals in digital innovation processes, as they provide both their own capabilities as well as those of their social network [10,75]. Thus, we derive the fifth design principle:

DP5: Provide platform configurations to enable the identification of innovative individuals to aford managers to gain an overview of the collective innovation capability, given that organizations aspire to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior among their employees.

To introduce the fifth design principle, we implement diferent design features in the platform. For instance, the feature “innovation control board” is based on this design principle as it provides an overview of all employees’ idea contributions and displays all currently circulating innovation ideas on the platform. “I would use the platform to see which people participate and then recognize people who are strongly interested in joining the innovation community. One could certainly identify particularly innovative employees with this tool” reported one manager. Therefore, the fifth principle enables an overview of the key in dividuals, and fosters the organization’s collective innovation capability as an IT service provider in the financial industry.

On the basis of these design principles, we designed a prototype of the digital intrapreneurship platform. The ADR team decided to implement the platform within the corporate wiki to connect the platform with a tool of similar purpose and to reach a user base as large as possible. The corporate wiki has no entry barriers for employees’ participation, and is available for all employees to contribute.

## Working proposition

Building on the detected challenges of the existing system in the problem stage, we proposed that a digital intrapreneurship platform designed according to the derived design principles would foster employee-driven ideas. More specifically, such a platform aims to achieve higher eficiency and efectiveness than the existing system (i.e., suggestion box). This means all design principles – considered appropriate for the design of a platform – should facilitate intrapreneurial behavior in the form of more eficient and efective employee-driven idea gen eration.

## 4.2.3. Alpha and beta cycles

As outlined in Section 3.3.2, the platform was iteratively designed in 2 cycles (alpha and beta cycles) applying the IT-dominant BIE. In the alpha cycle, the ADR team iterated and evaluated early designs [18], whereby researchers and the practitioners of the IT service provider contributed distinctive viewpoints on design and the use of the digital intrapreneurship platform. As a result of repeated iterations within the ADR team, a new alpha prototype was designed. In the beta cycle, end users were additionally engaged in the design process. The digital in trapreneurship platform prototype was introduced to a broader organizational audience through an extensive intervention, and evaluated in everyday work for a 12-week period with workshops and a pilot study.

Evaluation of alpha and beta cycles

The 3 stakeholders at the IT service provider presented the distinct structural pillars of the phase: BIE. The project team took the role of building the artifact, the department was responsible for the interventions, and the remaining employees evaluated the new platform as end users. The intervention of introducing the beta prototype, and the subsequent formative evaluation exposed both anticipated and unanticipated consequences [18] of the design principles (see Table 5).

With respect to DP1 – Transparency, interviews with employees reflected that they appreciated the increased visibility in the idea management process, exemplified by this quote “The transparency is much better now and the platform permits an open, flexible and tolerant approach toward everything.” Furthermore, they stated that measures to increase transparency facilitated their “identification with the organizational objective” of increasing the organization’s innovativeness through employee-driven ideas. For instance, the innovation control board, which highlighted the superior performance of individuals in each business unit, motivated several employees to participate in idea submission projects. Furthermore, contributors highlighted the new feature “Idea workflow,” “Due to this feature, I knew in which stage my idea sojourned in the evaluation process, and who was responsible for the respective evaluation.”

However, the implementation of DP1 – Transparency also revealed some unanticipated consequences. Employees expected that they would need to provide additional information or be pressured to develop their idea in addition to their normal tasks. Therefore, the perception arose that innovative individuals might risk facing a heavier workload when their high potential becomes visible to the entire organization. One employee confirmed this, “I think some things aren't meant to be seen by everyone. I don't like to be made known via platforms, which then lead to a lot more work for me.” We traced these unanticipated consequences to the absence of a user control mechanism.

Interviews with employees demonstrated that they valued the platform’s ability to capture and present submitted innovation ideas in real time (DP2 – Real-time capture and alert function). Especially those employees, who wanted to react immediately, support, and collaborate on submitted ideas, appreciated this feature a lot. The system helped to track down and alert individuals overdue for their evaluation of an idea. “Depending on my evaluation workload, it also ofered support in form of deadline extensions,” outlined one employee.

In addition to these outcomes, DP2 – Real-time capture and alert function also elicited unexpected outcomes. Some employees feared privacy violation and employee monitoring because of real-time tracking of idea submission. Employees were particularly concerned about the risk of micromanagement, because of detailed information on each project status. One interviewee explained, “I've noticed right away that people are tracking what I did and when I did it. I would wish for a function that prevents that.” For example, managers could observe the exact status quo of a submitted idea and trace, who has worked on it and for how long. This unanticipated consequence could also be attributed to the absence of user control mechanisms.

The analysis of data collected from multiple interviews showed that employees appreciated the platform’s implementation of DP3 – Community integration. These allowed everyone to participate in developing innovation ideas based on their mutual interest. Community integration was facilitated by the platform’s high accessibility through the organization’s commonly used wiki. Moreover, one interviewee stated, “the availability of multiple features to provide feedback, and to initiate knowledge sharing, encouraged me to engage in ongoing conversations to shape others’ innovative ideas.”

Nevertheless, the implementation also revealed unanticipated con sequences. Idea bashing in terms of demotivating comments through other employees, and the alteration of ideas through colleagues lowered the motivation of actual and potential contributors. One manager illustrated this situation, “There were comments that could have been avoided. For this reason, somebody from my team talked to me. This is demotivating and should be stopped in future.” Thus, we uncovered the necessity of community integration with prereview options.

DP4 – Infusion of innovation culture increased awareness about the organizational innovation objectives through directed communication by means of personal messages as well as public notifications displayed on the announcement area of the platform. However, some unanticipated consequences arose as well. As a result of frequent announcement of rewards through the platform, inappropriate idea sub missions occurred. The innovation manager criticized, “That was a bit clumsy, since we were overwhelmed by so many ideas that were neither meaningful nor thoughtful. It was an unnecessary waste of time for both sides.” This was caused by the absence of integrated idea criteria and lack of time dedicated to employee-driven innovation. Moreover, one manager revealed another concern, “Some members of my team were particularly motivated by the new innovation culture, sometimes you had to slow them down a bit, which is why I think the participation should be discussed first. Otherwise some employees neglected their main tasks.”

Finally, interviews with managers illustrated their appreciation for DP5 – Identification of informal roles. Proactive search mechanisms and the possibility to analyze an individual’s capabilities enabled the management to “identify innovative individuals, who submitted more ideas than other employees,” stated one manager. Moreover, managers were able to gain an overview of the organization’s collective innovation capability. Because of the possibilities to capture and track all submitted ideas within the platform over 12 weeks, a positive, unanticipated consequence arose. “There were some employees who turned out to be true intrapreneurs, and I never thought that of them” confirmed one manager. For the first time, previously unrecognized intrapreneurs with top-level performances (i.e., individuals, who often develop and promote ideas) could be recognized and discovered.

## 4.3. Reflecting and learning: revised principle

On the basis of the evaluation of the alpha and beta cycles that uncovered both anticipated and unanticipated consequences for all design principles, we revised the design principles (see Table 6; changes are in italics).

With respect to DP1 – Transparency, a revision was necessary with respect to users’ control over their ideas. As competent employees of the IT service provider feared heavier workload, when their ideas were made public, the main control over the submitted idea should remain with the idea contributor. For this purpose, diferent disclosure settings were integrated in the platform. In alignment with existing research highlighting the importance of idea evaluation criteria for a transparent handling of the idea management and the distinct steps of idea submission, selection, and evaluation [8,28], clear selection criteria were also implemented (i.e., crowd evaluation, suficient support from colleagues, business potential, ease of implementation, and costs < value).

Furthermore, DP2 – Real-time capture and alert function was revised by giving users control over the system’s representation of the submitted idea. Reason for this revision was that employees in the case organization had expressed fear of privacy violation and monitoring in the interviews. Through the implementation of new user control features, the specific project status of the idea is now by default only visible to the submitting contributor. For each idea, only a status quo classification into 4 broader levels is observable for the management and all employees: submission, evaluation, refining, and implementation. Existing research on user control confirmed our viewpoint; employees whose knowledge and competencies were captured and stored in a platform should have control over their information (e.g., [18]).

Because of the rise of unanticipated consequences within DP3 – Community integration, this principle was revised by adding review features. These features are discussed frequently in existing literature and considered valuable (e.g., [76]). At first, minor changes were introduced, such as the review of all comments through platform administrators, and changes in the editing settings that only allowed the submitting employee to accept proposed changes to her idea. Idea bashing employees were cautioned by the administrators to stop their behavior. Furthermore, as the idea bashing demotivated some contributors, the platform incorporated an option to request only a certain user group to comment on the submitted idea. Allowing contributors to have “private idea discussions” (see Ap pendix C) created a safe space focused on the specific motives of all associated individuals. Additionally, the bottom-up idea generation strategy was complemented through a top-down approach. Literature on idea management platforms showed that a top-down strategy (e.g., [7,10]) can be used to solicit inactive employees and can therefore increase the number of submitted ideas. Correspondingly, a new feature “idea challenge” (see Fig. 6; for a detailed description see Appendix C) was introduced that allowed managers and employees of the IT service provider to define a problem, set a deadline, invite certain groups of employees to find solutions for actual problems, and focus employees’ eforts on specific business areas.

DP4 – Infusion of innovation culture was also revised by incorporating integrated management actions. The configuration of the platform was further adjusted by requesting contributors to review their idea according to “idea requirements” (see Appendix C) before submission to avoid inappropriate idea submissions. Idea requirements ensured that a problem or an idea was clearly framed around a specific set of dimensions (context, constraints, and goals). Existing literature shows that idea criteria are important to increase, for instance, the traceability of idea rejections (e.g., [8,28]). Additionally, getting employees to generate, post, and pitch ideas remained a challenge, and depended on fundamental changes in employees’ innovative behavior. To reinforce intrapreneurial behavior, the reward program was changed to recognize diverse categories of valuable ideas, and reach a more comprehensive group of employees at the IT service provider. This change was in line with research that claimed that rewards can increase the quantity of knowledge contributions [77], and that incentive systems can increase the innovation involvement of employees (e.g., [8,27]).

Another feature that was implemented based on the revised design principle was a project-related temporal component, so called “slack time.” This is defined as a dedicated time to generate novel and potentially useful ideas [9,72]. Mainly practitioner-oriented articles have considered and addressed these approaches of innovation time so far [9,72,78]. For example at Google, the introduction of slack time aimed to solve employees’ conflict between performing their formal tasks and participating in employee-driven innovation by creating freely available time resources. This component was aimed to increase the quantity and quality of submitted ideas, and to infuse an organizational innovation culture at the IT service provider in the financial industry. The platform was designed to also manage and monitor this new management policy of “slack time” for innovation (see Appendix B and C). Employees have to register in the platform and the direct superior has to approve employees’ use of slack time.

Finally, DP5 – Identification of informal roles was revised by for malizing informal roles in the platform. The platform has helped to identify those employees representing key contributors in terms of innovation. After analyzing the collected data, key contributors in terms of innovation were nominated. They were not only characterized by a higher idea submission rate but also commented more frequently on the platform. Additionally, key contributors selected promising creative ideas and spread them among other employees through the platform’s “idea sharing” feature (see Appendix C). Furthermore, these key contributors recruited other employees proactively from various departments for their projects and circumvented idea bashing by leaving positive comments for ideas that had faced harsh criticism. To benefit from this observation, a new formal role was created. The informal roles of key contributors became the formal role of intrapreneurs.

After the beta cycle, intrapreneurs were expected, in line with existing literature (e.g., [7]), to coach and mentor colleagues, to encourage participation in innovation ideas, and to be the first to discuss ideas and run idea challenges. Two intrapreneurs, evenly distributed across the organization, were trained on how to manage the idea pipeline and promote innovation practices in their work areas. Furthermore, intrapreneurs were integrated in the platform with a picture and their contributed ideas were publicly presented on the platform. This necessity for formal roles is especially highlighted in current literature claiming that the intrapreneurial activities of an employee positively impact organizational outcomes (e.g., [2,4,5]). ## 4.4. Final evaluation of revised design principles and platform

A final evaluation of the revised design principles and the resulting platform artifact was conducted to validate that the designed digital intrapreneurship platform solves the organization’s challenges of gen erating innovation ideas (i.e., design objective). The purpose of this evaluation was to provide evidence that the proposed design principles can be instantiated in the form of an artifact that addresses the outlined problems (i.e., low acceptance rate as well as a low number and quality of submitted ideas) and achieves the expected environmental utility, i.e., fostering employee-driven ideas.

To test the revised design principles and the instantiated artifact, we conducted the final evaluation of the digital intrapreneurship platform’s design in the following way. We implemented the prototype in the case organization and observed the efects in a field study. The field study sought to test the launched and implemented platform’s efect on intrapreneurial behavior among employees within the IT service provider in the financial industry. Hereby, we first evaluated the digital intrapreneurship platform with discussion rounds, where the case organization’s key decision maker assessed and evaluated the design. To further verify the value, utility, and efectiveness of the digital intrapreneurship platform and the 5 design principles, semi-structured interviews with 22 employees were conducted. On the basis of what we learned from the end user/employee interviews and their usage experience when testing the platform in a daily routine as well as continuous insights from usage data extracted from the platform’s log files, we evaluated the digital intrapreneurship platform and the 5 de sign principles (see Table 7) to verify our working proposition.

Within 2 weeks, the method expert of the ADR team and the innovation manager prepared the intensive discussion rounds among key decision makers (managers) as well as the interviews with employees to conclude the field study. In total, 22 people attended the field study; 15 regular employees, 2 in-house consultants, 2 innovation managers, 1 HR manager, 1 software developer and the department head. The interview sessions lasted between 60 and 90 min and comprised 2 parts: (1) gathering feedback on the design principles’ utility (i.e., purpose and relevance), their direct applicability and adequacy [53,79] as well as, (2) the platform’s effectiveness and value for the organization’s innovation capability. Therefore, we asked all participants to provide feedback on their platform use. To obtain structured feedback, we asked the participants to assess the design based on the revised design principles in general, the distinct design features in particular, and the platform in its entirety. By asking semi-structured questions, we refined the collected data, which resulted in valuable evaluation of the artifact in a real-world context. In a nutshell, all the participants agreed that the platform is a useful and supportive instrument for facilitating the development of employee-driven ideas, and that it is beneficial to enhance the intrapreneurial behavior in the case organization. Table 7 summarizes the overall feedback on digital intrapreneurship plat forms that we collected in the final evaluation based on discussion rounds, interviews, and log files in the field study.

The employees’ feedback converges toward a strong agreement on the design principles and the platform’s utility. Alongside this general agree ment and common appreciation, qualitative data indicate that all participating employees of the IT service provider in the financial industry appreciated the functionality of the distinct design principles and the underlying features. Subsequently, we discuss employees’ feedback on the distinct design principles in detail, by elaborating on a selection of ex amples.

From interviews and evaluation, we gained insights into the first design principle. DP1′ – Transparency with user control was perceived as well implemented and very useful, especially in a situation where an employee’s idea was rejected. One employee highlighted the possibility to comprehend the reasons for an idea rejection, “with the help of the idea criteria I can now at least thoroughly grasp why my idea was not accepted and improve it if applicable.” The majority of employees liked the possibility to have detailed information about every step ahead. Another employee stated, “You get the feeling that you're being heard here, opposed to a suggestion box where you don't know if it's really being processed. Now I no longer have a black box in which I never knew if my ideas were going straight from the suggestion box into the trash.” Therefore, the idea process is depicted as more visible and standardized opposed to the previous work system (i.e., suggestion box). Taken all interview insights together, the DP1′ – Transparency with user control facilitates the utility of the platform, and therefore, contributes much to the platform’s efectiveness.

The participants also acknowledged the value of DP2′ – Real-time capture and alert function with user control. This principle fulfilled its purpose very well according to the interviewed employees. When collaborating on a new submitted idea or submitting changes to an idea, the integrated features “keep the throughput time short and let you keep the ball rolling.” While another one stated: “It is important, because nowadays you lose interest in it at some point. It has to be closely timed and cannot have processes that last for months.” Besides, some employee recognized features, which support “not forgetting evaluations or ideas in the process execution” and allow deadline extensions. Overall, the platforms eficiency and efectiveness could be strengthened.

Employees perceived DP3′ – Community integration with prereview option as very valuable and well implemented, too. The integration of the whole community for providing feedback and for developing ideas was perceived as very useful by the majority of interviewees. Additionally, the possibility to connect with and get feedback from people across company locations was considered by all interviewees as a major advantage over the suggestion box. Furthermore, 1 person especially highlighted the successfully implemented prereview feature, “Finally, nobody can tamper with my ideas anymore. I had the idea and now I can also decide which changes will be made and which not.” In addition, 1 manager emphasized the value of 1 feature, “With the idea challenge we were able to use the power of the community even more purposefully to find solutions for important problems as quickly as possible. And then there is always someone from somewhere in the company who gives new food for thought.” Overall, the third principle supports the purpose and relevance of the platform; thus, harnessing the whole innovation power of the organization, and increasing the intrapreneurial behavior among the employees.

DP4′ – Infusion of innovation culture with integrated management actions was perceived as very useful, especially in the organization’s situation where employees faced uncertainties, whether an innovation action was desired by the management or not. Almost all interview partners confirmed that with the integrated features the innovation culture inside the organi zation was better communicated compared to the suggestion box. For instance, one employee stated, “Setting up a suggestion box outside the canteen didn't motivate me that much. I was unaware that management had fostered innovation so strongly. A platform is far more meaningful. Since Mr. xxx [board of management] regularly writes something about it, one has the impression that innovation is being honored.” As we observed, the availability of slack time enhances the number of submitted ideas and speeds up their development, exemplified by this quote, “well, if I am given two hours per week to increase my intrapreneurial behavior, then this shows how much it is valued. And then I will surely take the time to develop ideas.” Overall, with this fourth principle, a higher awareness as well as commitment to employee-driven innovation among employees was created and the intrapreneurial behavior among the employee increased; thus, facilitating the organization’s innovativeness.

The employees acknowledged the value and relevance of DP5′ –

Identification and formalization of informal roles for the platform. One man ager highlighted the benefit of “identifying the right employee for formal roles as intrapreneurs” for increasing employee-driven innovation. As stated by another manager, “the employee of the month-style pictures of these in trapreneurs, integrated in the platform, not only motivate the intrapreneurial person herself, but also have an efect on other employees.” This was confirmed by 1 employee, “This had already aroused such a feeling in me, I want to try this, maybe I can do that too.” A manager further stated, “you usually need someone, who is a role model, to push the whole thing a little bit. If nobody does that, then you don't feel like doing it yourself. But when you see that you are getting dragged along. And when you formalize the roles of particularly innovative individuals, the efect becomes even stronger.” Therefore, interviewees acknowledged that the formalization of the role of intrapreneurs can further increase the innovativeness of the organization. In sum, the fifth principle is particularly important, because it functions as a mechanism for the identi fication and formalization of intrapreneurs that has not existed before. Therefore, the principle and the whole platform can fulfill their purpose, and contribute directly with their efectiveness and value to the organization’s innovativeness.

Overall, the results of the evaluation episodes confirm the validity of the digital intrapreneurship platform’s proposed design and provide indication for the positive efects of the platform on employees’ intrapreneurial behavior (i.e., employee-driven innovation) at the IT service provider. Our study reveals that the traditional way to involve employees in the devel opment of innovations (i.e., suggestion box) is not suficient to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior in IT organizations in the financial sector. Instead, employees prefer, for instance, collaboration and transparency in the development of innovation ideas and for the exchange on a digital platform. On the basis of the employees’ positive evaluation of the platform, we conclude that imbalances in the previous system could be overcome in the newly designed digital intrapreneurship platform that considers all 5 com ponents of STS successfully.

## 5. Discussion

In the following, we provide our theoretical contribution, highlight the importance of influencing factors for platform design, and discuss managerial implications for the 5 derived design principles.

## 5.1. Theoretical contribution

From a theoretical perspective, we approach a significant real-world challenge, and suggest a theoretically grounded and evaluated design providing end users with a tool to develop and handle innovation ideas. Our findings are in line with the results reported in related research on digital platforms (cf. Section 2.1, Table 1). For example, in their case study Benbya and Leidner [7,10] find a positive efect of providing an internal crowdsourcing platform on employees’ innovation capability. Similar positive efects on users’ performance are found in innovation management research on idea management systems (e.g., [8,29,30]). In our study, an increase in intrapreneurial behavior among employees on the platform measured by more submitted ideas indicates the usefulness of supporting innovation idea generation through a platform.

While some findings are in line with existing literature (e.g., [17,27,30]), our study in the context of an IT service provider in the financial industry also provides some new insights into digital intrapreneurship platforms for employee-driven innovation, which have not been considered in prior literature. Whereas, the role of innovation actors for the success of organizations’ innovation projects has already been widely covered and highlighted (e.g., [73,82]), prior literature is relatively silent when it comes to the “grand challenge” of identifying such innovative individuals in organizations. In that respect, our research shows that digital platforms may serve as a tool to identify innovation actors, such as intrapreneurs based on quantifiable measures (cf. Section 4.2.3 and 5.2). Prior research (e.g., [27]) broadly describes the importance of an innovation culture, but approaches specifying concrete measures to achieve such a culture are scarce. Thus, our study provides a comprehensive approach by combining slack time with the introduction of a digital intrapreneurship platform, which leads to a softening of the conventional hierarchical structures and initiates change in the organizational culture.

Given the challenges that IT organizations in the financial service industry face when renewing their innovation practices through the use of emerging technologies, this study contributes to research on digital intrapreneurship in 2 further important ways. We describe and discuss these contributions in the following subsections and contrast them to existing literature.

## 5.1.1. Design knowledge for digital intrapreneurship platforms

First, based on insights gained from an ADR project with an IT service provider in the financial industry, we derive design principles that are applicable to the design of digital innovation platforms facilitating the intrapreneurial behavior of employees. We present these resulting design principles in the form of design knowledge that can be used for the context of digital intrapreneurship platforms fostering employee-driven ideas. Furthermore, these design principles constitute important design knowledge and extend earlier work, which has rarely covered digital platforms. To date, research on topics related to intrapreneurship platforms in the ADR domain (e.g., [38]) and innovation management (e.g., [10]) has focused on the development of artifacts in another specific context. Their evaluation, if available, ofered only few insights into the underlying platform design. We addressed this shortcoming in our research project, by systematically deriving 5 theoretically grounded and empirically evaluated design principles, thereby extending the existing body of digital platform research (e.g., [11]) as well as intrapreneurship and innovation management in the IS domain. We instantiate the func tionalities described in the 5 design principles in a platform artifact.

Our findings confirm and suggest that the design of an intrapreneurship platform should 1) consider the transparency paradigm with user control elements; 2) consist of real-time capture possibilities constrained by user control; 3) allow community integration with prereview options; 4) involve the infusion of innovative culture with integrated management actions; and 5) allow identification and formalization of informal roles in the platform, as filtered out in our field study and complemented by existent literature (cf. Section 2).

The evaluation of a digital platform in an ADR study answers the call of de Reuver et al. [11]. Accordingly, Table 6 in Section 4.3 summarizes our findings in the form of design knowledge with underlying design principles ([58,83,84]). With this design knowledge, we contribute to design research by providing a better understanding of the design practices. For instance, within the ADR design cycles, we provide answers on the main concern of when and how to approach platform issues, and how design choices in a design cycle influence the platform’s development (cf. [85,11]), as elaborated in the following section.

## 5.1.2. Nonusers as social design factors in action design research

Observations in the BIE cycles resulted in an enhanced understanding of how the IT artifact and significant social roles intertwine and afect each other. More specifically, we found evidence that nonusers should be considered in the diferent ADR design cycles and processes, as they represent important stakeholders of the organizational environment getting afected by the design of socio-technical artifacts.

So far, only stakeholders directly involved in the use and development of the artifact had been included in the ADR method [18]. On the basis of a recent review of prior literature (e.g., [7]), we had assumed the importance of including stakeholders who do not actively use and develop the artifact, but are directly afected by the consequences of others’ use of the artifact. Therefore, we included a management representative in the ADR team who not actively used and developed the artifact. In the ADR process, we verified the importance of including nonusers. For instance, after the implementation of the intrapreneurship platform, the management representative expressed her concerns that employees work extensively on innovation ideas within the intrapreneurship platform, but spend less time on their formal tasks. These concerns of a nonuser led to the provision of slack time to provide employees with a specific and restricted timeframe to work on innovative ideas. In the platform design, slack time was incorporated through a formal registration and approval process.

Furthermore, the results exemplified that the created artifact can build and redefine social structures and relationships among individuals. More specifically, the implementation of the digital intrapreneurship platform led to a softening of the conventional hierarchical structures, and initiated change in the organizational culture. By using the platform, employees were interacting directly without considering each other’s hierarchical levels, rather than using the formal lines of communication. Thereby, some managers who were not using the platform felt circumvented, as their team made decisions on the platform without them. In this way, the organization’s formal processes were disrupted, communication patterns were changed, and stakeholders not using the platform were afected by the implemented artifact.

Overall. the experienced impact of the implemented artifact differs for users and nonusers, and future ADR research should consider the efects on other stakeholders not using the artifact in the design process. Once an artifact is implemented within an environment (e.g., an organization) with an existing social structure, the artifact is likely to cause social efects in this environment [86,87]. Hence, implementations may also yield unexpected consequences, which are frequently neglected in IS research dealing with the design of technological solutions to solve organizational challenges [88]. Thereby, artifacts in ADR projects should be purposefully designed for explicit social contexts, and should be evaluated by taking the possible ef fects on all social structures into account (cf. [87]). Accordingly, we see the inclusion of stakeholders (nonusers) directly afected by the consequences of others’ artifact use as an essential element in the ADR process, in parti cular in later stages like the beta cycle (indicated with a star in Fig. 8).

## 5.2. Managerial implications of ADR principles for digital intrapreneurship platforms

From a practical perspective, our research contributes by proposing a design for digital intrapreneurship platforms to support and facilitate employees’ intrapreneurial behavior in organizations. The results from our field study confirm that such a digital intrapreneurship platform has a positive efect on employees’ development of innovation ideas. By formulating general design principles and specific design features, the proposed design can serve as a blueprint that can be adapted to fit several contexts in different organizations. Practitioners can build their own instantiations of a digital intrapreneurship platform to increase their employees’ intrapreneurial behavior, and enable them to develop and generate more and better employee-driven innovation ideas for their organization.

Additionally, the qualitative evaluation of the artifact reveals interesting insights and entails managerial implications for all 5 design principles, which we will outline in more detail in the following.

1) DP1′ – Transparency with user control is the first design principle we recommend management should consider when implementing a digital intrapreneurship platform. Setting up a comprehensible and transparent idea management process for employees is critical to sustain their partici pation and the platform’s acceptance. Our study results reveal that the traditional ways of involving employees in the development of innovations, such as setting up a physical suggestion box without ideas being visible to everyone, is not suficient to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior in organi zations. Instead, employees prefer transparency in the development of in novation ideas and for the exchange on a digital platform. In particular, disclosing the terms of participation, goals, the workflow, and all phases of the idea process (i.e., the idea submission, selection, and evaluation) helps to avoid frustration among employees when their ideas get rejected. Thus, management could create the perception of fairness among employees by adhering to the transparency principle to increase employees’ participation on the platform, and to accelerate innovative behavior in the workplace. However, while the idea management process should be visible to the entire workforce, management should always provide employees with the option of controlling their own intellectual property by relying on integrated dis closure settings.

2) DP2′ – Real-time capture and alert function with user control is an im portant principle to enable employees to react quickly to innovation ideas. By sending alert emails employees’ attention could be directed toward newly submitted ideas and upcoming idea evaluations. Additionally, this function enables the management to identify the quantity and quality of submitted ideas and comments, to discover bottlenecks, and to maintain short throughput times. Moreover, as managers receive a real-time over view, they can always access the data, and if necessary, ideas could be merged together before they are pursued further. Nevertheless, our ob servations also exemplified that it is important to include user control mechanisms, to prevent employees from feeling monitored by the man agement because of real-time tracking possibilities.

3) DP3′ – Community integration with prereview options is the third design principle we propose management should follow to shape the features of the platform. To allow for a community-based innovation efort, configurational elements of the platform also need to be applied to the organi zation as a whole. While an organization might be guided by a hierarchical structure in day-to-day business, such a structural configuration hinders the creative process of idea generation and implementation in the community. Therefore, the hierarchical position of individuals should not play a role in the collaborative generation and implementation of creative ideas. Moreover, only if management allows employees to select team members beyond departmental boundaries on their own, employees with specific expertise will be able to contribute in community-integrated innovation projects. Additionally, management needs to take individual issues and fears into account, and counteract them by introducing a prereview feature controlled by the idea contributor (cf. Section 4.4).

Moreover, employees need to be ofered the opportunity to not only submit their ideas to the platform, but also to develop and implement their personal ideas. Even if some employees might lack the skills and knowledge to successfully implement innovative ideas, other innovative employees might become more motivated if they know that they have the option to implement their idea. Idea challenges that target a specific crowd ofer another way for management to implement a community-integrated ap proach to innovation. This strategy ofers management the option to steer idea submission and counterbalance fluctuations in the number of sub mitted ideas. Moreover, groups that were less involved in the intrapreneurship platform in the past can be targeted through specific idea challenges together with tailored incentives (cf. Section 4.4).

4) DP4′ – Infusion of innovation culture with integrated management actions does not simply arise after introducing an intrapreneurship platform in an organization. Instead, management actions need to nurture an innovation culture that values diversity and experimentation across all department and levels of the organization. Accordingly, management should articulate a vision and purpose for innovation. While the introduction of the digital intrapreneurship platform constituted an important factor for promoting intrapreneurial behavior, in our study only the introduction of “slack time” leveraged the full potential of employees’ innovativeness by increasing the number and quality of submitted employee-driven ideas. More specifically, our research demonstrates that employees should always have the opportunity to take advantage of slack time. Capacity restrictions in day-to-day business should not serve as a managerial excuse to decline an employee’s slack time request. Therefore, the organization has to allocate financing in the form of innovation time that may be used by employees to foster intrapreneurship. In a nutshell, management needs to account for both technical as well as social components when introducing and infusing an innovation culture.

5) DP5′ – Identification and formalization of informal roles in the platform are facilitated when managers acknowledge the importance of these roles. Employees’ eforts to promote innovative ideas should not be sufocated in organizational compliance. Instead, employees should receive support and reassurance that they will not be sanctioned for crossing formal boundaries. Our observations exemplified that in the process of pursuing an idea, one employee overstepped organizational boundaries and neglected given compliance guidelines. Consequently, she was directly sanctioned. Another employee was denied the choice of employees that she deemed most qualified for collaboration. Both incidents caused the involved employees to be demotivated and refrain from actively participating in future innovation projects. Accordingly, intrapreneurs need to receive as much freedom as possible to discover problems in the organization and explore novel ways for solving them.

Furthermore, building on the evaluation results, our exemplified platform acts as a tool to identify intrapreneurs who have the capabilities and potential to develop and promote innovations. As organizations face a grand challenge in identifying such individuals (e.g., [89]), this tool represents an appropriate solution to simplify and make identification using quantifiable factors (e.g., quantity and quality of submitted ideas and comments on platform) possible. Additionally, we observed that the existence of intrapreneurs in formalized roles was a key to the realization of successful ideas and their subsequent implementation. Thus, organizations that aim to enhance their long-term innovativeness should identify potential intrapreneurs through the platform, formalize their role, and give them the required freedom, space, and job autonomy to unfold and further develop their actual potential.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

The ADR project in our work faces some potential limitations, which we outline in the following. In addition, we propose several opportunities for future research on the digital intrapreneurship platform’s proposed design concept.

Although ADR is based on the premise that artifacts are formed by the organizational context during development and use [18], we need to acknowledge that the design knowledge was created and evaluated in cooperation with just one organization and its organizational environment. Consequently, the involved practitioners’ experience and knowledge are limited to this specific context and organization. Addressing other types of organizations beyond an IT service provider in the financial industry, could result in diferent outcomes. Additionally, the evaluation was conducted in an organization where employees are familiar with the implementation and use of new technologies, which could have afected the results. Though we integrated theoretical insights by studying existing literature from the research areas of innovation, IS, and design science, we cannot assure that the results are exhaustive, or completely independent of the specific research setting. As the contextual conditions under which the ADR project was executed played an instrumental role, we invite researchers and practitioners to provide further design insights for digital intrapreneurship platforms in other organizational contexts, and to apply, instantiate, evaluate, and extend the proposed design insights for digital intrapreneurship platforms; to advance the presented design knowledge to a nascent design theory for the whole class of digital intrapreneurship platforms in the future.

Second, we conducted an interview-based evaluation and additionally collected objective log file data by measuring employees’ platform usage and subsequent idea development. Interviews might be subject to social desirability [90], and employees might tend to report a higher usage of the platform or its efectiveness. To avoid such a bias, we framed the interview as a research study, and explicitly stated that the data would not be distributed to the case organization. Thereby, we intended to provide employees with a safe environment to report their actual perception of the platform and its efects. In addition, the data were gathered from employees who used a fully functional intrapreneurship platform in their daily work, in the form of a field study. Because of the relevance and evaluation of real-world problems, we can ensure a high level of external validity [23]. As we derived and tested our work proposition with a qualitative data analysis, we invite researchers to conduct quantitative evaluations to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

Third, we have argued that prior research is limited by not considering nonusers as a crucial design parameter. Accordingly, stakeholders (e.g., department head) not using the implemented artifact have to be included in the design process for intrapreneurship platforms as well. Our study calls for future research, which focuses on developing design methodologies that manage the trade-ofs between stakeholders who use and who do not use the artifact. For instance, future research could address the tensions and multifaceted relationships between stakeholders and their implications for design process dynamics.

Fourth, employees’ acceptance and the use of the intrapreneurship platform might vary because of implemented platform features, such as gamification mechanisms. As we uncovered that the few implemented features (e.g., “reward system”, “employee of the month” election) in centivized some employees extensively to innovate, and therefore, helped to infuse an innovation culture (cf. Section 4.2.2), future research could add further gamification functionalities (e.g., ratings, badges, leaderboards, or points) to our design and evaluate how these features enhance employees commitment and motivation to innovate on platforms.

## 6. Conclusion

This research was motivated by the case of an IT service provider in the financial industry squandering valuable innovation potential of employees, which is in need of a new IS-enabled system to facilitate intrapreneurial behavior (i.e., employee-driven ideas). As recent research [11] identified a research gap on design knowledge for digital platforms, this paper pursues the objective of deriving theoretically grounded design principles for digital intrapreneurship platforms and evaluating their instantiation as an IT artifact (i.e., final design) in a field study (real-world context), based on a naturalistic, ex post evaluation [23].

To enable informed decision-making on digital platforms’ design, our work contributes specific prescriptions for designing a viable digital

The analysis of the project derives design knowledge in the form of design principles for digital intrapreneurship platforms, by taking justificatory knowledge from prior platform research and practitioners’ assessment into account. Moreover, we outline the role of stakeholders afected by the design and use of the artifact. Additionally, important theoretical and managerial implications are provided for implementing a digital intrapreneurship platform successfully in the organization.

Overall, our design is aimed at organizations still struggling to reap the full innovation and intrapreneurial potential. With a particular focus on digital solutions, our research embraces digital platforms for leveraging IS-enabled innovation activities and processes for intrapreneurial behavior among employees. It, thereby, refers to challenges faced by many organizations, which need to calibrate and configure their established systems to digitally transform the very nature of how they and their employees innovate.

## Appendix B. Artifact description - the Brainstation platform

As an expository instantiation, we present how we use the design principles for the objective of design knowledge representation [58,83] to the case organization’s “Brainstation.” Although we generated several versions of our prototype during our ADR study, we only report about the final digital intrapreneurship platform version after completion of the initiative.

This new final digital intrapreneurship platform builds on the concept that ideas leading to innovation come from intrapreneurial employees, whose formal job description does not necessarily involve the development of innovation (in contrast to employees in the R&D department). The new platform ofers intrapreneurs the freedom to work on innovation ideas (e.g., the development of organization-specific commuting-sharing app)

Fig. B1 displays a screenshot of the final IT artifact, the so-called “Brainstation.”

To make submission to the platform possible, an innovative idea needs to fulfill specific requirements. The idea must be submitted with a standardized sketch including details of the suggestion. Contributors are required to describe their idea by evaluating a number of criteria, such as the description of the idea, its required monetary and timely resources, as well as its potential advantages for the organization. Overall, the contributor needs to undergo 2 approval processes and follow predefined requirements when working on the innovation project.

Depending on the outcome of the approval process executed by a (top) manager, the idea is then publicly released onto the digital in trapreneurship platform. In addition to this idea approval process, the contributor can apply for an exemption from daily work time for up to 2 h pe week (5% of weekly work time) to create innovations through the digital intrapreneurship platform. After the approval through the direct supervisor, the contributor can create and implement the idea either alone or communal as part of a team. A prerequisite of participation in the process is the complete and continuous documentation of all activities and steps on the digital intrapreneurship platform, which is freely accessible to all em ployees. The digital platform accompanies the contributor and her team during the entire process.

While the basic concept of the digital intrapreneurship platform, as described above, persisted over time, some features, and the related process were adjusted and added during the respective design cycles. We provide additional final design features of the instantiated IT artifact in Appendix C.

## Appendix C. Features of the digital intrapreneurship platform

The digital intrapreneurship platform had to cover 2 diferent aspects of employee-driven innovation. On the one hand, features were required to ofer intrapreneurial employees a way to submit all types of ideas. On the other hand, management or employees also sought means to steer the idea generation in certain directions. Therefore, 2 diferent idea submission features were instantiated. First, the feature “innovation idea creation” allow employees to submit all types of ideas by providing a description of the idea and its potential purpose on the standardized sketch mentioned above. The organization encouraged intrapreneurial employees to submit ideas regularly to address issues in their everyday work (see Fig. C1)

Second, the “idea challenge” feature enables idea solicitation and ofers the management (top-down) as well as employees (bottom-up) through private idea discussions the opportunity to collect employees’ innovative ideas for a certain type of issue in the organization. The challenger specifies the problem or opportunity that the challenge aims to solve, sets a timeframe, and invites other employees who the challenger deems suitable for the challenge (see Fig. C1).

The feature “idea criteria/requirements” consists of checkboxes, which have to be filled out and completed by a management executive. An innovative idea needs to fulfill 4 main requirements: It has to be supported by at least 10 other employees, the idea has to be useful for the organization, the idea implementation has to be feasible, and the value generated by an implementation of the idea has to exceed its cost.

The “idea workflow” feature makes the diferent stages of the idea management process visible and transparent to everyone (see Fig. 3 in section 4.2.2 and Fig. C2). There are 5 main stages an innovation idea can go through. First the idea sketch is submitted, second the framework conditions are examined, third a feasibility analysis is carried out, fourth the implementation decision will be made, and fifth the idea is implemented. In addition to these 5 stages, the idea contributor might observe 3 further stages upon accessing her profile. First, new or additional information might be required within the submission process of an idea. Second, the implementation might be moved to a later date and third, the suggested idea migh be rejected. With this new feature employees know the status quo of their idea within the evaluation process, and the person responsible for the evaluation of their submitted idea.

Additionally, 3 features enable idea graduation through community involvement. First, the “idea voting” feature allows employees to express their approval by means of a “thumbs up.” Idea voting, therefore, provides an indication about the support level for an idea among employees. Second, “idea commenting” grants all employees the opportunity to contribute to and improve submitted ideas by leaving comments. Taken together, voting and commenting on the platform allows for multifaceted evaluation and enrichment of innovative ideas. Third. the “idea sharing” feature offers the opportunity to share a submitted idea with selected employees, to receive feedback for further development. By actively soliciting other organi zational members’ support and feedback through voting and commenting, an idea contributor could get her idea noticed, shorten the evaluation cycle, discover new insights to strengthen the idea and identify new problems to solve. Combined, all 3 features, therefore, accelerate the process of getting feedback.

To facilitate community involvement and collaboration on innovative ideas, 2 additional features were implemented. The feature “private idea discussion” ofers a safe space for employees to discuss ideas with other invited employees. Second, the feature “alert e-mails” invites employees to collaborate on innovative ideas by sending an alert e-mail whenever a new idea, which matches the employees’ expertise and interest, has been submitted onto the platform. This feature was also incorporated to ensure short lead times in the evaluation process. Thus, individuals overdue with their evaluation are alerted when missing the deadline to evaluate an innovation idea.

Through the iteration cycles, it became clear that a high orientation toward innovations is favored in the organization, and high submission rates were only possible by providing employees with enough time to develop innovative ideas. Correspondingly, the feature “slack time” was introduced. This is a dedicated time slot each week for emplovees to generate novel and potentially useful ideas. The platform was used to manage and monitor slack time, as employees had to register and have the time slots approved by their supervisor through the platform

The “innovation control board” feature is a superordinate feature that provides an overview of all employees’ contributions to the organization’s employee-driven innovation initiative and displays all currently circulating innovation ideas on the platform. Additionally, the control board also presents statistics on the overall number of ideas generated, the duration of the evaluation process and the estimated value and costs.

The feature “reward system” provides an overview of possible rewards for accepted and successfully implemented ideas (see Fig. 5 in section 4.2.2). The system is aimed at further incentivizing and motivating employees to develop innovation. Initially, the platform scarcely ofered incentives for successful innovation ideas. After a rather modest participation rate in the beginning, a revised reward system based on the findings of interviews with employees was being implemented.

The feature “announcement area” is a space in the platform for management announcements regarding the organization’s mission as well as innovation vision and culture. The announcement area also displays weekly status updates from the management promoting innovation. Additionally, quotations of famous intrapreneurs are presented in this space to motivate employees

In general, the platform allows users to control their “disclosure setting” to mirror their personal privacy requirements. For instance, while some employees might choose to disclose their personal statistics, such as the number of submitted ideas, to all platform users, others might not share them at all or just provide a distinct user group in the platform with this information. Accordingly, employees can control the settings for all personal information shared on the platform with this feature.

Furthermore, some additional functionalities are provided by the platform. The feature “observation list” in the platform enables employees to create a list of ideas that they want to follow and contribute to. This feature provides full transparency with respect to changes made by other employees. Furthermore, each employee has a personal profile, comparable to a social media profile (see Fig. C3), which includes a picture and short information summary (e.g., department, ideas submitted, etc.). Employees can write each other personal messages over the platform. Furthermore, help features, such as a handbook (called “How-to”) and Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) provide general information about the Brainstation.

All these features are not entirely new features for platforms in general; however, they have not been used in the domain of digital intrapreneurship yet.
