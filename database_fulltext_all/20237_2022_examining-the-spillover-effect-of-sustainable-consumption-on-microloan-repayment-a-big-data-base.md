# Examining the spillover efect of sustainable consumption on microloan repayment: A big data-based research

Yuanqiang Ye<sup>a,1</sup>, Xianghua Lu<sup>a,</sup>\*<sup>,1</sup>, Tian Lu<sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Fudan University, Shanghai, China <sup>b</sup> Carnegie Mellon University, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Sustainable consumption Green consumer Social value Credit assessment Microloan

## A B S T R A C T

Social value-oriented consumers perform more sustainable consumption than conventional consumers do because consumers’ choices reflect their latent social values on environmental protection. However, whether sustainable consumption prompts more social value-oriented behaviors outside the consumption domain remains uncertain. The increased availability of consumer-level big data presents an opportunity to investigate consumers' cross-domain behavior subsequent to sustainable consumption, which broadens the comprehension of sustainable consumption by going beyond the boundary of consumption behavior. Supported by a joint dataset comprising information on both consumers’ consumption behavior and their microloan repayment behavior, this study examines the efects of sustainable consumption on consumers’ subsequent debt default behavior to empirically test the cross-domain spillover efects of sustainable consumption behavior. The results suggest that the default probability of green consumers overall was 4.34 % lower than that of nongreen consumers, even though this positive efect on repayment disappears when sustainable consumption is for health reasons. The findings contribute to research on sustainable consumption by providing empirical evidence indicating that sustainable consumption has positive spillover efects in other domains. The results also provide an alternative perspective for identifying high-quality borrowers for microloan platforms.

## 1. Introduction

Consumers are becoming more socially conscious in their purchases [1,2] by purchasing products that, for example, cause less environmental harm, buying organic produce, or looking for products with minimal packaging [3]. This is often referred to as sustainable con sumption, green consumption, or environmentally responsible consumption [4]; that is, the tendency of consumers to express the value of environmental protection through the goods and services they purchase [5,6]. More than half of American consumers reportedly say they are willing to pay extra to use environmentally friendly (green) products.<sup>2</sup> This number is even higher for younger generations: According to a Nielsen global online study, millennials represent the age group most inclined to pay extra for sustainable oferings, with almost three of four willing to do this.<sup>3</sup>

Because sustainable consumption typically costs more money and time than unsustainable consumption does, but ofers more benefits to the entire community than to the individual per se [7–9], sustainable consumption behavior is considered more social value-oriented than conventional consumption is [10,2]. In this regard, sustainable con sumption is a manifestation of consumers’ high social and moral standards [2]. Considerable research has explicitly examined the relationship between consumers’ latent social or moral values and sustainable consumption. For instance, Nguyen et al. [11] reveal that consumers with greater adherence to collectivism and with longer-term orientations tend to engage in green purchase behavior. Jansson et al. [12] indicate that values, beliefs, norms, and habit strength determine individuals’ willingness to adopt eco-innovations. Pinto et al. [13] recognize that responsible consumers generally confer more importance to personal values, such as conformity and personal virtues. These studies have confirmed that better people (people who are more ethical and more social value-oriented in general) are more likely to be green consumers. However, the reverse cannot be assumed intuitively; in other words, whether green consumers are also more likely to be better people and hold similarly high social values in subsequent behaviors remain questionable. To the best of our knowledge, the empirical evidence on this reverse relationship is limited—compared with the wealth of research regarding how sustainable consumption values afect another sustainable consumption—and merits additional research, parti cularly regarding how sustainable consumption afects behaviors outside consumption.

In the literature on sustainable consumption, Truelove et al. [14] and Nilsson et al. [15] frame the type of research question as the spillover efects of proenvironmental behavior (PEB), which investigates whether engaging in one sustainable consumption behavior afects the probability of engagement in another prosocial behavior. The spillover efect is critical in proenvironment literature because it predicts that the policy interventions targeting one environmental behavior have the capacity to promote or prevent future prosocial behaviors. Most policies and interventions are presumably based on positive spillover efects. For instance, green lending ofers low-interest loans or credit cards to green manufacturing firms or individuals and expects a higher repayment rate from them than from other firms or individuals [16]. However, there is evidence suggesting that the respective spillover efects comprise a variety of negative spillover efects (i.e., less subsequent prosocial behavior) caused by moral licensing efects or rebound efects [2,17]. These findings of negative spillover efects necessitate additional empirical evidence to test the relationship between sustainable consumption and subsequent prosocial behaviors among consumers.

The increased availability of consumer-level big data presents a new opportunity to investigate this relationship with real-world data, and might ofer more factual evidence than other experiments or surveybased studies. In this study, supported by a joint dataset comprising information on both consumers’ consumption behavior and their microloan repayment behavior, we examine the efects of sustainable consumption on consumers’ subsequent debt default behavior to empirically test the spillover efects of sustainable consumption behavior. Our preliminary analysis demonstrates that green consumers (i.e., consumers who have purchased green products more than three times within 6 months) have a 4.34 % lower probability of defaulting on microloans than do nongreen consumers, which support the idea that “green consumers are more likely to be better people.” However, further analysis on the altruistic motivations of sustainable consumption suggests that when sustainable consumption is for health purposes, the positive spillover efects in relation to microloans disappear.

As one of the first studies empirically examining the relationship between consumers’ sustainable consumption and social credibility for microloan repayments, these findings contribute to research in various ways.

This study makes significant contributions: (1) Unlike other studies that prove “better” people are more likely to be green consumers, this paper explores whether green consumers exhibit more social value-oriented behaviors beyond the consumption domain; findings extend the research on social value-oriented sustainable consumption, and suggest that societies should incentivize more altruistic sustainable consumption to leverage its positive spillover efects on encouraging more social-oriented behavior. (2) Taking advantage of the availability of consumer-level big data, this study measures sustainable consumption behavior and its impacts on the subsequent behavior more objectively than do survey-based studies. This contributes to the traditional sustainable consumption literature by providing more factual evidence to the debate on spillover efects of sustainable consumption. (3) A large body of studies has examined the spillover efects of one sustainable consumption behavior on a second sustainable consumption behavior [18–20], but the cross- domain spillover efects studies are less documented. This paper addresses the gap by providing an examination of empirical cross-domain spillover efects for sustainable consumption. (4) This paper provides an alternative perspective to manage risk assessment for the microloan industry, which may guide future practices for selecting high-quality borrowers.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the literature on sustainable consumption and green consumers. Section 3 provides hypotheses development. Section 4 presents the research context and variable definitions. Section 5 provides empirical analysis that includes both model-free analysis and a regression model with results, followed by several robustness check analyses in Section 6. Finally, Section 7 ofers a summary of the contributions and limitations of this study.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Sustainable consumption

Sustainable consumption, also called green or responsible consumption, is defined as a consumption behavior intending to meet the needs of the current generation and benefit the environment without jeopardizing the ability of future generation to satisfy their needs [22]. The concept has existed for decades and encompasses a broad range of consumption activities focusing on protecting and preserving the natural environment [23]. Examples include purchasing products that cause less environmental harm, buying locally produced foods, and selecting products using minimal packaging [3]. Although the definition of sustainable consumption is easy to understand, the measurement of consumers’ sustainable consumption behavior is not straightforward. Four methods in research literature that attempt to explicitly measure consumers’ sustainable consumption are presented in Table 1. Among these four methods, real-world data could most objectively capture sustainable consumption behavior if green products could be clearly defined because the social desirability bias is excluded to a large extent.

## 2.2. Research on the relationship between green consumers and their social value

The remarkable growth of sustainable consumption has inspired numerous studies to investigate which individuals are more likely to purchase responsibly (we define this type of consumer as green consumer<sup>4</sup> hereafter). Some research posits that the sociodemographic characteristics of green consumers diferentiate them from other con sumers [31], typically age [32,13], gender [33,11], education [13,34], income [32,34], marital status [35,36], political afliction [37], and so on. But consumers’ psychological proneness, particularly in relation to the environment, is most closely associated with sustainable consumption behavior [3,4]. Three types of psychological variables are widely discussed in sustainable consumption literature: (1) Environ mental consciousness, defined as the individual’s awareness of en vironmental problems and their willingness to be part of the problem solution [38,39]. (2) Perceived consumer efectiveness means faith in the positive contribution of specifically sustainable consumption behavior [40]. Straughan and Roberts [41] observed that perceived con sumer efectiveness explains sustainable consumption behavior well. (3) Social value is a standard for attitudes and behaviors, which means a hierarchical relationship from value to product-specific attitudes to purchase intention to purchase behavior [42]. Among these three psychological variables, consumers’ social values or social responsibility is the most studied and is also the focus of this study.

Researchers define social values in sustainable consumption as “sustainable consumption values,” the tendency of consumers to express ecological values through the goods and services they purchase [43]. These studies acknowledge that purchasing choices express not only price and quality preference but also social norms, values, and responsibilities. For example, Follows and Jobber [42] described a positive relationship between self-transcendence values, conservation values, self-enhancement values, and environmental purchasing behavior. Chan [44] recognized the influence of subjects’ man-nature orientation and collectivism on their attitudes toward green purchase. Samarasinghe [45] highlighted the strong correlation between cultural values and environmental attitudes, and both collectivism and longterm orientation are efective predictors of environmental attitude formation.

The extensive research on sustainable consumption values has confirmed that social-oriented values and characteristics increase responsible sustainable consumption. In this regard, better people with more social values are more likely to be green consumers. However, how sustainable consumption reflects consumers’ social responsibilities and afects their behaviors beyond consumption requires further understanding [2]. In practice, some organizations intuitively assume that consumers’ latent social values influence both sustainable consumption and other responsible behaviors outside consumption; thus green consumers are expected to maintain a similarly high sense of social responsibility outside the consumption domain. Some policies and interventions are already based on this belief. For instance, the Zhima Credit score of Alibaba.com highlights the relationship of sustainable consumption with credibility. Sustainable consumption at Alibaba.com qualifies people for higher consumer credit scores. Moreover, green lending is a new phenomenon in finance, whereby low-interest loans or credit cards are ofered to green manufacturing firms or individuals [16]. The literature on the positive and negative spillover efects of sustainable consumptions, however, questions the aforementioned as sumption and provides a more systematic analysis of the underlying mechanisms between sustainable consumptions and subsequent beha viors.

## 2.3. Literature review on the spillover efects of sustainable consumption

The spillover efects of sustainable consumption have been thoroughly reviewed by Truelove et al. [14] and Nilsson et al. [15]. As mentioned, the evidence suggests that a mixture of positive and negative spillover efects occur. Multiple mechanisms have been suggested to explain these positive or negative spillover efects. For example, negative spillover efects are explained by moral licensing effects, rebound efects, or single-action bias, whereas positive efects are supported by theories such as cognitive dissonance theory (consistency efects), self-perception theory (identity efects), or action-based learning perspectives. Table 2 provides examples of some studies of these two spillover efect types.

The positive spillover of proenvironment behaviors is supported by extensive research. For instance, Baca-Motes et al. [46] found that guests who made a specific commitment to reuse their towels were less likely to leave their lights on in an unoccupied room. This study is based on cognitive dissonance theory [53], demonstrating that individuals tend to maintain consistency with behavior they have previously performed; otherwise, they experience a sense of discomfort or uneasiness. Moreover, Van der Werf et al. [48] observed that people who were reminded of their previous PEBs were more likely to make green product decisions than people who were reminded of environmentally unfriendly actions they had previously adopted. This result can be ex plained by identity efects, whereby an individual’s identity as an environmentalist was shown to influence PEB in the environmental realm [54,47]. In addition, Thøgersen and Noblet [50] described a positive spillover from green consumerism to the acceptance of wind power, suggesting a catalytic efect whereby the “small behavior” of green consumerism partially mediated the relation between environmental concerns and the “larger behavior” of supporting wind power use. This is explained by the action-based learning theory, according to which increased awareness regarding the outcomes of a specific behavior can afect other behaviors.

However, some studies have claimed that sustainable consumption contradicts natural human tendencies, and thus, sustainable consumption does not necessarily imply favorable behaviors in other areas [9]. According to Griskevicius et al. [9], sustainable consumption behavior is not a long-term reflection of human values but a short-term decision reflecting concerns related to social moral, self-image, and social image. This argument is consistent with findings that a negative spillover efect is exerted on PEB. For example, Jacobsen et al. [83] noted that voluntary participation in a green power program increased subsequent household electricity consumption due to rebound efects. One study [2] observed that people behave less altruistically and are more likely to cheat and steal after purchases of green products than conventional products. Their explanation for this counterintuitive finding is based on moral licensing theory, which suggests that purchasing green products afirms individuals’ sense of social responsibility and ethical consciousness, which licenses selfish and morally questionable behavior elsewhere. Hahnel et al. [55] similarly observed that participants in green condition were less willing to volunteer for unrelated research. Similarly, Karmarkar and Bollinger [56] noted that consumers who take their own bags to supermarkets always purchase more indulgent foods, such as desserts, candy, and snack chips. This result is also built on the psychological moral license efect theory, whereby awareness of having taken a virtuous action can cause people to believe they have earned a more indulgent reward [57].

The mixed results and the contradicting theory predictions regarding the spillover efects of sustainable consumption prompts a critical empirical question regarding the relationship between sustainable consumption and consumers’ subsequent behaviors. Together with the measurement issue of current sustainable consumption research, there is a research gap on the real-data validation of the spillover efects of sustainable consumption on cross-domain behaviors. This study attempts to fill in these research gaps by (1) collaborating with the research institute of Alibaba.com to measure sustainable consumption behavior more objectively, and (2) investigating how borrowers’ sustainable consumption is associated with their default behavior in financial service with a joint dataset.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Ajzen and Fishbein’s [58] theory of reasoned action conceptualized the links between beliefs, attitudes, perceived social value, and behaviors by building on the expectancy-value theory through the incorporation of normative social influence on behavioral intention [59,60]. According to the consistency efects, people have social motivations for acting consistently [14]. Consumers’ self-perceptions re garding their own social identity also makes them behave in accordance with how the person they perceive themselves to be might behave [61]. In this regard, consumers’ social value preferences that regulate sustainable consumption also regulate individuals’ other behaviors, such as donation [9], energy-saving [25], and social norm compliance [62].

In the context of microloans, green consumers who have made more sustainable consumption have stronger social value preferences, and are less likely to violate their self- perceived social identity and harm lenders, so they are less likely to default than nongreen consumers. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. (H1): Green consumers are less likely to default on microloans than those who are not green consumers.

According to Batson’s model of prosocial behavior [63,64], consumers buy green products either for egoistic or altruistic motives, that is, beyond concerns for the environment, consumers might choose green products for more individualistic reasons. For example, organic foods are perceived to be beneficial for health in addition to having environmental benefits [65,66]. Such health-related sustainable consumption can be viewed as stemming more from health-related concerns than from concerns about the community [67]. If sustainable consumption is more egoistically oriented, the underlying mechanism that green consumers are deemed better people might not necessarily hold. With altruistic motivations being weaker, such borrowers may exhibit the same debt repayment behavior as other nongreen consumers do. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. (H2): Compared with nongreen consumers, those who purchase green products from altruistic motivations are less likely to default, but this does not apply to green consumers who purchase green products only from self-interest.

In addition, individuals’ conceptions of themselves can influence their behavior [68]. An online experiment demonstrates the importance of consumers' ethical self-identity in the mental processing of socially conscious consumer decisions [68]. In the context of sustainable consumption, as the consumption of green products increases, according to the self-confirmation theory, consumers are more likely to identify themselves as high social value-oriented consumers. With the increase of ethical self-identity, consumers are less likely to default on their debts. In this study, we measure sustainable consumption activeness by the number of sustainable consumption transactions as a proportion of the total number of transactions. Therefore, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 3. (H3): Consumers who have higher sustainable consumption activeness are less likely to default on microloans than those less active in sustainable consumption.

Hypothesis 4. (H4): Consumers who have higher sustainable consumption activeness are less likely to default on microloans, but this does not apply for consumers more active in self-interested sustainable consumption.

## 4. Research data and variables

## 4.1. Research context and data

The microloan business has expanded rapidly for loans issued both by online financial organizations and on peer- to-peer platforms. Nevertheless, microloans are more vulnerable to default risks than traditional financial organizations’ loans, because borrowers typically lack collateral and verifiable credit histories; for microloans, the risk is reportedly 2.279 times higher than that of traditional loans [69]. Consequently, applicants’ default risk assessment is crucial for the success of this industry [69,70]. In addition to quantitative assessments based on big data, more latent social value-oriented factors should be considered in borrowers’ credit assessments [71].

A leading microloan platform in China collaborated with us to investigate whether borrowers’ sustainable consumption constitutes a suitable proxy of social values or responsibility and generates positive spillover efects on borrowers’ repayment behavior. This microloan platform was founded in 2011 and usually issues microloans of approximately RMB 3000 approximately US\$ 460. The loan term range is 1–7 months, and the annual interest rate is 12 %–16 %. The platform currently has more than 200,000 borrowers. The dataset provided by this platform includes two parts.

The first part comprises all loans issued from August to September 2017, with loan attributes and borrower characteristics. Borrowers must repay installments every month until the loan is paid of. Because the longest loan had a 7- month span, the repayment period of our sample was from September 1, 2017 to April 30, 2018. If an installment was not collected 3 months after the loan due date (i.e., the last monthly repayment due date), a loan default was confirmed. Therefore, we collected the monthly repayment records of these loans from September 2017 to July 2018, to permit suficient time for observing the ultimate loan default.

The second part of the dataset comprised borrowers’ online con sumption records on Taobao.com (including Tmall.com) and Jd.com from March 2017 to August 2017; in other words, 6 months before submitting their loan application. Taobao.com and Jd.com are two top online retailer shopping websites in China, which dominated approximately 85 % of the Chinese individual online shopping market in 2018.<sup>5</sup> Thus, the transaction records from Taobao.com and Jd.com are suficiently representative to capture borrowers’ online consumption behavior. The supplied online consumption records included transaction times, product titles, product categories, purchase quantities, and expenditures. The microloan firm obtained the online consumption records from a third-party website with borrowers’ consent during the loan application stage.<sup>6</sup> All these borrowers had online shopping experiences 6 months before submitting their loan applications. The dataset eventually contained 740,000 monthly repayment observations and 710,000 online consumption observations for 12,215 borrowers.

## 4.2. Variables

In our research context, like many previous researches (e.g., [72,73]), sustainable consumption is mainly reflected by consumers’ green product purchase behavior. To identify the borrowers’ sustainable consumption behaviors from their online consumption information, we collaborated with Aliresearch institution from Alibaba Inc., which has defined 344 green product keywords for six product categories, based on their extensive knowledge in each industry. Aliresearch has abundant experience in discerning, analyzing, and labeling the consumption characteristics of large consumer populations based on its commercial ecosystem, which is the largest globally.<sup>7</sup> This ensures the efectiveness of measuring sustainable consumption with keywords defined by Aliresearch. Appendix A provides sample keywords for each category identified by Aliresearch. For example, for the electronic appliance industry, product descriptions including keywords such as energy saving and first-level energy eficiency are defined as green products; for the food and beverage category, representative green product keywords include organic and no anticorrosion.

We matched these specific keywords and categories with borrowers online consumption information. Subsequently, 9514 borrowers were found to have purchased green products 6 months before submitting their loan applications. To exclude random green product purchase behavior in our dataset, we count only the 4810 consumers with more than three green product purchases in the past 6 months as green consumers, which represents 39.38 % of all sample borrowers. This definition of green consumers is consistent with that of Aliresearch, who defines green consumers as those consuming green products at least six times within one year. For a subsequent robustness check, we adopted five green product purchases as the threshold for green consumers.

In addition, this study diferentiated two types of sustainable consumption, namely ecofriendly (or proenvironmental) and health-related consumption, based on the keyword feature ofered by Aliresearch. We posited that ecofriendly consumption is a more altruistically driven consumption [74], whereas health-related consumption might be more egoistically driven. Ecofriendly products were labeled with 193 of 344 keywords: These included cotton and linen, solar-powered, E0-level environmental protection standard, and first-level energy eficiency in the categories for clothing, electronic, and household appliances. The other 151 keywords, such as radiation-free, sterilization, antiseptic, and organic, in categories for daily necessities, nuts, baby and infant products, skincare products, and so on were used to indicate health-related consumption.

In our sample of 4810 green consumers, 2826 had purchased ecofriendly products more than three times; 249 had purchased healthrelated products more than three times; and the other 1735 consumers had purchased both types of green products multiple times. Thus, we further defined these green consumers according to three subgroups: ecoconsumers only, health-motivated consumers only, and individuals qualifying both as ecoconsumers and health-motivated consumers.

Based on the widely adopted recency–frequency–amount model, we coded the Recency (i.e., time duration between loan application date and the last green product purchase date), sustainable consumption transaction number as a proportion of the total transaction number (Ratio\_green (frequency)), and sustainable consumption as a proportion of total transaction amount (Ratio\_green (amount)) to describe consumers’ sustainable consumption characteristics. Among these three variables, given our data window is only 6 months long, the variation on recency is less significant in our study, thus we focus more on frequency and amount dimension. Notably, we found the correlation coeficients between these two types of variables are high (greater than 0.6). Hence, we included and reported only the results of frequency in the main regression, and the results of amount are provided in robustness check.

We also coded several groups of variables to describe these borrowers from diferent perspectives. The first group is for borrowers additional consumption information (Additional\_Consump\_Variables): expenditure of nongreen purchase (Amount\_nongreen), the total quantity of consumptions (Num\_consump), and the borrower’s average monthly online consumption amount as a proportion of monthly income (Ratio\_consumpinc). Moreover, we controlled the transaction number proportions for each product to the total number of transactions for six product types (Ratio\_clothing, Ratio\_elec, etc.) to capture the relative purchasing frequencies of various product types. The second group is for loan attributes (Loan\_Attributes) including loan amount, loan term and interest rate, and loan purpose. The third group is for borrower characteristics (Borrower\_Characteristics) including age, sex, education level, income level, marital status, and the number of children. The correlation matrix (Appendix B) indicates no potential problem of multicollinearity, given the low correlation coeficients for each pair of covariates. ## 5. Empirical analysis

## 5.1. Endogeneity of sustainable consumption

Before we present the empirical analysis, self- selection must be ruled out for green product purchase behaviors of borrowers. Namely, except for the choice of green products, borrowers with sustainable consumption should exhibit similar shopping patterns, income, and other demographic characteristics as borrowers without sustainable consumption. In this study, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to control for the selection of observables. In particular, three possible mechanisms for self-selection between green consumers and nongreen consumers are considered to address the endogeneity problem.

First, shopping product categories might account for inherent sustainable consumption diferences. For example, if consumer A purchased baby products (she or he bought baby products ten times), but consumer B did not (so she or he bought baby products 0 times), then consumer A is more likely to be a green consumer than consumer B is, because the percentage of green products in the baby products category is much higher than in other categories. Thus, the discrepancy in their sustainable consumption behavior stems not from social consciousness but from demand heterogeneity. The shopping structure therefore had to be balanced between green consumers and nongreen consumers to exclude this type of demand heterogeneity. We categorized all the products in our sample into six common categories according to the Nice Classification published by the Ministry of Commerce of China<sup>9</sup> : clothing, electronic products, food and beverages, household products, personal products, and cosmetics and medicine (a few sample keywords from the six product categories are reported in Appendix A). As seen in Table 4, the proportion of green products difers across the six categories. For example, green household products represent 12.03 % of all the household products, whereas green electronic products represent only 5.94 % of all the electronic products. Therefore, we coded six dummy variables, that is, Category\_clothing, Category\_elec, Category\_food, Category\_household, Category\_personal, and Category\_cosmetics, and included them in the PSM procedure to balance the category distribution between green consumers and nongreen consumers.

Second, green consumers and nongreen consumers may have different incomes or dispensable cash flow, given that green products usually cost more than nongreen products [74]. Ensuring the similarity between green and nongreen consumers’, historical consumption quantities might largely rule out the efects of income. We thus included two relevant consumption variables: Amount\_nongreen and Ratio\_- consumpinc in the PSM procedure.

Lastly, the heterogeneity of borrowers’ demographic characteristics might also cause the endogeneity of sustainable consumption [3]. Balancing demographic characteristics between two groups can further enhance the causal inference of sustainable consumption on repayment behaviors. All the coded variables feature in Table 3.

With the aforementioned variables as explanatory variables, and a green consumer as the dependent variable, we calculated propensity scores for each consumer with a logit model. The nearest neighbormatching strategy was used to create a matched sample. After PSM, we obtained 4790 green consumers and 6107 nongreen consumers. Table 5 reports the PSM results, which suggests that the bias for all covariates decreased to less than 10 %. In addition, we further conducted a coarsened exact matching (CEM) method to verify the robustness of our findings. The results are reported in Appendix C.

## 5.2. Model-free analysis

After matching the green consumers and nongreen consumers, we developed three indicators to reflect borrowers’ repayment behaviors: whether loans were defaulted on, the proportion of monthly installments paid (i.e., repayment rate, whereby a higher rate is considered to be better), and period of time during which loans were overdue. The definitions of these key dependent variables are detailed in Table 6. In the dataset, the overall default rate was 31.99 %, and the overall repayment rate was 72.25 %. The default rate was relatively high on our study’s microloan platform. This is why the platform made extensive use of external information, such as borrowers’ shopping records, to improve credit risk assessment accuracy. Table 6 indicates that green consumers exhibit better repayment performance overall than nongreen consumers. In particular. green consumers had a 4.34 % lower default probability and 5.65 % higher repayment than did nongreen consumers (p < 0.01 for t test); the time period during which a loan was overdue was also shorter for green consumers than for nongreen consumers.

Fig. 1 details comparisons of repayment behaviors for three subgroups of green consumers and nongreen consumers. Green consumers, especially those in the group containing consumers of both ecological and health products, outperform nongreen consumers in every specific repayment indicator. Furthermore, if we compare the repayment behaviors of green consumers whose purchases were exclusively altruistic (i.e., ecofriendly) with those whose purchases were exclusively self interested $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { i } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . ,$ health- related), altruism-motivated consumers performed better than self-interest-motivated consumers in every repayment indicator.

## 5.3. Model specification and results

To address unobserved heterogeneity in model- free analysis, we further applied the following model specification to systematically test the efects of sustainable consumption on borrowers’ loan repayment behavior. Borrowers’ sustainable consumption type (H1 and H2) and sustainable consumption frequency (H3 and H4) were separately examined as described in the following section.

## 5.3.1. Borrowers’ sustainable consumption types as independent variables

## 6. Robustness check

We enhanced our findings with four robustness checks. First, we defined green consumers as individuals who had purchased green products five times or more in the past 6 months. Second, we replaced the green product consumption frequency ratio with the green product consumption amount ratio to represent sustainable consumption tendency. Third, we reexamine ecofriendly and health-related consumption with stricter measurement criteria. Finally, we performed a further investigation of product categories.

## 6.1. Alternative definition of green consumers

We defined those who had purchased green products more than five times as green consumers (Greenconsumer), those who had purchased ecofriendly products more than five times as ecoconsumers (Ecoconsumer), and those who had purchased health- related products more than five times as health- borrowers (Health-consumer). According to this definition, 3020 individuals were green consumers and 9195 were green and nongreen consumers, respectively.

Using analysis similar to that in Section $^ { 5 , }$ Table 9 reports the regression results by setting sustainable consumption types as independent variables after the PSM procedure. The results are similar to our main findings in Table 7: Green consumers exhibited better repayment behavior, represented by lower default rates and shorter durations with loans overdue as well as higher repayment rates. Furthermore, ecoconsumers exhibited better repayment behavior than health consumers did.

## 6.2. Alternative description of sustainable consumption

Because consumption frequency does not always reflect consistent purchasing behaviors, we replaced frequency ratios with amount ratios to calculate the sustainable consumption ratio. Table 10 reports the results after the PSM procedure. Similar to the main findings in Table 8, the results of Table 10 suggest that a larger ratio for green product consumption as a proportion of total consumption had a significantly positive efect on reducing borrowers’ default rates and durations with loans overdue as well as a significantly positive efect on improving the repayment rate. Furthermore, when borrowers behaved more altruistically during green product consumption, they performed better in the repayment process; but these results did not apply for self-interested (i.e., health-related) green product consumption.

## 6.3. Stricter measurement criteria for ecofriendly consumption and health related consumption

Currently, ecofriendly and health-related product keywords may not be completely distinct, potentially confounding the diference between ecofriendly and health- related consumption. In this robustness check, we narrow down ecofriendly consumption into the purchase that is with explicitly environmental protection keywords such as energy saving, bamboo textile, E0-level environmental protection standard, and first-level energy eficiency [84,85]. Similarly, we narrow down health-related purchases into purchases that are explicitly health-related, such as additive-free, disinfected or sterilized, and nontoxic [86,87]. We exclude keywords that cannot dis tinguish ecofriendly products from health-related ones, such as cotton and silk. The keywords employed in the stricter measurement criteria for ecofriendly consumption and health-related consumption are marked in italic in Table A1 in Appendix A.

Tables 11a and 11b report the regression results for ecological or health consumers and for ecological or health-related consumption, respectively. The analytical operations in Tables 11a and 11b are identical to those in Tables 7 and 8, except for the definitions of ecological or health consumers and ecofriendly or health-related consumption. We obtained results similar to those in Tables 7 and 8, in dicating the robustness of our findings.

## 6.4. Product categories

As mentioned, the product pool contains six product categories, and the proportions of green products purchased might difer across dif ferent product categories. We thus deeply explored the efects of green product consumption across diferent product categories on borrowers’ repayment behaviors. Specifically, we separately calculated the ratio of the green product consumption frequency to the total consumption frequency in each product category as six variables of interest: Ratio\_green\_clothing, Ratio\_green\_elec, Ratio\_green\_food, Ratio\_green household, Ratio\_green\_personal, and Ratio\_green\_cosmetics.<sup>10</sup> We then investigated their efects on borrowers’ repayment behaviors using Equation (1).

Table 12 demonstrates that the green product consumptions of electronic products (Ratio\_green\_elec) and personal products (Ratio\_- green\_personal) were significantly positively related to better repayment behavior, which means lower borrowers default ratios, shorter overdue durations, and higher repayment rates. For personal products, the sig nificance was even stronger. The green product consumption of clothing (Ratio\_green\_clothing) also has the same efect, but the significance is slightly weaker. Nonetheless, the green product consumptions of food (Ratio\_- green\_food), household products (Ratio\_green\_household), and cosmetics (Ra tio\_green\_cosmetics) had no significantly positive efect on repayment beha vior.

The results are reasonable because the green product purchases of electronic products and personal products more likely reflect consumers’ altruism features, whereas the green product consumptions of food (Ratio\_green\_food), household products (Ratio\_green\_household), and cosmetics (Ratio\_green\_cosmetics) are more likely to reflect consumers egoism. Thus. these results are consistent with H2.

## 7. Conclusions

This paper investigates the cross-domain spillover efects of sustainable consumption behavior on consumers’ subsequent debt default behavior and questions whether green consumers maintain a similar sense of social responsibility and morality beyond the consumption domain. Our big data based empirical analysis demonstrates that green consumers exhibit better repayment performance than nongreen consumers do, but the findings do not hold if sustainable consumption is self-interest driven. Thus, the findings of this study suggest that societies should incentivize more altruistic sustainable consumption, which would encourage more subsequent social-oriented behaviors. These findings contribute to research in various ways.

First, this study provides empirical evidence on whether green consumers exhibit more social value-oriented behaviors beyond the consumption domain, an idea that is rarely examined in current individual sustainable behavior literature. The results of this study suggest that green consumers maintain an overall 4.34 % lower default probability than do nongreen consumers when we have controlled many other potentially influential factors. Such an efect is nontrivial, showing a somehow strong social responsibility of consumers reflected by their sustainable consumption. Our findings enrich the personality analysis literature of sustainable consumption by associating sustain able consumption with other cross-domain behavior.

Second, a few prior sustainability and sustainable consumption studies have attempted to use data analytics to favor sustainable manufacture (e.g., cleaner architecture manufacture [75]) and environmental policy making (e.g., promoting energy conservation [77] and low- carbon lifestyles [78]). These studies require and take advantages of within-domain data to facil itate sustainable activities. However, we show that in many cases extraneous to sustainable activities, such as the microloan credit risk assessment in our focal context, the availability of sustainable consumption “big data” can also contribute to business operation. Specifically, unlike the research in which sustainable consumption is measured with survey data, we take ad vantage of consumer-level data to measure sustainable consumption behavior and its impacts on the subsequent behavior more objectively. This contributes to the traditional sustainable consumption literature by providing more factual evidence to the debate on the spillover efects of sustainable consumption.

Third, most of the spillover efects of sustainable consumption research focus on the within domain efects, namely, how engagement on one sustainable consumption behavior afects a second sustainable consumption. This study extends this stream of literature by examining cross-domain spillover efects of sustainable consumption. Our findings confirm positive spillover efects predicted by consistency efects and countering the negative spillover efects. This is consistent with the findings of Urban et al. [88], which also fail to replicate the negative spillover efects of Mazar and Zhong [2]. Thus, we argue that sustainable consumption behavior is less likely to trigger cross-domain licensing efects than formerly believed.

In addition, theoretically and managerially, this study contributes to the microloan research literature and practice by providing a new perspective to assess borrowers’ credit and risk. Microloan platforms face more challenges and constraints than traditional agencies do in credit risk screening. The high penetration of mobile technology and internet access ofers new and unparalleled sources of fine- grained user-behavior data, including online consumption data [79]. These types of data become less expensive to acquire and could demonstrate significant potential to enhance the accuracy of existing credit risk assessment [80]. This study demonstrates that the availability of online consumption data may provide online microloan platforms with an alternative data source to identify high-quality borrowers. On the other side, one of the intrinsic goals of microfinance is to enable more “thin-file” unbanked users to get financial supports [81]. Some “thinfile” good consumers with high social responsibility are excluded from financial services because of their lack of oficial loan histories [82]. The efectiveness of alternative data sources such as consumption data on financial credit risk assessment demonstrated by our study is beneficial fo realizing the financial inclusion and mitigating social inequality.

This study is not without limitations. First, we use debt repayment be havior as a proxy for consumers’ social responsibility outside the consumption domain and directly examine the efects of sustainable consumption on repayment behavior. Such analysis cannot aid the opening of the black box and examining the underlying factors that afect this relationship. Subsequent studies might fill in this gap by conducting surveys or experiments to learn how sustainable consumption afects debt repay ment behavior. Second, national culture has a major efect on consumers behavior. Findings for China may difer from those in other countries. The empirical analysis on one platform might likewise limit the generalizability of our findings. Similar research in other countries or microloan companie might provide additional insights into sustainable consumption and mi croloan repayment behavior. Finally, we coded only green product purchase behavior to reflect sustainable consumption in this study. We might also code other consumption behavior, such as indulgent consumption or irresponsible consumption in the future and examine the efects of social responsibility from diferent perspectives.

## Appendix D. Instrumental variable analyses

To enhance the causality of our analysis, we figured out a group of instrumental variables: (1) Search Index of “Environmental Protection” on Baidu.com<sup>11</sup> of diferent consumer located cities during the observation period of online shopping, (2) Search Index of “Pollution”, and (3) City Environmental Levels $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the ratio of release of major pollutants to population)<sup>12</sup>. The reason why we chose these factors as instrumental variables (IVs) is that these factors reflect consumers’ sense of local environmental status and thus highly associate with the sustainable consumption behaviors of consumers from diferent geographical areas, but are weakly related to people’s repayment decision in microloan (they passed the IV validity test). We employed the two-stage least squares procedure (2SLS) with these instrumental variables and reported the regression results in Table D1. The results patterns are consistent with our main analyses.
