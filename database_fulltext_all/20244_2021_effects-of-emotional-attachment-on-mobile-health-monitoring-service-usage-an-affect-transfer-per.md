# E<sup>f</sup>ects of emotional attachment on mobile health-monitoring service usage: An a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer perspective

Xiaofei Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Xitong Guo<sup>b,</sup>\*, Shuk Ying Ho<sup>c</sup>, Kee-hung Lai<sup>d</sup>, Doug Vogel<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Business School, Nankai University, Tianjin, China

<sup>b</sup> School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China

<sup>c</sup> Research School of Accounting, Australian National University, Canberra, Australia

<sup>d</sup> Faculty of Business, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Kowloon, Hong Kong

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: mHealth monitoring service Emotional attachment Device satisfaction Feedback satisfaction A<sup>f</sup>ect transfer Health rationality

## A B S T R A C T

Mobile health-monitoring services (MMSs) empower patients with chronic illnesses to self-manage their health concerns; however, in practice, many patients become inactive users after employing MMSs for a short time. The reasons for this usage pattern remain unclear. By extending the literature that focuses on the cognitive reasoning behind system usage, our study takes an a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer perspective to examine how these patients develop emotional attachment to MMSs that subsequently drives their usage. Drawing on a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer theory, we hypothesize that patients’ satisfaction with MMS components in<sup>fl</sup>uences their emotional attachment to the service through both cognitive and misattribution routines, and that the combined e<sup>f</sup>ects of these two routine are contingent on patients’ health rationality. Our hypotheses are tested with survey data collected from 228 patients with chronic illnesses. This study contributes to the mobile health (mHealth) literature by investigating patients’ actual behavior based on their interactions with MMSs from an a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer perspective. It also informs service providers of MMSs regarding how to motivate service usage by patients with chronic illnesses by adopting a strategic design.

## 1. Introduction

The term “mobile health services” refers to the provision of healthcare support, delivery, and intervention via mobile technologies in the healthcare sector, such as smartphones, tablets, and wearable technology. It connects health professionals with patients and engages patients in the healthcare process, with the aim of enhancing health service quality and reducing costs [1]. One group of mobile health (mHealth) services enables patients to track their health conditions at home and self-manage their health concerns. We refer to this group as “mobile health-monitoring services” (MMSs), which are designed to help patients with chronic illnesses.

Chronic illnesses are long-lasting illness conditions; examples include heart disease, diabetes, and cancer. The World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that health services for chronic diseases are a vital “investment” for the healthcare industry, particularly given that ischemic heart disease and stroke are the world’s biggest killers, accounting for a combined 15.2 million deaths in 2016 [2]. In the United States (US), chronic diseases are among the most prevalent and costly health conditions, with around 133 million Americans su<sup>f</sup>ering from at least one chronic disease [3]. Thus, this paper focuses on MMS usage by patients with chronic illnesses.

Over the past decade, the global healthcare industry has invested signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in MMSs. The worldwide market of MMSs is projected to reach US\$90 billion by the end of 2025, accounting for more than half of the total mHealth market [4]. However, prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings in relation to MMS usage are mixed. Some studies have indicated that MMSs for chronic illness healthcare provide favorable clinical outcomes [5], streamlined clinical processes [5–7], reduced medical expenses [8,9], strong compliance behavior among patients [5,9], and health-related quality of life [7].

However, real users only seem to remain interested in MMS innovations for a short period of time, and active users comprise only a small percentage of the user population. In China, Chunyu Doctor—a leading mHealth technology developer—launched MMS products to 30 million patients; of these, only 900,000 patients (3%) became frequent users [10]. Helander et al. [11] collected the behavioral data of nearly 190,000 people who planned to regulate their diet in the long term by adopting a mobile app, called Eatery. The authors examined these people’s usage patterns and found that less than 3% of them remained active Eatery users six months after starting to use the app. Possibly, these mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings have arisen because most studies in the former group employed cross-sectional data, yet MMS usage does not seem to be sustainable over a longer time period. Given that the core value of MMSs derives from their ability to e<sup>f</sup>ectively monitor patients’ health conditions over time [12], patients’ inactive usage compromises the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of MMSs. Therefore, our study examines how the interplay of cognition and a<sup>f</sup>ect in<sup>fl</sup>uences patients with chronic illnesses to continue their MMS usage.

We draw on two theories to guide our study. We use the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer theory to examine how patients with chronic illnesses may transfer their a<sup>f</sup>ective response to two MMS components (device and feedback) to their perception of the entire MMS, and how this perception in<sup>fl</sup>uences their continuance usage. Information Systems (IS) researchers have empirically demonstrated the dominant role of cognition in service usage (e.g., Bhattacherjee [13],Soongeun et al. [14],Venkatesh et al. [15]). However, we consider their <sup>fi</sup>ndings less applicable to MMSs for patients with chronic illnesses for two reasons. First, using a cross-sectional cognitive view to predict user behavior fails to explain actual usage at the post-adoption stage—particularly usage frequency and intensity [15], which are our foci. Second, patients with chronic illnesses experience long-term su<sup>f</sup>ering, such as pain, fatigue, and mood disorders. Therefore, they have a stronger emotional need than typical system users [16] and their cognitive evaluation of MMSs may not play an important role in technology use.

A<sup>f</sup>ect transfer theory describes a phenomenon in which people’s favorable attitudes toward components of an object (i.e., device and feedback, in our case) can facilitate the development of their overall attitude toward the object [17,18]. The key concept of this theory is emotional attachment, which refers to the strength of the a<sup>f</sup>ective bond between a person and a speci<sup>fi</sup>c object [19]. In our case, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c object is MMSs. We argue that patients with chronic illnesses may experience negative emotions as a result of their conditions. However, because the two components of MMSs—device and feedback—relieve these negative emotions [16,20], these patients emotionally attach to MMSs, which alters their continuance usage. We examine how interactions between patients with chronic illnesses and MMSs may lead to such emotional attachment, which, in turn, facilitates their continuous usage of MMS. Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question is as follows:

RQ1. How do patients’ interactions with MMSs in<sup>fl</sup>uence their emotional attachment to and continuous usage of MMSs?

To examine the interplay between cognition and a<sup>f</sup>ect of patients with chronic illnesses, we propose two a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer routines—cognitive and misattribution routines. Cognitive routine is based on cognitive evaluations and is thus considered rational [21]. By contrast, misattribution routine concerns a misattributing a<sup>f</sup>ect derived from other objects and is thus considered irrational [22]. This dissension renders the underlying mechanism of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer underexplored and has resulted in mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings [23,24]. For instance, individuals can hold di<sup>f</sup>erent a<sup>f</sup>ect regarding a given object based on the same a<sup>f</sup>ect transferred from related objects. To uncover the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer, we draw on contingency theory and identify health rationality as the boundary condition [25]. Health rationality is a personal characteristic of patients and de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which individuals make rational health decisions. We anticipate that it plays a pivotal role in di<sup>f</sup>erentiating the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the two a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer routines on health decision-making. By examining the moderating role of health rationality, we seek to understand how patients with di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics form an emotional attachment to MMSs. Thus, our second research question is as follows:

RQ2. To what extent is the transfer process contingent on patients health rationality?

To test our six hypotheses which are to be elaborated in subsequent sections, we collaborated with a hospital in Beijing, which helped us disseminate our survey to their patients with chronic illnesses. We collected 228 usable responses and conducted a partial least square (PLS) analysis. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have the potential to enhance researchers and practitioners of how patients with chronic illnesses may form a<sup>f</sup>ect toward MMS components and transfer this to their overall attitude toward MMSs, and whether patient characteristics (i.e., health ration ality, in our case) in<sup>fl</sup>uence this transfer process.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Two key MMS components: device and feedback

MMSs comprise two components that empower patients to self manage their health conditions and obtain advice for critical healthrelated decisions [26,27]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst component is a health condition tracker and analyzer. This regularly collects data related to patients health conditions, and then sends it to a centralized system or processes it in the local device in real time. The entire tracking and analyzing process is transparent to patients. We refer to this <sup>fi</sup>rst component as “device.” The second component involves generating personalized health recommendations for patients and sending them to patients via email or mobile messaging [28]. These recommendations can be automatically generated by healthcare IS or manually customized by health professionals. We refer to the second component as “feedback.” These two components have a subtle di<sup>f</sup>erence. The “device” component enables patients to self-assess their health conditions, which em powers them to understand their health conditions and o<sup>f</sup>ers them a sense of control. By contrast, the “feedback” component places patients in a passive role because they receive health recommendations from external parties, and may not know who generated these recommendations for them.

Given that patients with chronic illnesses have stronger emotional needs, we are interested in how these patients a<sup>f</sup>ectively interact with these two components (device and feedback) and how their a<sup>f</sup>ective responses toward service components combine to drive actual MMS usage. Thus far, most mHealth studies have focused on patients’ behavioral intentions (not actual system usage), such as adoption intention [29–32], use intention [33–35], and continuance intention [36,37]. Only a few studies have examined actual usage behaviors (see a review by Wu and Du) [38], such as compliance [39] and individual usage [40]. More importantly, these studies have been largely con<sup>fi</sup>ned to cognitive factors, such as perceived ease of use and usefulness [29,32,41], service quality [37,42], perceived risk [30,33], and cogni tive attitude [39]. Only a few a<sup>f</sup>ective factors have been explored in relation to mHealth. These factors include user satisfaction [36,37], a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment [43], and perceived enjoyment and fun (which manifest as intrinsic motivation) [30,34,44].

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of these studies are far from directly applicable to our study problem, which aims to examine how patients’ cognition and a<sup>f</sup>ect interplay to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their perception of MMSs and actual usage. In addition, these studies do not discuss how the two e<sup>f</sup>ects can exert a combined in<sup>fl</sup>uence on patients’ perceptions and actual usage of MMSs, and in which situations one e<sup>f</sup>ect is stronger than the other.

## 2.2. Emotional attachmen

The IS literature suggests that users develop a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to a technology when they interact with it [45]. As a psychological bond between a user and a service, emotional attachment can exert enduring and stable e<sup>f</sup>ects on user behavior [46]. Individuals can develop emotional attachment to numerous objects, including branded goods [47], pets [48], places [49,50], online relationships [51], and human beings [52]. Hazan and Zeifman [53] posited that emotional attachment is important in adults’ lives because it cultivates enduring relationships. In marketing, emotional attachment to a brand exerts an e<sup>f</sup>ect on consumer loyalty [54–56], behavioral commitment [54,57], and behavioral intentions [58–60].

The formation of emotional attachment to a technology or service occurs as follows: users start to interact with the technology or service and gradually generate positive feelings toward it, and then develop strong attachments over time through long-term interactions [47]. That is, the formation of emotional attachment takes time. The IS literature has explored the e<sup>f</sup>ects of emotional attachment on intention to donate [61], intention to use avatars [59], eBook reader adoption [62], and team performance [63]. mHealth is ubiquitous and of high mobility, making it easy for patients to develop mobile-self connectedness [64]. These features of mHealth give patients the impression that they are receiving constant assistance; thus, mHealth easily leads patients to form an emotional attachment [65].

We anticipate that patients will form an emotional attachment to MMSs. When patients with chronic illnesses <sup>fi</sup>rst adopt MMSs, they seek healthcare advice or psychological support from them. Through their interactions, patients develop strong emotional attachment to MMSs because, when they need help, the MMSs provide instant monitoring and health recommendation services. This can help patients overcome the depression and anxiety induced by chronic illnesses [56]. In a general setup, emotional attachment forms quickly when patients appreciate the health services they receive can protect their health [66]. In addition, patients’ emotional attachment to health services is found to exert a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on their self-management outcomes [67] and compliance [68]. Emotional attachment can surpass the e<sup>f</sup>ects of cognitive evaluation on service usage, and make individuals more emotionally dependent on MMSs [69,70], which can be the main cause of preventive health behavior.

Several IS researchers have highlighted the importance of identifying the determinants of emotional attachment [47,54,57,58]. Some have suggested explaining emotional attachment from a cognitive perspective [55,56,59,60]. The unique features of mobile technologies mean that emotional attachment to mHealth services has become a new research domain [65]. Personalized health feedback leads patients to appreciate and feel comfortable with mHealth services. Patients’ emo tional attachment to health resources has been shown to have positive e<sup>f</sup>ects on their compliance with their health procedures [71] and positive health outcomes [67]. However, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of emotional attach ment by patients with chronic illnesses on actual continuance usage behaviors are under-investigated. It is also unclear whether people’s emotional attachment to an individual mHealth component can in<sup>fl</sup>uence their overall perception of mHealth. Therefore, we draw on a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer theory to examine these issues.

## 2.3. Afect transfer theory

A<sup>f</sup>ect transfer theory refers to a person’s a<sup>f</sup>ect in response to object components being transferred to the overall object [72,73], thus developing their attitude toward the object as a whole [17,18]. A<sup>f</sup>ect is thought to transfer from an object that induces strong a<sup>f</sup>ective feelings to a second object that arouses little or no a<sup>f</sup>ective responses [74]. This is called afect transfer, and describes the process whereby individuals pre-existing a<sup>f</sup>ect (mood, emotion, and feelings) toward an object is transferred to another closely related object [75]. A<sup>f</sup>ect transfer has been used in marketing to compare people’s favorable attitudes toward advertisements and brands [24] and toward two brands [17]. The theory has also been extended to explain a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer from a parent brand to its subsidiary brands [76] and to explain people’s psychological change [22,77], but has thus far received limited research attention in the IS literature.

The underlying route through which an individual’s a<sup>f</sup>ect toward an object component is transferred to the object itself remains unclear. A<sup>f</sup>ect transfer may rely on a cognitive process, which is likely conscious, for instance, an individual evaluates an unfamiliar object based on its similarities and the relations between its components [17,24]. The theoretical foundation of this postulation is that (1) cognition of an unfamiliar object is <sup>fi</sup>rst developed based on one’s a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to its components and the relations between them, and (2) cognition then induces one’s a<sup>f</sup>ective response to the unfamiliar object [78]. Given that this routine is based on cognition, we use perceived value to manifest cognitive routine.

In addition to cognition, prior research suggests that the process of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer can be unconscious—that is, it does not necessarily provoke any cognitive e<sup>f</sup>orts [22]. This unconscious process is an a<sup>f</sup>ect misattribution routine. This occurs when an individual cannot separate his or her responses to an unfamiliar object and its components, thus misattributing the a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to components as responses to the whole object [79].

Thus far, the literature has not achieved consensus on whether the valence of the outcome a<sup>f</sup>ect is similar to that of the originally transferred a<sup>f</sup>ect. This lack of consensus has arisen because, even though it is generally believed that humans have a similar a<sup>f</sup>ect toward similar objects [23], prior studies have found that people can transfer di<sup>f</sup>erent a<sup>f</sup>ects from similar objects, thereby leading to mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the outcomes of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer [23,24]. For example, the a<sup>f</sup>ective attitude toward an object component may not be the same as the a<sup>f</sup>ective attitude toward the object itself [80], and the a<sup>f</sup>ective attitude toward a new brand may di<sup>f</sup>er from that toward similar brands where the a<sup>f</sup>ect is transferred [24]. A possible reason for these mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings is the fact that these studies did not distinguish between the two a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer routines (cognitive and misattribution routines) or explain the extent to which the individual relies on each transfer routine accordingly.

In the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer from an object’s component to the entire object—in our study, the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer from MMS components to the MMS as a whole—both aspects manifest similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences simultaneously. Thus, when forming a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to an unfamiliar object (i.e., the MMS, in our case) based on a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to its components (i.e., device and feedback, in our case), patients can only distinguish similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences between these aspects to a certain extent. Therefore, when transferring a<sup>f</sup>ect from an MMS component to the entire MMS in such situations, patients may rely on cognitive and misattribution routines at the same time. Their simultaneous reliance on cognitive and misattribution routines may cause di<sup>f</sup>erent outcomes of the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer from MMS components to the MMS. For example, in evaluating the MMS, when patients focus on the similarities between the components and the MMS and if they cannot <sup>fi</sup>nd remarkable di<sup>f</sup>erences between the two, then they are likely to rely more on the misattribution routine. These patients are thus likely to form similar evaluations of the MMS components and the MMS. By contrast, when patients focus on the di<sup>f</sup>erences between the MMS components and the MMS and if they have a better understanding of the components, they are likely to rely more on the cognitive routine, hence inducing them to formulate di<sup>f</sup>erent evaluations of the components and the MMS.

To explain the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the outcomes of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer, our study explores the two a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer routines and in vestigates the direct and indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects of component satisfaction (satisfaction with using the device [device satisfaction] and satisfaction with receiving health feedback [feedback satisfaction]) on the a<sup>f</sup>ective response to using MMSs. The indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects are exerted through a cognitive process. We follow the rational decision-making literature to use “perceived value” to capture a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer through the cognitive routine [81]. That is, we contend that patients form perceptions of how valuable a health service is on the basis of both cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ec tive reasoning [82]. Our rationale is that a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations of MMS components are transferred to a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to guide patients forming an evaluation of the entire MMS through both cognitive and misattribution routines, and the relative e<sup>f</sup>ects of the two routines are contingent on decision rationality.

## 2.4. Cognitive routine and perceived value

In our study, perceived value is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an evaluation based on comparing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of using MMSs for health management. Our de<sup>fi</sup>nition is based on rational choice theory [83], which posits that people are rational when making decisions and comparing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs to maximize outcomes [84]. Perceived value mea sures net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in the comparison as an overall cognitive evaluation [81]. It has been used to measure the overall cognitive outcomes of engaging in certain behaviors, such as switching to a system [81], making a purchase [85], or using a service [86]. In a<sup>f</sup>ective responses, perceived value has been considered to mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of a<sup>f</sup>ective responses on behavioral intentions [87,88]. Furthermore, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived value on overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations have also been studied—for example, in relation to customer satisfaction [86] and emo tional attachment to brands [89]. Taking all these studies together, we use perceived value to measure cognitive routine, in which satisfaction with an MMS s components in<sup>fl</sup>uences the overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation of the MMS. Following the logic of the IS success model [90], we argue that satisfaction with service components can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ cognitive evaluation of the entire service (manifested as perceived value).

However, rational choice theory has been criticized for having limited capacity to explain behaviors in low-rationality conditions, such as when emotions are involved in decision-making [91]. Hence, perceived value has thus far been generally applied in previous research to study decision-making in which a<sup>f</sup>ective factors are not incorporated. That is, perceived value has been used only for cognitive decisionmaking [81,92], whereby the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a<sup>f</sup>ective judgment has been largely overlooked. Based on the argument that perceived value manifests as the cognitive routine in a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer, our study demonstrates that even in relationships between two a<sup>f</sup>ective factors, the rational decision lens still works. In doing this, we provide empirical evidence that rational choice theory can be used to explain the partial decision process in less rational decision-making.

## 2.5. The contingent role of health rationality

Cognitive and misattribution routines display di<sup>f</sup>erences in the analytical model. Cognitive routine relies more on an analytical process and is more rational [78], while the misattribution routine is an in tuitive process and is less rational [22]. Which routine is more dominant hinges on patients’ health rationality. Rationality refers to the extent to which individuals follow an explicit, systematic, and analy tical approach when making decisions [93]. Individuals with a high sense of rationality are likely to make rational decisions, based on their cognitive evaluations of all the alternatives [94]. Conversely, individuals possessing a low sense of rationality are likely to rely more on spontaneous or intuitive approaches, rather than a cognitive approach [94]. The di<sup>f</sup>erent reliance between these two groups can result in a di<sup>f</sup>erent outcome of the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer. In our study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne health rationality as the extent to which individuals make rational decisions in relation to their health and health management.

Patients with chronic illnesses tend to make less rational health decisions in their daily lives [95]. As a result of enduring the adverse e<sup>f</sup>ects of chronic illnesses on their psychological state, these patients often have biases toward their present and near future, which means they cannot make rational decisions about the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts related to their health conditions [95]. In addition, they are emotionally dependent on other people and require additional care to manage their health, which may be typically unnecessary [95]. Furthermore, their emotional distress as a result of their chronic illnesses in<sup>fl</sup>uences their cognitive evaluations by producing more cost or risk perceptions in their decision-making [96]. Thus, these patients are more likely to make less rational health-related decisions and to possess a low sense of health rationality compared with the general population.

We propose that health rationality is an important trait of patients with chronic diseases that di<sup>f</sup>erentiates their tendency to follow cognitive or misattribution routines. This is because health rationality guides them to put weight on cognition or a<sup>f</sup>ect when making evaluations, which di<sup>f</sup>erentiates the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer process—that is, weighing to combine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the cognitive and misattribution routines. Therefore, we anticipate that health rationality will moderate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the two routines in these patients’ a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer when evaluating MMSs. Patients with low health rationality are likely to rely more on the misattribution routine, and are thus anticipated to generate similar a<sup>f</sup>ect toward the service based on their a<sup>f</sup>ect toward the components, leading to a less rational evaluation of the MMS. Conversely, patients with high health rationality bypass their a<sup>f</sup>ect toward MMS or MMS components in their cognitive process, resulting in a di<sup>f</sup>erent outcome of the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Based on the theoretical foundation discussed above, we developed a research model to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of emotional attachment to the two MMS components (device and feedback) on actual usage. Fig. 1 depicts our research model.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the e<sup>f</sup>ect of emotional attachment on MMS usage. Emotional attachment re<sup>fl</sup>ects an a<sup>f</sup>ective bond between patients and the MMS [47]. In general, a user who possesses an emotional bond with a service often perceives the interactions with the service as unique and irreplaceable [97]. The stronger the user s attachment to the service, the more likely the user will commit to maintaining interactions with it [47]. In our context, when patients with chronic illnesses experience body-function restrictions and psychological stresses caused by their chronic illnesses, they are likely to seek physical or psychological support from an object—in our case, the MMS. Given that the MMS is designed to provide advice on health problems and meet emotional needs [47], these patients attach emotions to it. This motivates them to frequently and intensively use the MMS to maintain their interactions with it. We anticipate that emotional attachment to the MMS will lead to active MMS usage, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by high frequency and high intensity. We hypothesize:

H1. Emotional attachment positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the MMS usage of patients with chronic illnesses

We then discuss the e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived value of MMSs on emotional attachment. According to rational choice theory, perceived value cap tures the extent of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (compared with costs incurred) that patients with chronic illnesses receive by using the MMS to manage their chronic conditions [81]. A high-value perception means that these patients believe their interactions with the MMS are consistent with their expectations and meet their basic medical needs in relation to their chronic illnesses. As a result, they develop a strong a<sup>f</sup>ective bond with the service [47,98]. Conversely, a low perceived value indicates that patients experience more costs than bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the MMS and, accordingly, may conclude that their interactions with it are less consistent with their expectations and can barely meet their healthcare needs. Therefore, they will develop a weak attachment to the MMS. Taken together, we hypothesize that a patient’s value perception of using the MMS should in<sup>fl</sup>uence their emotional attachment to it. We hypothesize:

H2. Perceived value of the MMS increases the emotional attachment of patients with chronic illnesses to the MMS

In addition to perceived value, we examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of device satisfaction and feedback satisfaction on the emotional attachment of patients with chronic illnesses. Patients emotional attachment to MMSs develops from their interactions with the MMS. As stated in section 2.1, we conceptualized MMS according to two components: the device itself and the personalized feedback it provides. Patients with chronic illnesses interact with MMS devices, as well as receiving personalized health feedback from the MMS, and they form their satisfaction with these components through retrospective evaluations. Emotional attachment is a more forward-moving evaluation that captures the strength of the relationship between patients and MMSs [99]. Retro spective evaluation provides a psychological basis for the forwardmoving evaluation—that is, emotional attachment [47]. Being satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with a device and with personalized health feedback implies that patients have formed positive a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations regarding the outcomes of their interactions with the two components—device and feedback—and have thereby generated positive feelings, such as pleasure, enjoyment, and fondness. Given that the device and personalized feedback are the two key MMS components, they are connected and share similarities; thus, it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult for patients to distinguish between the sources of their positive feelings [79].

According to a<sup>f</sup>ective primacy, patients’ a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to in dividual components may spill over into their evaluation of the whole service, as their interactions with the service are conducted via their use of the components [22]. Thus, their positive feelings toward service components are misattributed to the MMS to a certain extent [79]. These positive feelings can alleviate patients’ negative feelings derived from the functional limitations and psychological stresses caused by their chronic illnesses, and motivate patients to maintain and improve their interactions with the MMS [56]. In sum, we hypothesize that device satisfaction and feedback satisfaction will increase patients emotional attachment to MMSs:

H3a. Device satisfaction increases patients’ emotional attachment to MMSs.

H3b. Feedback satisfaction increases patients’ emotional attachment to MMSs.

We postulate that the indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects of patients’ satisfaction with the device occur through a cognitive process that is mediated by perceived value. This value represents a cognitive evaluation of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of using the MMS [81]. Patients form satisfaction with individual components, and the e<sup>f</sup>ects of this component satisfaction on the overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation of MMSs have been empirically proven in the literature on the IS success model [90,100]. Device satisfaction indicates the degree of positive feelings toward the device, and, when patients experience greater satisfaction, they consider using the MMS to be less costly [100]. Feedback satisfaction indicates the degree of positive feelings regarding the outcomes of information received from the MMS. When patients feel more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with this personalized feedback, they will <sup>fi</sup>nd the service bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for their health concerns [100]. Therefore, we hypothesize that device satisfaction and feedback satisfaction lead to a positive value perception of MMSs:

## H4a. Device satisfaction increases the perceived value of MMSs

## H4b. Feedback satisfaction increases the perceived value of MMSs

We examine the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of health rationality on the relationship between MMS components and perceived value through the cognitive routine. Health rationality captures the extent to which patients make rational cognition-based decisions; patients with high health rationality often rely on their perceptions of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs when making health decisions [94]. Thus, when forming a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to an MMS based on interactions with individual MMS components, patients are more likely to use their a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to the components to assess their cognitive evaluation of MMS usage in the initial stage, and to subsequently form their a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations of MMS usage—that is, their emotional attachment. We hypothesize that both device satisfaction and feedback satisfaction exert a stronger positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on perceived value for patients with high health rationality than for patients with low health rationality:

H5. Health rationality moderates the positive e<sup>f</sup>ects of (a) device satisfaction and (b) feedback satisfaction on perceived value, such that the e<sup>f</sup>ects are stronger for patients with high health rationality than for patients with low health rationality.

Finally, we examine the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of health rationality on the relationship between MMS components and perceived value through a misattribution routine. Patients with low health rationality rely more on spontaneous or intuitive approaches to making health decisions [94]. Thus, when forming a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to using the MMS, they are more likely to rely spontaneously on their pre-existing a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations of the service and to directly misattribute their previous evaluations of individual MMS components to the entire service, without making cognitive e<sup>f</sup>orts [79]. Therefore, patients with low health rationality are likely to rely on their a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations of MMS components to form an emotional attachment to the MMS. We hypothesize:

H6. Health rationality moderates the positive e<sup>f</sup>ects of (a) device satisfaction and (b) feedback satisfaction on emotional attachment, such that the e<sup>f</sup>ects are weaker for patients with high health rationality than for patients with low health rationality.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Study context and data collection

For our data collection, we collaborated with a major hospital in Beijing that launched an mHealth service for patients with chronic illnesses in 2016. Before launching this health-awareness campaign, the hospital invited 500 patients with chronic illnesses to pilot-test the service. Our experiment was based on the results of this test. Patients who agreed to subscribe to the healthcare service could use their mobile devices to monitor their health indexes, such as exercise, blood pressure, and blood glucose readings, and upload the data for advice by health professionals through a mobile app. Resident doctors at the Beijing hospital could retrieve the patients’ indexes, analyze them, and, whenever necessary, give patients health recommendations and warn ings. To start the test, the patients were requested to download the hospital’s app to their mobile phone and then subscribe to the mobile monitoring device with the app to use the services. After they began using it, their continuance was voluntary.

Through the Beijing hospital, we disseminated a survey to these patients for responses. The survey was administered about one month after the patients started to use this MMS. Before dispatching the survey, we randomly selected and interviewed seven participants to evaluate whether they had a valid understanding of their use of the MMS, and whether they would seriously consider using the service continuously. The interview results indicated that six of the seven users were aware of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of using the service and they had decided to continue it, while the remaining one user terminated the service after the initial adoption. We observed some variations in their usage patterns and, on this basis, decided to disseminate our survey to investigate what caused this di<sup>f</sup>erence in service usage. We sent a survey questionnaire to 351 (out of 500) patients who had started using the service through the mHealth app. The remaining 149 participants opted out for further participation in this study. Our survey questionnaire included three sections: (1) an introduction to the services we were studying, (2) measures of the theoretical constructs in the model, and (3) questions about the participants demographic characteristics.

We referred to the literature to develop the construct measures. The details of the measurement items, together with their references, are presented in Appendix A.<sup>1</sup> We <sup>fi</sup>rst developed the English version of the survey and then translated it into Chinese with three researchers working independently. The measurement items of the constructs—that is, device satisfaction, feedback satisfaction, perceived value, emotional attachment, and health rationality—were adopted from the previous literature [47,81,90,101]. These constructs can be measured as <sup>fi</sup>rstorder re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs [90,101] or second-order formative constructs [56]. Given a restriction imposed by the Beijing hospital in relation to the number of questions in the survey, we simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed the instrument and measured all constructs as <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs.

In the data analysis, we included patient characteristics as control variables because the patients illnesses would cause negative emotions regarding their health conditions, which could further induce emotional attachment to using the MMS. Among the demographic characteristics, we incorporated patients’ age, gender, and educational level in the model, as the literature shows that these characteristics are more relevant in user–mHealth interaction than others [102]. Furthermore, because our research participants were patients with chronic diseases, the length of the chronic experience was also controlled to rule out possible di<sup>f</sup>erences.

To reduce common method bias, we measured MMS usage by par ticipants’ usage experiences two months after their adoption (i.e., one month after the survey) and extracted behavioral data from the system log. Prior research mentions three usage dimensions: duration, frequency, and intensity [15]. In our context, duration referred to how long the individuals used the service over a given period; frequency referred to how many times individuals had used the MMS over the previous month; and intensity indicated how many features of the MMS they had used over the previous month. Duration was less appropriate in our study because once the patients had measured their indexes, the indexes were automatically uploaded to the system and the data collec tion/monitoring step was short and instantaneous. Therefore, we used frequency and intensity to operationalize MMS usage.

A total of 262 (out of 351) MMS users completed the survey (with a 74.9 % response rate), and all had at least one type of chronic illness. We collected 228 valid returns from the respondents, where 34 were excluded because of incomplete responses. Of the 228 valid responses, 51.8 % were from males; 18.9 % of the respondents were in their 30 s, 51.3 % in their 40 s, and 29.8 % in their 50 s. The majority had obtained a college degree (93.4 %), while 29.8 % had obtained a master’s degree or higher. We compared the demographic characteristics of respondents and non-respondents with regard to age, education, gender, and usage experience, using a series of t-tests. We found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences between the two groups, thereby indicating a minimal threat from non-response bias.

## 4.2. Measurement model

The measurement model was <sup>fi</sup>rst tested using Smart PLS (version 3.2) to ensure the validity and reliability of the measurement items. The results are presented in Tables 1 and 2. The composite reliability of each construct was above 0.870—signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than the threshold of 0.700—while the average variances extracted (AVEs) were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater than 0.500, thereby indicating good construct reliability [103]. Each construct had item loadings higher than 0.700, thereby suggesting convergent validity [104]. All item loadings of each construct were much greater than the cross-loadings on other constructs, and the correlations between any two constructs were smaller than the square root of their AVEs, thus indicating discriminant validity [104].

Given that the correlations of some constructs were relatively high, we examined possible multicollinearity issues. The results indicate that the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIF) of the constructs were smaller than 6.0—lower than the recommended cuto<sup>f</sup> of 10.0 [105]. Furthermore, some constructs, such as feedback satisfaction, device satisfaction, and perceived value, were patients’ subjective evaluations and they may have relatively high cross-loadings. This situation has also been found in the IS success models [90,100]. Nonetheless, multicollinearity (with VIF = 5.9) was not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concern in the current study because the VIF was lower than the cuto<sup>f</sup> of 10.0, as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Cohen et al. [105].

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Key findings

Our study investigated how patients with chronic illnesses develop emotional attachment to MMSs based on their a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to two MMS components: device and personalized feedback. We adopted an a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer lens to capture the transfer of a<sup>f</sup>ect from each of these components to the entire MMS through cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective responses. We theorized the moderation of health rationality on the above relationships and set the boundary condition for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the two transfer routines. Table 4 summarizes the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the hypotheses testing.

Our study yields three key <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, patients form emotional attachment to the overall MMS from their interactions with the device and personalized feedback, and emotional attachment can induce their active usage of such services. When satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the device and per sonalized feedback, patients obtain physical or psychological support and develop an emotional bond with the service. This emotion-laden bond further nurtures their commitment to using the service. The literature reveals that emotional attachment promotes commitment [54,57] and behavioral intentions [58–60]. This paper adds to the lit erature by empirically showing that emotional attachment contributes to service usage.

Second, patients’ satisfaction with service components directly and indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uences their overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation of using the MMS. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that device satisfaction and feedback satisfaction can directly and indirectly (i.e., through perceived value) transfer users’ emotional attachment to MMS usage. When they are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the device or personalized feedback, they are more likely to make a positive a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation of their service usage through emotional attachment. In line with the IS success model, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that component satisfaction contributes to service usage through an overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation. The direct and indirect routines proposed in this study provide a clear explanation of how the a<sup>f</sup>ect from service components constitutes an overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings enable us to understand the aggregation e<sup>f</sup>ect of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer: the transfer process can occur through both the cognitive and misattribution routines, and, in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts, users can have di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of reliance on the two routines, which can induce di<sup>f</sup>erent behavioral outcomes.

Finally, patients reliance on service component a<sup>f</sup>ective evalua tions in the course of making an overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation of using a service is contingent on their decision rationality. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that health rationality positively moderates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of device sa tisfaction and feedback satisfaction on perceived value. However, the proposed negative moderation of health rationality on the direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of device satisfaction and feedback satisfaction on emotional support was not supported. The non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results of H6a and H6b indicate that, whether making a rational or less rational decision, there is no di<sup>f</sup>erence in a user’s reliance on a misattribution routine to transfer the learned a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations to the new a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation. One possible reason for this is that the misattribution routine in an a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer is attributable to one’s lack of ability to distinguish the cause of the a<sup>f</sup>ect [79]. In this situation, regardless of a rational or irrational decision, individuals misattribute their a<sup>f</sup>ect when evaluating a service. The moderation of health rationality can help explain the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer literature to a certain extent. If users have a high level of rationality, they can generate a di<sup>f</sup>erent a<sup>f</sup>ect from a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer because they do not rely to any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant degree on misattributing the previous a<sup>f</sup>ect, but on a cognitive evaluation of using the service. Simply put, in some cases, even when users are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the service components, high rationality can also induce a low emotional attachment if they cannot perceive much cognitive value from using the service.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

We contribute to the literature in several ways. First, our study re veals the strongly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships between a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to components and overall a<sup>f</sup>ective responses. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that patients’ a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations of service components can directly and indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uence their overall a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations of MMSs, which further promotes patients’ MMS use. We provide a novel approach for investigating a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations regarding MMSs. The technology-based design of emerging services makes human interactions with such services more complicated, prompting the need for further research on human‒service interaction from the component design view [110]. Therefore, from the a<sup>f</sup>ect perspective, it becomes essential to examine users’ a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to employing such services according to their interactions with core service components. In doing this, our study provides insights into the underlying mechanisms of how a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to service components can in<sup>fl</sup>uence a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to the entire service. It also enriches the current litera ture on human‒service interaction (such as Hovorka and Germonprez [110] and Immonen et al. [111]) by proposing a brand-new a<sup>f</sup>ect perspective that predicts the a<sup>f</sup>ective response to the service based on the service components.

Second, our study contributes to the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer literature by revealing the underlying mechanisms of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer and proposing a plausible explanation for the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings thus far. Although a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer has been applied to examine applications in various contexts, there is no consensus about the underlying mechanisms of how a<sup>f</sup>ect is transferred, and there are mixed outcomes regarding the transfer process [23,24]. Furthermore, scant research has discussed a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer in the human‒service interaction literature [112]. Our study explored the transfer process in human‒service interaction from two perspectives of cognitive routine and misattribution routine, proposing that their combined e<sup>f</sup>ects are contingent on decision rationality. Faced with diverse levels of decision rationality, patients rely on their cognitive processes to a di<sup>f</sup>erent extent during a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer, which can lead to various outcomes of the transfer process. Accordingly, this study adds to understanding the underlying mechanisms of how a<sup>f</sup>ect is transferred explicitly in the human‒service interaction literature, and provides a plausible explanation for the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer literature.

Third, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide new insights into IS usage in the in novation di<sup>f</sup>usion literature. Although system usage has been an important research topic in recent decades, most of the literature has adopted a cognitive perspective by examining the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation between expectations and outcomes in predicting post-adoption beha vioral intentions [29,33,39], and does not provide a holistic view from the a<sup>f</sup>ect perspective. By contrast, our study provides IS usage research with a new perspective: we investigated the role of emotional attachment in predicting actual usage in the mHealth context. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed emotional attachment as an important factor in patients’ usage of MMSs and further explored its antecedents and ef fects. Consistent with previous propositions in the a<sup>f</sup>ect literature [45], our <sup>fi</sup>ndings not only demonstrate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role of emotional attachment in service di<sup>f</sup>usion, especially in predicting actual usage behavior at the post-adoption stage, but also extend knowledge to the IS usage literature by identifying the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role of a<sup>f</sup>ective factors in determining user behavior.

Fourth, by exploring the contingency role of health rationality, our study contributes to the literature on human decision-making processes. Prior research on rational choice theory argues that this theory cannot be generalized when emotions, biases, and errors exist, and further frameworks have been called for to “make connections with research on emotions and cognition” [91]. The literature has empirically shown that even with biases and errors, individuals can still make rational choices, which extends the boundary of rational choice theory [113,114]. However, scant empirical research has been conducted to explore whether the theory remains valid in the presence of emotions. We propose that a<sup>f</sup>ect can be transferred through a cognitive routine (i.e., manifested as a rational decision-making process) and patients reliance on this routine is contingent on their health rationality. Our study not only conceptualizes health rationality as a bridge between a<sup>f</sup>ective responses and the cognition process, but also extends our knowledge of decision-making styles in the presence of both cognition and emotions, which contributes to rational choice theory by extending it to include the situations in which emotions are involved.

Finally, by investigating the moderation of a decision characteristic that patients with chronic illnesses possess—health rationality—on the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer process, we provide insights into how actual MMS users (i.e., patients with chronic illnesses) respond to such services. Patients with chronic illnesses are a growing population, yet their health concerns have been largely neglected in the previous IS literature. They have unique yet more complicated a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to using health services because of the long-term adverse e<sup>f</sup>ects caused by their chronic illness. By testing this characteristic, our study responds to the call by Liang et al. [115], who encouraged a focus on unique groups in IS research and remarked that “we need future research to understand how people with other types of disability are likely to face di<sup>f</sup>erent challenges and have di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics that in<sup>fl</sup>uence their use.” Our study is not only one of the earliest studies explaining how patients with chronic illnesses respond a<sup>f</sup>ectively to using health services by revealing their usage behavior to <sup>fi</sup>ll the void in the IS literature, but also indicates how these patients’ long-term battle with their illnesses shapes their health decision-making.

## 5.3. Practical implications

The group of patients involved in this study is important. In the US, as of 2012, about half of all adults (around 117 million people) were living with one or more chronic health conditions, and one in four adults were living with two or more chronic health conditions [116]. In China, chronic illnesses kill three million people annually [117]. Given this large number of patients, it is infeasible for health professionals to provide face-to-face health services to every patient [118]. Furthermore, since patients with chronic illnesses often play an active role in their health decision-making [119], MMSs can empower them to self manage their health [120].

mHealth leverages the core values of mobile information and communication technologies—mobility, ubiquity, and portability. They enable service providers (such as hospitals and health professionals) to deliver timely and personalized health services to patients located at a remote distance [37]. They adopt a revolutionary approach to sup porting patients’ self-health management and medical decision-making [26,27]. mHealth has become progressively popular over the last decade, to the extent that, in 2011, more than 80 % of WHO member states had mHealth initiatives [121]. Our study highlights several important implications of MMSs for providers, patients with chronic illnesses, and health professionals.

First, service providers must evaluate the design of their core service components. Patients’ satisfaction perceptions of these service compo nents encourage them to form positive evaluations of MMS usage and to employ the services more frequently. By contrast, unfavorable perceptions may deter active usage. Thus, service providers are encouraged to devote attention to every core service component, such as the device design and personalized feedback for patients. Moreover, because emotional attachment to using a service can in<sup>fl</sup>uence active usage, service providers are advised to adopt measures that can increase the emotional bond between users and their services. Since that patients with chronic illnesses require daily monitoring of health conditions to ensure that their conditions are stable, there are many ways for providers to strengthen patients’ emotional bonds. For instance, providers can motivate users to treat the MMS as an essential part of their daily routine, especially in the case of patients with chronic illnesses, or they can induce patients’ relatives to make the service subscriptions for them.

Second, service users (i.e., patients with chronic illnesses, in our case) must be aware of the misattribution process in their service evaluation, given that they sometimes misattribute their evaluations of service components to the service itself, which may produce less rational decisions. For instance, these users may be favorably in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a service with a well-designed portal, and fail to understand whether such a service can fully meet their needs. Therefore, they should notice their misattributions and focus more on whether they can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from using health services to make more rational health decisions. To achieve this, users are advised to have a clear understanding of the service and its di<sup>f</sup>erent components, distinguish the origins for a<sup>f</sup>ect, and then carefully evaluate the service from a cognitive perspective. To enable MMSs to generate the most bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for patients with chronic illnesses, health professionals should provide guidelines to these patients to help them objectively assess MMS performance, and regularly send them surveys to understand which of their healthcare needs can (or cannot) be addressed by MMSs.

Third, our study also provides suggestions to doctors and nurses. Our survey revealed that patients exhibit various levels of health rationality when making health decisions, and health rationality may in<sup>fl</sup>uence their responses to health services and treatments. To increase the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of health services and treatments, health professionals should observe patients’ health rationality based on their interactions with patients, such as whether patients rely on analytical approaches to make health decisions. If patients have a high sense of health rationality, health professionals can emphasize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the services and treatments to increase patient compliance. Otherwise, health professionals can increase patients’ positive a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations of the services and treatments or arouse their health anxiety about non-compliance.

## 5.4. Limitations and directions for future research

Our study is not without limitations. First, the focus on actual usage behavior in the research design and measure of MMS usage from a second data source limited explanations regarding actual use, especially frequency of use. Future studies shall incorporate other a<sup>f</sup>ective and cognitive factors to explain actual usage behavior better. Second, this work focused on a special group—patients with chronic illnesses—to test a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer from service components to the MMS. There may be concerns about the generalization of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Therefore, future studies shall test the transfer process in other populations in di<sup>f</sup>erent research contexts. Third, to measure the service components, only two major components (device and feedback) were included in the model. Other components—such as the software supporting the service, health professional engagement, and the mobile phone model used—might also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of emotional attachment and require further investigation. Finally, to explain the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer, our study drew on health rationality as the leverage for the combined e<sup>f</sup>ects of cognitive and misattribution routines. Future studies shall consider other contingency factors, such as emotion regulation capability and temporary negative emotion.

## 6. Conclusion

Through their rising popularity, MMSs have captured a large share of the mHealth market and acquired an increasing user population. However, the reality is that patients do not make active usage of these services. Thus, our study aimed to explore MMS usage through the theoretical lens of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer. We developed and tested six hypotheses with tracked behavioral data and administered a survey distributed among MMS users who were patients with chronic illnesses. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings illustrate that patients’ emotional attachment to using the MMS positively promotes active usage, while their device and feedback satisfaction can be directly and indirectly transferred to emotional at tachment. Moreover, the a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer process is contingent on patients’ decision rationality. Our study contributes knowledge to understand the mechanism by which a<sup>f</sup>ective response is developed from learned a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations. It also explored the underlying mechan isms of a<sup>f</sup>ect transfer with insights generated regarding how patients with chronic illnesses a<sup>f</sup>ectively respond to MMS usage. Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the design of service components and a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations can promote more active service usage, and prove useful fo service designers in terms of how best to promote MMS usage, parti cularly for patients with chronic illnesses.
