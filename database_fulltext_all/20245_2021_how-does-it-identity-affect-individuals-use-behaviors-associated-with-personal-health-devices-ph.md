# How does IT identity a<sup>f</sup>ect individuals’ use behaviors associated with personal health devices (PHDs)? An empirical study

Pouyan Esmaeilzadeh

Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, College of Business, Florida International University (FIU), Miami, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT identity Personal Health Devices (PHDs) IT characteristics IT experience Use behaviors Perceived health status

## A B S T R A C T

This study investigates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of information technology (IT) identity on the use of personal health devices (PHDs). We conducted an online survey of individuals who used a PHD to achieve a health-related goal. The results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that IT identity is a multidimensional construct with three re<sup>fl</sup>ective dimensions. The results exhibit that the identity veri<sup>fi</sup>cation process (IT characterizes through IT experience) fosters IT identity, which, in turn, encourages individuals to engage in bene<sup>fi</sup>cial post-adoption behaviors such as feature use and enhanced use. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also reveal that perceived health status moderates the relationship between IT identity and positive use behaviors

## 1. Introduction

mHealth (mobile health) has tremendously changed how people involve in performing di<sup>f</sup>erent roles in their social relations [1]. Technology has created new expectations and changed how people perceive themselves, their behaviors, and their pro<sup>fi</sup>le within the community with which they are a<sup>fi</sup>liated [2]. Information technology (IT) is intertwined with social structures of relationships with others as people expect on-demand and timely information. For instance, telemedicine alters face-to-face doctor/patient encounters, and it is expected to be less expensive than conventional in-person examinations. Using telemedicine and mHealth, medical professionals consult a pa tient remotely, interpret the necessary data across distances, and monitor and supervise patients distantly [3]. Patient portals and med ical health devices can also improve communication between patients and their health providers. These technological advances in the healthcare industry, can decrease the frequency of patient visits and make healthcare providers reachable outside work settings, as healthcare services are more accessible to patients through online consulta tion, telemonitoring, and patient self-management tools [4].

In recent years, the use of personal health devices (PHDs) as plat forms for monitoring and controlling health conditions has grown signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Many manufacturers (such as Samsung, Jawbone, and Fitbit) have developed smartwatches and smart bracelets to help individuals ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their health-related goals. A key reason for this growth is that people are increasingly relying on the internet and wearable smart devices to <sup>fi</sup>nd answers to their medical issues by engaging in selfmonitoring and self-management programs [1]. These devices are designed to help users access their full health report and live a healthier life. The adoption and use of patient self-management tools are consistent with the notion of patient-centered e-health applications, which revolves around patients as pivotal actors in the healthcare ecosystem [5]. However, recent studies highlight that many users discontinue using PHDs after only a few months [6]. Individuals show less interest in using PHDs actively because many PHDs cannot engage the majority of current users [7]. Nevertheless, little is known about what factors may motivate users to eagerly use such electronic devices and explore more features and functions for additional health-related tasks. Thus, further research is required to investigate individuals’ beliefs and behaviors during the postadoption of PHDs to uncover users’ requirements and preferences for e-health services and to pose suggestions for the construction of useful smart devices, interactive mHealth apps, and e<sup>fi</sup>cient e-health systems.

In many contexts (such as healthcare services), it is naive to believe that day-to-day activities can be adequately performed without IT. Using an IT helps individuals feel competent and accepted in social networks of relationships in which their roles are de<sup>fi</sup>ned based on cultural expectations and norms [8]. For instance, PHDs enable individuals to monitor their health status, manage chronic diseases, control <sup>fi</sup>tness and wellness, and view personal health information in real-time [9]. In this study, PHDs are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as smart devices mainly used for monitoring and controlling health status, not for providing diagnosis and medical treatment remotely [9]. People may continuously engage with their PHDs to monitor their health status or check their healthcare information [10]. Thus, the use of PHDs is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to people as this IT object provides additional resources in managing personal healthcare, which is highly important to them. The use of these devices can be saliently related to who people think they are, as PHDs provide a wide range of applications across a broad range of technology networks and social contexts. These smart devices can be integrated into an individual’s sense of self as one may spend a considerable portion of his/her time interacting with them as a repeated behavior. This fact is consistent with Reychav, Beeri, Balapour, Raban, Sabherwal and Azuri [11] that repeated behaviors can directly contribute to identity construction if they lead to emotion, dependence, and a<sup>fi</sup>liation.

The use of IT can expand the actual self as well as the meanings and perceptions attached to the self [12–14]. By incorporating PHDs into all aspects of health-related activities, achieving personal goals (such as walking distance, steps, nutrition, heart rate, and so on) will be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly connected to the PHDs [15]. Consistent with Zhang [16], the capacity of an IT will increase the personal and social resources of individuals in achieving their goals. Digital devices represent an opportunity to expand the self by providing valuable resources and capabilities that help users achieve self-management goals. As individuals continuously interact with features of PHDs, the devices o<sup>f</sup>er selfcon<sup>fi</sup>rming feedback to users. Recent studies manifest individuals’ attachment, enthusiasm, and relationships with an IT by IT identity construct, which is the extent to which an individual views use of an IT as integral to his or her sense of self” [17]. In particular, a robust IT identity represents self-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (use of an IT is integral to being who one is), while a weak IT identity represents self-dis-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (use of an IT is irrelevant to being who one is).

As social networks in the healthcare industry and technology are closely related, IT identity can explain this interdependence and better describe the implications of technology and digital devices for communities and patients. Exploring the association between identity and IT may be essential to predict individuals’ meanings, expectations, and behaviors regarding their everyday use of PHDs in social contexts. Consistent with the current literature, there are two categories of research on IT identity. The <sup>fi</sup>rst category considers IT and identity as two di<sup>f</sup>erent phenomena with no connections [18]. The other line of thoughts demonstrates the importance of identity by synthesizing technology and its social structure to highlight the mutual relationship between IT and social networks [17].

Individuals may interact with many technologies on a daily basis, but may consider some IT devices as an inherent part of themselves, which can in<sup>fl</sup>uence their behavioral choices [19]. A digital health device (such as a wearable smart device or an intelligent bracelet) is a consumer IT that can become part of people's identities due to its everyday use for self-monitoring and self-management purposes [20]. It can be argued that because PHDs are regularly used by individuals to help them monitor their health status, they may need to carry these devices with them to keep healthy. Thus, individuals may consider PHDs as an integral part of the self and identify themselves in relation to IT. This IT object is more suitable for the IT identity concept due to its features, which make it multiuse, portable, and networked IT. IT identity is the set of meanings that people attach to themselves about IT as a product of personal histories of interacting with IT, as well as a force that shapes their thinking and guides their IT use behaviors. According to Carter and Grover [17], individuals with strong IT identities would be more eager to use IT to resolve various types of problems. Users’ continuing interactions with PHDs will contribute to their dependency on such technology and also create a feeling of empowerment, emotion, and competency. This sense of empowerment and excitement may also encourage users to <sup>fi</sup>nd new situations to apply the PHD in daily roles they maintain. Therefore, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between IT identity and bene<sup>fi</sup>cial use behavior could propose that individuals with a strong IT identity may think of using a PHD in dif ferent situations to approach various types of health-related issues. For instance, in the PHD context, an IT identity holder may think of using the body temperature function, high heart rate noti<sup>fi</sup>cations, or insulin delivery feature to monitor his health status rather than making an appointment with a physician. Another example would be a female user with a strong IT identity who uses the menstruation feature on her PHD to control her menstrual cycle. By doing so, she will enact her identity as a competent woman who is able to leverage IT to keep records of her health conditions. In this case, the more she associates the use of a PHD with the enactment of a valued identity, the stronger the IT identity related to her device will be [17].

This study is mainly built upon identity theories and studies focusing on structural symbolic interactionism, which articulates how individual identities are framed [17,21]. This work is designed to investigate why people are attached to their digital devices by exploring the IT identity concept and its implications in using PHDs to monitor, control, and track health data. Capturing the interactions between PHDs and their social context can contribute to the literature by explaining and predicting individual thoughts, expectations, and postadoption behaviors within this context. Previous studies have not articulated the degree to which the use of PHDs may be considered as an integral part of a user’s sense of self and whether the IT identity helps individuals express their self-concepts in interactions with PHDs. This study is di<sup>f</sup>erent from prior use behavior models by focusing on the IT identity construct, which describes the extent to which an IT can contribute to an individual s self-concept and self-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [17] to better explain the nature of technology use behaviors in today’s digital healthcare services. Interactions with IT are inevitable parts of individuals’ relationships with the world. As personal and social roles are inseparable from individuals interactions with IT, IT identity would be a better manifestation to represent how much IT is incorporated and infused into the self and all aspects of daily life. In spite of the importance of IT identity, the nature of this phenomenon has not been thoroughly conceptualized, clearly measured, or fully delineated in this context. Previous studies mainly investigate the use of PHDs based on widely-accepted adoption variables (e.g., ease of use, usefulness, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and trust), and relatively little attention has been given to IT identity construct. Moreover, we believe that situational factors (such as design factors, dynamic feature sets, and privacy terms and conditions) can play an essential role in describing postadoption adoption behaviors in the PHD context. In brief, this study aims to address the following research questions:

1 How does IT identity in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ postadoption use behaviors in relation to PHDs (i.e., feature use and enhanced use)?

2 What are the antecedents of IT identity in the context of PHDs?

3 How do situational factors (i.e., IT design, IT dynamism, and privacy policy) in<sup>fl</sup>uence PHD use behaviors?

In this research, we follow the same arguments used by Carter and Grover [17] to de<sup>fi</sup>ne IT. By IT, we mean any mobile health devices that a person [22] consciously engages with to monitor his/her health status or check his/her health care information. PHDs are a unit of technology (both hardware and applications) with characteristics attributed to both software and hardware. For example, general PHDs’ characteristics such as <sup>fl</sup>exibly, mobility, and functionality are associated with the unit of technology, not the software or hardware separately. Recently, the use of smart devices and wearables as a means to monitor and control health status has grown signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. The mobile health devices can be accessible to that person across time and space and may connect him/ her to others (physicians, healthcare organizations, or individuals) in his/her social structures.

Authors reviewed major healthcare journals and digital device studies in six electronic research databases (Science Direct, PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Academic Search Premier, and Google Scholar). To the best of our knowledge, studies on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity in the context of PHDs have not included IT identity as an antecedent of use behaviors in their models. Current literature did not describe PHD characteristics and how these features and enhanced use di<sup>f</sup>er in this context. Moreover, they did not address why PHD characteristics form the experience, which leads to IT identity. This research is an attempt to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap by achieving the following four goals. The <sup>fi</sup>rst goal is to validate the possibility of conceptualizing IT identity as a multidimensional construct (which is suggested by Carter [21]) in the context of PHDs. The second goal is to explore the impact that IT identity may have on individuals’ postadoption use behaviors (i.e., feature use and enhanced use). The third goal is to identify the antecedents of IT identity in relation to PHDs. The last goal is to investigate whether perceived health status moderates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity on use behaviors.

From a theoretical perspective, this research extends the identity theories by incorporating IT identity construct, its antecedents, and consequences into previous models and applies the extended model to the context of digital health devices. The results of this study contribute to the literature primarily in the areas of IT identity, mHealth, health informatics, user participation, self-monitoring, and self-management tools. In particular, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide theoretical implications based on the following three dimensions: IT identity importance, the contents and features that will lead to IT identity veri<sup>fi</sup>cation, and possible results of IT identity formation in the context of using PHDs. Prior research on IT identity has not explicitly distinguished health-related content from other types of personal information [23]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent in dustries have diverse levels of compliance due to the various degrees of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation requirements and the di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of information sensitivity [24]. Therefore, the inherent sensitivity of personal health information may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the process of IT identity enactment and, in turn, may change individuals’ use behaviors. To the best of our knowledge, no published research study has developed a comprehensive model, including IT identity, its antecedents, and consequences in the context of digital health devices. Therefore, in the current study, we attempt to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap by focusing speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on the IT identity in relation to PHDs as a consumer IT, which is mostly used on a regular basis.

From a practical standpoint, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings could o<sup>f</sup>er bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for health device providers and healthcare organizations that adopt selfmanagement tools and mHealth apps to participate in patient-centered health models. The results of this study can be useful for PHD devel opers and designers to better identify what functionalities and features need to be added to PHDs to help users enact their IT identity and expand themselves through feature use and enhanced use behaviors. Understanding the dimensions and determinants of IT identity can help PHD vendors and developers address situational factors, improve the enactment of IT identity, and promote the active participation of IT identity holders in using a wide range of features in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations. This e<sup>f</sup>ective participation can ultimately lead to the growth and success of those smart devices compared to their competitors.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Definition of identity

Identity is generally the concept that de<sup>fi</sup>nes who a person is in relation to some phenomena, groups, objects, and social behaviors [25]. Material objects, personal characteristics, or group norms can be an integral part of identity if individuals use them to identify themselves in communities [26]. Identity has mainly been studied from two per spectives: collective and individual level. At the collective level, social identity is framed based on membership in a social group, the group’s values, and intergroup relations [27]. Social identity theory supports identity development at the collective level [28]. At the individual level, individual identity stems from networks of characteristics, roles, and relationships with other entities that may a<sup>f</sup>ect the person’s thinking, expectations, and behaviors [29]. Thus, there are three subcategories of individual identity: role identity, personal identity, and material identity. Role identity theory explains a set of expectations internalized by individuals about how to be competent in performing their roles [30]. Personal identity encompasses a collection of characteristics, attributes, values, and norms that people choose to hold to distinguish themselves from others [27]. Material identity refers to identities that were built as a result of specialized attention and attachment to material objects surrounding a person such as places or personal possessions (e.g., technology and digital devices) [26].

People actively attempt to change their situations and behaviors to verify their identities [31]. For instance, in the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance, people would be more likely to try to change the situation than to change the meanings contained in the identity. Personal identity can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed when people behave in a way to be recognized as unique and distinguished individuals. According to Carter and Grover [17], material identities can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed during the interaction with a material object when people are able to control over its feature set. A person can develop, verify, and maintain many identities that are organized according to a hierarchy of importance [32]. Based on the self-weighting structure, identity with greater importance is more likely to impact the person’s behaviors [33].

Previous studies use identity theories to demonstrate how identities internalized and enacted by people can change human behavior. However, the majority of studies put more emphasis on role identities [17]. Little is known about antecedents of material identity (particularly in relation to IT) and how this individual-level identity in<sup>fl</sup>uences human behavior. Moreover, prior studies mostly consider material objects as resources that can be used by people to achieve a situation congruent with their expectations and values to verify identity (such as role identity). For instance, how the implementation of a new electronic health record (EHR) system con<sup>fi</sup>rms and evokes healthcare professionals’ role identities [34]. Few studies treat material objects as a symbolic representation of the self [29]. Hence, there is a gap in the literature on what features of technologies make them become an integral part of the self, how individuals identify themselves in relation to IT, and how IT identity a<sup>f</sup>ects users’ behaviors.

## 2.2. Studies related to IT identity

Prior researchers have studied the topic of IT and identity and their relationship based on di<sup>f</sup>erent approaches. Various researchers and theories outline what people can do with IT in generally <sup>fi</sup>ve ways. One group of studies proposes IT as a medium to support individual or group identity formation to describe who people are [35]. According to theories of labeling and self-presentation, IT is mostly considered as a means that facilitates the veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of identity. For instance, individuals can use IT as members of organizations to perform their roles to present who they are to others [36]. Ma and Agarwal [37] indicate that identity-related information promotes knowledge sharing and increases the participation level in the context of online forums. A study shows that employees use an online discussion forum to protect the valued identity and deal with changes perceived as threatening to their identity [38].

The second category of studies considers IT as a determinant to shape individual or group identities. Based on this perspective, individuals’ role identities can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the changes (e.g., mobility) resulting from the implementation of a new IT. For example, Arbore, Soscia and Bagozzi [39] demonstrate mobile TVs as symbolic drivers of the personal self. In the context of the enterprise system (ES) adoption, Alvarez [40] found that the implementation of ES in an or ganization strongly impacts the development of organizational structure and identity. A study in Australia shows that the assimilation process of an enterprise-wide health information system among radiology practitioners signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ects role identity, social interactions, and individual self-perceptions of radiologists [41]. In a healthcare setting, EHR systems give rise to the evolution of physicians’ role identities and social identities [42]. A study on bloggers reports that there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between websites (as artifacts) and role identity veri<sup>fi</sup>cation [43]. In a rural setting, Mosse and Byrne [44] highlight that the implementation of a new computerized health in formation system is connected with the process of collective identity formation within the health sector.

The third category approaches IT as both a medium to support identities as well as a cause to construct identities and impact social positions. For instance, the results of a longitudinal case study show how the group identity of librarians exposed to a new electronic library is created [45]. This study also reveals that librarians attached to technology (as an integral part of their group identity), consider the new digital work environment as a tool and eventually as a trademark. Lamb and Davidson [46] investigate how the use of basic communication technologies and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c technologies with information and communication technology components help scientists shape and maintain scienti<sup>fi</sup>c professional identities.

The fourth category examines the relationship between IT and identity by considering IT as a consequence of identity and individuals self-perceptions. For instance, Kim, Jung and Ball-Rokeach [47] examine the e<sup>f</sup>ect of ethnicity on individuals’ feelings of connectedness to the internet. Walsh, Ke<sup>fi</sup> and Baskerville [48] propose a framework to study the impact of the human factor (i.e., IT user culture) during IT implementation and adoption. In a study about virtual team develop ment, <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that microlevel communication among members of a virtual team and the environmental context can create an integrative identity at the team level, which is directly related to enhanced collaboration [49]. According to Ray, Kim and Morris [50], levels of engagement in online communities can emerge from a sense of communal identity and validation of self-identity. The relationship between individual di<sup>f</sup>erences (especially gender di<sup>f</sup>erences) and social IT identity has been examined in prior studies to highlight its in <sup>fl</sup>uences on the professional development and working lives of women IT professionals [51]. Ollier-Malaterre, Rothbard and Berg [52] indicate that identity negotiation opportunities that help employees construct their professional and personal identities can guide their social interactions and behaviors in online social networks. In the context of online communities, Ren, Harper, Drenner, Terveen, Kiesler, Riedl and Kraut [53] propose that building member attachment by strengthening either group identity or interpersonal bonds causes some online communities to be more successful than others. Based on Liu and Chan [54], the three components of social identity (cognitive, emotional, and evalua tive) strongly a<sup>f</sup>ect perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and barriers of seeking social support in the online healthcare community. Finally, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of self identity on technology acceptance decision in the context of a webbased class support system has been examined, and results show that self-identity has a strong impact on the acceptance in voluntary and experienced situations [55].

Although the mentioned approaches explain who people are in re lation to IT, they generally do not treat IT as part of self-perception and identity. The last category examines the possibility of considering IT as an integral part of the self. For instance, Schwarz and Chin [56] encourage researchers to investigate other psychological notions of IT acceptance. Based on their <sup>fi</sup>ndings, technology can become a salient part of individuals’ identities if they are emotionally attached to it. Nass [57] suggests the role that agenti<sup>fi</sup>cation plays with devices and digital services, which has proven very useful in explaining how interactions with technology engage human-centered perspectives and reactions. For instance, the <sup>fi</sup>lling of roles traditionally <sup>fi</sup>lled by humans can lead to increased emotion, interactivity, and engagement. According to Walsh, White and Young [58], young people’s involvement with their mobile phones is highly related to their self-identity and approval from others. Carter and Grover [17] conceptualize IT identity using theories on social structures and self-concept to describe how people categorize themselves in relation to an IT object. They de<sup>fi</sup>ne IT identity as “the extent to which an individual views use of an IT as integral to his or her sense of self.”

Recent studies examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts. According to Costa Netto and Maçada [59], social media technological features allow individuals to receive constant feedback loops that in<sup>fl</sup>uence their self-concept and re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their identity construction while online. Schmalz, Carter and Lee [60] investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity on IT project behavior and the development of IT. They claim that an employee’s identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the technological outcome of a project can a<sup>f</sup>ect risk behaviors. A study by Weng, Wang and Patel [61] suggests that IT identity forms as a result of a <sup>fi</sup>t between technology characteristics and a person’s value system. Curley and Polites [62] propose that group members’ IT identity and social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the group will be strong predictors of group <sup>fl</sup>ow experiences. Heister and Yuthas [63] explore how technical aspects of blockchain technology in<sup>fl</sup>uence conceptions of the self by explaining that blockchain technology has the potential to change how we understand ourselves and others and, in turn, how we act and interact. Craig, Thatcher and Grover [64] report that the IT Identity threat o<sup>f</sup>ers a parsimonious means to explain and predict IT resistance behaviors. Nash and Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld [65] examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of environmental passion and green IT identity on behavioral intention to practice green IT. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that green IT identity has a direct impact on green IT intentions. Balapour, Reychav, Sabherwal and Azuri [7] suggest that perceived mobile technology identity, perceived related IT experience, and perceived self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence patients intentions to adopt mHealth apps provided by clinics or hospitals. Reychav, Beeri, Balapour, Raban, Sabherwal and Azuri [11] indicate that perceived mobile technology identity directly a<sup>f</sup>ects self-report reliability when mobile apps are used as the point of contact between patients and healthcare providers. Shi, Booth and Simon [66] propose that employees with strong IT identity are apt to project strong security behavioral intentions.

IT can change individuals’ self-perceptions by recognizing their original selves in using capabilities and resources o<sup>f</sup>ered by the IT device. For instance, in the presence of IT, people may feel empowered, productive, autonomous, and accessible. According to Carter, Grover and Thatcher [67], young adults’ interactions with their mobile phones lead to enhanced perceptions of empowerment, self-authenticity, and autonomy. In the context of PHDs, self-concept and personal resources of users can be expanded by capabilities provided by the devices. For example, people can use wearable smart devices to reduce time and place constraints in controlling <sup>fi</sup>tness and wellness, measuring different physical changes, and handling emergency cases. These functionalities may enhance individuals’ original self-perceptions and may make them feel independent, empowered, and smart.

Prior studies on IT identity suggest that the construct of IT identity is expressed through relatedness, dependence, and emotional responses of individuals in relation to IT [17]. Relatedness refers to a sense of connection when interacting with an IT. For instance, a strong feeling of connection with an IT can turn individuals’ perceptions about the self to what they can do with the IT [68]. Emotional energy indicates levels of emotional attachment, enthusiasm, and con<sup>fi</sup>dence that an individual attributes to an IT when thinking about his/her interaction with it. Long-term interactions with IT can raise individuals’ levels of emotion and con<sup>fi</sup>dence with the IT experience and help them to be more spontaneous with IT [69]. Dependence explains the extent to which people rely on IT to represent their self-perceptions. For instance, people rely on digital forms of communication to manage their interactions and relationships with others to satisfy social expectations [70].

## 3. Hypotheses development

Previous research studying identity in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts outlines that identity is a motivator of behaviors [71]. Individuals desire to interact more with others (e.g., people or objects) that provide self-con-<sup>fi</sup>rming feedback to reinforce their identities [72]. Therefore, interacting with an IT that contributes to self-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation can be a considerable opportunity for IT users. A strong IT identity can motivate individuals to use a variety of IT features in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations. When a person considers a PHD as an integral part of the self, he/she may become more likely to use a great number of its features in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>tness and emergency). This interaction will happen because he/she may attribute higher levels of emotional attachment, con<sup>fi</sup>dence, and enthusiasm to his medical device. In particular, being con<sup>fi</sup>dent, enthusiastic, and inspired by PHDs create a positive attitude toward its features; hence, people are more prone to use such devices in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts. Users’ dependency on their PHDs occurs as a result of their reliance on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they provide, such as communication, support, health management, and so on. Therefore, dependency contributes to a user’s postadoption behaviors as he/she becomes more likely to depend on a wide range of features and functions of a PHD to deal with various health issues. An individual with a robust IT identity tied to a smart device (used in healthcare management) would like to use its features to achieve multiple health-related purposes in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations. For instance, he/she may use his/her PHD to track activity, diet levels, and calories burned, handle emergency cases, control <sup>fi</sup>tness and wellness, manage chronic diseases, and so on [9]. Thus, we expect that IT identity can enhance the extent and breadth of PHD use.

## H1. IT identity positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the feature use of PHDs

This paper argues that higher levels of IT identity lead to the higher motivation to actively use extensive features of a PHD in numerous situations. A feeling of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, energy, and enthusiasm in relation to an IT device can result in continuous, e<sup>f</sup>ective, and long-term use on a daily basis [7]. Users with a heightened IT identity are more likely to develop autonomy and empowerment to use interactive technology. People high in IT identity tend to know their context as well as their ability, and they are more open to search for new features to complete further health-related tasks deliberately. Positive feeling, sense of dependence, and connection to a PHD may develop an attraction toward the device that encourages users to look for new ways of using it in other situations. Thus, they may use additional IT functions to complete more tasks to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the optimal self-identity expression [73]. A close relationship between the user and a PHD may empower him/her to discover previously unused features and push the demand for access to its feature extensions in everyday life. For instance, users may explore an underutilized set of features to frequently report their health status during the week. This sense of empowerment and excitement may also encourage users to <sup>fi</sup>nd new situations to apply the PHD in daily roles they maintain.

Therefore, reinforced IT identity may encourage people to engage in enhanced use behavior. According to Bagayogo, Lapointe and Bassellier [74], individuals may actively involve in gathering more evidence and indications about the features of an IT, which is left unexploited to apply them for additional tasks. An IT identity holder may seek more information, increase his/her knowledge about its features, and <sup>fi</sup>nd out how to apply previously unused features to perform di<sup>f</sup>erent tasks. In the context of PHDs, a strong IT identity can inspire users to explore more resources and capabilities embedded in smart devices and use them for additional health-related tasks. The logic behind this behavior has roots in the sense of connection and attachment that users may have with their PHDs. This feeling may give more con<sup>fi</sup>dence to users to rely on PHDs for performing further tasks related to monitoring and checking health measures. Thus, users with stronger IT identities will explore more functions and use a formerly unused set of features of PHD for additional tasks. For instance, a user (with formed IT identity) who was not aware of remote care and surveillance capabilities af forded by his PHD will attempt to explore it and add it to his to-do list. Hence, individuals with a stronger IT identity may desire to verify themselves through the use of a broader application of PHDs.

## H2. IT identity positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the enhanced use of PHDs

IT identity studies are conducted based on the assumption that a person has a history of interacting with technology before identity formation [75]. Guichard [76] demonstrates that experience has a prominent role in the process of individual self-construction. An individual’s experience with IT generates feelings of belongingness and relatedness as he/she participates in social networks and organizations [77]. A study conducted on social media suggests that a positive social media user experience will positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the continuing engagement in the processes of self-awareness, self-monitoring, and selfveri<sup>fi</sup>cation that, in the sequence, will re<sup>fl</sup>ect on IT Identity [59]. This point is echoed by Curley and Polites [62] proposing that positive experience and skill with technology are accompanied by heightened enjoyment and connection.

PHD-related experience refers to an individual’s knowledge of how to leverage its features to achieve a goal. Thus, the greater the knowledge an individual possesses, the higher his/her con<sup>fi</sup>dence in using PHDs because of an expectation that the experience will be familiar. Individuals’ background knowledge in using similar PHDs will contribute to their dependency on such technology and also create a feeling of empowerment. The cumulative positive experience that results from the extensive use of the mobile device encourages the ability of the individual to infuse it into the self in a variety of situations [78]. An individual’s prior interactions with a mobile device will attach memories and emotions to the device [79]. Hence, users may experience the absence of the device as the loss of an essential part of themselves. As knowledge of how to use mobile devices increases, users’ dependency on these devices grows, and their emotional energy will be elevated, which makes them identify themselves by using a mobile device [80].

Literature indicates that interacting with an IT can reinforce in dividuals’ e<sup>fi</sup>cacy-based self-esteem, such as computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy [7]. As people feel more con<sup>fi</sup>dent in their own ability to use features of PHDs, they are more likely to consider their devices as integral to their sense of self. Previous studies also show that people are more prone to integrate IT with the self if signi<sup>fi</sup>cant material bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and intrinsic grati<sup>fi</sup>cations are provided from the target IT. For instance, Rai, Lang and Welker [81] show that actualized bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the IT system can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceived dependency on IT because if users expect to receive some informational and emotional rewards from PHDs, they will possibly feel highly attached to their devices. Moreover, Singh, Sinha and Liébana-Cabanillas [82] demonstrate that levels of satisfaction from past experiences exert a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on enduring commitment to using IT. Prior studies propose that dependence, connection feelings, and energy attached to an IT are consequences of IT embeddedness [79]. The embeddedness of IT implicates the amount of investment in IT in terms of using its features in a wide range of situations [17]. Overall, we may reasonably argue that if a user associates past interactions with a PHD with high levels of actualized reward, embeddedness, and computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, he/she is more likely to exhibit a stronger IT identity in relation to this smart device.

## H3. Pleasant experience with PHDs positively shapes IT identity

According to the IT adoption literature, technology characteristics signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact individuals’ initial perceptions toward IT [83]. For instance, system characteristics such as task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t in<sup>fl</sup>uence users attitudes toward the use of mobile information systems [84]. Van Slyke, Ilie, Lou and Sta<sup>f</sup>ord [85] indicate that perceptions of the characteristics of new technology such as compatibility, complexity, and observability in<sup>fl</sup>uence attitude toward use, which in turn, shapes use intentions. Following a work by Carter and Grover [17], IT characteristics consist of four dimensions: functionality, malleability, bandwidth, and mobility. Functionality refers to a set of capabilities that o<sup>f</sup>er bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to users. In the context of our study, functionality can be di<sup>f</sup>erent features and applications that would be used by individuals to manage diseases, improve communication, and monitor health status data. An IT with higher functionality can provide a user with more capabilities to be applied in various situations to collect, maintain, and process a wide range of information. Malleability re<sup>fl</sup>ects a multipurpose IT that can be used in di<sup>f</sup>erent scenarios with no technical customization [86]. For instance, PHDs are <sup>fl</sup>exible enough to be used in a variety of healthcare situations. As PHDs can be utilized for different purposes (such as communications, competitions, and social networking), they can provide more rewards and increase users’ engagement with such smart tools. Moreover, malleability can facilitate PHDs to be incorporated into several social situations.

Bandwidth indicates the capacity of IT to convey a wide range of data [87]. PHDs can be used to communicate a variety of data related to di<sup>f</sup>erent health conditions. The social presence of individuals is improved by higher bandwidth because they can access and deliver en ormous amounts of wellness data and health-related information. Mo bility implies the ability of IT to move or be moved freely and quickly [88]. For example, PHDs are always accessible in di<sup>f</sup>erent locations and provide health information to users regardless of places. Mobility makes smart devices highly portable, which encourages people to experience its features across distances.

Overall, understanding the nature and characteristics of a PHD can increase individuals’ sense of control over its feature set. High levels of functionality, malleability, bandwidth, and mobility encourage individuals to embed PHDs in various personal, social, and technological settings. Moreover, advanced PHD characteristics extend the scope and scale of using those devices in routine practices and in relation to other IT systems, which in turn, promote computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and increase the likelihood of receiving rewards (e.g., emotional and informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts). Thus, we can argue that PHDs with extended product characteristics allow individuals to gain more experience with these smart devices and use them in their day-to-day activities.

## H4. Individuals’ experience with a PHD is positively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by its set of characteristics

We argue that PHD characteristics alone cannot shape IT identity. It is conceivable that some characteristics of digital devices such as functionality or mobility evoke emotions, but these e<sup>f</sup>ects are temporary and not able to leave a strong mark on the way people identify themselves in relation to the PHD. However, dimensions of IT identity (i.e., dependence, relatedness, and emotional energy) will be constructed based on long-term interaction with IT [17]. Thus, character istics of PHDs will in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT identity only when smart devices are used in a variety of situations, individuals are con<sup>fi</sup>dent in using the devices, and some informational and emotional rewards are expected to be actualized.

## H5. Past interactions with PHDs mediate the relationship between PHD characteristics and IT identity

In this study, we also consider some critical situational factors that may a<sup>f</sup>ect IT behaviors. These situational factors may in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ willingness to use a wide variety of IT features in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations to achieve their goals [17]. Designers and producers of PHD can create such situations through some managerial interventions to promote bene<sup>fi</sup>cial postadoption behaviors resulting from strong IT identities. Possible situational factors are the extent to which technology is dynamic, whether the privacy policy is robustly created and communicated, and the degree to which the smart device interface is clearly designed. Thus, developers of PHDs may directly promote feature use and enhance use behaviors by providing access to technological changes, devising a <sup>fi</sup>rm privacy policy to protect health information, and designing a clear interface. The <sup>fi</sup>rst situationa consideration is the extent to which an IT has dynamic feature sets [89]. IT dynamism highlights whether IT has features that are continuously upgraded or relatively static. We expect that a PHD with dynamic feature sets, characterized by new applications and constant upgrades, will motivate individuals to use its features in a wide range of situa tions. Moreover, interacting with a dynamic smart device with changing feature sets encourages users to apply its various features for new tasks. Thus, we argue that dynamic feature sets of PHDs are more likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence use behaviors than static feature sets.

## H7. IT dynamism positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the enhanced use of PHDs.

Interface design is the extent to which a user perceives that an application is generally designed well [90]. According to a <sup>fi</sup>eld study by Cyr, Head and Ivanov [91], design aesthetics and the ease of use are recognized as essential factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting users to evaluate mobile commerce. In a study, Gebauer, Tang and Baimai [92] analyze and code user reviews to highlight that display, design, and ease of use signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the overall usability of mobile applications. An empirical study shows that innovative functions and design features help promote users’ perceived usability and satisfaction of using mobile applications [93]. Users suggest that using applications should be effortlessly, and inputting information should take little time and attention to maintain the high usability of applications [94]. According to Adipat, Zhang and Zhou [95], if users have to input the same information more than once, they will be dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the mobile application, and they are less likely to use features in a variety of situations. Mobile applications should be e<sup>f</sup>ectively designed to allow users to instantly recognize the function of applications [96]. Moreover, it is suggested that mobile application controls should be immediately obvious and intuitive to use [97]. Thus, it is important that the user interface of a PHD is clear when using its features for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time. Finally, mobile applications should have a logical and predictable path for navigation purposes [98]. Therefore, it is reasonable to argue that an increase in the level of feature and function design will be associated with an increase in the overall use of PHDs (i.e., enhanced use and feature use).

## H8. IT design positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the feature use of PHDs.

## H9. IT design positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the enhanced use of PHDs.

Prior studies highlight that the security of mobile applications and security safeguards utilized by providers to protect users’ information are important attributes a<sup>f</sup>ecting individuals to use various features of smart devices [99]. Consumer concerns (particularly in medical practices) re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the increase in the volume of collected health in formation, the possibility of privacy violations (e.g., unauthorized access or hacked personal data), secondary use of medical records (e.g., data mining purposes), lack of control over how medical records are collected, and how such information will be used [100 102]. The lit erature emphasizes the role of privacy statements in use behaviors in other contexts, for example, online shopping, website registration, and mobile internet use. The completeness and transparency of online privacy statements in<sup>fl</sup>uence online consumers’ perceptions and behavioral intentions to purchase products [103]. In e-commerce settings, the content of privacy statements is found as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor in predicting consumer trust in websites [104]. According to Callanan, Jerman-Blažič and Blažič [105], user awareness of privacy policy has a direct e<sup>f</sup>ect on using mobile internet. The presence of a robust website privacy policy heightens online shoppers' trust and, in turn, reduces their privacy concerns [106]. Framing a rigorous privacy statement, which shows organizational compliance with personal data protection regulations, can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ buying decisions [107].

As reported by Tsai, Egelman, Cranor and Acquisti [108], if online retailers provide accessible and transparent privacy policy guidelines, consumers are more likely to pay a premium to purchase services from privacy-protective websites. When websites or applications clearly present a privacy statement, consumers are more willing to read it carefully to get further online services [109]. Recent studies indicate that adults avoid using mobile applications or opt-out of online services because of the absence of strong privacy statements [110]. If consumers are well informed of online privacy terms and conditions, they provide more information to websites [111]. On the contrary, if no details are presented in privacy policies, customers are not aware of collecting and sharing procedures. Privacy policy dimensions contain details that empower customers by clarifying the rights and options they have to better control the use of health information. For instance, if they can opt-out of information sharing with a third party, they will feel more control over their personal data, and this feeling makes online services appear more trustworthy to them [112].

With the advance of technologies used for information exchange, a high number of consumers are anxious about the disclosure, transfer, and sale of personal information that organizations collect from them. Privacy policies should be framed to address patients’ privacy and security concerns. Privacy policy statements de<sup>fi</sup>ne how a healthcare organization collects, manages, uses, and disseminates personal health information (ranging from less sensitive to highly sensitive) that directly and indirectly relates to an individual. Prior studies described that providers should create informative and comprehensive privacy policies to reassure individuals that collecting their health information is a low-risk practice [113]. For instance, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is the recent legislation that protects users’ private data and applies across the European Union (EU) [114]. Under GDPR, policies should clearly explain data management requirements, including data lifecycle, storage limits, data transfers outside the EU, and user rights [115].

Privacy policies are mainly devised based on <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions of Fair Information Practices Principles as follows: notice, choice, access, security, and enforcement [104]. Notice refers to the commitment of organizations to send timely announcements to consumers about their information collection practices before personal information is col lected. The choice indicates that consumers should be given options about how the collected information would be used. Access means de<sup>fi</sup>ning the consumers’ rights to view their own data and check whether such data is accurate and complete. Security outlines the required steps and actions that should be taken by organizations to ensure the safety and integrity of consumers’ personal information. Enforcement articulates which national/international mechanisms, guidelines, and instruments are in place to enforce principles of privacy protection. Thus, we argue that PHDs should communicate their privacy policy standpoint to users by encouraging them to apply various features to do existing and new tasks in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations.

According to Bansal and Gefen [116], individuals' characteristics (such as current health status) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the way they analyze the utility of using IT. Perceived health status is a common health factor that highlights overall individual health [117]. Personal health status evokes privacy concerns related to health information disclosures and tools used to share such information [118]. Perceptions of people about their health stability may make them more sensitive to their health information and the technology leveraged to collect and exchange such data [119]. Consequently, it is reasonable to argue that poor health conditions negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the extent to which people express feelings of connectedness, reliance, and positive energy when thinking about themselves in relation to PHDs. Individuals who consider themselves unhealthy may become less likely to invest in interacting with a PHD and less prone to use a wide variety of its features and functions to perform health-related tasks in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations.

Therefore, the impact of IT identity on postadoption use behaviors in a given situation may depend on an individual’s perception of his/her health status. We expect that the IT identity-behavior relationship may be variable depending on the current health status of users. Individuals willingness to invest in interacting with PHDs is motivated by the extent to which they perceive that they are healthy enough to explore di<sup>f</sup>erent functions and features of those smart devices in various situations. Carter and Grover [17] suggest that the enactment of IT identity to use an extensive feature set of IT in new contexts may depend on the personal ability to use attention and resources. Unhealthy individuals (e.g., with chronic diseases) perceive more strain due to the presence of physical/mental in<sup>fi</sup>rmity, and this health condition makes them more anxious and vulnerable to digital devices surrounding them. Therefore, it is likely that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IT identity on speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviors depends on whether an individual perceives their health status to be good or poor. People with good health conditions tend to assume less severe demands on their strength or abilities, perceive more control, and experience less mental/emotional strain. Thus, healthy people may be more likely to enact their IT identity by using a wide range of features and functions of a PHD (feature use behavior). Additionally, people with better health status experience less physical or mental weaknesses and anxiety, and in turn, are more prone to expand the self by applying a PHD to new tasks and situations (enhanced use behavior). Accordingly, we argue that IT identity will exert a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on use behaviors for people who perceive to be healthy than individuals with poor health status. Perceived health status can create situations that intensify the relationship between IT identity and bene<sup>fi</sup>cial postadoption behaviors that engage individuals in the use of PHDs.

## 4. Research model

The research model indicates that past interactions with PHDs can shape IT identity, and in turn, it leads to positive use behaviors. Four PHD characteristics (i.e., functionality, bandwidth, mobility, and malleability) may increase the strength of experience with such devices. The model also shows that situational factors can directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT behaviors (i.e., feature use behavior and enhanced use). According to Carter and Grover [17], individuals do not always attempt to use IT to present who they are to others, even when IT is advantageous to them. Thus, based on suggestions from previous studies in other contexts [120], we propose that IT identity veri<sup>fi</sup>cation in relation to PHDs may depend on the health status of users. As the probability of IT identity is evoked by health status, the relationships between IT identity and behaviors may be moderated by this health factor. Fig. 1 shows the proposed research model.

This study drew on the existing literature to measure constructs included in the model, and minor changes were made to the instrument to <sup>fi</sup>t the PHD context. Consistent with prior studies, we consider IT identity as a second-order construct with three re<sup>fl</sup>ective factors [21]. The rationale behind this measurement is because IT identity is re-<sup>fl</sup>ective of the three dimensions as well as the expected interactions among them. Therefore, all these dimensions can re<sup>fl</sup>ect the same theme and may covary. To measure the three interrelated dimensions of IT identity (i.e., relatedness, emotional energy, and dependence), we adapted the items reported in Carter and Grover [17]. According to Kayhan [121], re<sup>fl</sup>ective modeling is a better option than formative when <sup>fi</sup>rst-order factors are expected to interact, correlate, or share a common theme. Thus, a set of interrelationships among these factors is an important component of measuring IT identity. For instance, dependence, which de<sup>fi</sup>nes a person’s sense of reliance on a PHD may be related to the emotional energy dimension that describes feelings of attachment in relation to the device.

PHD experience is modeled as a formative second-order construct with three dimensions. Accordingly, we expect that PHD experience is a function of 1. embeddedness, 2. computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and 3. actualized rewards. None of the three dimensions necessarily needs to covary. For instance, an individual may have used a wide range of a PHD’s features in a variety of situations but perceived low bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and little satisfaction resulting from past use. Besides, a user may associate previous interactions with low levels of computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, but he/ she has realized that he/she received several emotional and informational rewards from using the digital device. Another reason for mod eling experience as a multi-dimensional formative construct is that experience can be represented by a combination of elements rather than just the number of years. Doing this starts to get at the quality of the experience rather than only the quantity. This study adapted items to measure computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy from Compeau, Higgins and Hu<sup>f</sup> [122], embeddedness from Carter and Grover [17], as well as actualized rewards from Rai, Lang and Welker [81], Venkatesh and Bala [83], and Bhattacherjee and Premkumar [123]. Items re<sup>fl</sup>ecting feature use be haviors were adapted from Lucas Jr and Spitler [124], and items measuring enhanced use behaviors were adapted from Bagayogo, Lapointe and Bassellier [74]. Items measuring IT dynamism were adapted following suggestions from Carter and Grover [17]. We adapted the items reported in Hoehle and Venkatesh [90] to measure IT design. Items re<sup>fl</sup>ecting privacy policy were adapted from Chua, Herbland, Wong and Chang [125] and Wu, Huang, Yen and Popova [104]. Finally, items measuring perceived health status were adapted from the scale developed by Bansal and Gefen [116].

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Pretest assessment

Once the initial questionnaire was developed based on previous research, we used an expert judgment approach to enhance the content validity of the survey. The questionnaire was sent to <sup>fi</sup>ve experts who are well established in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of health informatics and identity, to check for completeness, accuracy, readability, and format of the survey.

The content validity index testing was used to analyze the feedback and suggestions. Using this approach, the team of experts indicated whether each item on a scale was congruent with (or relevant to) the construct. Then, the percentage of items deemed to be relevant for each expert was computed, and <sup>fi</sup>nally, the average of the percentages across experts was taken. The average congruency percentage (ACP) was 94, which was higher than the threshold of 90 % [129]. Therefore, the ACP was considered acceptable for the current survey used in this study. We then removed the marked ambiguous words and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed questions based on experts' suggestions to ensure that the instrument was <sup>fl</sup>awless and easy to understand for potential participants. Before conducting the main study, we conducted a pilot test with 248 graduate students at a large Southeastern university in the United States to ensure the reliability and validity of the instrument. The Cronbach's alpha was computed for each construct (functionality: = 0.91, malleability: = 0.90, bandwidth: α = 0.89, mobility: α = 0.88, embeddedness: α = 0.85, computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy: = 0.88, actualized rewards: = 0.92, IT identity: α = 0.89, IT dynamism: α = 0.92, IT design: α = 0.90, privacy policy: α = 0.84, enhanced use: α = 0.90, feature use: α = 0.90, perceived health status: α = 0.86). All Cronbach's alpha values were above the cuto<sup>f</sup> point of 0.7, which indicated that the instrument was internally consistent [130]. This study uses 5-point Likert scales with anchors ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree.

## 5.2. Data collection procedure

Data was collected in February 2019 from Amazon's Mechanical Turk (MTurk) to obtain a representative group of subjects. As PHDs may not be considered as a routine technology for many individuals, to get more robust, reliable, and applicable <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we speci<sup>fi</sup>ed three additional quali<sup>fi</sup>cations that individuals had to meet to participate in the survey. First, as the main objective of this study is to examine e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity, we used a scenario in which IT identity exists to test the proposed model e<sup>f</sup>ectively. Thus, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned a screening question to only include individuals who have been using a PHD. We attempted to distinguish between PHDs (hardware device with applications) and mobile health apps (software application) and only include users of PHDs. The logic behind this screening is that we de<sup>fi</sup>ned IT as a unit of technology (hardware device and software application). Accordingly, dimensions of IT characteristics and IT identity can be adequately measured and examined based on individuals’ interaction with devices (as a unit), not through their interaction with application environments or software applications. For example, participants of this study were users of any PHDs (such as wearable smart devices, wearable activity monitors, and intelligent bracelets). When individuals use a PHD, they are exposed to its features and characteristics. Therefore, the likelihood that they become more familiar with its functions and mechanisms is higher, and IT identity is more likely to be enacted. Thus, we ensured that the participants had used a PHD when they took part in this study. The incentive for participation was a monetary reward (\$4). At the beginning of the online survey, a detailed description of PHDs was provided to ensure that respondents completely comprehend the context and purpose of the study. A total of 724 individuals attempted the survey.

Thus, concerns that online respondents might reply randomly or haphazardly to complete the survey quickly were alleviated. Before data were statistically analyzed, normality was evaluated as this is important for distributions of data to exhibit this trait to facilitate unbiased and consistent models [137]. According to Hair, Black, Babin, Anderson and Tatham [138], skewness expresses the symmetry while kurtosis explains the peakedness of distributions. Thus, all constructs used in the model scrutinized against normality assumptions. An examination of the skewness and kurtosis of the constructs showed a skewness range from 0.045 to 1.164 and a kurtosis range from 0.017 to 1.531. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, all the values fall within the prescribed limit and maximum acceptable levels of 2 for skewness and 7 for kurtosis tests [139].

To test the proposed research model, we apply a two-step assessment process using SmartPLS: 1) measurement model and 2) structural model assessments [140]. The SmartPLS method simultaneously as sesses the theoretical propositions and properties of the underlying measurement model. We test hypothesized relationships using partialleast squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) technique based on four main reasons. First, although the model is mainly developed based on prior research in IT identity, hypothesized relationships have not been tested earlier, indicating the exploratory nature of the research model [140]. Second, PLS-SEM is a better technique to test a structural model that is complex and integrated (including many constructs and indicators) [141]. Third, PLS-SEM is preferable to covariance-based structural equation modeling to assess a hierarchical construct model with multidimensional variables [142]. Finally, regarding construct type, PLS-SEM supports both re<sup>fl</sup>ective and formative constructs in a structural model [130]. Therefore, PLS-SEM <sup>fi</sup>ts the structure of this study.

## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Key findings

This study is conducted to address the three research questions by identifying the antecedents and consequences of IT identity in the context of PHDs and investigating the role of situational factors (i.e., IT design, IT dynamism, and privacy policy) in describing PHD use behaviors. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that PHD characteristics signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enact IT identity by creating PHD experience. Then, IT identity signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improves PHD feature use and enhanced use behaviors. Among the three situational factors, only IT dynamism is likely to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shape both positive use behaviors (i.e., feature use and enhanced use behaviors). Moreover, the results highlight that perceived health status positively moderates the relationships between IT identity and positive use behaviors. Therefore, this study adequately answers the de<sup>fi</sup>ned research questions.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study indicate that relatedness, emotional en ergy, and dependency are interrelated dimensions of IT identity and contribute signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to IT identity associated with PHDs. Regarding the three dimensions of IT identity, our results are consistent with existing literature suggesting that a<sup>f</sup>ection, connection, and emotional attachment to an object can increase the likelihood of commitment to that object and shape interaction behaviors [156]. The results further indicate the importance of the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT experience and the indirect impact of IT characteristics in enacting IT identity for users of PHDs. This study implies that the strength of IT identity will be am pli<sup>fi</sup>ed when individuals infuse PHDs into their routine activities through experiencing multiple features of PHDs in numerous situations because of ongoing interactions with these smart devices. These results are consistent with arguments posited in the identity theories and align with propositions made by Carter and Grover [17].

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that PHD characteristics do not directly shape IT identity but instead is fully mediated by PHD experience. As previously discussed, much of the theoretical justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for the role of IT experience suggested mediation; our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm this e<sup>f</sup>ect [157,158]. Thus, mechanisms and features of PHDs (i.e., functionality, malle ability, bandwidth, and mobility) designed to verify IT identity may be most e<sup>f</sup>ective when they encourage users to experience such devices on regular-basis interactions. Regarding the importance of IT character istics in creating IT identity, results are consistent with prior studies indicating that product features are basic requirements for users of PHDs (such as a variety of functions and reliability of features) [159].

The stronger the IT identity attached to PHDs, the more willing people are to use such devices for performing various tasks. Findings suggest that people with a strong IT identity in relation to PHDs may exhibit a greater willingness to engage in positive use behaviors. Results demonstrate that once IT identity formed, individuals are more likely to search for previously unused feature sets of the PHDs (enhanced use), apply smart devices in additional situations, and use components of extension capabilities (extended features). Positive relationships be tween IT identity and postadoption use behaviors provide empirical evidence for prior studies suggesting that a person’s behavior is a function of activated identity [160].

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that of the three situational factors examined; feature use behavior is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced more by IT design and IT dynamism, which exert more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on enhanced use behavior. However, the impact of design factors on enhanced use of PHDs is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. One plausible justi<sup>fi</sup>cation is that designing e<sup>f</sup>ective PHDs can primarily entice users to extend the number of features used, the degree to which di<sup>f</sup>erent features of a device are employed, and the number of situations in which the device is used. Extrapolating from prior studies [e.g., [161]] to the PHD context, <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that design factors may not necessarily encourage users to seek a formerly unused set of features or use a smart device to perform additional tasks. Accordingly, we can argue that design elements (such as easy format, intuitive menu, and self-explanatory features) may only shape initial adoption decisions [93]. Design factors may not go beyond the scope of promoting feature use behaviors, and by itself, it may not motivate users to step forward and engage in enhanced use behaviors through exploring previously unused features or using feature extensions.

The results relating to the impact of the privacy policy on behavior point out that a transparent and comprehensive privacy policy sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cantly contributes to the enhanced use of PHDs. Nevertheless, there is no guarantee that the development of a clear privacy policy necessarily shapes feature use behavior. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the transparency of privacy policy may not necessarily in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ initial adoption behaviors, as users may only desire to try new technology and its di<sup>f</sup>erent features [162]. Therefore, a clear privacy policy (which explains what data are collected or shared, what security measures will be used to protect personal information, and who can access such health data) will not necessarily promote feature use behaviors. However, it is worth noting that <sup>fi</sup>nding new situations to use a PHD and its feature extensions (i.e., enhanced use behavior) is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly directed by a transparent privacy policy. A plausible reason for this result is that a user will be more likely to engage in enhanced use behaviors, such as exploring formerly unused features if one has assured about the privacy policy of his/her PHD of choice. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that adopting a PHD and trying its di<sup>f</sup>erent features in various situations (i.e., feature use behavior) may not be promoted by a clear privacy policy, but the presence of a robust and rigorous privacy statement will strongly support further attempts such as exploring the device deeply to <sup>fi</sup>nd unused functions (i.e., enhanced use behavior). These observations are consistent with those reported by Keith, Thompson, Hale, Lowry and Greer [163].

The moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived health status suggests that for users who perceive themselves healthy, the presence of IT identity strongly a<sup>f</sup>ects how they would engage in bene<sup>fi</sup>cial use behaviors. For instance, a good health status could increase the relatedness to a PHD focused on <sup>fi</sup>tness/well-being, and in turn, encourage the user to explore more features of his/her device and use them in various situations. On the other hand, for people su<sup>f</sup>ering from chronic health issues, the impact of IT identity on use behaviors will be attenuated. For example, a poor health status could decrease the emotional energy the user can invest in the device, and in turn, discourage the individual from using it for performing additional tasks. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with prior research, which suggests that individuals who consider themselves physically and mentally healthy tend to feel that they can adequately interact with devices and control their environments [118].

## 7.2. Theoretical implications

This work also has implications for researchers conducting IT identity studies in the healthcare context and mHealth area. First, this study contributes to theory building on IT identity in the context of smart devices used for healthcare purposes and ways in which this type of identity can be fostered. It also provides insights into the conceptualization of IT identity in relation to PHDs by providing empirical evidence to the existence of three dimensions of IT identity. The model posits that relatedness, emotional energy, and dependence are three facets of IT identity, and all these dimensions share the same theme. Moreover, the empirical validity of measuring IT identity as a re<sup>fl</sup>ective second-order construct was veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study, which is consistent with previous research [17]. As IT identity is proposed to play an important role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing positive use behaviors [74], researchers need to shed more light on the dimensions of IT identity. As shown in the results, all three dimensions of IT identity strongly contribute to the operationalization of this concept. Without a clear picture of beliefs associated with all three dimensions, postadoption IT behaviors in relation to feature use and enhanced use may be compromised. The dimensions of IT identity will increase the possibility of achieving in creased usage of familiar PHD features. As a result, we provide evidence that IT identity holders have more tendency to use existing features of their PHD in various situations.

Second, as stated by Carter and Grover [17], the question of how IT identity enacts and how it can be fostered has not received much attention in research studies. Craig, Thatcher and Grover [64] appeal to studies that help understand conditions that give rise to the develop ment of IT identity in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts. The proposed model delineates how PHD characteristics and user experience with PHD in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT identity and how strong identity in using PHDs impact users postadoption reactions. The results indicate that IT identity is concerned with the context of using PHDs to keep users engaged and enhance their use behaviors. In this study, we adopt IT identity as a theoretical lens to predict factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting PHD use behaviors. We use a research model (including determinants and outcomes) to gain a comprehensive view of the IT identity role during the postadoption usage of PHDs. Thus, this study provides insights that can help researchers distinguish IT identity, its dimensions, antecedents, and possible consequences. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the validity and robustness of the IT identity construct in explaining positive use behaviors in the context of digital devices in healthcare management. Our results extend applications of IT identity by indicating that variables used in the model predict a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant percentage of variations in individuals’ postadop tion behaviors related to feature use and enhance use of PHDs. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings shed more light on the explanatory power of IT identity in predicting postadoption behaviors.

Third, this work validates the proposed model that highlights the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity on human behaviors in the context of digital devices. The IT identity-behavior relationships con<sup>fi</sup>rm prior studies that describe why individuals try to maintain consistency between identities and behavior [164]. Thus, IT identity can be incorporated into the postadoption research stream, as it can help researchers better understand why people use technologies in their own ways. According to Lyytinen [165], the majority of studies use models to test static and single-use IT within the boundaries of organizations. These models mostly explain the situations in which people were exposed to technologies in their workplace and then used them in their personal lives. Individuals start using consumer technologies (such as PHDs) in their personal lives and then try to apply them in the workplace, too [166]. As a construct that is relevant to postadoption use, incorporating IT identity into postadoption literature provides new insights into understanding factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting actual use behaviors of individuals in relation to consumer ITs. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that IT identity holders have more control over the feature set of their devices, and in turn, will be more likely to engage in feature use and enhance use behaviors. An understating of IT identity’s antecedents and consequences may be useful for researchers studying technology assimilation and user experience with IT. The results of this work may contribute to humancomputer interaction (HCI) literature by shedding light on issues such as how to improve user interactions with devices dealing with personal health information.

Fourth, IT identity provides a basis for understanding the self in relation to IT. People who are a<sup>f</sup>ected by unique IT characteristics (i.e., functionality, malleability, bandwidth, and mobility), as well as past interactions and experience (i.e., embeddedness, computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and actualized rewards), will develop a strong IT identity in relation to a PHD. This result may contribute to IT identity literature by providing empirical evidence to show how IT identity can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in the context of self-management tools. We theorize that IT identity associated with PHDs may not always transform into bene<sup>fi</sup>cial behaviors (i.e., feature use and enhanced use). Our theoretical model suggests that the perceived health status of users intensi<sup>fi</sup>es the IT identity-behavior relationship. The moderating role of perceived health status demonstrates that IT identity is dynamic in the postadoption PHD usage stage and amenable to change through the manipulation of individuals health status perceptions. Healthier users (who may not be su<sup>f</sup>ering from a chronic disease) are more likely to exhibit a stronger IT identity and explore various features of their devices to use them in a variety of situations. Therefore, users of a PHD who perceive to have a good health status will develop further awareness and alertness about its applications and highly tend to engage in more nuanced use. Moreover, situational factors (i.e., IT dynamism, design factors, and privacy policy) encourage users to exhibit bene<sup>fi</sup>cial postadoption behaviors.

Finally, this study contributes to the IT identity literature by demonstrating the mediating role of IT characteristics in the veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of IT identity. Through empirical research, our work validates the proposition of Carter and Grover [17] that an IT (just as a medium) and its characteristics cannot lead to IT identity development. The characteristics of a PHD will help enact IT identity only through experiences and interactions with the device. Thus, IT characteristics will help shape IT identity only when an individual has con<sup>fi</sup>dence in using his/ her device to perform di<sup>f</sup>erent tasks in a wide range of situations in hopes of actualizing informational and emotional rewards.

## 7.3. Practical implications

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study have important managerial implications for those tasked with the responsibility of developing PHDs. First, as many PHD alternatives are available, developers and vendors of such digital devices need to realize the importance of IT identity formation if they desire to engage users and promote bene<sup>fi</sup>cial postadoption behaviors. A lack of attachment and connection between individuals and PHDs can create negative feelings toward them, such as boredom. In looking at IT identity, vendors should take an interest in the appreciation of its three dimensions $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ dependence, relatedness, and emotional energy) and how to reinforce each of them. It is also important that vendors have an in-depth understanding of factors contributing to the enactment of IT identity. In trying to form IT identity in relation to PHDs, vendors need to consider the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT ex perience and the indirect impact of IT characteristics. By describing IT identity and explaining its relationships with active use behaviors, we can provide practitioners and PHD developers with practical recommendations about how to advance users’ value derived from PHD and how to retain and engage potential users.

Second, there is considerable interest in understanding the interplay between perceived health status and IT identity. We think that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be a useful means for exploring this relationship in more depth. Regarding the moderating role of perceived health status, the use behaviors of individuals are likely to be varied. This variability makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult for a single vendor to be able to generate health-re lated content and features, which are comprehensive enough to embrace a wide range of health issues and topics. Thus, vendors need to decide the optimal scope of health-related functions and features on which their PHDs desire to focus. They can choose to o<sup>f</sup>er a broader set of functions to cover a variety of health conditions or to focus on spe ci<sup>fi</sup>c health issues (chronic diseases or typical ailments). Therefore, PHDs need to be relevant to the users, and developers should conside the target audience when designing their features, functions, and applications. For instance, a PHD may only o<sup>f</sup>er features and applications required to quit smoking or alcohol use. Another example would be a PHD that is required to monitor more severe issues such as cancer or HIV. According to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we can argue that more focused devices with functions and applications devoted to a particular health situation, may increase the chances of encouraging target users to exhibit bene-<sup>fi</sup>cial use behaviors.

Third, we believe that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this paper could be particularly informative for mHealth app designers. Regardless of the focus of a smart device, vendors can enhance use behaviors and encourage users to take advantage of di<sup>f</sup>erent features and applications by leveraging e<sup>f</sup>ective design elements. With a better understanding of IT identity, PHD system designers may be in a better position to design systems supportive of IT identity to enhance post-adoption use behaviors. For example, consistent with Wobbrock, Myers and Aung [167], as PHDs are handheld, systems designers should consider that these devices need features to display the health information transparently. Thus, clear designs should be used to communicate the purpose of a device better and foster active and engaged use behaviors.

Fourth, as we show that past interaction with PHDs is a strong antecedent of IT identity, one practical way to enhance IT identity in the context of smart devices could be through elevating users’ experi ence and interactions. In an e<sup>f</sup>ort to enrich the experience with PHDs, vendors need to use tactics to make individuals view their past usage experiences positively. For instance, focusing on the emotional energy dimension of IT identity would make the experience of using PHDs more energizing and exciting. Moreover, an individual’s perception of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts resulting from past use can be fostered through using a systematic feedback system. According to Hoehle and Venkatesh [90], mobile applications should provide clear and understandable results and feedback regarding users’ progress. For instance, PHDs can give clear feedback on the process of losing weight to help users understand if they are on the right track in pursuit of the planned goals. A well designed feedback system can help to increase enjoyment, satisfaction, and net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and in turn, actualize rewards.

Finally, respecting the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact of the privacy policy, making PHDs private and accessible only to relevant individuals, and providing e<sup>f</sup>ective security settings and policy statements may encourage users to explore for more functions, features, or even more tools from the same brand. Privacy protective actions and policies of personal tools may be regulated and enforced at the government level by incorporating rules and guidelines into traditional policies such as Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) security rules [168].

## 7.4. Limitations and future study

Even though this study provides theoretical and practical implications, it has several limitations. The limitations also o<sup>f</sup>er possible directions for future research. It should be mentioned that the study is based only on a sample of respondents drawn from the United States. Hence, the results may not be generalized to all users of PHDs. It is recommended that future studies consider drawing samples from wider geographical areas, including other developed countries and also developing countries where technological infrastructures and internet services are less developed than in the United States. While investigating cultural factors is beyond the scope of this study, future research can examine the role of cultural dimensions in creating IT identity. For example, it is interesting to identify whether individualism or collectivism may create di<sup>f</sup>erent versions of IT identities, such as manifestations of American IT identity or Japanese IT identity. Future research could leverage our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and investigate the possible interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects between IT identity, personality traits (e.g., agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, neuroticism, openness), and use behaviors. Another potential direction for future studies is to in vestigate whether gender di<sup>f</sup>erences can lead to the formation of different levels of IT identity.

Our study used an online survey to recruit participants digitally. Since a self-rated sample of participants on MTurk was used, there is still a small chance that some individuals were not completely aware of mobile technology and formed their own mental construal of the IT artifact. Therefore, we suggest that further studies use a di<sup>f</sup>erent method to ensure that subjects are knowledgeable about PHDs. For instance, future research can recruit informed patients who are directly referred by providers using patient self-management tools. Moreover, using an online survey might create a sample selection bias. Thus, we only considered individuals who could access the computer, mobile devices, and the Internet to participate in the online survey. Future studies can use other data collection means and sampling strategies to reach out to a sample that is generalizable to a wide range of healthcare consumers.

This study can also serve as a starting point for design science studies in the context of individual use of smart devices. In this study, no speci<sup>fi</sup>c PHD was examined, but the general concept of a PHD was studied. For example, it would be interesting to investigate whether alternative PHD brands in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT identity enactment and, in turn, a<sup>f</sup>ect user loyalty. Moreover, as there are many forms of consumer technologies (such as smartphones and tablet computers) with di<sup>f</sup>erent IT characteristics, one promising research avenue would be to explore the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity in other contexts rather than PHDs. In this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned IT as a unit of technology (hardware and software), and the IT identity was examined in the context of general PHDs. Another promising area of research is expanding this study by in vestigating IT identity for users of mobile health apps (software appli cation) and analyzing the plausible di<sup>f</sup>erences.

This study highlights the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating role of perceived health status between IT identity and positive use behaviors. Future studies could expand this moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect. For instance, additional research with a new study design is required to address what dimension (s) of IT identity could play a more important role in shaping the postadoption behaviors of users su<sup>f</sup>ering from chronic diseases or ailments. Future research should also compare the e<sup>f</sup>ects of speci<sup>fi</sup>c health status $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ physical and mental health stability) deeply to articulate whether a lack of physical or mental health stability can exert di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects on IT identity and its relationship with positive use behaviors. Furthermore, results indicate that together factors were able to explain 30 % of the variance in IT identity. Although we controlled confounding variables through randomization, we need to acknowledge possible confounding e<sup>f</sup>ects of age, education level, technology experience, and employment status in the proposed model. Another research avenue is examining additional factors that may enhance the amount of variance in IT identity explained (such as trust in smart devices and social support).

Furthermore, this research can be considered as a basis for future empirical studies to examine how negative IT identity may impact human behavior, such as IT adoption in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts (organizational or personal). In an organizational context, more research is needed to investigate how managerial interventions (such as training, technical support, or acceptable use policy) may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the enactment of IT identity. Another interesting research could be examining the relationship between IT identity and stress experienced by em ployees from the implementation of evolving technology in the work place. We also suggest that future studies extend our work by investigating the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT identity on individuals’ perceived threat to professional autonomy as a result of implementing new technology in companies. It can be of interest for future research to study IT identity in situations where work role identity is very prominent (such as a physician) and expand our understanding about the interplay between IT and role identities.

## 8. Conclusion

e-health technology is an integral part of healthcare systems mainly developed to improve medical accessibility and medical service quality. As the terminal devices of e-health systems, PHDs are designed to be smart, wearable, and able to carry out multiple health-related tasks. However, many users either stop using such devices after only a few months or are not willing to actively use them on a daily basis. Thi study uses IT identity as the theoretical lens to examine the postadop tion behaviors of PHD users. To examine hypothesized relationships, we collected data from current users of PHDs in the United States. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings articulate the mechanism in which IT characteristics and IT experience result in IT identity enactment in relation to PHDs. In addition, we con<sup>fi</sup>rm that IT identity is a second-order construct with three re<sup>fl</sup>ective dimensions. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that links between IT identity and positive use behaviors are stronger for individuals who perceive themselves healthier. Finally, our results indicate that although IT identity is essential for using PHD’s features in di<sup>f</sup>erent situations, other situational factors (such as IT dynamism, IT design, and privacy policy) are also necessary to encourage individuals to extend their use or explore an unused set of features. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study provide insights into the phenomenon of IT identity formation and add to the literature on IT identity, e-health, mHealth, self-management tools, and health informatics. Thus, vendors may need to put their e<sup>f</sup>orts into means of increasing IT identity and reinforcing situational factors to serve, retain, and engage a wide range of target users. Theoretical and practical contributions of this study are noticeable because they could result in a deeper understanding of what human means in an evolving digital world.
