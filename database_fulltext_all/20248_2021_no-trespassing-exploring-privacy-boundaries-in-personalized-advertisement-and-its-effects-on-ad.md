# No trespassing: exploring privacy boundaries in personalized advertisement and its efects on ad attitude and purchase intentions on social media

Yu-Qian Zhu\*, Kritsapas Kanjanamekanant

National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, No. 43, Section 4, Keelung Rd, Da’an District, Taipei City, 106, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Personalized Advertisement online purchase intention Information Boundary Perceived Privacy ad attitude

## A B S T R A C T

Based on the Communication Privacy Management theory, we examined how two boundary conditions (information boundary and social boundary) and two relationship conditions (co-ownership relation and human computer relation) contribute to perceived privacy and ad efectiveness in the personalized ad context on social platforms. With 349 survey responses, we found that personalized ads based on internal data source, perceived personification and co-ownership of Facebook are positively related to perceived privacy, which leads to better ad attitude and higher purchase intentions. Perceived personification and co-ownership further moderate the relationship between internal data source and embarrassment to perceived privacy.

## 1. Introduction

As data analytics and technologies develop, personalized adver tising, or advertising that incorporates individual information, such as demographic information, browsing history, and brand preferences, has witnessed increasing popularity among marketers and consumers alike [1,2]. Researchers discovered that users perceive personalized ad con tent as more appealing and more connected to their interests, and have higher purchase intentions for the product recommended in personalized advertisements [3,4] Firms reportedly spend up to 50% of their total marketing budgets on personalized advertising [5].

The efectiveness of personalized ads rests upon the wealth of information about consumers [6]. The source of information can be from social media platforms or users’ browsing and search behaviors, collected knowingly or unknowingly to the users. This practice, however, has the potential to lead to user backlashes for overstepping the bound of expected information practices [7]. Extant research supporting ad personalization, however, has mostly studied users who were largely unaware that their data dictated which ads they saw [8]. With increasing user awareness of the personalization practice, personalized ads could decrease ad performance if it activates concerns about privacy and provokes users’ negative responses [8]. The Target case was a classic example. Target created promotions that were based on individual shopper’s consumption data and sent coupons for maternity related products to women whom it inferred was pregnant. A furious father complained about the coupons only to find that his teenage daughter was indeed pregnant. When this story got into the media, many consumers were outraged at the idea of a company figuring out a highly personal situation like being pregnant [9].

Marketers, therefore, need to understand what makes the use of consumer data in personalization accepted or resisted by consumers, so that they can honor consumers’ expectations about how their information should be used [8]. Failure to do so would result in negative feelings and attitudes and invoke the feeling of privacy invasion. The whole purpose of personalized advertising would be nullified as negative attitudes towards the ad or the brand in the ad would influence consumer’s purchase intention [10–12]. Our understanding of what constitutes acceptable and unacceptable data practices, or how the boundaries are formed and kept in utilizing consumer data in personalization, however, is still largely absent.

To address this gap and enhance our understanding of what users expect about how their information is used in personalization and what influences their expectations, we propose a research model that examines the where, what, who and how questions of privacy in the context of personalized advertising on social media platforms. We focus on social media platforms because of their significant roles in our daily lives: users spend an average of 2 hours and 22 minutes per day on social media platforms such as Facebook [13]. Thus, the understanding of how people make decisions regarding personalized advertisements on these platforms would help us to explain a significant portion of our online behaviors. Based on the communication privacy management (CPM) theory [14,15], we look into CPM concepts such as information co-ownership, collective information boundary and boundary keep rules, and explore how they apply in the personalized ad on Facebook, one of the leading social media platforms. Specifically, we investigate the impact of where the data come from (information boundary of whether it is internal to what has been shared) on perceived privacy; we look into the influence of what type of data (social boundary of whether it is of embarrassing nature) on perceived privacy; we examine the linkage between personification (who is Facebook) and data ownership/co-ownership (who owns the data? To what degree users believe social media platforms such as Facebook co-owns their information), and privacy conditions; finally, we explore how users’ perceptions of Facebook as a person (personification) impacts the relationship between the where and what questions to privacy perceptions: does seeing Facebook as person strengthen or weaken the efect of internal data and embarrassment on privacy? We contribute to extant literature by enhancing our understanding of how the two boundary conditions (information boundary and social boundary) and two relationship conditions (co-ownership relation and human–computer relation) influence perceived privacy and ad efectiveness.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, we review the relevant literature and develop the model. We then outline the descriptions of our research methodology and report our analysis and results. The final section provides discussions, conclusions, theoretical and managerial implications, and directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical Framework and Hypothesis

## 2.1. Communication Privacy Management

Communication Privacy Management is a comprehensive frame work that illustrates how people safeguard private information by establishing privacy rules and information boundaries between themselves and collective parties or other stakeholders to control the flow of information. CPM has been used extensively to examine the management of private information in social network environments (e.g., [16–20]). Prior researchers found that the degree of perceived privacy turbulence [18], cost–benefit perception [19], perceived risk [21], perceived privacy control [22], and perceived personalization (how the ads are perceived as a personal match) [23] afect our sense of privacy, which impacts how we interact with the platform and our responses to the advertisements.

Individuals maintain and coordinate privacy boundaries with various communication partners [14]. Privacy boundaries draw a division between what is deemed as private information and public information. An individual's self-disclosures are governed by his or her privacy boundary. Once a disclosure of private information is made, the information moves from individual ownership to collective ownership, in which there is a joint responsibility and obligation by the original information owner and co-owners to guard the information with mutually agreed rules [24]. The collective information boundary, therefore, guards all the shared information from other individuals who do not have the permission to access them. The negotiation of privacy rules between the two parties (the one who shares the information and the one who is shared with) helps to clarify what information can or cannot be dis closed to third parties [24]. Poor privacy boundary coordination or maintenance results in the so-called boundary turbulence, with undesirable outcomes such as anger, distrust, other radical cognitive ap praisals, and emotional or behavioral displacements [24,25].

Fig. 1 shows how collective information boundaries are formed. The platform assumes the responsibility to guard this collective information boundary with certain rules, so that individual privacy is preserved. Similarly, when the individual shares with Platform B, a collective in formation boundary with Platform B is also formed (collective in formation boundary with Platform B). Information in the collective boundaries is expected to remain within its respective boundary. For example, a piece of information in the collective information boundary with Platform A is not to permeate the boundary into the collective information boundary with Platform $\mathbf { B } ,$ or boundary turbulence would occur.

To form privacy rules to guard the collective information boundary, information owners take into consideration the other party’s gender, attractiveness [24] or certain attributes of topics being discussed (about themselves, close relatives versus activity-related) [26]. In the hu man–computer information boundary context, it is more obscure how each private information owner builds such rules, as information regarding potential third-party or online audiences is not readily available for them when they sign up or even after a period of using such social media platforms. Therefore, the applicability of CPM is likely to be broader than just interactions among people and could include people’s privacy boundary management with computers as well. In fact, when we share things on Facebook, most people understand that we are not only sharing to our circle of friends, but also to the platform, Facebook as well, which was explicitly spelled out in the user agreement when we signed up for the service. This understanding, in some sense, is true since we leave numerous digital traces online, willingly or unwillingly, that could be compiled into a holistic view of who we are.

## 2.2. Perception of co-ownership and perceived privacy

Under the purview of CPM, once an individual shares his/her information with somebody, the sharee assumes co-ownership of the information. CPM sees collective privacy boundaries as marking “the lines of ownership” and indicating “status of co‐ownership” [29,30]. When the original information owner shares the information to another party, co-ownership status fundamentally alters the system from “an individual focus to a collective focus concerning the information shared” [30]. Co-owners are advised to negotiate with the sharer an agreement about how much control the co-owners have to independently manage the private information [29,30]. This echoes with social contract theory [31,32], which predicts that when consumers willingly disclose personal information with a company for something such as services or products, both parties develop an implicit agreement that the company has certain rights to the consumer’s personal information. Information co-ownership perception reflects the degree to which individuals believe their information belongs to the one they shared their information with [33]. In the context of information disclosure among friends, sharers reported negative emotional reactions to dissemination of personal information when they perceived their friends that they shared information to as having less ownership, and positive emotional reactions when they perceived their friends as having more ownership [33]. In consumer relationship management, the implicit co-ownership creates expectations for consumers and the company, in our case the social media platform, in terms of the right to utilize the consumer’s information. While perceived self-ownership leads to higher perceived privacy risks in social commerce [34], when consumers are compensated for providing their personal information to marketers, they lower their privacy control expectations due to the belief that marketers have a legitimate co-ownership right to control and use their information [35]. Therefore, in the personalized advertising context, we expect that the higher consumers perceive the co-ownership of personal data with the platform, the more accepting they will be to the personalization practices on their data by the platform, and the higher the perceived privacy with the platform.

## 2.3. Personification and perceived privacy

Since CPM was originally developed under the interpersonal, rather than human–computer interaction context [14], it is important to un derstand to what degree users treat computers as human beings. Personification demonstrates how much we feel inanimate objects are human-like [36]. Delbaere, McQuarrie, & Phillips [37] discussed how personification in printed advertisement could deliver positive results. By portraying a product like a person engaging in human behavior, the ad encourages viewers to anthropomorphize, i.e., treating the product like a person. Facebook utilizes several techniques that encourage users to anthropomorphize: it asks you what you are thinking about every time you log in, remembers your birthday every year, and gives a place for you to share, show of, rant, or complain. Since human relationships are based on attributions of personality and emotional connections, triggering an anthropomorphic response would help consumers to attribute increased personality and develop stronger emotional connections with the ad [37]. Research shows that personification metaphors make positive emotional responses to the brand more probable, produce more positive attributions of brand personality, and ultimately increased liking for the brand in printed ads [37,38]. Personification enhances customer relationship development and customer engagement in social media [39]. Personification in intelligent personal assistants such as Alexa leads to higher customer loyalty and satisfaction [40]. When users perceive the social media platform such as Facebook as a person that they regularly share ideas and thoughts with, they are more likely to accept personalized ads as an enthusiastic acquaintance making recommendations for them. Therefore, the higher the personification users perceive the social media platform, the more likely they are to tolerate personalized ads and have less concerns over privacy.

H2. Personification is positively related to perceived privacy.

## 2.4. Internal data source and perceived privacy

Once users share their personal information with the social media platform, their personal information efectively goes into the collective privacy boundary, which the user and the social media platform coowns [24]. Co-ownership is accompanied by certain implicit rights for the social media platform, which justifies their use of the data [35].

Thus, if ad personalization is based on data internal to the collective privacy boundary, users will assume that it is within the social media platform’s rights to use the data, without invoking concerns for privacy. In this scenario, information within the collective privacy boundary does not permeate through the boundary or exposed to any third parties, ensuring privacy [24]. On the other hand, if ad personalization is based on data external to the collective privacy boundary, e.g., data from third parties like Google search, purchasing history at Amazon, etc., boundary turbulence is likely to occur. Diferent types of internet platforms evoke diferent degrees of perceive privacy invasion as users disseminate information to each platform for diferent purposes [41]. Therefore, we posit that personalization based on data within the collective privacy boundary would be positively related to perceived privacy.

H3. Personalization based on data internal to the collective privacy boundary is positively related to perceived privacy.

## 2.5. Ad embarrassment and perceived privacy

Prosser [42] argued that unwanted disclosure of embarrassing information of others constitutes privacy invasion. Robbins and Parlavecchio [43] saw the revelation to an audience of something that one prefers to keep hidden as the root of embarrassment. McCreary [44] observes that today’s highly eficient data gathering and disseminating mechanism have made our previous idea of privacy as a keep of personal information secret obsolete. Instead, he argues for privacy in today’s world as being a feature of the social contract that keeps boundaries in social interactions: one that every culture has negotiated for itself over time in order to preserve dignity, civility, and cohesion [44]. He used the small-town example to illustrate this: in a small town, everyone knows a lot about everyone else, but all tacitly agrees to pretend not to know certain things that are deemed as sensitive that might cause embarrassment. If users are aware that the ads are personalized for them based on their personal information, seeing an embarrassing ad (e.g., a marriage counseling ad for someone who is hiding his/her marital problems) would feel that the implicit social boundary of preserving dignity, civility, and cohesion to be broken [44], and that they have less information control [45,46], leading to lower perceived privacy.

H4. Ad embarrassment is negatively related to perceived privacy.

## 2.6. Perceived privacy and ad attitude

Perceived privacy could afect attitude toward the personalized ad both cognitively and afectively. With high perceived privacy, users evoke positive cognitive responses via increased trusting belief and less perceived risk; whereas low perceived privacy elicits negative emotions such as stress, frustration, and fear [47]. Morris, Woo, Geason, & Kim [48] found that cognitive and afective responses to the ad, particularly afective response, are powerful predictors of ad attitude. With higher perceived privacy, the disposition to trust also increases [49–51]. Thus, we postulate that perceived privacy would be positively related to attitude toward the ad.

H5. Perceived privacy is positively related to ad attitude.

## 2.7. Ad attitude and purchase intention

The theory of planned behavior [52] views attitude as a key driver of behavior intentions. Attitude afects consumers’ purchase decisionmaking process [53], and diferent perceptions towards the ad lead to diferent levels of purchase intention [54]. In a meta-analysis of 47 papers published in top journals, ad attitude is found to have stable positive direct relationships with purchase intentions. On the one hand, ad attitudes can afect purchase intentions directly. When afective responses are evoked, consumers may decide what they would buy based on ad attitudes only, without completely processing all brand information [55,56]. On the other hand, Ad attitudes can also influence purchase intentions indirectly through brand attitude [55,57,58]. Accordingly, we posit that:

H6. Ad attitude is positively related to purchase intention.

## 2.8. Personification and co-ownership perceptions as moderators

As the principles of CPM were developed with human interactions as the context [24], the efectiveness of CPM in explaining users’ atti tudes toward online privacy hinges upon how much users really treat computers like a person as the media equation theory predicts [28]. We argue that the more users view the social media platform as a person, the stronger the positive efect between personalization from internal data sources to privacy and the weaker the negative efect between ad embarrassment and perceived privacy. This is because that with per sonification, users develop emotional connections to the social media platform [37]: they view the social media platform as a person who they share their ideas and thoughts with, which is similar to friendship. When users see the social media platform as a friend, they are susceptible to the relationship-enhancing biases, which lead the individual to interpret their relationship partner’s behaviors in a way that assumes those behaviors are guided by good intentions [59]. They exaggerate the importance of partners’ positive actions and minimizing the importance of negative behaviors [60]. The relationship-enhancing biases occur in intimate relationships, friendships, and even acquaintanceship (compared with strangers) [59]. Therefore, we expect personification to moderate the relationship between personalized ads from internal data sources to privacy, so that the higher the personification, the stronger the positive effect between personalized ads from internal data sources to perceived privacy due to the exaggeration of positive outcomes in relationship-enhancement bias. Similarly, we expect high personifica tion to weaken the negative efect between ad embarrassment and perceived privacy due to the minimizing of negative behaviors in relationship-enhancement bias.

H7a. Personification moderates the positive relationship of personalized ads from internal data sources to perceived privacy in such a way that it strengthens the positive relationship when personification is high.

H7b. Personification moderates the negative relationship of ad embarrassment and perceived privacy in such a way that it weaken the negative relationship when personification is high.

Co-ownership may also moderate the relationship between internal data sources and ad embarrassment to privacy. Users that view the data they shared with Facebook as solely their own may not think that the data have permeated into the collective information boundary and have a higher possibility of considering the data as still within their own private information boundary [24]. With co-ownership comes certain implicit rights for the social media platform, which justifies their use of the data [35]. Co-ownership leads to more willingness to share information [61]. From the user perspective, without co-ownership, however, the social media platform could not claim the right to use their data for personalization. Figure 2 Therefore, for users with low coownership perceptions, even the personalized ads are based on shared data, they would still think their privacy is not well protected; whereas for users with high co-ownership perceptions, they are likely to feel less concerned with the personalization practices based on the shared data. Similarly, when the ad embarrasses, for users with low co-ownership perception, this may trigger a double reaction of social boundary and information boundary violation: the use of information that does not belong to the social media platform and the use of socially taboo topics, leading to higher levels of privacy concerns; while for users with high co-ownership perceptions, they are likely to be less ofended by the social media platform’s use of the embarrassing data, since the social media may also claim ownership on it.

H8a. Co-ownership moderates the positive relationship of personalized ads from internal data sources to perceived privacy in such a way that it strengthens the positive relationship when co-ownership is high.

H8b. Co-ownership moderates the negative relationship of ad embarrassment and perceived privacy in such a way that it weakens the negative relationship when co-ownership is high.

## 3. Research Methodology

## 3.1. Procedures and Participants

We adopted the survey method, and data collection was contracted to an online market research firm in Taiwan that has over 200,000 active members. Invitations to participate were sent out via email to randomly selected members and participants were compensated with “survey cash,” which could be converted into monetary compensation. We first explained to them that this research was about user’s responses to Facebook ads and screened those without a Facebook account. We informed the users that all Facebook ads were personalized according to the information users shared with the platform unless you specifically turn of the feature, so that users fully understood the personalized nature of the ads they saw. We then asked them whether they had turned of the personalized ad feature of on their Facebook page. Only those that had the personalized ad feature turned on were allowed to continue to ensure that all the ads viewed were personalized. Participants were then instructed to log in to their Facebook page and pay specific attention to the first advertisement on the right of their Facebook page. Then, they were asked to fill out questionnaires regarding the advertisement that they just saw and their feelings and attitudes towards personalized advertisements. Finally, we asked them what the ad was about and what the brand was. The ads reported covered a wide range of products and services, including notebooks, travel agencies, cosmetics, credit cards, motorcycles, hotels, etc. We received a total of 425 responses, of which 349 were valid ones without missing data. Table 1 summarizes the demographic information of our participants. The sample comprised 52.6% women and 47.4% men, with the majority of people aged 30–39 (29.9%) and 20–29 (25.0%), respectively. ## 3.2. Measurements

Scales were adopted from prior literature when available. To ensure translation quality and meaning equivalency, two-way translation were performed by two bilingual specialists [62]. Unless otherwise specified, all the measures in the questionnaires used 5-point Likert scales, with 1 indicating “strongly disagree” and 5 indicating “strongly agree.”

Measurement items for information co-ownership were adopted from Jarvenpaa, & Staples [63]. The measures for internal data source were custom-developed based on Facebook data practices. Items for personification were from Delbaere, et al. [37]. Ad embarrassment items were from Apsler [64]. We measured perceived privacy as an individual’s self-assessed state in which external agents have limited access to information about him or her. The items were adapted from Diney, et al. [45]. We adopted measures of purchase intention from

Park, & Kim [65]. The measurements for ad attitude were adopted from MacKenzie, et al. [58]. Based on previous research on online purchase intention and the recommendations of our anonymous reviewer, we controlled for respondents’ evaluation of brand reputation [66], perceived quality [67], and perceived price [68], as well as respondents age, gender, income, and frequency of Facebook usage. The complete survey items and sources are listed in Appendix A.

Perceived privacy was positively related to ad attitude, and ad attitude was positively related to purchase intention, supporting H5 and H6.

## 5.1. Theoretical Implications

Overall, we investigated the boundary perspective (information boundary and social boundary) and relationship perspective (co-ownership relation and human–computer relation) on perceived privacy and ad efectiveness. Our research contributes to literature in several ways.

First, based on the collective information boundary concept in CPM, we defined and operationalized the concept of collective information boundary as data that users have explicitly shared with Facebook, in forms such as posts, profiles, likes, and messages. This boundary rule helps to clarify the murky boundary conditions in personalized ads and provide clear guidance as what should be used in personalized ads in a privacy-preserving manner.

Second, while CPM argues for explicitly negotiated or implicit privacy rules for each piece of shared information, it has not specified how implicit rules are formed. We proposed the social boundary as one of the implicit rules. The social boundaries preserve people’s dignity, civility and cohesion [44], and thus enhances privacy perceptions, especially for those with low co-ownership and personification perceptions. This echoes with prior research that sensitive information tends to lead to lower disclosure intentions [80] and higher privacy concerns [81]. Understanding the social boundaries helps us develop a more nuanced understanding of what can or cannot be used in personalized advertising.

Third, we expanded CPM and its applicability in the online context by exploring the role of personification and co-ownership in perceived privacy. The results highlight the roles personification and co-ownership play, confirming the applicability of CPM in social media. These two relationship constructs provide a new perspective to examine privacy concerns on social media. They illustrate the efects of relationships on perceived privacy and resonate with the media equation theory [28], confirming people’s innate tendencies to treat computers as humans and develop relationships with them.

Fourth, with the investigation of personification and co-ownership as moderators to the boundary conditions, we further validated the fluid nature of privacy boundaries online and the subjective nature of boundary fluidity [24]. While there are certain explicit or implicit rules for boundary keeping, all these rules, and their application, are subject to the perceived relationship between the users and the social media platform. This evidences the complexity of online privacy and calls for our approach to privacy from multiple perspectives via an interactional lens.

## 5.2. Managerial Implications

For practitioners, we ofer the following suggestions based on our research. First, practitioners of personalized ads should develop the awareness that, among the huge quantities of data that they have access to about users, there is information that is acceptable to be used in personalized advertising, versus information that should not be used in personalized advertising. There is an invisible boundary between the information that you know about users, that the information that the users implicitly allow you to use. It is important not to trespass col lective information boundaries and social boundaries and to keep a rapport relationship with your advertising audience.

Specifically, it is suggested that personalized ads should be based on information shared to the social media platform, instead of external sources such as website browsing history and search history to preserve privacy, as our data show that personalized advertisements that are based on internal data sources are better received: users think they have more privacy, develop better ad attitudes, and are more likely to ex press intentions to purchase the product/service advertised.

Third, cultivate co-ownerships with users. Despite the fact that users are required to sign an agreement with social media platforms to grant co-ownership of their data when they create their social media accounts, our data show that users vary in their perceptions of co-ownership of their data with the social media platform. The majority of users do not feel very strongly that Facebook could claim a co-ownership of their data. Our research shows that the perception of co-ownership helps to alleviate privacy concerns and enhance ad attitude and purchase intentions. Therefore, how to increase co-ownership percep tion will be an important issue for social media platforms that hope to enhance their users’ experiences and responses with personalized ads.

Finally, be like a person and develop an emotional connection with users. The more users think of the social media platform as a person, the more tolerant they are towards practices involving privacy concerns. Therefore, social media platforms should invest in soliciting the feeling of anthropomorphism and building connections with users. Instead of a profit-seeking business, presenting the social media platform as a caring person, a friend that listens to all you want to say will help you to mitigate privacy concerns and achieve better results for personalized advertising.

## 5.3. Limitation and Future Research

Our research has several limitations that, in turn, may be avenues for future research. First, although we proposed and defined the col lective information boundary and social boundary, we only tested several conditions that fall into the scope of these two boundaries, rather than the gamut of them. That is, embarrassing topics may only be one condition of the social boundary, there is still much to be explored about the complete scope of these two conditions. Therefore, future research could further explore what collective information boundary and social boundary consist of. Second, personalized ads can be applied in various platforms, such as news portals, e-commerce websites, social media, or search engines. Personalized ads at diferent platforms may likely generate diferent levels of privacy perceptions. We only focused on personalized ads on social media, and did not investigate how per ceptions of privacy vary on other platforms. Future research only further explores the possible variations of privacy on diferent platforms. Finally, our dataset was taken from a single country, thus, our sample may exhibit country- or culture-specific preferences for privacy, as privacy is related to cultural values and may difer internationally [82]. Therefore, future research could draw samples from other countries with different culture backgrounds and introduce culture dimensions as variables.
