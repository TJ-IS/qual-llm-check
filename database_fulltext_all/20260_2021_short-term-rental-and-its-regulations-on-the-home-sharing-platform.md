# Short-term rental and its regulations on the home-sharing platform

Yuanyuan Chen<sup>a</sup>, Yuxin Huang<sup>b,</sup>\*, Chuan Hoo Tan<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, Statistics, and Management Science, The University of Alabama, AL, USA

<sup>b</sup> Department of Management Science and Engineering, Tongji University, Shanghai, China

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems and Analytics, National University of Singapore, Singapore

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Sharing economy Short-term rental Airbnb Regulation

## A B S T R A C T

Home-sharing platforms, such as Airbnb, Inc., serve as a peer-to-peer (P2P) marketplace for users to rent out empty lodging spaces to others for a short-term period. The concerns over the disruptive nature of such home sharing platforms, particularly on the traditional industries and the neighborhood in which the lodgings are provided, have resulted in lawmakers imposing or considering to impose short-term rental (STR) regulations. This research, which anchors on the public interest theory and the prospect theory, examines the overall e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation and identi<sup>fi</sup>es e<sup>f</sup>ective individual regulatory passages on the supply side of P2P home-sharing platforms. Our subsequent empirical analysis results suggest that the regulatory intervention is less likely to restrain the growth of the home-sharing economy in the long-run even though there is a short-term deterrent e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation on new entrants to the market. Di<sup>f</sup>erent regulatory passages exert varying e<sup>f</sup>ects on the new entrants and the exit of the incumbent hosts. In speci<sup>fi</sup>c, three regulatory passages, namely, cap, standards, and eviction restrictions, have a parsimonious e<sup>f</sup>ect on containing both the new listing and the delisting. This study not only advances our understanding of the emergent sharing economy in the space of STR services but also provides insights into the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of STR regulatory passages in containing STR activities.

## 1. Introduction

Home-sharing platforms, such as Airbnb, Inc., HomeAway, Inc., VRBO, and CouchSur<sup>fi</sup>ng International, serve as a digital peer-to-peer (P2P) marketplace for users to rent out empty lodging spaces to others for a short-term rental (STR) [1,2]. Although the home-sharing plat forms provide massive e<sup>fi</sup>ciencies and create economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in certain respects [3], they also disrupt the existing property and hospi tality industries and create disturbance to the neighborhood in which the lodgings are provided [4,5]. The concerns with the STR activities have consequently resulted in lawmakers considering regulating these activities. Lawmakers in several cities, such as New York and San Francisco, have even proceeded to impose STR regulations in varying forms [6–9]. Given the rapid development of the home-sharing platforms and the high interest in STR regulations, insights into STR regulation and the regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ect on the P2P home-sharing economy are very helpful both to lawmakers and industries. Legalizing a new economy through regulations can be tricky [10]. For example, much of the discussion on regulating the P2P home-sharing economy is to control the supply-side participants of the market (i.e., hosts of STR listings). Inappropriate regulation (i.e., over- or under-regulate the supply side of the market), however, can impede the organic growth of innovation in an emerging industry like STR. This could hinder society from bene<sup>fi</sup>ting from digital innovation [11,12]. The question then, which is of interest to this research, is what would be the appropriate STR regulation?

Our review of the extant literature suggests that despite the interest from the lawmakers to regulate home-sharing activities, no comprehensive empirical study has examined STR laws and their e<sup>f</sup>ects in detail. The bulk of our current understanding remains largely focused on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and the disturbance of the STR to the society, neigh borhoods, and the existing industries (e.g., hotels, tourism, long-term rentals, and housing markets) [2,10,13–17]. Empirical evaluations of STR and its regulation, however, are surprisingly still at the embryonic stage. We found a few conceptual-theoretical and normative essays that discuss in general sense the role of government intervention as a potential approach to adjust the STR industry [13,17,18]. A detailed guideline of an STR regulation that can practically guide the government remains unclear.

Our study, hence, aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this research gap by conducting a comprehensive empirical analysis of the impacts of STR regulation on the supply-side participants of the market (i.e., hosts of STR listings). We focus on the supply-side participants of the market since they are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to be a<sup>f</sup>ected by the STR regulation: hosts may be deterred from entering the market because operations costs and legal compliance costs increase after the implementation of an STR regulation. As a result, the number of listings and users of the home-sharing platforms (both consumers and hosts) decreases, which, in turn, hampers the home-sharing economy growth. We seek to gain a comprehensive empirical understanding of the impact of STR regulation with the fol lowing two considerations in mind. First, we assess the impact of the STR regulation beyond the immediate market response by considering a longer observation period (i.e., with a dataset that has an observation period of two years from November 2014 to November 2016). It is plausible that the initial deterrent e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation might fade out, and thus, this degrades the original intent of imposing STR regulation. Second, based on our on-ground understanding, STR regulations could vary with di<sup>f</sup>erent regulatory passages, which may result in varying impacts on STR activities. Thus, a more detailed understanding of the e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulation could be achieved by examining, at a more granular level, the impacts of individual regulatory passages. With these considerations in mind, we empirically seek answers to the following two research questions:

(a) How does STR regulation afect the supply-side of STR markets (i.e., number of new listings and delisted properties)?

## (b) Which regulatory passages are efective in regulating STR activities?

Our empirical analysis is governed by a theoretical framework that we built to theorize hosts market entry and exit decisions under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of STR regulation. This theoretical framework is anchored on the public interest theory [19] and the prospect theory [20], which have been used by the behavioral law and economics scholars to study the regulatory impacts on individuals’ behavior using the expected utility calculation. Public interest theory predicts that STR regulations can in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ market entry and exit decisions through imposing compliance costs, which reduce the hosts’ expected utility of listings [21]. Prospect theory adds psychological factors into this utility-based decision model and posits that individuals’ perceptions of institutional environments and loss aversion attitude in<sup>fl</sup>uence the expected utility calculation regarding the market entry [22]. Implementing a stringent regulatory of entry leads hosts to weigh legal compliance costs and risks of loss more heavily than gains from the market. We incorporate these psychological arguments in the theoretical framework and construct hypotheses regarding the impacts of STR regulation on hosts’ market entry.

We subsequently validate the hypotheses using a set of data collection to assess how e<sup>f</sup>ective the regulatory passage is on the supply side of STR markets. By doing these, our study a<sup>f</sup>ords four major contributions to the information systems (IS) research and sharing economy literature. First, our theoretical framework anchors and integrates the public interest theory and the prospect theory to theorize the impact of STR regulation on digital economies. Examining how to regulate innovation to foster technology development requires us to combine legal, economics, and information technology (IT) perspectives. The theoretical framework developed in this study can be adopted to examine the regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ects on IT innovations and di<sup>f</sup>erent types of digital platform economies.

Second, this research is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst batch of studies that empirically examine the short-term and long-term regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulation on the P2P sharing economy and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, on the supply side of STR marketplaces. Additionally, our study complements the existing few works that focus on either the general e<sup>f</sup>ect of the regulation or a speci<sup>fi</sup>c regulatory passage (i.e., taxation) by empirically examining the regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ects of di<sup>f</sup>erent regulatory passages (e.g., license, standard requirement, restricting property use for STR). This is in recognition that the variances of the compliance costs imposed by each regulatory passage could di<sup>f</sup>er. Our research <sup>fi</sup>lls the research gaps and provides a comprehensive understanding of di<sup>f</sup>erent aspects of STR regulation.

Third, our study has important policy implications. Lawmakers need to be informed of the consequences of regulation to identify the appropriate mix of regulatory passages. Evaluating a regulation entails an empirical inquiry after it has been implemented to determine how it has changed behavior. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our empirical study make viable recommendations about how to regulate the supply-side participants behavior, helping lawmakers in choosing the “right” policy regulating the market. Additionally, the study provides shreds of evidence to address the enduring debate on whether city governments, who serve as lawmakers for the geographical regions they are responsible for, should intervene in the market and how to formulate an e<sup>f</sup>ective STR regulation [13,18,23–27].

Lastly, our study provides managerial implications for entrepreneurs and digital platform proponents. Understanding the impacts of regulation is critical for them to know how to take non-market strategies such as political competition to compete in the markets, especially in the face of increasing government interventions [28]. Our empirical analysis results indicate that the negative impact of STR regulation on the growth of the home-sharing market becomes insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in the long term, suggesting that constructive cooperation with lawmakers is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to digital platform companies and users.

## 2. Related literature

## 2.1. Two-sides of sharing economy

The sharing economy literature has provided several fronts of understanding of its economic impacts [14,15] and social impacts $[ 2 , 1 3 , 1 6 , 1 7 , 2 7 ]$

From a positive perspective, the sharing economy can provide the supply and demand sides with relevant, lucrative bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. For example, in the housing context, sharing ownership can shift some burdens of ownership from the hosts by renting out part of their living space to transient visitors [11]. Burtch, Carnahan and Greenwood [15] reported that the entry of the sharing economy platform would provide unemployed or underemployed individuals with a choice of starting en trepreneurial activities. Meanwhile, sharing economy platforms generally provide lower prices than market alternatives. Home-sharing platforms, such as Airbnb, compete directly with low-tier hotels [14], while car-sharing companies o<sup>f</sup>er lower hiring prices than licensed taxi services [29]. Environmentally, sharing is less resource-exhaustive because consumption does not require new goods or facilities. The sharing economy can help in the e<sup>fi</sup>cient use of resources, such as vacant housing units [30]. The desire to connect with others socially can also motivate people to participate in sharing. Residents and tourists can strengthen cultural exchanges by staying together.

However, sharing economies also face several issues, such as disturbance to the existing industries, insurance, and neighborhood environment [31]. For example, Zervas, Proserpio and Byers [14] examined how STR activities will a<sup>f</sup>ect the traditional hospitality industry. They found that the hotel industry, particularly low-tier hotels, will su<sup>f</sup>er from revenue lost with the increasing popularity of the STR business in the area. Lee [32] found that the STR business will exacerbate the housing crisis in San Francisco by removing the housing stock from the long-term rental market to the short-term rental market. From the customers’ perspective, their stay in STR properties will have no guarantee, that is, self-hosted accommodation will increase the complexity of customer protection compared with that in licensed hotels [18].

The current literature shows that the sharing economy exerts important impacts on society. Nevertheless, most studies have focused on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c e<sup>f</sup>ects of the sharing economy on a particular market or industry, such as the traditional accommodation industry [14]. The discussion on how the sharing economy is contained and managed remains unclear. In the current study, we focus on the STR industry and discuss how STR regulation can a<sup>f</sup>ect the property hosts’ behavior of

listing their homes for STR.

## 2.2. Regulating the sharing economy

To address the concerns on sharing economy, some researchers have proposed approaches, such as providing the product and service review sites or online reputation systems [13,18]. In the context of the STR, platforms, such as Airbnb, have established a reputation system to reduce information asymmetry between customers and hosts. The feedback system aims to maintain records of historical reviews and feedback, thereby providing additional information to potential customers and ensuring that unquali<sup>fi</sup>ed listings in the market are known to potential customers [10]. Customers can obtain additional information on the listings through external links to social network platforms, such as Facebook [13]. However, online rating systems can be <sup>fl</sup>awed, that is, ratings and reviews can be biased, and reviews may be written by customers whose experience diverges from the average experience [33]. The ratings may also be manipulated to impress customers [18].

Another approach that some other researchers suggest is to impose STR regulation [13,17,18]. City governments can rely on regulations to achieve customer protection and incumbent market protection [10]. Koopman, Mitchell and Thierer [17] argued that STR regulation should adapt to accommodate the realities in the industry if lawmakers aim to achieve customer protection. It is added that imposing taxation is universally adopted in the traditional hospitality industry, such as hotels, and should also be weaved into STR regulation [8]. However, STR regulation may pose profound legal, political, and ethical questions. Lawmakers must understand whether sharing economy practices are worth protecting, identify the goals to regulate the STR industry, and determine whether regulation can adapt to the evolving industry during its implementation [12]. Although there are a small handful of conceptual-theoretical and normative essays discussing the role of government intervention as a potential approach to adjusting the STR industry, the comprehensive empirical evaluation of STR regulation is under-investigation. As far as we know, there is no study that has ex amined the regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ect of individual passages of STR regulation.

## 2.3. Theoretical framework and hypothesis development

The home-sharing economy is di<sup>f</sup>erent from the traditional business-to-business or business-to-consumer businesses in the way that it is a decentralized model whereby two persons interact to buy or sell services directly with each other. Therefore, the theoretical conjecture that explains the impact of regulations on STR activities shall account for economic and psychological factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ decision-making and behaviors. In this regard, the theoretical under pinnings of this study are based on two theories: public interest theory and prospect theory, which have been widely used in explaining in dividuals’ decision-making under uncertainties.

Public interest theory posits that individuals are rational, trying to get the most value for their money and resources [19]. Thus, individuals’ decision to enter or exit a market depends on expected utility. Public interest theory argues that legal intervention regulates the dis tributions of pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and returns among participants in the market, thereby in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individuals’ decisions and behavior [34]. In speci<sup>fi</sup>c, entry regulation normally speci<sup>fi</sup>es procedures and standards that individuals have to comply with in order to conduct businesses in a market. Stringent regulation imposes compliance requirements and creates a heavy legal burden on market participants. The imposed regulatory requirements raise the costs of compliance and squeeze pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins [35]. An incumbent business will stay in the market if the revenue is higher than the sum of operating costs and legal compliance costs; otherwise, he will exit the market [36,37]. In the same vein, potential entrants will be deterred from venturing into the market if they perceive a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant pro<sup>fi</sup>t drop after the implementation of a regulation.

Prospect theory posits that psychological factors, such as loss aversion and perceived institutional environment, in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ calculation of expected utility. The theory argues that people are loss aversion [38]. Therefore, when choosing among several alternatives (e.g., stay in or exit a market), people avoid losses and optimize for sure wins because negative things such as compliance costs, penalties, <sup>fi</sup>nes, as well as the inconvenience are weighted more heavily psychologically than positive things in the process of decision-making [22]. Additionally, people’s perceptions of institutional environments will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the value of expected utility. By implementing a stringent regulatory of entry, governments signal a deterrent institutional environment to potential entrants and incumbents in the market, which, in turn, leads them to believe that the compliance costs and the probability of being penalized are high in a market. Such perception of the institutional environment and loss aversion attitude reduce individuals expected utility from market entry. Incumbents are more likely to exit the market while potential hosts may hesitate to enter the market. Furthermore, individuals’ perceptions of institutional environment may change after they gain experience and knowledge of legal compliance, which, in turn, in<sup>fl</sup>uences people’s loss aversion attitude. Hence, understanding how the impact of regulation on markets require us to examine both short-term and long-term regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ects.

Building on these theoretical underpinnings, we develop a set of hypotheses that focuses on STR regulation. We focus on STR regulation emblematically rather than drilling into developing individual sub-hypotheses on individual STR passages in that the referenced theories and the current literature do not lend su<sup>fi</sup>ciently sound theoretical basis for developing the predictions at such granular level of STR regulation passages. Furthermore, as mentioned in the previous section, the debate over the e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation remains, which is the primary thrust of this research. Despite so, we have performed a granular examination of the STR regulation in the empirical analysis.

## 2.3.1. Short-term efect of STR regulation

Explicitly, STR regulation can be viewed as a regulatory of entry that raises the entry barriers and the compliance requirements of STR businesses. In an STR market, new entry refers to a new listing that did not exist prior to a home-sharing platform. STR regulations create barriers to the entry of new listing through a number of regulatory approaches that include the added registration fees, the increased taxes and penalties, the expanded di<sup>fi</sup>culties and costs in permitting new operations, and the stricter regulatory standards, and hence, prohibit unquali<sup>fi</sup>ed properties from entering the market or remove them from the market. Meanwhile, these compliance requirements increase the costs to hosts, thereby reducing their incentive to list their properties on the STR platform. Consequently, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of regulation will re<sup>fl</sup>ect in the <sup>fl</sup>uctuations of the numbers of new listings on the STR platform.

Listed properties on the platforms may also be removed by their hosts because they weigh the increased compliance costs heavily psychologically and try to avoid losses. Aligned with the public interest theory, imposing STR regulation would require the hosts to comply, which can create a legal burden on them [35], and deter potential new market entrants due to loss aversion as inferred from the prospect theory [39]. A recent study by Kim, Leung and Wagman [5] has de monstrated that STR regulation could be e<sup>f</sup>ective in reducing property sales prices on Anna Maria Island, Florida. They found that the share of non-resident ownership of properties on the island decreased following the STR regulation due to the restrictions on STR rentals, thus suggesting the prohibitive impact of entry barriers. Therefore, we posit:

H1(a). STR regulations that place restrictions on property use for STR are negatively associated with the number of new listings on the homesharing platform.

H1(b). STR regulations that place restrictions on property use for STR are positively associated with the number of delisting on the homesharing platform.

## 2.3.2. Long-term efect of STR regulation

Another important question is about the longitudinal e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation. The long-term dynamics of STR regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ects on new entry can be understood through the concept of the demand-supply market equilibrium. STR prices are determined by both demand and supply of the market. Since STR regulation deters the new entrants and causes some incumbent hosts to leave the market, the reduced property supply would boost up the market prices before the market reaches a new equilibrium. As a result, hosts can earn supernormal pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, which would attract potential new entrants to enter the STR market. With increasing property supply, the market price would self-adjust downwards and eventually stay at the average pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing point, resulting in a new market equilibrium. Thus, when an STR market reaches a new equilibrium, the deterrent e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation on the new entrants will become insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant.

From the incumbent hosts’ perspective, another factor that would in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decision to remove or list their properties for STRs is the learning curve of legal compliance. The marginal costs of running STR businesses decline as the host learns by doing [35]. The more a host deals with regulatory compliance, the more the host learns which regulatory requirements apply to his/her property listings and how to e<sup>f</sup>ectively handle the requirements; and how to modify the processes best to carry out these tasks. According to the public interest theory, incumbent hosts are less likely to remove their properties from STR markets when the marginal costs of running STR businesses drop and their expected utilities from STR businesses increase. For those onlookers who are initially loss-averse due to the STR regulation, they may see the aversion to be alleviated [39,40]. In other words, compliance learning translates into lower per unit compliance costs for hosts [41].

Therefore, arguing from both the market equilibrium perspective and the learning curve perspective, one could expect that the number of new listings will eventually bounce back, whereas the initial uprising trend of the number of delisting will reverse after hosts learn how to comply with the law. Thus, we argue:

H2. The impact of STR regulation on the number of new listings and delisting will be reversed over time.

## 2.4. Regulatory approaches of STR regulation

City governments enact STR regulations based on local political priorities and social and economic values [42]. An FTC report about the sharing economy [24] summarizes the major goals of STR regulation, which include the following: (1) to standardize the process of listing properties on the platform and enhance the quality of listing properties, (2) to preserve the neighborhood quality of rented real estates, (3) to impose taxes on STR transactions, and (4) to sustain the supply and a<sup>f</sup>ordability of housing in the property market [32]. Several policy advocacies and market research companies have discussed viable ap proaches that an STR regulation shall seek to achieve the above-men tioned objectives. ## 3. Empirical analyses

## 3.1. Data collection and measures

The panel used for the empirical analysis consists of the data col lected from multiple sources. We obtained a set of data of monthly STR activities on Airbnb in the period of November 2014–November 2016 from AirDNA, LLC, an analytical data company that tracks the daily performance of Airbnb. We searched news about the regulations of short-term home sharing on the LexisNexis.com and Airbnb websites. Using a batch of keywords, including “short-term rental,” “short-term home sharing,” “room sharing,” “regulation,” “legislation,” “ordinance,” and variations with dashes, we found 15 U.S. cities that enacted tailored laws for regulating short-term home-sharing activities from November 2014 to November 2016.<sup>4</sup> The list of identi<sup>fi</sup>ed cities is the same as that in R Street’s RoomScore 2016 Reports [43] and geographically represents the distribution of STRs from the East Coast to the West Coast and from north to south of the United States of America (Table A-1 in Appendix provides detailed information of the cities). We collected the STR regulations of these cities from the city governments’ websites. Although cities have existing zoning ordinances regulating the land use and neighborhoods, our study focuses on the ordinances and laws that govern the STR activities. The city-level STR ordinances and laws were included when we coded STR regulations. We also manually checked the state laws and other city ordinances that govern STR activities. We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any state in our sample that had a statelevel-tailored STR regulation within the study period.

For each city that had a tailored STR regulation, we recorded the month and year when the city lawmakers passed and enforced the regulation. We coded a binary variable, enforce, to indicate that a city has an STR regulation. Most of the cities enacted STR regulations in 2016, thereby providing su<sup>fi</sup>cient time to investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the regulations. We also coded the regulatory passages that impose restrictions and obligations on the properties used for STR. For example, if a city’s law has a provision that requires hosts to register units to obtain a license or permit for STR, then the regulatory passage “license” was coded as “1″ for the month when the STR regulation took e<sup>f</sup>ect in the focal city and each of the following months; otherwise, it was coded as “0.″ The same coding method was applied to the other regulatory passages in Table 1.<sup>5</sup> Table A-2 provides examples of regulatory passages. To ensure coding consistency and reliability, one author of the paper and one undergraduate student who majored in IS independently carried out the coding of all the collected regulation documents. The Sustainable Economies Law Center’s guidebook was used as a reference for coding because the guidebook is comprehensive and provides detail examples of regulatory passages. We checked the inter-coder reliability for all coding and found them to be high (Cohen’s k = 0.95 or above). Another author of the paper who received law training also independently coded all the regulations following the same coding rule to ensure the reliability of the coding further. Throughout the coding process, the results of recoding were almost identical to the previous coding results. Overall, we believe that this process resulted in a highly reliable coding of the STR regulations. More than half of the cities require a li cense, standard requirements, neighborhood protection, and tax for STR activities.

Airbnb monthly dataset provides descriptive information of unit and hosts, including the zip code, address, property type, and earliest listing date in Airbnb, along with the number of days of availability, bookings, and reservations of units in each month. Given that the numbers of new listings and delisted units re<sup>fl</sup>ect the dynamics of markets [44], prior studies frequently used these two to measure the impacts of STR regulation [44]. Following the measures used by Li, Moreno and Zhang [44], we used the aggregated numbers of new list ings and delisting on Airbnb per month to measure the dynamics of the STR market for each city. The number of new listings is the sum of the listings on the Airbnb website for STR purposes, whereas the number of delisting is the sum of the units that had been previously listed for STR but were removed from Airbnb within the study period.

We counted the number of new listings based on the creation date and the <sup>fi</sup>rst date of STR availability on Airbnb. If a unit had been listed on Airbnb before but was not on the website for three consecutive months after the focal month. then this unit was considered delisted. Given that the STR regulations in our study period did not require STR platforms to remove units that did not comply with a city’s STR reg ulation, STR platforms, such as Airbnb, did not have the incentive to delist units. Hence, the delisted units in the sample were removed vo luntarily by the owners. Hosts may remove their units from the listing because they exceed the maximum number of days allowed for STR (i.e., the caps), negative reviews, complaints, lack of business, and transfer of ownership. To rule out these alternative reasons for delisting, we used reservation days to calculate the total number of days that each listed unit was rented out for STR. The number of units that were removed after reaching the caps was less than 5% of the listings in three cities. Furthermore, given that the reservation rate indicates whether units lack business, we compared the reservation rate of the delisting with the average rate of the total listings in a city. The t-test statistics do not suggest a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence in the reservation rate between the delisting and the total listings.

The number of new monthly listings in our sample ranged from 17 units to 1464 units, with a mean of 271 units and a standard deviation of 274 units, whereas the number of delisting diverged from 1 unit to 5695 units in all the studied cities, with an average of 255 units and a standard deviation of 742 units. The total listings in 15 cities ranged from 264 units to 13,428 units, with the growth rate spreading from −35 % to 62 %. Several cities, including Seattle, Philadelphia, San

Jose, and San Francisco, experienced a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant drop in the number of total listings (approximately 30 % decrease). The large standard deviations of these STR listing measures indicate that our sample includes cities with di<sup>f</sup>erent development status levels of STR markets.

We also collected a set of local demographic data to account for regional economic and social conditions that may determine the STR regulatory framework. Data on the total number of households, median household income, unemployment rate, ethnicity ratio (measured by the proportion of white people to the total population), hotels, and their number of employees were collected from the American Census Government website (Census.gov). To measure the number of hotel employees, we categorized hotel sizes from 1 (1–4 employees) to 9 (more than 1000 employees). We then multiplied hotel size and the number of hotels in each category and aggregated them as the proxy measure of the total number of employees working in a local city’s hotel industry. We collected air tra<sup>fi</sup>c data from the American Bureau of Transportation Statistics and used it as a proxy measure of a city’s shortterm visitors. A city’s crime rate indicates its safety condition, which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision of visitors to stay in hotels or P2P homesharing units. We collected crime rate data from FBI crime reports.

For each city with a tailored STR regulation, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a matching city that has a similar population size and average household income and is located in the same state. ## 3.2. The efect of STR regulation on the supply side of the market

Our hypotheses 1(a) and 1(b) are about the overall effect of STR regulation. We used a city-level dataset, which contains 711 city–month observations of 30 cities from November 2014 to November 2016. This time series, cross-sectional dataset exhibits an unbalanced structure due to the missing data of STR activities. Given that STR regulations were enforced at di<sup>f</sup>erent times, we require a data analysis technique that can account for the omitted characteristics and the time-varying confounders. The synthetic control method [45,46] provides a solution. Compared with the di<sup>f</sup>erence in di<sup>f</sup>erences (DID) and similar techni ques, the synthetic control method can account for the e<sup>f</sup>ects of timevarying confounders and match the covariates and outcomes between the treated and control units using pretreatment periods as the criteria for good matches. The synthetic control method constructs a “synthetic control $\mathrm { { \ u n i t } } ^ { \dag }$ as the counterfactual for the treated unit by weighting the control units and then reports the exact weights for the control units, which makes the comparison between the treated and synthetic control units transparent [45–47]. This method has been used in the <sup>fi</sup>elds of political science, economics, and health policy to evaluate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of an intervention in comparative case studies [48]. We used the generalized synthetic control (GSC) method proposed by Xu [47] to examine the causal inference of STR regulation on the listings on Airbnb. The GSC passage compares the evolution of an average outcome of the treated group to the evolution of the same average of the control group [47].

In our case, the treatment is the existence of a tailored STR reg ulation that regulates STR activities. We used the cities that did not enact tailored STR regulations to construct a weighted synthetic control group (i.e., counterfactual). The counterfactual represents what would have happened to the STR listings of the a<sup>f</sup>ected cities if these cities had not enacted their tailored laws to regulate STR activities. Then, we assessed the e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulation by comparing the counterfactual to the actual outcome using the following model:

$$
\delta_ {i t} D _ {i t} = Y _ {i t} - Y _ {i t} ^ {N}\tag{1}
$$

where $D _ { i t }$ is the treatment indicator, which is equal to ${ } ^ { \mathfrak { a } } 1 ^ { \prime \prime }$ if city i has implemented its tailored STR regulation at time t and equal to ${ } ^ { \ast } 0 ^ { \prime \prime }$ otherwise. $\delta _ { i t }$ is the heterogeneous treatment e<sup>f</sup>ect. $Y _ { i t }$ is the observed outcomes. Lastly, $Y _ { i t } ^ { N }$ is the no-treatment counterfactual and is estimated using the following factor model:

$$
Y _ {i t} ^ {N} = \alpha_ {i} + \xi_ {t} + x _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \beta + \lambda_ {i t} ^ {\prime} f _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{2}
$$

where $\alpha _ { i }$ and $\xi _ { t }$ are the city and time <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects, respectively; $f _ { t }$ is a vector of the unobserved time-varying common factors; $\lambda _ { i t }$ denotes city speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor loadings; $x _ { i t }$ includes a vector of the observed covariates; $\beta$ contains a vector of the parameters of the observed covariates; and ε represents the unobserved idiosyncratic shocks for unit i at time t.

We used the R package (gsynth) to estimate the average treatment e<sup>f</sup>ect on the treated (ATT) [47]. The gsynth package runs an interactive <sup>fi</sup>xed-e<sup>f</sup>ect model using the control group data to obtain $\hat { \beta }$ and then selects the model that minimizes the mean square prediction error (MSPE) to estimate ATT. We controlled for the city, month, and year <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ects and included the following observed covariates in the GSC estimation: crime rate, unemployment rate, number of hotel employees, and air tra<sup>fi</sup>c<sup>6</sup> in estimating Eq. (2). On the basis of estimates obtained from Eq. (2), we used the following model to estimate the ATTs of STR

regulation on new listings:

$$
A T T _ {t, t > T _ {0}} = \frac {1}{N _ {t r}} \sum_ {i \in \mathcal {T}} [ Y _ {i t} (1) - Y _ {i t} (0) ] = \frac {1}{N _ {t r}} \sum_ {i \in \mathcal {T}} \delta_ {i t}
$$

where N is the number of cities in the treatment group, Y (1) stands for the actual outcome, and $Y _ { i t } ( 0 )$ is the counterfactual outcome of the treated units. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 3 summarize the results for the model of new listings. No covariate is included in Column (1), whereas the city demographics and covariates of STR demands are controlled for in Column (2). The GSC procedure did not <sup>fi</sup>nd unobserved in<sup>fl</sup>uential factors with both speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. The estimated ATT on the new listings is $- 0 . 3 1 5 \ ( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), thereby suggesting a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant reduction in the number of newly added listings to the STR platform after implementing STR regulations. Therefore, H1(a) is supported. However, H1(b) is not supported as the estimated ATT on the delisting is positive but not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The result suggests that the number of delisted properties increases after STR regulation though the extent of the impact is not substantial.

Figs. 1(a) and (b) compare the average actual outcomes (solid line) with the average predicted counterfactual in the absence of STR regulations (dashed line). In Fig. 1(a) (new listings), the average actual outcome and the average predicted counterfactual match well during the pretreatment periods (i.e., before the enforcement of STR regulation) but diverged after the treatment (i.e., an STR regulation took effect). These <sup>fi</sup>gures graphically demonstrate that imposing requirements on STR hosts through legislation is e<sup>f</sup>ective in forestalling new entrants for STR. However, Fig. 1(b) shows that the line of the actual outcome of delisting overlaps with that of the predicted counterfactual, indicating no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on delisting on Airbnb.

We also ran a GSC estimation to explore the impact of STR regulation on the number of total listings, which indicates the market size. We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between STR regulation and the number of total listings. Combining the results regarding the impact on the new listing, delisting, and total listing, we deduced that STR regulation is e<sup>f</sup>ective in deterring potential new entrances but not on the incumbent hosts who have listed their properties for STR use before regulation.

As a robustness test, we also used a two-step DID econometric mode [49] as a robustness test to estimate the impacts of STR regulations passed at di<sup>f</sup>erent times. Compared with the one-step DID model that was commonly adopted in previous studies, the two-step model was selected in this study because the implementation date of STR regulations di<sup>f</sup>ered from city to city. We incorporated a linear time variable and the legislation interaction term to control for changes in regulation e<sup>f</sup>ects over time in the DID model. As shown in Table A-4 in Appendix, the DID results are consistent with the GSC estimates.

## 3.3. The efects of individual regulatory passages

H2 is about the longitudinal e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation. We constructed a zip-code-level panel dataset to examine the longitudinal ef fects of STR regulation and identify which regulatory passages exert substantial impacts on the number of new listings and delisting. We mapped the ZIP code tabulation areas (ZCTAs) to the zip-code-based neighborhoods. The new monthly listings and delisting were aggregated at the zip-code level. For each regulatory passage, we counted the number of months since a focal regulatory passage took e<sup>f</sup>ect.

The statistics of the household number, unemployment rate, and average housing price of each zip-code-based neighborhood were used to account for the demographical di<sup>f</sup>erences. Household income was removed from the estimation because too many values of this variable were missing at the zip-code level. City demographics, such as the number of hotel employees, air tra<sup>fi</sup>c, ethnicity ratio, and crime rate, were also included as control variables. Moreover, we included two additional control variables, namely, the one-time lag of STR demand, which is equal to log(average number of bookings \* average reservation days), to account for the local market demand for STR services, and STR regulation prevalence, as an indicator of the pervasiveness of STR legislation. STR regulation prevalence is denoted by the number of cities, except for the focal city with STR legislation, divided by 59,<sup>7</sup> which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision of the focal city’s lawmakers to enact a tailored city regulation but will not directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the local hosts decision to list their units. Table 4 provides the correlations of the main variables in the zip-code-level sample set. The correlations suggest no major collinearity problems among the main variables.

Our zip-code-level dataset has an unbalanced panel structure because the number of cross-sectional units (neighborhoods) is larger than the number of time points (maximum of 24 months) for each zip-code based neighborhood. Thus, we need to account for longitudinal and cross-sectional variabilities and address several estimation issues. We <sup>fi</sup>rst ran Fisher-type unit root tests based on the augmented Dickey–Fuller test to check whether the measures of STR supply are stationary. The unit root test for the dependent variable is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at p < 0.01, thereby indicating that the dependent variables are stationary [50,51]. Furthermore, the panel Durbin Watson statistic of the dependent variables is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at $\begin{array} { r } { p < 0 . 0 5 . } \end{array}$ , thereby indicating that autocorrelation is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant issue [52]. We also conducted Granger causality tests [53] to check the reverse causality between STR new listings and the number of months of STR regulatory passages. The panel VAR estimation results [54] suggest a rejection of the null hypothesis, which indicates that the number of months of STR regulatory passage causes changes in the new listings on Airbnb. We also checked the reverse causality between delisting and the number of months of regulatory passages. The results suggest that no reverse causality exists.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst ran a base model – a system GMM estimation using the number of months since a focal city implemented STR regulation as the explanatory variable. The base model is to demonstrate the overall longitudinal e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation. We then ran a system GMM estimation for each regulatory passage using the number of months since a focal regulatory passage took e<sup>f</sup>ect as the explanatory variable. We also included the quadratic terms as an explanatory variable. In the models of individual regulatory passages, we added a variable, namely, the extensiveness of STR regulation, to account for the potential complementary between regulatory passages because the e<sup>f</sup>ect of one passage may depend on the others. Extensiveness is equal to the total number of regulatory passages that impose requirements and restrictions on STR units in the regulation, excluding the focal passage. Y _ {i, t} = \alpha_ {0} Y _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {1} X _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} X _ {i, t} ^ {2} + \gamma^ {\prime} C o n t r o l s _ {i, t} + \mu_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}\tag{3}
$$

where Y stands for the dependent variables, log(new listings) and log (delisting), respectively; $X _ { i , t }$ is the number of months of a regulatory passage in the city i at time t; and $X _ { i , t } ^ { 2 }$ is its square term. Control variables include the total number of households, unemployment rate, average house price in the neighborhoods, city-level air tra<sup>fi</sup>c, crime rate, number of hotel employees, ethnicity ratio, extensiveness, along with the city, month, and year dummies. $\mu _ { i }$ stands for the time-invariant unobservable factors; $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is the random error; and $t = 1 , 2 , 3 ,$ T, which indicates the periods for each <sup>fi</sup>rm i. We also included a lagged value of the dependent variables in the model to account for inertia and persistence in the new listings and the delisting [55].

Several endogeneity problems are addressed in the analyses. For example, unobservable time-invariant city or neighborhood characteristics and market factors can correlate with the explanatory variables (i.e., omitted variable bias). Moreover, city legislators may self-select STR regulatory passages based on local social, economic, and cultural factors. To address these endogeneity concerns, we used the Arellano–Bover/Blundell–Bond system method of moments (GMM) passage [56,57] to estimate Eq. (3), considered the potential dynamics feature of STR listings, and eliminated the biases caused by unobservable factors and self-selection issues. The estimations of the system GMM passage helped remove the unobserved city-speci<sup>fi</sup>c timeinvariant <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ect $\mu _ { i }$ from the <sup>fi</sup>rst di<sup>f</sup>erence transformation as follows:

$$
\Delta Y _ {i, t} = \alpha_ {0} \Delta Y _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {1} \Delta X _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} \Delta X _ {i, t} ^ {2} + \gamma^ {'} \Delta C o n t r o l s _ {i, t} + \Delta \varepsilon_ {i, t}
$$

where $\Delta Y _ { i , t } = Y _ { i , t } - Y _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $\Delta \varepsilon _ { i , t } = \varepsilon _ { i , t } - \varepsilon _ { i , t - 1 }$ . We included the <sup>fi</sup>rst three lagged di<sup>f</sup>erences of the variables as GMM instruments in the estimation. In this manner, we accounted for the potential concerns caused by the correlation between the lagged dependent variables and the error terms. We regarded the explanatory variables as endogenous and the city and neighborhood characteristics as predetermined factors. The XTABOND2 routine in STATA was used to separately estimate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulatory passages on the number of new listings and the delisting.

The analysis results of the models with new listing as the dependent variable are reported in Table $^ { 5 , }$ and the results of delisting as the dependent variable are shown in Table 6. The AR(2) test computed the correlations between $\Delta \varepsilon _ { i , t } = \varepsilon _ { i , t } - \varepsilon _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $\Delta \varepsilon _ { i , t - 1 } = \varepsilon _ { i , t - 1 } - \varepsilon _ { i , t - 2 }$ which indicated whether error term $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is serially correlated. The p values of all the AR(2) test results are greater than 0.05, thereby suggesting that the di<sup>f</sup>erenced error terms are not correlated. The Hansen tests of overidentifying restrictions veri<sup>fi</sup>ed the validity of the GMM instruments used in the estimations. The $p$ values of all the Hansen test results are above 0.10, which indicates the validity of the GMM instruments.

Column (1) of Table 5 shows the GMM estimation of the base model. The results exhibit a curvature relationship between STR regulation and new listings (Column (1) in Table 5). The linear e<sup>f</sup>ect coe<sup>fi</sup>cient is negatively signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( - 0 . 0 3 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , whereas the squared term is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (0.002, $p < 0 . 0 1 \AA$ , thereby indicating that the number of new entrants in the STR platform initially dropped after the enforcement of a tailored STR regulation. ## 3.4. Robustness tests

We conducted several tests to further assess the robustness of the long-term e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulation and individual clauses. First, the dependent variables in our sample may contain an abnormal situation. We used the <sup>fi</sup>ve percentile Winsor data of residuals to remove the extreme values and rerun the system GMM estimations. We also used Windmeijer [58]’s method to correct the downward bias of the variance of the two-step estimators in the system GMM and control the <sup>fi</sup>nite sample bias and heteroscedasticity. We further included additional non-GMM-generated instrumental variables in the estimations to check whether the analysis results would change if we used a di<sup>f</sup>erent set of instrumental variables [59,60]. We used two additional instrumental variables in the system GMM models: (a) R Street’s total score of STR regulations to represent the city government’s openness to commerce conducted through STR platforms and (b) Google trend data for determining the trend of STR in a focal city, which indicates the popularity and bandwagon e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR activities [61]. The results of these robustness tests are consistent with the major <sup>fi</sup>ndings in Tables 5 and 6.

Furthermore, we conducted the two-stage control function estimations, which are <sup>fi</sup>tted well to address the self-selection bias [62]. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst-stage estimation, we ran a nonlinear binomial estimate (glm pro cedure) with the number of months of STR regulation as the dependent variable. We used one lag of law prevalence as an excluded variable in the <sup>fi</sup>rst-stage regression. This variable represents the percentage of cities that enacted a tailored STR regulation before the focal city and correlates with the endogenous variable (i.e., STR regulation) and the outcome variable. In the second stage, we ran a regression (xtreg procedure) of STR regulation on the number of new listings. The results are consistent with those from the system GMM estimations. Hence, our results are robust after controlling for possible endogeneity issues. Tables A-5 and A-6 in Appendix report the estimated coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of the explanatory variables in these robustness tests.

## 3.5. Post hoc analyses

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported in Tables 5 and 6 o<sup>f</sup>er unequivocal evidence, demonstrating the e<sup>f</sup>ects of di<sup>f</sup>erent STR regulatory passages on the supply side of STR markets. From the lawmaker’s perspective, understanding how STR regulation a<sup>f</sup>ects STR activities in di<sup>f</sup>erent neighborhoods is a much more relevant question for them to enact appropriate zoning ordinances for di<sup>f</sup>erent neighborhoods. The physical conditions of the housing units, geographic characteristics, and concentrated use of the facilities in the area are important perspectives of neighborhood quality [63]. The units in a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods are more likely to provide high-quality accommodations for short-term visitors [64,65]. Housing price is frequently used to measure neighborhood quality because economic theory suggests that high-quality neighborhoods will have high bid rents [66]. Hence, we divided the zip code-based dataset into two subsets based on the average housing prices in neighborhoods and separately performed an econometric analysis on these two datasets to account for the variance in di<sup>f</sup>erent regions. If a neighborhood’s average housing price is above the city’s average, then this neighborhood is categorized as an a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhood; otherwise, it is a non-a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhood [64,65]. Among the 296 neighborhoods in 15 cities, 123 are a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods, whereas 173 are located in non-a<sup>fl</sup>uent areas.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst graphically contrast the impacts of STR regulation in two types of neighborhoods in Fig. 2. This <sup>fi</sup>gure shows that approximately six months were necessary for the new STR entrants to regain a growing trend of new listings in a<sup>fl</sup>uent areas compared with 11 months in nona<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods. The result suggests that the deterrent e<sup>f</sup>ect of STR regulation on new entrants is more notable on housing properties in non-a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods.

We then proceeded to use the system GMM method to analyze the impacts of di<sup>f</sup>erent regulatory passages in these two types of neighborhoods (see Tables 7 and 8). An interesting observation from Table 7 is that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the license, standard requirement, and tax clauses on new listings in non-a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and depict a U-shaped relationship. By contrast, these three clauses do not exhibit a substantial e<sup>f</sup>ect on a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods. Table 8 shows that two regulatory passages, standard requirement, and conversion restriction have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on the number of delisting in non-a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods but not in the a<sup>fl</sup>uent ones. Given that these clauses will increase the hosts’ monetary and time costs in operating STR units, the results suggest that the cost concern is a major factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the decision operating on the STR platform for the hosts having properties in non-a<sup>fl</sup>uent neighborhoods who are more cost-sensitive than those in the a<sup>fl</sup>uent areas.

Understanding which mechanisms or elements of STR regulations will in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ market entry and exit decisions allows us to derive the recommendations about the optimum design of STR regulations. Table 9 summarizes the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the individual passages.

As shown in Table 9, three regulatory passages, cap, standards, and eviction restrictions, not only restrain the growth of new listing but also force some incumbent hosts to delist their properties from STR markets. Cap restricts the number of days that a property can be used for STR, thereby reducing the expected revenue from STR operations. Standards impose pre-requisites of license, while most of the pre-requisites are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in multiple municipal laws and ordinances, including the ordinances of <sup>fi</sup>re, health, and building safety. The costs of complying with these standards increase because hosts have to obtain permits from di<sup>f</sup>erent city government agencies $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ <sup>fi</sup>re department and Housing and Urban Development Department) and pay all applicable fees. Similarly, eviction restriction also raises the costs of market entry because it requires hosts to prove that their properties meet the criteria stipu lated in the related city ordinances. Except for the increased monetary costs, it is also challenging for the majority to understand how the compliance processes interact and how to meet all legal requirements in the related laws and ordinances. As hypothesized in H1, potential new entrants could be deterred from the market entry because their expected revenue from STR operations is uncertain while the start-up cost is high. Therefore, these three regulatory passages are e<sup>f</sup>ective me chanisms to regulate the STR markets.

We found that the other six commonly used regulatory passages have di<sup>f</sup>erent impacts on new listings and delisting: license, neighborhood protection, taxation, and conversion restriction have prominent e<sup>f</sup>ects on constraining the growth of new listings but not on delisting; however, host stay and record-keeping work on managing the number of delisting but not on new listings. The contrasting e<sup>f</sup>ects can be explained using our theoretical framework. Complying with license, neighborhood protection, and conversion restriction constitutes upfront and immediate costs for property hosts. The upfront and immediate costs may become sunk costs if the properties do not generate revenue from STR. In contrast, the costs of host stay and record-keeping are implicit and non-monetary costs (e.g., inconvenience, time, and e<sup>f</sup>ort required for record-keeping, etc.) that happen in the process of STR operations. As immediate costs and monetary expenses are more salient and easily calculated than the implicit and non-monetary costs, individuals weight the upfront monetary costs heavily, thereby reducing their incentives to enter STR markets. This is consistent with the pre diction based on public interest theory and prospect theory.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that taxation is not e<sup>f</sup>ective in regulating the number of delisting. An explanation for this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that incumbent hosts realize that they can bypass the tax to consumers by subsuming tax rate into the STR pricing. Also, as tax is only payable when revenue is earned, it is more of a question of whether one gets su<sup>fi</sup>cient business rather than the tax that hosts need to pay.

Overall, our key <sup>fi</sup>nding from the empirical research is that while some passages (e.g., license) are restrictively related to the number of new listings or the number of delistings, three passages, namely, cap, standards, and eviction restrictions, are related to both outcome vari ables. This raises the question of whether these three passages would be able to su<sup>fi</sup>ciently and parsimoniously contain the STR activities, which could be essential for STR regulation. Explicitly, we would need to answer whether the remaining passages, which have di<sup>f</sup>erentiated e<sup>f</sup>ects on the new listings and delisting, are needed considering that there is enforcement cost incurred for each additional passage in the STR regulation. While it is beyond this study to consider the enforcement cost of each passage, we would like to put forward the inquiry of whether it would be better and more e<sup>fi</sup>ciently served to have a parsimonious set of passages that have encompassing restraints on the STR activities.

## 4.1. Contribution to research

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, we develop a market entry decision-making framework that included psychological factors (e.g., loss aversion and perception of the institutional environment) in the expected utility-based framework to study the impacts of STR regulation on hosts’ behavior. The theoretical framework can be adopted to examine the regulatory e<sup>f</sup>ects on IT innovations and other types of digital platform economies.

Second, it elucidates the e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulation and its ability to contain and regulate STR activities on the STR mediatory platform. We add to the IS literature in the aspect of law and economics, with empirical evidence suggesting that regulation has the power to in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ market entry and exit decisions in online transactions.

Third, the focus of this study is to investigate the role of STR regulation in restricting STR businesses in the long term. The existing lit erature regarding STR regulation comprises conceptual-theoretical and normative essays that discuss the role of government intervention in general. No comprehensive empirical study has examined the shortterm and long-term impacts of STR regulation on the market. We advanced the understanding of the role of government intervention in regulating the home-sharing economy. STR regulation is frequently discussed as restrictions designed to “crackdown” the industry. However, the empirical results suggest that STR regulation is not going to hamper the growth of the STR markets in the long-run. Instead, government intervention might deter those unquali<sup>fi</sup>ed properties from the market through standard requirements, thereby enhancing the quality of listing properties. Several regulatory passages such as cap, eviction restriction, and standard requirements might help in sustaining the supply and a<sup>f</sup>ordability of housing in the property market.

Fourth, we added to the literature of the sharing economy and STR regulation an empirical examination of individual regulatory passages. Existing research on STR regulation mostly investigates from the legislative understanding of the regulation. However, the relationship between STR regulation and the STR business requires further investigation regarding the actual e<sup>f</sup>ects of regulation, particularly the understanding of the regulation at a granular level [32,67]. Although the literature suggests several potential measures that the city government can take to restrict the STR industry [11,32], whether these regulatory passages can achieve what the city government expects is unclear. The current study <sup>fi</sup>lls in the gap in previous studies with the empirical evaluation of the e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulation at the granular level.

## 4.2. Contribution to practice

Inquiry into the impact of STR regulations on entry and exit has not been directly examined. Inquiry into this area holds promise for increasing understanding of the complex interaction between STR regulations and the competitive dynamics of industries. This study provides a theoretical and empirical examination of the entry-deterrent e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR control requirements.

Practically, we provided empirical evidence of the e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulation on the supply side of STR markets. In particular, we analyzed the e<sup>f</sup>ects of individual regulatory passages separately and helped evaluate how these passages can in<sup>fl</sup>uence hosts’ market entry and exit decisions. The results can help city governments understand the e<sup>f</sup>ects of regulation better and provide a guideline for them to formulate their own STR regulations.

## 4.3. Limitations and suggestions for future research

Our study has certain limitations, including the di<sup>fi</sup>culty in controlling all the variances in the zip-code level of neighborhoods. We were able to control the economic perspectives of the neighborhoods and the demographics, such as the household number that accounts for the basic variance among the neighborhoods that are widely adopted in previous studies as control variables [68]. However, other factors, such as the distribution of tra<sup>fi</sup>c information or the aesthetic elements of neighborhoods, are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to observe.

Second, we used the number of new listings on the STR mediatory platform to evaluate how the regulation will a<sup>f</sup>ect the STR business based on the aggregate level, i.e., the neighborhood level of data. At the individual property level, we were unable to observe the listing beha vior of individual hosts, which can be a future direction.

Third, our study elucidates the debate regarding whether and how the STR business should be regulated. The fact that di<sup>f</sup>erent groups of people may respond to the same regulation di<sup>f</sup>erently might have been disregarded in previous studies. A future study can consider the demographics of the hosts, such as income, age, and their responses to regulation implementation.

Fourth, our data sample is in the period of November 2014–November 2016. It is during this period when there was much discussion on whether STR activities to be regulated [6–9]. Despite so, it could still be a researcher’s judgmental call when it comes to determining the period of data collection. To this end, we further elaborated on the data period. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we referred to the AirDNAs index, which indicates that the general trend of STR markets has not changed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly since November 2016.<sup>8</sup> This could add con<sup>fi</sup>dence to our selection of the data period. Despite so, we call for future studies to collect data of more recent years and examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of STR regulations on the market. To this end, this research focuses on the new listings and delistings, which are supply-side factors. Further research could also consider including additional variables, e.g., reservation rate and the average price, which are resulted from the property supply and the market demand.
