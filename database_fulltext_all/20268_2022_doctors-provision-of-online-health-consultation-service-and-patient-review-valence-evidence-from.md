# Doctors’ Provision of Online Health Consultation Service and Patient Review Valence: Evidence from a Quasi-Experiment

Shan Liu<sup>a</sup>, Hao Wang<sup>a</sup>, Baojun Gao<sup>b,</sup>\*, Zhaohua Deng<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Management, Xi’an Jiaotong University, Xi’an, 710049, China

<sup>b</sup> Economics and Management School, Wuhan University, Wuhan, 430072, China

<sup>c</sup> School of Medicine and Health Management, Huazhong University of Science & Technology, Wuhan, 430030, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: online health community expert service platform sustainability doctor–patient relationship online review

## A B S T R A C T

In this study, we focus on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of doctors’ provision of online health consultation service (OHCSV) on the review valence provided by patients. We develop a unique panel dataset based on over 3,000,000 reviews for 493,548 doctors on a leading online healthcare community in China. Coarsened exact matching and di<sup>f</sup>erencein-di<sup>f</sup>erence estimators are used to determine causality. Findings corroborate that doctors’ provision of OHCSV increases the review valence posted by patients and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly changes the valence structure by increasing and decreasing the proportion of positive and negative valences, respectively. E<sup>f</sup>ects of OHCSV also di<sup>f</sup>er among various review dimensions and doctors' hierarchical levels. Doctors at lower hierarchical levels experience stronger positive provision e<sup>f</sup>ect than their counterparts. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to literature on sustainable doctor patient relationship and expert service, and similarly generate implications for the sustainability of health platforms.

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of information technology and public interest in health triggers the rapid growth of online searches for health information. According to the Pew Research Center, 59% of American adults address their medical issues through the Internet [1]. Numerous people participate in online health communities (OHCs) to meet their health-related needs. An OHC provides a virtual space for patients to discuss diseases and post reviews about doctors’ services [2,3]. This platform reduces the long-existing information asymmetry between doctors and patients [4,5]. A recent survey indicated that more than 70% of patients selected new doctors after reading online reviews [6]. A doctor with a good online reputation as a result of patient-reported positive review valence and experience of positive treatment of his/her medical service in online reviews can attract a considerable number of patient appointments and online and o<sup>fl</sup>ine consultations [7]. By contrast, negative reviews may not only ruin doctors online reputation, but also lower patient’s intention to consult with them o<sup>fl</sup>ine and minimize the revenue of hospitals with which the doctors are associated. Thus, doctors constantly expect positive reviews from patients to demonstrate their professional capabilities and attract additional patients.

In the past, OHCs were simple websites developed for patients to report their opinions on doctors through online reviews and ratings. This function can help doctors understand their own image and assist patients in selecting suitable doctors. However, in such a platform, doctors can only communicate with patients o<sup>fl</sup>ine and cannot provide online services. Recently, OHCs underscore the importance of interac tions between doctors and patients, and encourage the former to provide the latter with online health consultation service (OHCSV). This new function enables the sustainability of the previously temporary doctor–patient relationship through online interactions. OHC managers also expect active patient participation and doctor–patient communications to maintain the vitality and sustainability of the platform [8,9].

Although OHCSV appears to help doctors and patients, most doctors do not provide this service. According to the statistical results in a leading OHC in China, more than two-thirds of doctors do not use the OHCSV function [3]. The decision of doctors to provide OHCSV often depends on their evaluation of the bene<sup>fi</sup>t they can gain with minimal costs [10,11]. The provision of OHCSV may enable doctors to develop positive review valences and reputation through reciprocal, professional service for patients [12,13]. It may also satisfy doctors’ needs for knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, leading to the delivery of favorable service and positive review valence [12,13]. However, highly valuing their reputations, doctors may not receive their expected review valence from reports by patients because online interactions via OHCSV in a remote, invisible environment are often insu<sup>fi</sup>cient. The limited time and in visibility of interaction via OHCSV may also impede doctors from using their expertise and knowledge fully to address patient health problems, thereby resulting in a negative review valence. Thus, investigations on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of providing OHCSV on patient review valence are essential.

The e<sup>f</sup>ects of OHCSV provision may also be unclear for doctors at di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical levels $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ resident, attending, associate chief, and chief doctor). Doctors at high hierarchical levels may already possess a good reputation. Thus, their need to provide OHCSV is lower compared with those at low hierarchical levels. However, patients prefer doctors at high hierarchical levels over those at low levels be cause the former may have higher professional capabilities. This preference re<sup>fl</sup>ects the potential di<sup>f</sup>erences in the e<sup>f</sup>ects of providing OHCSV among doctors at di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical levels. Doctors at higher hierarchical levels are more likely to obtain positive review valence because of their richer expertise and knowledge [14]. However, patients may have higher expectations for doctors at higher hierarchical levels, which causes patients to be more fastidious in leaving a positive review. Therefore, the di<sup>f</sup>erences in the e<sup>f</sup>ects of providing OHCSV for doctors at various hierarchical levels must be understood.

This research aims to explore the e<sup>f</sup>ect of doctors’ provision of OHCSV on patient review valence and the di<sup>f</sup>erences among doctors at various ranks in the hierarchy. We focus on a leading Chinese OHC with 493,548 doctors and 3,145,226 reviews, and design a quasi-experiment to examine the causality of doctors’ provision of OHCSV. Considering the research context, we combine a coarsened exact matching (CEM) procedure and a di<sup>f</sup>erence-in-di<sup>f</sup>erence (DID) estimation to capture the causality. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that OHCSV exerts a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on patient review valence. Patients are more likely to post positive valence on service attitude satisfaction and therapeutic e<sup>f</sup>ect satisfac tion for doctors who have provided OHCSV than for those who have not. The provision e<sup>f</sup>ects di<sup>f</sup>er to a certain extent on the basis of doctors levels in the hierarchy. The e<sup>f</sup>ect on service attitude satisfac tion for doctors at low hierarchical levels is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant for doctors at high levels. Furthermore, the results reveal that doctors’ provision of OHCSV changes their valence structure by increasing the proportion of positive valence and decreasing that of negative valence. Valence increases due to the increasing reviews of positive valence after doctors’ provision of OHCSV. Moreover, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the volume increase on the positive valence for doctors with low ranks is stronger than that for doctors with high ranks.

Theoretically, this paper contributes a new perspective to the lit erature on the provision of online expert service. The majority of previous studies explore the outcomes of online reviews [2,3], whereas the current paper o<sup>f</sup>ers a pioneering e<sup>f</sup>ort to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of experts’ (doctors’) decision to provide online services on the evaluations of their clients (patients). The consideration of di<sup>f</sup>erent review di mensions and levels of expertise extends the understanding of patients emphasis on medical service and the disparity among experts at dif ferent hierarchical levels. Our work can enrich the knowledge of col lateral consequences, considering the great demand for online expert service. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also bear critical implications for long-term doc tor–patient relationship and OHC sustainability.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. First, we review extant studies and related theories. Second, we illustrate our data collection and research methodology. Third, we present the results and conduct further analysis. Fourth, we discuss the implications of this study. Finally, we highlight the theoretical and managerial contributions of our work.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Doctor–patient relationship in OHCs

The application of medical technology and revolutions of healthcare policy lead to several transitions of doctor–patient relationship. In the past, a doctor provides treatment decisions on the basis of mostly verbal interaction and interrogation, which enable the doctor to comprehend the patient’s situation and their family’s medical history [15]. Doctors are also often treated as authority <sup>fi</sup>gures [16]. Such tight consultation processes facilitate long-term doctor–patient relationship, which is described as paternalism or parent–child relationship [17]. However, with the emergence of sophisticated diagnostic technology, doctor’s reliance on patients’ report is attenuating [18,19]. The instant medical service resulting from technical innovation commercializes the doctor–patient relationship [17], which becomes <sup>fl</sup>eeting [15,20]. This medical consumerism, to a certain extent, implies that patients can challenge the doctors’ decisions [15], viewed as “revolt of the client” [21,22]. Scholars and doctors are aware of a “de-professionalization” in healthcare industry [15,23] because expense-centered healthcare policy replaces the patient-centered one [15]. The medical technology reduces clinical errors, thus improving patient satisfaction and doctor–patient relationship [24,25]. However, in the expense-oriented service mode, certain doctors defraud patients with costly services rather than more a<sup>f</sup>ordable substitutions $[ 2 6 , 2 7 ]$ , which gradually jeopardizes the doctor–patient trust and generates con<sup>fl</sup>icts.

Long-term and trusting doctor–patient relationship is highly important in addressing illnesses [16,28]. However, current social and environmental trends seem to no longer support such relationships [15]. The prevalence of information technology, especially OHC, o<sup>f</sup>ers a unique and possible outlet for re-evoking this long-term doctor–patient relationship. OHCs connect doctors and patients without temporary and spatial limitations through online texting or telephone consultation [3]. A patient can seek experts’ professional information on the Internet before meeting doctors o<sup>fl</sup>ine. Thus, patients’ con-<sup>fi</sup>dence about the doctors is enhanced and the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of clinical consultation is improved [29]. Nevertheless, as mentioned above, many doctors still hesitate to provide OHCSV for the fear of their reputations being ruined by unfair online ratings [5]. Given that doctors’ online participation may be the key to developing long-term doctor–patient relationship in the information era, this study aims to examine the effect of doctors’ provision of OHCSV.

## 2.2. Online service and interactions in OHCs

Online service has been widely studied as a <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy, including pricing strategy [30], channel competition [31], and marketing strategy [32]. These studies also emphasize the great advantage of such a virtual channel $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the Internet) on selling products. Expert service, as a type of credence good [5], is professional and personalized at the individual level, which leads to di<sup>f</sup>erences from traditional and general commodities and services. The opinions of contributors directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence experts’ reputation and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. In the context of OHCs, the review valence that doctors receive is considered signi<sup>fi</sup>cant [3] because reviews directly re<sup>fl</sup>ect patient satisfaction and the professional competence of doctors. This study aims to address research gaps by investigating the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online service on review valence posted by patients.

Table 1 presents a list of representative studies that are relevant to online health interactions and consultations. These studies mainly represent three streams. The <sup>fi</sup>rst research stream highlights the patients’ online interactions. Patients need to obtain social support from peers [33] in terms of informational support, emotional support, companionship, and instrumental assistance [34,35]. Yan and Tan [33] in dicate that informational support prevails in OHCs, and emotional support can help patients become healthy. To e<sup>f</sup>ectively exchange medical information and obtain social support, patients can <sup>fi</sup>nd similar others through <sup>fi</sup>rsthand treatment experiences posted on OHCs [36]. Moreover, focusing on an OHC on rare diseases, Goh et al. [37] underscore that patients’ interaction on this platform can help alleviate urban–rural health disparities. Although these previous studies emphasize the importance of patients’ communication with others, the results cannot help understand the e<sup>f</sup>ect of doctors’ participation in OHCs on the patients’ online behaviors.

The second research stream underscores the perspective of economic pro<sup>fi</sup>tability on whether doctors should provide online consultations and the related pricing strategy [4,39,40]. These studies, which have generally employed stylized mathematical models, provide contingent practical suggestions on o<sup>f</sup>ering online consultations for medical experts. For example, Ozdemir [39] suggested that doctors can set higher prices for OHCSV than for o<sup>fl</sup>ine services to attract considerable patients. However, a market-wide provision of OHCSV intensi<sup>fi</sup>es price competition among doctors [4]. Aside from economic revenue, nonmonetary factors also play important roles in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the experts’ decision to participate in online consultations, especially in the healthcare context. Therefore, this research stream focuses mainly on the economic perspective, but overlooks nonmonetary factors (e.g., review valence) that may result from the provision of OHCSV.

The third research stream focuses on the strategy of operating an online channel (e.g., how doctors should act to gain economic and social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) [3,14,41]. For example, Guo et al. [3] contended that doctors can obtain various returns while contributing knowledge online. Yang et al. [41] observed that doctors’ speedy response and long conversation increase patient satisfaction in OHCSV. Liu et al. [14] found a positive relationship between doctor voice speed and patient satisfaction. Although these studies consider social factors and provide practical suggestions for doctors, they assume that doctors have opened online channels and o<sup>f</sup>ered online consultations. Thus, they could not compare the e<sup>f</sup>ects of doctors who open OHCSV and those who do not.

Di<sup>f</sup>erent from extant studies that emphasize the patient community, our work focuses on the interactions between doctors and patients. This study takes a further step from prior literature (e.g., [33,36,40]) by investigating whether doctors can obtain bene<sup>fi</sup>ts via an OHCSV from the perspective of nonmonetary factors (e.g., reviews). Di<sup>f</sup>erent from previous studies that focus on the operations of OHCSVs, our study examines the e<sup>f</sup>ects and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of OHCSVs. In summary, the current study aims to enrich our knowledge by examining the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of OHCSVs on patient-reported review valence [3].

We posit that a doctor’s provision of OHCSV increases patients’ subsequent review valence. The provision of OHCSV implies that doctors are willing to interact and socialize with patients spatially and temporally, which may evoke patients’ belongingness and enhance patients’ satisfaction. Patients’ need and willingness to gain social support and a sense of belonging are intensive in OHCSV [33,44,45], which reinforces their well-being [46] and further leads to positive emotions and actions [47]. Moreover, providing OHCSV requires doctors to sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce their limited spare time despite being extremely busy with their work [48]. This behavior can engender patients’ subsequent return motivation because of gratitude [49]. Patients may demonstrate gratitude toward doctors who provide OHCSV, which would cause them to post reviews with positive valence.

## 4. Data and measures

Collected in December 2017, our data consist of doctors’ pro<sup>fi</sup>les (e.g., whether the doctor opened a personal website/OHCSV and the opening date) and reviews. Our sample comprises 3,145,226 reviews from 493,548 doctors personal information centers. The dataset also contains time stamps and review contents, including review valence. We leverage an indicator variable to identify reviews of doctors who launched their personal websites.

The rating system on the Good Doctor consists of service attitude satisfaction (SAS) and therapeutic e<sup>f</sup>ect satisfaction [50]. Prior research underscores the critical role of these two salient dimensions in patient satisfaction [51,52]. We divide the reviews into three levels according to the review valence on satisfaction, that is, negative (1 point and 2 points), neutral (3 points), and positive (4 and 5 points) to uncover the underlying mechanism of the e<sup>f</sup>ect of doctors’ provision of OHCSV. ## 4.1. Econometric Identification

The econometric identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in this study hinges on a quasi-experiment pertaining to the opening of doctors’ personal websites on the Good Doctor. Such a change can be considered as a type of exogenous shock to potential content generators for a given doctor in terms of changes on patients’ available functions. We leverage DID estimation to identify the e<sup>f</sup>ects of doctors’ provision of OHCSV on patient review valence. The DID approach is widely used in research to analyze the e<sup>f</sup>ects of events by mimicking a laboratory experiment with observational data [53] when experimental manipulation is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to execute.

To ensure the reliability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of OHCSV, we estimate an average treatment efect on the treated (ATT) instead of an average treatment e<sup>f</sup>ect [53]. For any responding doctor i, the ATT is given by

$$
E \left[ Y _ {1 i} - Y _ {0 i} | D _ {i} = 1 \right] = E \left[ Y _ {1 i} | D _ {i} = 1 \right] - E \left[ Y _ {0 i} | D _ {i} = 1 \right]\tag{1}
$$

where $Y _ { 1 i }$ indicates the monthly average review valence for doctor i from the treatment group (who provided OHCSV); Y is the monthly average review valence for doctor i from the control group (who did not provide OHCSV); and $D _ { i } ~ = ~ 1$ indicates that doctor i belongs to the treatment group; and $E [ Y _ { O i } | D _ { i } = 1 ]$ expresses the expected monthly average review valence for doctor i belonging to the treated group if he/she has not launched his/her personal website or OHCSV.

## 4.3. Estimation model

As mentioned above, we leverage several DID models to estimate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the provision of OHCSV, which are shown as Model (3). To explicitly control the unobserved constant e<sup>f</sup>ects of di<sup>f</sup>erent individual doctors, we deploy <sup>fi</sup>xed-e<sup>f</sup>ect estimators to estimate the unobserved e<sup>f</sup>ects model.

$$
\begin{array}{l} V a l e n c e _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} T r e a t m e n t _ {i} + \beta_ {2} A f t e r _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} T r e a t m e n t _ {i} \times A f t e r _ {i t} \\ \qquad + \sum_ {t = m o n t h 1} ^ {2 3} \gamma_ {t} D _ {t} \\ \qquad + H i e r a r c h y _ {i} + D e p a r t m e n t _ {i} + H o s p i t a l _ {i} + a _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

In these models, Valenc $e _ { i t }$ indicates the monthly average valence of di<sup>f</sup>erent dimensions of satisfaction. $A f t e r _ { i t }$ is a dummy variable that equals 1 for the observations occurring after the cuto<sup>f</sup> day of the provision of OHCSV and 0 for previous observation in the t-th month for both doctors in each pair. Hierachy indicates doctor i’s rank in the clinical hierarchy, namely, resident doctor, attending doctor, associate chief doctor, or chief doctor. Department and Hospital indicate the department and hospital where doctor i works. a mainly indicates the time-constant and unmeasurable factors that may a<sup>f</sup>ect dependent variables in the six-month observation period such as doctor’s professional capabilities. In this case, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of these dummies, Treatment , Hierachy , Department , and Hospital are collinear with the individual <sup>fi</sup>xed e<sup>f</sup>ect $a _ { i } ,$ and are thus dropped from the estimation. The provision of OHCSV is determined by actors’ personality and characteristics and may be the key but unobservable factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence their service quality. Thus, we assume that these individual-level factors do not change in a few months, and $a _ { i }$ can be treated as a time-constant variable during the observation period. The key parameter in these models is $\beta _ { 3 } ,$ the DID estimate, which captures the main e<sup>f</sup>ect of the provision of OHCSV from the treatment group to the control group [53].

We deploy <sup>fi</sup>xed-e<sup>f</sup>ect estimators to analyze the target dataset in stead of random-e<sup>f</sup>ect estimators or <sup>fi</sup>rst di<sup>f</sup>erencing estimates for several reasons. First, in some situations, $\mathrm { C o v } ( A f t e r _ { i t } , a _ { i } ) = 0$ cannot be supported in such a context, which is the key assumption of randome<sup>f</sup>ect estimators [56], because doctors’ characteristics may in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decision to provide OHCSV. The <sup>fi</sup>xed-e<sup>f</sup>ect approach allows for the arbitrary correlation between a and explanatory variables, where the random e<sup>f</sup>ects method does not. Thus, using a <sup>fi</sup>xed-e<sup>f</sup>ect model is more appropriate than employing random-e<sup>f</sup>ect estimators [56]. Second, <sup>fi</sup>rst di<sup>f</sup>erencing is suitable for a situation where idiosyncratic errors are serially correlated [56]. No evidence demonstrates that $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is serially correlated in this study.

## 6. Discussions and Implications

## 6.1. Key findings

This study aims to investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of doctors’ provision of OHCSV on patient review valence. We capture the causality with DID estimators by leveraging a quasi-experiment in the context of the Good Doctor, an OHC in China. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that (1) doctors’ provision of OHCSV improves their review valence; (2) doctors’ provision of OHCSV changes the valence structure by increasing the positive proportion and decreasing the negative proportion; (3) the main source of the valence increase is the volume increase of positive valences; and (4) the provision e<sup>f</sup>ect varies among doctors hierarchical level. The provision of OHCSV by doctors at low hierarchical levels generates greater review valence and volume of reviews with positive valences than that of doctors at high hierarchical levels.

An OHCSV enables patients to receive additional professional and contrapuntal responses because doctors become familiar with the situations of patients who they constantly communicate with. Online management response can increase positive ratings [57]. Furthermore, by identifying with an online social group, particularly by drawing emotional signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, patients are likely to demonstrate a collaborative attitude toward the group [58] and may post positive reviews. Therefore, OHCSV can lead to an increase in review valence, especially the positive valences.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it contributes to OHC and doctor–patient relationship literature. The majority of OHC studies only focus on doctors who have provided OHCSV, while ignoring the other but much larger population, that is, those who have not provided OHCSV [3,14]. This paper shows that providing OHCSV changes the structure of patient review valence and improves doctor–patient relationships by increasing the positive valence. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings emphasize the importance of doctors’ OHC parti cipation, which may stimulate patients’ positive emotions. Moreover, di<sup>f</sup>ering from prior studies that focus on the o<sup>fl</sup>ine strategy of improving the doctor–patient relationship [59–62], this study enriches the strategy from an online perspective. We empirically identify the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of OHCSV that strengthens the patient review valence and the doctor–patient relationship.

Second, the results add novel knowledge to the potential value and in<sup>fl</sup>uence of providing an online service channel [30–32,63–65]. Extant research focuses on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of online service channels on <sup>fi</sup>rm and pricing strategy [31,64,65]. However, the online expert service channel di<sup>f</sup>ers substantially from a marketing channel. It facilitates communication between experts and patients by providing not only professional and informative suggestions but also emotional solicitude. Our results suggest that experts’ participation in online service channels bene<sup>fi</sup>ts them by increasing online review valence [5] in terms of therapeutic e<sup>f</sup>ect and service attitude. This <sup>fi</sup>nding di<sup>f</sup>ers from previous results by showing the nonmonetary in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the online service channel.

Third, this research contributes to the literature on the doctor–patient relationship by linking the doctor’s service with di<sup>f</sup>erent dimensions of patient review valence. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings emphasize that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the provision of OHCSV di<sup>f</sup>ers across patient satisfaction dimensions. In the context of OHC, patients may expect more from a therapeutic e<sup>f</sup>ect than doctors’ service attitude. Patients are also likely to express positive emotions toward the service attitude for doctors at high hierarchical levels. The increase in review valence (e.g., patientreported therapeutic e<sup>f</sup>ect satisfaction) resulting from OHCSV also extends our understanding of the antecedents of patient satisfaction.

Finally, this study contributes to the service literature by showing the disparity among experts’ hierarchical levels. Prior research underscores the importance of experts’ status capital in gaining online returns [3,7,41], that is, a high rank can attract a large number of patients. The present study indicates that for experts at high hierarchical levels, their inherently high online returns lead to minimal space for improvement, whereas experts at low hierarchical levels can experience a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase in review valences. Therefore, this study provides a unique perspective for the research gap among experts’ hierarchical levels.

## 6.3. Practical implications

This study has several practical implications relevant for doctor and OHC platforms. First, given that provision of OHCSV increases patient review valence, doctors can open and use OHCSVs to interact with patients and develop their online reputation. Providing OHCSVs can help doctors acquire more positive valences. Previous research con<sup>fi</sup>rms that the expected evaluation of a given doctor tends to be low if he/she has no online reviews [5]. Comparatively, our <sup>fi</sup>nding underscores the importance of online rating management for doctors. Doctors can use their fragmented time to provide online consultations for patients, given their heavy work o<sup>fl</sup>ine. Enhancing communication and maintaining social bonds with patients can help medical profes sionals gain an awareness of their image in patients’ minds [66].

Second, given that doctors at low hierarchical levels can obtain higher valences on service attitude satisfaction and more reviews with positive valences than those at high levels, the former should be encouraged to provide OHCSV. Interactions with patients based on OHCSV not only increase the experience and reputation of doctors at low hierarchical levels but also enable them to obtain considerable feedback from patients. Doctors can strengthen the use of OHCSV to improve their service provision to patients. For example, they can develop their own patient groups to enable patients to communicate with one another. They can also share their experience or professional medical knowledge to address common issues faced by patients.

Finally, given that the provision of OHCSV increases patient review valences, managers of OHCs or health platforms can develop the function or encourage the use of OHCSV to enable doctors and patients to actively participate in the platform, communicate with one another, and generate reviews, especially those with positive valences. This action is helpful for platform sustainability because the newly generated reviews, especially the positive ones, can attract additional patients to use OHCSVs and to interact with doctors [67]. Health platforms can strengthen the function of OHCSV to encourage usage by doctors and patients. For instance, they can enable remote consultations through video, so that doctors can provide treatment or suggestions e<sup>f</sup>ectively. Health platforms can also allow doctor groups or teams to provide consultations collectively to address complex diseases. Overall, health platforms should try their best to improve OHCSV and similar services for doctors and patients.

## 6.4. Limitations and directions for future research

This study is subject to several limitations. First, we are unable to recognize the moderating variables that may potentially exacerbate or attenuate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of OHCSV on the outcomes. Second, we did not include the doctors’ inherent and unmeasurable factors when performing CEM. Doctors may have distinct professional experiences and capabilities that are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure and analyze. However, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of this issue on our results may be insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. Doctors at the same hierarchical level of a hospital department are not supposed to have a large discrepancy in their professional capabilities. No evidence can demonstrate that doctors who provide OHCSV possess higher capabilities than those who do not provide such a service. Third, owing to the observational nature of our sample, treating self-selection as a partial explanation for our <sup>fi</sup>ndings is inevitable, although multiple empirical checks suggest that self-selection is not a serious issue. In particular, this limitation may imply that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are mainly due to a subset of doctors who decide to o<sup>f</sup>er online services, and these doctors tend to attract higher review valence than others. The function for providing OHCSV has been developed since the establishment of the Good Doctor. Therefore, patients are already familiar with the platform. As the parallel trend check suggests no di<sup>f</sup>erence between the treatment and control groups in the pre-treatment period, no evidence can demonstrate the patients’ preference for doctors in the treatment group. Although our sample does not enable us to identify the individual intrinsic di<sup>f</sup>erences between doctors in a matched pair, the matched doctors in two groups are comparable and we do not consider such di<sup>f</sup>erences as an imperative concern. Fourth, this study is conducted by only one platform, which may a<sup>f</sup>ect the generalization of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Fruitful potential research topics can be derived from the present study. First, our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be applied to other OHCs and countries before practical guidance and policy recommendations are implemented. Second, we merely conducted parallel analysis on one website by using CEM. Future research can test the <sup>fi</sup>ndings on various websites by expanding the sample sources. Third, we only focus on review valence due to the limitation of our research sample. Researchers can explore the linguistic characteristics and conduct sentiment analysis on the review text in the future. Fourth, this study focuses only on the online outcomes of OHCSV. The e<sup>f</sup>ect of OHCSV on o<sup>fl</sup>ine outcomes (e.g., outpatient volume) can also provide abundant and notable research directions.

## 7. Conclusion

This study leverages the DID estimator to capture the causality of providing OHCSV on patient review valence, <sup>fi</sup>lling the research gaps by exploring the consequences of providing online expert services. The main contribution lies in the <sup>fi</sup>nding that doctors’ provision of OHCSV increases the patient review valence. Novel knowledge likewise lies in the discovery that such provision e<sup>f</sup>ects vary across di<sup>f</sup>erent review dimensions and experts’ hierarchical levels. Thus, di<sup>f</sup>erent doctors and online platform stakeholders should formulate suitable strategies to optimize their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.
