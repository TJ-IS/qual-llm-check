# Engagement in online health communities: Channel expansion and social exchanges

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online health communities Information support Emotional support Social Exchange Channel expansion Media richness

## A B S T R A C T

This paper contributes to the discussion on patient engagement in online health communities (OHCs) by inte grating channel expansion and social exchange theories. Using survey data from 348 users of OHC. we examine the impacts of perceived channel richness, social support, and willingness to exchange information on OHC engagement. Results show that the perceived channel richness positively influences OHC engagement. The effect of patients’ willingness to share and seek information on engagement is moderated by health status. Finally, engagement in OHCs enhances self-care efficacy and perceived health outcomes. We propose several theoretical implications for researchers and practical contributions for OHC administrators.

## 1. Introduction

In the not-so-distant past, health information for patients was solely delivered from the perspective of the medical world. This model was understandable, as patients traditionally looked to their healthcare providers as the primary, and possibly only, source of information on health topics and diseases. Although this approach may have been valuable in reducing access to misinformation, it also limited the range of information available to patients or consumers and placed the patient in a less engaged role (as a subordinate). During the past decade, involving consumers in the process of health care has been increasingly emphasized, with anticipation for its positive impact on healthcare outcomes. This paradigm shift from physician-centered to patient centered care and the effect of Internet access to health information has formed the basis for the development of consumer health infor matics [1]. With advancements in information technology (IT), more and more individuals seek general and specific health-related informa tion online [2]. Both seeking and sharing health information (such as treatment options and care planning) are behaviors that are essential for successful healthcare management [3]. Several studies have shown that information sharing and social support among patients, either face-to-face [4] or computer-mediated [5], are associated with positive health outcomes. Online health communities (OHCs) provide a means to seek and share information and emotional support [6], with no tem poral, geographical, and spatial limitations [7]. OHCs are developed to support the continuous availability of information related to a wide range of diseases and treatments. Members of an OHC can share infor mation and experiences with their peers and provide support and encouragement in coping with different illnesses [8]. Online informa tion and support shared through OHC can enhance participants’ psy chological and functional well-being [9].

OHCs are considerably different from other social media. OHC members have cost-efficient access to diverse support networks and information about various health topics. While sharing such valuable resources and services, users expect no returns or monetary reward. Such a gift economy environment of OHCs is inherently antithetical to the profit-seeking culture of other businesses or the advertising model of other social media. Therefore, the business challenge of OHCs is to provide services primarily based on internal motivations. In addition, health information is inherently more private compared with other topics that can be discussed in online media, and it requires more trusted relationships among members of the community. OHC users can share and seek the support and information that they are interested in while remaining anonymous to avoid any embarrassment or judgment [10]. Scaling up the number of members in OHCs is not as important as in other social media. Thus, the emphasis is to keep smaller groups that are focused on issues relevant to the members in order to improve the homophily among them [11,12]. User experiences and engagement are critical indicators of success and vital for the sustainability of OHCs [13]. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate user engagement in the context of OHC as a key indicator of success for these communities.

Despite the benefits that OHCs offer, many of them fail to engage patients in this media. Studies have shown that more than 90 % of OHC users are only short-term observers [14], and suggest psychological factors as important drivers behind the dynamics of user engagement in OHCs (e.g., [15,16]). However, the factors that motivate engagement in OHCs are rarely examined [17]. Questions remain about what factors can encourage users to engage in OHCs [18]. In this study, we use two theoretical perspectives, namely, social exchange theory (SET) and channel expansion theory (CET), to address this gap in the literature and identify the motives for patient engagement in OHCs.

An important driver of individuals’ decision to participate in social media platforms is their perception of media richness [19]. Based on CET, users’ knowledge-building experience in four domains forms perception of media richness. The four domains are as follows: 1. experience with the channel (perceived competence), 2. experience with the messaging topic (knowledge of content), 3. experience with the organizational context (knowledge of a system’s culture), and 4. expe rience with communication coparticipants (comfort with interactants). When users have more experience in these four domains, they acquire more knowledge, and they may perceive the channel richer [20]. The environment of interactions and media richness could be a critical factor that may influence engagement. A channel of communication and its characteristics likely influence individuals’ perceptions of social and personal benefits and, in turn, become the ultimate driver of use [21]. However, little is known about how patients’ experiences and their perception of OHC richness may impact their engagement in these communities. In this study, we extend CET to the context of OHC to understand the impacts of perceived channel richness on patient engagement in this environment.

Moreover, OHC provides benefits as an alternative channel for in dividuals to support each other [22]. The perceived value of using OHCs may determine the extent to which an individual decides to engage in virtual communities to manage his/her healthcare. Social support in online settings is considered as the perceived value and categorized as (1) informational support (such as providing detailed information or facts); (2) emotional support (such as being empathetic and caring; [23]). SET has been used by extant literature to articulate people’s be haviors involved in the process of information sharing [24]. This theory indicates that individuals share resources (e.g., information) with others in hopes of receiving some benefits through their interactions. We use SET to better describe the motives of being engaged in the OHC context.

Participation in OHC has two aspects: sharing and seeking informa tion. Extant literature indicates that the use of an information system (e. g., OHC) is a function of users’ willingness to share their resources (i.e., information) with others and intention to search for more information [25]. He and Wei [26] demonstrate that there is a distinction between knowledge contribution and knowledge-seeking behaviors, and they have different driving factors. The focus of many studies is on sharing activity and its motives; however, seeking health information could be another arm of engagement [27]. Based on the SET, fairness perceptions (resulting from sharing information with others and receiving infor mation from them) can make greater social interactions possible in the future [28]. Therefore, investigating information-sharing and information-seeking behaviors could advance our understanding of how individuals engage in OHCs and, in turn, improve the outcomes of using OHCs.

Finally, perceived health status is a common health factor that shows an individual’s perception of his/her overall health [29]. Extant litera ture highlights that health status both directly and indirectly affects the willingness to disclose information [15,30]. However, little is known about the effects of perceived health status on patient engagement research. Thus, how the perceived health status strengthens or sup presses the relationship between willingness to share and willingness to seek information with patient engagement should be investigated in the context of OHCs.

This study is aimed at identifying factors shaping individuals engagement in OHCs from a theoretical perspective. In brief, the main objective of this research is to identify the antecedents of user engage ment as it is one of the critical success factors of OHCs. Thus, this study aims to address the following three questions:

1 How user experiences influence their perception of the OHC richness and impact user engagement in OHC?

2 How the perceived benefits and information exchange behavior shape user engagement in the OHC context?

3 What are the outcomes of user engagement in the OHC context?

This study offers several significant practical implications as well as contributions to the literature on the antecedents and consequences of patient engagement in OHCs. First, we use a multi-dimensional construct of willingness to exchange information. Most of the extant research focuses only on one dimension, either self-disclosure or infor mation seeking [31]. By conceptualizing information exchange as a multi-dimensional construct, we may provide a more comprehensive understanding of information exchange intention compared with a one-dimensional construct. Second, this study is among the first to apply the CET to investigate how users’ experiences shape their perceptions of OHCs. Third, this study shows how factors from the SET play a role through perceived value in influencing users’ intentions to seek and share health information as well as engagement in OHCs. The last contribution is that the integrated model can better demonstrate how patient engagement in the context of OHC can lead to positive conse quences, which would be useful for healthcare management systems.

The remainder of the paper is set out as follows. Section 2 reviews the theoretical foundations. Then, based on the theoretical foundation and prior studies, our hypotheses are presented in section 3. In section 4, we explain the research method, followed by the results. Section 5 offers a discussion and details theoretical and practical implications as well as limitations of the study. Moreover, we offer some directions for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Engagement in online health community

User engagement is described in the literature as a psychological state when the system can hold users’ attention, and users are attracted to the system for intrinsic rewards ([32], p. 58). Earlier literature relates engagement to flow theory [33,34] and defines it as the user’s intention to be involved due to various reasons, such as interest and motivation. Other studies describe engagement as a subset of flow in a more passive way without user control. An engaging system can capture and captivate users’ interest while they are interacting with it [35]. A more recent study by Di Gangi and Wasko [36] defines user engagement as “a user’s state of mind that warrants heightened involvement and results in a personally meaningful benefit (i.e., involvement to fulfill a need).” In social media engagement theory, Di Gangi and Wasko [36] differentiate between the psychological state and behavior. This separation aligns with Ray et al. [37] that define engagement as the extent of pleasure and involvement in an activity. User engagement embeds personal meaning and capability that drives prosocial behavior. In this study, we adopt the user engagement definition as explained by Di Gangi and Wasko [36] and differentiate it from user experience and behavior.

Several studies have investigated user engagement and attachment in virtual communities. Bishop [38] explains that individuals are driven to action by desires. Their desires lead to plans that need to be in har mony with their goals, values, and beliefs. Bishop’s framework explains that the actions of online communities’ users will depend on their interpretation of their environment. Tsai and Pai [39], studied new comers’ intention to participate in virtual communities. They explain that the fulfillment of needs regarding autonomy, relatedness, and competence, evokes newcomers’ cognitive, affective, and evaluative social identities. They show that participation in virtual communities is influenced by these various identities in which their fulfillment in fluences newcomers’ participation decisions. However, more research is needed to investigate multidimensional factors that also could influence the fulfillment of these psychological needs.

The goal of online communities is to build personal relationships by providing opportunities for participants to exchange personal informa tion [40]. Therefore, creating member attachment and engagement becomes more valuable. Ren et al. [41] examined three levels of attachment to online communities: identity-based attachment to a group within the community, bond-based attachment to individual members, and attachment to the large community. They draw insights from the ories of group identity and interpersonal bonds and found stronger ef fects of the identity-based features compared with the bond-based features. Based on the previous literature, we understand that users perception of online communities, both from personal and social per spectives, can influence their engagement in it. We focus on the psy chological state of engagement and use a broader set of theoretical concepts, such as SET and CET, to explain the personal experiences as well as social interactions that lead to user engagement in the context of OHCs.

## 2.2. Channel expansion theory

CET is a mix of social presence and social influence theories that emphasizes the dominant role of media richness in choosing and use of social media (Carlson and Zmud; 1999). This theory is mainly built based on the assumption that experiences and knowledge may directly shape users’ perception of the channel’s richness. According to this theory, experience with the social media platform may enable users to learn the features, options, and limitations of the channel. In turn, users are more likely to achieve efficient communications as well as better interactions with other members and obtain a high perception of media richness [42].

Despite other social media, the challenge of OHCs is not to expand the number of members. The OHCs prefer to keep the number of members small. but provide an engaging environment for their users that encourage them to share their experiences and knowledge with their peers [18]. Prior studies reveal that when users have more expe riences and awareness about a channel of communication, they tend to perceive it as a richer tool (D’Urso, & Rains, 2008). Media richness refers to a medium’s capacity to facilitate shared meaning [43]. A rich medium facilitates insight and rapid understanding (Daft, Lengel, & Trevino, 1986). Several studies have shown that with the emergent of new technologies, the objective features of a communication channel are not the sole measure of channel richness [44–47]. Individuals may reflect differently about the richness of a communication channel based on their competence and experiences they gained while using it. Percep tions of higher richness about a channel may lead to more effective communications [48].

The central premise of CET is that an individual’s relevant experi ences are the main factors that influence perceptions of a channel's richness [49]. Experience is essential because it allows communicators to “develop associated knowledge bases that may be used to more effectively both encode and decode rich messages on a channel” ([49], p. 155). As explained earlier, CET posits that the individual’s perception of media richness is based on their knowledge-building experience in four domains: 1. Experience with the channel refers to the individuals’ level of competence and confidence in using all features of the channel of communication. 2. Experience with the topic relates to an individual’s level of knowledge and understanding of the topic of interest in communication. 3. Experience with the organizational context refers to an individual’s level of knowledge about the policies, culture, and vo cabulary in terms of jargon and expressions that are commonly used in the organization of interest for communication. 4. Experience with communication peers refers to an individual’s feelings about being involved with their peers and how comfortable they feel in communi cating with their peers through the channel. The more experience people have with these domains, the more knowledge they gain, and the richer they perceive the channel [50]. Early research in human behavior has shown that self-reported channel experience and peer experience predict perceptions of email richness [49]. Moreover, research has demon strated that topic and peer experience predict perceptions of media richness across a range of media, such as phone, voice mail, email, and instant messaging [50,51]. Studies on patients’ technology-mediated communication suggest that experience in specific domains (channel, partner, topic, and context) facilitates perceived media richness (e.g., [48,52]). CET can likely advance the study of patients’ peer-to-peer communication. In this study, we extend CET in the OHC context to investigate the antecedent of OHC users’ perception of richness to get involved in such communities and explain the motives of patients engagement.

## 2.3. Social exchange theory

SET, which can be traced back to the 1920s for studying workplace behavior, connects different disciplines such as anthropology, social psychology, and sociology [53]. SET involves a series of interactions that depend on the actions of exchange partners. SET suggests that these interdependent transactions may lead to high-quality relationships be tween members of a group or community. One of the core ideas of SET is that under certain rules of exchange, the connections could evolve into trusting and mutual commitments. People usually expect reciprocal benefits (such as personal affection, gratitude, and economic return) in their interactions. SET emphasizes that individuals are prone to be involved in interpersonal interactions in order to maximize their re wards and minimize their costs [54].

SET has been mostly used to explain knowledge sharing in different contexts, such as social commerce sites, social media, social networking websites, and virtual communities ([55]; [56]). The context of virtual communities in the healthcare setting is different from other business contexts (e.g., using social media by companies for data mining pur poses) because health care consumers are more likely to be sensitive to potential benefits and risks associated with their health-related de cisions [15]. Before searching for health information or sharing knowledge in OHCs, individuals perform an analysis to ensure that the benefits of sharing and seeking information outweigh the risks. This theory posits that in social behaviors (such as information sharing and seeking), individuals identify potential benefits and costs associated with such interactions to analyze whether the activities are beneficial [57].

SET is mainly used to explain individual behavior in various do mains, including IT adoption [58], consumer behavior [59], information sharing [60], and behavior in online communities [24]. Previous studies examined users’ engagement in online communities, such as members knowledge contributions to online communities (e.g., [61–63]). How ever, few studies have applied this perspective to describe information-seeking and information-sharing intentions in the OHC context. For instance, Yan et al. [64] applied this perspective to propose a benefit vs. cost knowledge-sharing model for OHCs. They developed a model that consists of perceived benefits and costs on knowledge-sharing behavior. The benefit factors include reputation, sense of self-worth, face concern, as well as social support, and the cost factors include cognitive and executional costs. Jin, Park, and Kim (2010) used this theory to propose a model that posits that two aspects of online community attributes (Sociability and Usability) facilitate members’ participation and bring social and functional benefits to members.

SET is also consistent with the cost-benefit analysis of customer value in different business contexts [65]. According to this perspective, cus tomers (online users) may evaluate value by comparing what they expect to receive (e.g., benefits) with what they have to give up $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . ,$ costs). Hence, SET can be used to access how a trade-off is performed when online users decide to seek and share health information in OHCs. Much of extant research explains perceived benefits and costs in the context of e-commerce, social networking websites, mobile services, and blogs (Liu et al., 2016). However, it is worth noting that the benefits and costs in the OHC context may be different from other online services. For instance, in e-commerce, individuals may prefer to share information to receive financial rewards and personalized services [66].

Drawing from prior studies in OHCs, we identify that social support is the main reason that entices people to engage in information exchange activities [67]. Online health support, which is the net value of participating in OHCs, has two dimensions: informational support and emotional support. According to Åkesson et al. [68], OHCs can empower consumers to provide informational and emotional support. Based on SET, individuals may involve in social interactions with peers to enrich their daily informational needs. Thus, OHCs facilitate sharing and acquiring health-related knowledge, information, and experiences. Moreover, individuals may become motivated to participate in an OHC to visit emotionally-support peers as well as to provide other members with positive energies in order to sort out similar health issues. Hence, OHCs can facilitate the exchange of emotional support among users and help them feel better even if it does not provide direct assistance to solve their health issues.

Prior studies compared perceived benefits and costs of participating in online communities. For instance, [69] indicate that the primary motivation of individuals to engage in OHCs is to exchange information and share emotions. In the presence of potential costs, users exchange information and suggestions about symptoms and treatments as well as search for other similar people who they can exchange their emotions and empathies with [70]. Using privacy calculus perspectives, [17] suggest that in the presence of privacy concerns (as the main perceived risks), people are still likely to engage in information exchange activities in OHCs. They highlight that perceived benefits (informational and emotional support) outweigh perceived risks (health information pri vacy concerns). Admittedly, there are some costs in using OHC (i.e., time and attention spent). However, because participating in OHC is not mandatory, and people usually participate in this media for positive aspects, we assume that current users have been able to handle perceived costs. Therefore, since we attempt to focus on factors that facilitate information exchange and help shape user engagement, we identify informational and emotional support as the net value of participating in OHCs.

## 2.4. Generalized conceptual model of user engagement in OHCs

User engagement in OHC is one of the critical indicators of success for these communities. OHC has many advantages in providing health care information. OHC empowers health care consumers by providing them with immediate access to a vast amount of health information and a variety of topics related to healthcare services. OHC not only allows users to search for health information, but also facilitates information sharing activities. A study shows that 40 % of people seeking health information online also share their experiences about diseases and treatment protocols [71]. Individuals can easily share their health care knowledge, experiences, and treatment options, and they can post re views about health products, medicines, and physicians [72]. These reciprocal activities tie individuals who have similar health condition to form online support groups [73]. According to Maloney-Krichmar and Preece [74], these online communities can provide individuals with the informational and emotional support that helps them cope with their diseases. Despite the mentioned advantages of being part of virtual communities, OHCs still face considerable challenges regarding patient engagement $[ 8 , 7 5 ]$ . Understanding the predictors of health information exchange can provide several insights about how to facilitate informa tion sharing in online communities [76]. Informational and emotional support are the main benefits of disclosure of personal health informa tion in online communities. However, these two support types remain ambiguous, and their impact needs to be further investigated [77]. Several studies highlighted factors such as health information privacy concerns and information sensitivity that can refrain online users from engaging in OHCs [15]. Other studies focused on sociability and us ability scores, such as the trustworthiness of content and user satisfac tion to inform better design of OHCs (e.g., [16]).

In this study, we adapt the generalized conceptual model of the use and perceptions of communication channels [49] to investigate the antecedents of OHC user engagement. Since engagement represents users’ perception of pleasure and involvement in OHCs, we focus on users’ perceptions of the communication channel. We use two theoret ical perspectives of CET and SET to explain the antecedents of user engagement in OHC. The conceptual model is presented in Fig. 1. CET explains users’ experiences that form their perception about a channel of communication. This theory helps us to explain user experiences in the OHC environment and understand how their experiences influence their perception of channel richness. On the other hand, SET helps us to explain and understand the user behavior based on their social support behavior as well as their willingness to share and seek information. We posit that using these two theoretical perspectives, we can explain user engagement in OHCs.

## 2.5. Perceived health status

According to Bansal and Gefen [15], individuals’ characteristic (such as current health status) significantly impact the way they analyze the utility of using IT. Perceived health status shows the extent to which people are ill or well. Individuals are more prone to involve in infor mation exchange behaviors when they encounter severe health-threatening issues rather than other routine problems, such as personal money, investment, or property [78]. Prior studies suggest that the relationships between individuals' health-related decisions and their antecedents can be moderated by perceived health status [79,80]. Although previous research highlights the significant effect of intentions to participate in OHCs on shaping engagement, users may still be unable to obtain the expected benefits through information-exchange activities due to their physical disabilities or mental limits [81].

Prior studies argue that perceived health status may change the way people think about themselves, their capabilities, and the world around them [82]. Unhealthy individuals (e.g., with chronic diseases) perceive more strain due to the presence of physical/mental infirmity, and this health condition makes them more anxious and vulnerable to digital information disclosure surrounding them. People with good health conditions tend to assume less severe demands on their strength or abilities, perceive more control, and experience less mental/emotional strain. However, little is known about whether the overall evaluation of a person about his/her health status can affect patient engagement development and willingness to seek or share information. In this study, we expect that perceived health status moderates the relationship be tween willingness to exchange information in OHC and levels of patient engagement.

## 3. Hypotheses and research model

The conceptual model proposed by this study is presented in Fig. 2. The model is mainly built upon a synthesis of CET and SET to explain the antecedents and predict possible consequences of patient engagement in the OHC context. The framework describes the casual link begins with channel expansion factors, to perceived value, willingness to exchange information, as well as patient engagement, and finally ends with pos itive outcomes of participating in OHCs.

We use CET [49] to explain the importance of patients’ experiences in OHCs to shape how they develop perceptions about the richness of the channel. Online health platforms vary in their feature’s usage logic and ease of use [83]. The more experience patients acquire through using OHC, the more positive they perceive their experience [84]. The tech nical applications and features of the online health platforms are very different from each other in terms of the type of information and depth of communication they can convey [85]. For example, some commu nities such as Patients Like Me are more research-oriented and empha size the effectiveness of therapies, side effects of medications, and providing disease progression information for academics and clinicians. In contrast. others such as Care Opinion provide an environment for patients and healthcare providers to share their experiences and develop solutions for challenges they face. Some other communities may be more focused on policy developments and fundraising for their patients. When users are familiar with the applications and technical features of the community that they participate in, they perceive it as a more useful and rich channel. Therefore, we posit that the extent of experience members gain through being familiar with OHC applications will lead to a better understanding of how rich and effective the OHC is.

OHCs provide the platform for patients to actively connect with other patients and healthcare providers. Patients can learn from each other through online communications and form ties with peers. According to Kim et al. [86], the amount of time and attention spent by members of an online community would result in more positive effects on community responsiveness only when the extent of bridging nonredundant com munities and cohesion among members is greater. Participation in OHCs grants patients with chronic illnesses immediate access to a care support workforce, particularly concerning their condition. Online ties, as oppose to offline relations, require significantly less maintenance and reciprocation. They are also easier to negotiate, where patients can ac cess information and support as a public good, with no immediate expectation of reciprocation [87]. A systematic review of online healthcare platforms has shown that patient use of OHC affects the relationship between patients by leading to more equivocal communi cation and harmonious relationships [88]. Therefore, we posit that more experiences with peers will lead to higher perceptions of OHC richness as a medium of communication.

Moreover, as members have deep interactions with other members and know the health issues which they are suffering from, they become more likely to consider the OHC as a rich media to enhance communi cations, sharing, and seeking health-related information [89]. Patients with chronic illnesses, such as diabetes who have to manage their dis ease over time develop more experience and knowledge about their illness. When members are familiar with the subject of communication, they can communicate more effectively and understand each other in a better way. Besides, having similar topic experiences, such as similar medications for an illness or symptoms of a disease, individuals can use specific terminologies or jargon that helps them reach a shared under standing of communication with their peers. Therefore, richer messages could be sent among peers about a similar topic. Communication about the topics that are more familiar among the users may increase their ability to interpret messages more richly. We posit that this will increase the perception of channel richness among users.

Further, each OHC has a different cultural norm within the com munity that is sensitive to the culture of the illness that can encourage users’ willingness to create bonds with each other [90]. As individuals develop experience communicating within an OHC, they may use shared symbols or cultural references, and they will learn to interpret messages received within the OHC context more richly. Therefore, we posit that the more the users are familiar with the culture of OHC, they perceive it as a richer channel of communication.

Social media platforms support the creation of user experience to meet the user’s needs. User experiences on these platforms enhance user engagement [36]. Users of online channels may have a certain level of expectations as they begin to use these platforms. As they use the channel, they become more familiar with the environment, and they learn more about the OHC services, culture, and dynamics among members. Research suggests that when these communication channel provide the tools for creating content that matches users’ current needs, users perceive the channel richer [48]. Consequently, they are more likely to become highly involved and participatory [91,92]. Hence, we propose that the perception of channel richness influences user engagement in OHC.

H1e. Patients’ perception of channel richness positively affects their channel engagement perception

With advancements in information communication technology (ICT), there are various channels that patients can use to communicate with each other and with care providers. Previous studies show that there are multiple types of OHCs. Some mainly focus on exchanging experience and socialization, while others are more intelligence prolif eration communities [93]. Patients’ perception of channel richness varies across different OHCs. In addition, some of the OHCs are devel oped to provide emotional support, while others are more focused on providing information support for patients [72]. To understand the threads of support-seeking and sharing behavior of individuals, we first need to understand motivators that inspire them to disclose information and become involved in the information-exchanging community [94]. Literature shows that users in a virtual environment are highly auton omous, and their cognitive processes of information sharing could be affected by conditions such as the dynamics of people they interact with and the quality of content they share [95]. In the context of OHCs, the situational strength and richness of the virtual environment can form an individual’s willingness to share or receive information.

According to Ogara et al. [96], interpersonal and immediate re lationships determine the social presence of users on social media. The higher the social presence, the greater the social support that the members have on each other’s behavior. If members of an OHC believe that it is rich enough to convey the right messages to the right persons or it is competent enough to gather required information related to their health issues, they are likely to spend more time and effort to engage in seeking and sharing such information. OHC that enables users to have immediate and interpersonal relationships with other peers will also increase their willingness to exchange information online.

Additionally, it may propose an impact on the type of support pa tients expect by referring to OHCs. Previous research indicates mixed results in terms of the effects of media richness on participants’ sharing intentions. Several studies show that features of media, a growing circle of the network, and other users who supply the content of the media have a significant impact on participants’ continued engagement in the media (e.g., [74]; [75]). On the other hand, several studies report a declining path of information sharing in OHCs when the richness of network features to pass a certain threshold (e.g., [95]). This confusion in the literature calls for more studies that investigate the impact of media richness on participants’ perceived values derived from the in teractions and willingness to share and receive information. We posit that patients’ perception of channel richness will influence their perceived value (information and emotional support) as well as will ingness to exchange information.

H2a. Patients’ perceived richness of OHC positively affects their informational support-seeking behavior

H2b. Patients’ perceived richness of OHC positively affects their emotional support-seeking behavior

H2c. Patients’ perceived richness of OHC positively affects their will ingness to share health information

H2d. Patients’ perceived richness of OHC positively affects their willingness to seek health information

People use OHCs as virtual social spaces to obtain and provide in formation or social support [97]. Informational support consists of obtaining or providing the information and knowledge around specific disease symptoms, treatments, medical procedures, or detailed infor mation about healthcare providers, such as hospitals, clinics, and phy sicians [98]. Emotional support refers to receiving or exhibiting encouragement, empathy, and active listening [99]. Users reduce feel ings of isolation and try to learn more about symptoms or treatment options through disclosing and receiving health content in OHCs. Members attempt to share their specific experience and knowledge related to their personal treatment information to obtain social support and beneficial information from others [64]. People disclose their own experience to support others when those users are in despair emotion ally. Sharing personal information about treatments and medicines can help users promote healthy behavior and, in turn, find social support from other community members.

Members of an OHC use discussion boards to communicate with others. The communications take place through self-disclosure behav iors and information sharing activities [100]. The expected value (i.e., informational and emotional support) is the main driver for communi cating health information in this context [101]. For instance, individuals perceive that by disclosing personal experiences in OHCs, they will be more likely to find people with similar health conditions. In turn, more relevant information and empathy will be obtained. By receiving in formation, members can learn about medical issues of interest, and by disclosing personal information, they can share useful health-related experiences and advice. Therefore, making social ties and exchanging their own experiences may directly depend on the extent to which they perceive value from OHCs.

Many users of OHCs experience pain and stress because coping with a health issue (chronic disease, in particular) is a challenging process [3]. Relevant information and empathy can enhance information exchange, which helps members identify and solve similar health issues [7]. As mentioned by Chung [102], communications in OHCs provide an op portunity for people to make social ties with others who may have experienced similar medical procedures. The interactions through exchanging wellness-related information offer incentives to users to learn from others’ relevant knowledge [3]. The availability of practical advice about health issues and emotional support offered by others who are suffering from the same health conditions may encourage in dividuals to engage in exchange activities [103]. Communicating health information can also provide a sense of relief [104]. Thus, individuals may be motivated to exchange information in OHCs to access a wide range of opinions, helpful experiences, and emotional aids provided by other members.

As highlighted by Yan et al. [64], the most important reason for people suffering from diseases to engage in OHCs is to access medical information and obtain social support. Informational and emotional support, which are the key targets for testing social support in OHCs, increase individual identification with these channels [69]. People feel engaged in OHCs when they maintain a balance between obtaining and providing informational and emotional support. If a user always expects to receive care and information without giving the same type of support, they feel guilty and ashamed [17]. Social support (informational and emotional aids) in the context of OHC can satisfy individuals’ unmet needs with face-to-face medical encounters or offline health services [105]. Addressing unmet needs may entice users to significantly engage in OHCs.

OHCs help engage members in online health-related discussions by providing access to both information and emotional support [106]. Meeting information needs and providing emotional comfort will meaningfully evoke users’ desire to involve in solving health-related issues with the help of other members. When users are cognitively and emotionally energized, they engage in social behavior [107]. Members of an OHC can achieve cognitive energy through informational support and obtain emotional energy through emotional aids from other mem bers. Provision and receipt of informational and emotional support can change the attitude of members toward engagement in OHCs [9]. Members can be engaged in OHCs if informational and emotional ben efits are to be realized [37]. Both provision and receipt of social support play an essential role in enhancing the feeling of empowerment for OHC’s participants [108]. The risk of dropout from OHCs reduces as users get more emotional support from their peers [18]. As a result, we hypothesize that the informational and emotional support expected to obtain from participation in an OHC can create a strong patient engagement in OHCs.

The success of OHCs relies on collaboration among users, which indeed needs individuals to actively participate in not only searching for health information, but also sharing their knowledge and experience [109]. Thus, it is essential to distinguish between seeking and sharing activities because factors affecting willingness to seek information in OHC may be different from that influencing willingness to share expe riences. Moreover, several studies have examined the antecedents of information seeking as well as sharing activities in a general business context (e.g., [110]). A higher volume of seeking and sharing informa tion on OHC is an indicator that users actively engage in OHCs. Communicating health information with other members can lead to constructive conversations and feedback, which may make them feel empowered to face their anxieties, doubts, and fears [101]. According to Bateman et al. [111], sharing and seeking information in an OHC will encourage members to get more involved and become emotionally attached to the OHC. Individuals’ participation in OHCs, which are re flected by sharing health information, positively contributes to emotional attachment to those communication channels [112]. Accordingly, we can argue that the more members are willing to participate in online information-sharing and information-seeking behaviors, the more they feel attached to an OHC. Shared interests or goals encourage a group of users to share information in OHCs and, in turn, become more willing to engage in safety-related behaviors [113]. OHCs are effective channels to engage patients because they are patient-focused tools that allow community engagement and empower patients through information exchange experiences [114]. People’s preference for involvement in safety-related behaviors will be increased through discussing health topics, such as diagnosis, treatment, drug side effects, or symptom descriptions [115]. Thus, it can be argued that sharing and seeking health information among members of an OHC can lead to higher levels of engagement.

People with good health conditions tend to assume less severe de mands on their strength or abilities, perceive more control, and expe rience less mental/emotional strain [116]. Those with better health status experience less physical or mental weaknesses and anxiety, and in turn, are more prone to engage in their care process through information sharing. Furthermore, users of OHCs who perceive their health to be good would feel less depressed compared with unhealthy individuals. Previous studies indicate that OHC members, especially those who recovered from the symptoms of a disease or condition, may share their personal experiences with others suffering from similar health problems [117]. Therefore, we expect that the perceived health status can mod erate the relationship between information exchange intentions and engagement behaviors. People who perceive themselves to be healthy may need less information and advice related to diagnosis, symptoms, and treatment options than those with poor health conditions. Thus, they are less inclined to seek health information, but may become more likely to disclose their information about their illnesses and medicines to participate in discussion boards of OHCs.

Unhealthy individuals (e.g., with chronic diseases) perceive more strain due to the presence of physical/mental infirmity, and this health condition makes them more anxious and vulnerable to digital channels surrounding them. Users with poor health may decide to engage in OHC discussions and involve in self-care management by seeking others personal experiences and knowledge [64]. Unhealthy individuals may become more engaged in their healthcare management by seeking related information about their diseases and treatments. Also, they may not necessarily engage in OHCs by disclosing their personal health in formation. Accordingly, healthy individuals can be more likely to emphasize the importance of releasing their personal health information to participate in safety-related behaviors than unhealthy users who may put more emphasis on seeking relevant information. As suggested by prior studies, patient engagement is considered as a vital factor in building a high-quality healthcare system [121]. According to Barello et al. [122], patient engagement encourages individuals to participate in their self-health management and treatment process, which in turn, helps them achieve better health outcomes and gain better treatment options.

People would like to take an active role in their healthcare by participating in OHCs [123]. As a useful health information resource, participating in OHCs allows members to make informed decisions about their health and meet their health information needs [124]. Active participation in OHCs helps members enhance their health literacy. Thus, they can interpret health information better based on their knowledge learned through engaging in health discussions [125]. Engaged people go beyond online participation in OHCs to archive positive healthcare outcomes. It has been argued that engagement in an OHC as a social community exerts a positive effect on psychological pathways such as self-efficacy [126]. Positive energy resulting from attachment to OHCs plays a significant role in improving health be haviors and outcomes [127]. ## 4. Data analysis and results

We designed and used an online questionnaire using Amazon’s Me chanical Turk (MTurk) to survey a national sample of patients. MTurk has been used in several studies as an acceptable means to collect individual-level data [129,130]. Using MTurk, researchers can analyze the perceptions of samples that are more representative of the general population of interest, including a wide range of ages, ethnically, and work experiences [131]. In total, 400 participants took part in the sur vey, from which we used 348 complete usable responses. The incentive for participation was a monetary reward. The range of completion time was between 10 and 15 min that implied acceptable responses in terms of timing. To avoid random responses and haphazard questions, we incorporated some attention trap questions in the instrument as a reverse Turing test. We removed insufficient answers and dropped responses that failed the response quality questions. We limited the re spondents’ location to the United States and used a screening question at the beginning of the questionnaire to ask about how often they use OHCs. The respondents who have never used OHC were stopped from taking the survey. Table 2 summarizes the demographic information in our final dataset. All participants are from the US and suffer from at least one illness (such as diabetes, arthritis, heart disease, etc.). All partici pants are frequent users of virtual health communities: 18 % of the participants declared daily usage, 27 % reported 1–2 times a week, 41 % note 1–2 times a month, and 15 % stated once every few months.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Key findings

This study helps to understand the process in which perceived media richness shapes positive outcomes of engagement in OHCs. Our results also reveal that the perceived richness of OHC has positive effects on the expectation of informational support and the expectation of emotional support. These findings are similar to the channel expansion studies that media characteristics and perceived richness of channels of communi cation will have an impact on personal cognition [139]. However, the effects of media richness on the willingness to exchange information are not consistent. Results show that media richness perceptions can moti vate users to share health information but does not significantly shape their desire to seek information. A plausible justification for this finding is that users may be more concerned about their responsibility of sharing the right amount of information with other members suffering from the same health problems. They may need to use a useful media to convey the appropriate and accurate amount of data. Thus, they require rich media that is more effective than others in resolving ambiguity and uncertainty associated with sharing personal information.

Our results also show that familiarity with an OHC and its features exerts a significant effect on the expectation of media richness. Individuals can leverage useful interactive functions of OHCs to interact with others directly. Thus, they may believe that this media is more effective in communicating and learn from other patients about their illness and other health topics. However, the findings indicate that experience with the disease does not shape users’ perceptions of media richness. One plausible justification for this result is that knowing the signs and symptoms of the disease will not affect the immediate and interpersonal interactions on OHC. Thus, patients with chronic or nonchronic illnesses may have the same perceptions about the richness of OHC.

The results demonstrate that the perceived value resulting from both informational and emotional support has a significant influence on in dividual engagement in the use of OHCs as well as patient participation in care management. This finding is in line with the SCT literature suggesting that outcome expectations (e.g., expected social support) are strong predictors of an individual’s behavior [140].

The findings also provide empirical evidence to support the fact that engagement with OHC will increase positive outcomes about the enhancement of health self-management competence and health out comes. Consistent with Lin and Chang [76], this study provides additional empirical evidence that individuals expect to use health in formation shared in OHC to find the relevant information they need to improve their self-care efficacy and competence.

Contrary to our expectations, willingness to share and seek infor mation does not have a significant direct effect on individual engage ment with OHC. A plausible justification for this result is that engagement with OHC is meaningful when users are willing to actively seek and share health information. Therefore, there should be a driving factor to convert sharing and seeking willingness to the active partici pation of members on OHC. The findings demonstrate that this driving factor is the perceived health status. This means that even if users exhibit a higher willingness to seek and share information on OHC, this desire may not shape active participation in OHC unless users perceive a good health status. Accordingly, the impacts of willingness to share as well as a willingness to seek health information on engagement with OHCs become significant only through interaction with perceived health sta tus. The results highlight that perceived health status positively mod erates the relationship between information sharing intention and engagement with OHC, which indicates that individuals with suitable health perceive greater effects of information disclosure on their engagement behavior. In particular, healthy people perceived that sharing health information in OHCs can exert more positive effects on their ongoing interactions with the features and functions of OHCs.

Nevertheless, health status negatively moderates the relationship between information seeking and engagement with OHC, which implies that users with poor health perceive more significant effects of infor mation seeking on their engagement behavior. This negative moderating effect suggests that unhealthy individuals who may suffer from chronic diseases perceived that searching for health information in OHCs can exert more positive effects on their continuing use of OHCs. One plau sible justification is that individuals with better current health status are more likely to participate in online discussion forums to obtain companionship by disclosing their health information and personal ex periences to help others suffering from the same health issues [31]. The results are similar to the findings of previous literature [141], which indicates that seeking additional information about health topics from online resources will improve patient participation in the process of care, but may not necessarily increase engagement in OHC. According to prior studies (e.g., [30]), more information-disclosure interactions in OHCs will empower patients to take a more active role in their personal healthcare management. Thus, the willingness to share information on OHCs may enable patients to engage in healthcare decisions by increasing their health literacy through interactions with other mem bers. However, it may not necessarily encourage them to spend more time and effort engaging with OHC. These findings imply that healthy users perceived that sharing personal experiences on OHCs can increase the possibility of taking a proactive approach to their healthcare decisions.

## 5.2. Theoretical contributions

The results of this study provide several implications for theory. First, prior studies extended the aspect of CET to knowledge transfer in virtual settings [142]. However, little attention has been paid to inves tigate the association between channel expansion factors and engage ment in OHCs. Our study synthesizes the CET and SET to provide a more comprehensive picture of the extent to which online users believe that OHC is rich and effective enough to facilitate the receipt of informa tional and emotional support from others. This study is among the first to apply CET to investigate individual intentions to seek and share health information in the context of OHCs. This theory integration can help future studies better analyze the perceived benefits and costs associated with OHCs in light of the media richness perspective to encourage user engagement.

Second, this study contributes to our current understanding by differentiating seeking and sharing behaviors in the OHC context. To show their conceptual distinction, we use a multi-dimensional construct of willingness to exchange information. This construct has two di mensions: seeking information and sharing information. Thus, in the model and hypotheses, we did not combine seeking and sharing activ ities, and we have separated hypotheses for sharing and seeking be haviors. None of the two dimensions necessarily needs to covary. For instance, a user may be willing to seek more health information related to his/her disease but not be likely to share his/her experiences with other peers. Therefore, it could be argued that willingness to exchange information construct should be modeled as a second-order formative structure because each dimension is more likely to vary independently from the other dimensions since a change in one of the lower-order factors can imply an unequal shift in the other. According to Jarvis et al. [143], these characteristics are more indicative of a formative structure. Moreover, we provide evidence to demonstrate that these two dimensions have their distinctive predictors and outcomes. As suggested by results, perceived richness is a significant predictor of willingness to share information but cannot shape individuals’ willingness to seek in formation in OHC.

Third, although previous studies examined the antecedents of health information sharing in OHCs [64], members of OHCs are likely to obtain a fair balance between what they share and what they seek from others [73]. The information exchange behaviors among members of OHCs directly affect the success of those communities. Thus, it is important to identify the predictors of health information exchange (sharing and seeking) to enhance our current understanding of how to encourage OHC members to share their personal experiences as well as seek health information from others. The findings of this study can allow researchers to better understand the differences between the two aspects of health information exchange (i.e., seeking and sharing) behaviors in the OHC context.

Fourth, another research contribution of the study relates to the provision of empirical evidence on how perceived media richness can be developed and formed about OHCs. Although previous studies investi gated how interactive characteristics of social media impact personal cognition and behavior in social media [76], few studies considered OHC functionality and features to explain information exchange. Using the familiarity with OHC functionality, familiarity with peers, and fa miliarity with OHC culture as environmental factors, our research con tributes to the current channel expansion literature by explaining the association between the interactive features of OHCs and personal media choice for information exchange behaviors.

Fifth, previous literature in the context of OHCs mostly studied the extrinsic and intrinsic motivations as well as perceived risks of knowl edge sharing ([17]; [31]). Nevertheless, little is known about the con sequences of value expected from information exchange in OHCs. This study contributes to understanding the consequences of information exchange expectations in the context of OHCs by providing insights into the conceptualization of patient engagement. Our work extends the concept of patient engagement to differentiate engagements in OHCs and engagement in the process of care. The results of this study contribute to the current patient engagement literature and patient-centered studies by examining the direct relationships between health information exchange in OHCs and patient engagement with OHCs.

Sixth, this study advances the theoretical development in this field and adds to the existing literature by investigating the role of perceived health status in information exchange behaviors in OHCs. We examined the moderating effect of perceived health status in the relationship be tween individual information exchange intentions and engagement. Perceived health status has been rarely considered in existing studies on information exchange in OHCs. Few empirical studies examined the direct or indirect effects of health status on an individual’s intention to disclose information [17]. Our study empirically supports that the perceived health status has a moderation effect on patient engagement behaviors (engagement in OHCs). In particular, the result suggests a moderating effect of perceived health status on the relationship between willingness to exchange information (both sharing and seeking in tentions) and patient engagement in OHCs.

Finally, our study contributes to the OHC engagement literature by theorizing the outcomes of information exchange in OHCs. A theoretical model on the factors affecting users’ engagement in OHCs and its possible outcomes are proposed. The results validate the appropriate ness and robustness of our proposed model, which can provide a foun dation and guidance for future research by highlighting the need for more integrative theoretical approaches. Our findings extend existing knowledge on individual engagement in OHCs by conceptualizing its consequences as self-care efficacy and health outcomes. Our work sug gests that it is important for researchers to pay greater attention to the multidimensional aspect of the outcomes when investigating users engagement in OHCs.

## 5.3. Practical implications

The findings of this study have important managerial implications for those tasked with the responsibility of developing and management of OHCs. First, the results show that social support (including informa tional and emotional support) is an important factor that may increase members’ engagement and information exchange in OHCs. To enlarge the benefits of social interactions in OHCs, administrators of OHCs need to improve the usefulness and credibility of the health information ex change in this setting and highlight emotional support as well as infor mational support expected to receive from others. To advance the usefulness and credibility of health information, the manager can in crease the convenience, comprehensiveness, immediacy, and quality of the health information on OHCs. Moreover, health information can be presented in more user-friendly formats (e.g., images, videos, and audio). As suggested by prior studies, video sharing can increase the effectiveness of health information exchange [114].

Second, to enhance the perceptions of informational support, the managers of OHCs should use some practical strategies and marketing tactics to promote human-to-human interactions. For instance, man agement should encourage members to exhibit more transparent infor mation about their personal profile. such as disease-related information. background, and experience, to increase human-to-human interactions for informational support purposes in OHCs. Moreover, providing various complementary applications that are compatible with popular health services can also strengthen interpersonal interactions by exchanging information easier in discussion forums of OHCs [144]. Thus, the availability of more complementary applications can encourage users to find opportunities for posting more messages, responding to others’ messages quickly, and engaging in conversations frequently.

Third, administrators of OHCs can enhance emotional support by connecting members who have similar health concerns or users who are suffering from similar health issues. Furthermore, by building a culture of mutual respect in the context of OHCs, members can be encouraged to make friends and help others in the OHC health groups. Both managers and users should establish a warm and trusting community culture, which comforts members to discuss their sensitive health issues and receive emotional care from other peers.

Fourth, the perceived richness of OHCs is a significant predictor of engagement with OHCs and health information exchange. As suggested by prior studies (e.g., [145]), administrators of OHCs can use the po tential effects of emoticons to enhance the exchange of social informa tion through providing further social cues beyond the digital textual information. Thus, the media richness of OHCs can be improved by managers by adding appropriate visual cues to enhance users’ emoti cons. Management can also use additional features to enable effective interpersonal interactions and enrich user perceptions of virtual pres ence when sharing health information in OHCs. For instance, managers of OHCs can develop and utilize some synchronous communication tool that enable members to carry out two-way conversations with others immediately.

Fifth, regarding the moderating role of perceived health status, the users’ levels of engagement are likely to be varied in OHCs. This may make it difficult for a single OHC to set the same policies which are comprehensive enough to embrace a wide range of people with various health status. One practical solution is that managers of OHCs should decide the optimal scope of health-related functions and features on which their OHCs desire to focus. They can choose to offer a broader set of functions and services to cover a variety of health conditions or to focus on specific health issues (chronic diseases or typical ailments). Therefore, OHCs need to be relevant to the users, and developers should consider the target audience when designing their features, functions, and applications. For instance, an OHC may only offer features and applications attractive to users who are struggling with quitting smoking or alcohol use. Another example would be an OHC that concentrates on exchanging information related to more severe issues such as cancer or HIV. According to the findings, we can argue that more focused OHCs with functions and applications devoted to a particular health situation may increase the chances of encouraging target users to engage in in formation exchange.

Sixth, the moderating effect of perceived health status also implies that members with different health conditions have different views on involvement in information sharing and seeking. One solution is that managers of OHCs should align incentive policies with the type of health status. For instance, to encourage healthy individuals to engage in active interactions with others, management should highlight the benefits and outcomes of seeking information and consultations in OHCs. On the other hand, managers should promote the benefits of disclosing personal experiences to entice unhealthy individuals to engage in creating social support for other peers. According to these different health demands, personalized health services should be developed with a focus on nonstigmatizing service design.

Seventh, the user interface may impact the quality of information sharing in OHCs [64]. To facilitate the exchange of personal experi ences, the environment of sharing platforms should be friendly and pleasant. Using inspirational images, music, or videos in OHCs may smooth the exchange process between members. OHCs should actively publicize the significant outcome resulting from users disclosing their personal experiences and success stories.

Finally, the findings of this study reconfirm the strategic value of OHCs for the healthcare industry. OHCs can be a practical way to in crease the engagement of patients in the health sector. Thus, the health services and providers need to develop OHCs to facilitate the social interaction of patients. The availability of online social support in OHCs can attract patients to join these communities and engage in their care management, which, in turn, will lead to service quality and satisfaction. Managers of OHCs should inspire patients to participate by setting up mechanisms and regulations that facilitate self-care efficacy. For instance, a credit-and-rating promotion system can be established for members or health experts can be joined to the community to organize information exchange activities.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

Although this study offers several interesting findings, certain limi tations should be mentioned. First, the results might have been influ enced by selection bias because the participants of this study were limited to active OHC members who had experience in exchanging health information with their peers in some online communities. Thus, they might have had a higher interest in health and health management. However, individuals who have not used OHCs or have adverse expe riences in the health care system might exhibit different perspectives. It would be interesting for future studies to investigate whether the findings can be generalized to nonparticipants, less experienced, or disaf fected participants as well.

Second, we recognize that some of the correlations among a few of the constructs are higher than the benchmark 0.7 that could alarm for multicollinearity. However, the square root of the AVEs of each construct is greater than the correlation coefficients between that construct and others in the model. Therefore, according to [146], the model satisfies the discriminant validity criterion. Hence, multi collinearity is a minor concern.

Third, it should be noted that the study is based only on the sample of respondents drawn from the United States. Therefore, the results may not be generalized to all users of OHCs in other countries. It is recom mended that future studies consider drawing samples from wider geographical areas, including other developed countries, as well as developing countries where technological infrastructures and internet services are less developed than in the United States. While investigating cultural factors is beyond the scope of this study, future research can examine the role of cultural dimensions in creating patient engagement. For example, it is interesting to identify whether individualism or collectivism may create different levels of information exchange participation in the context of OHCs. Our work also opens the door to future cross-cultural studies on health information-seeking and sharing behaviors.

Fourth, we used OHC as the context of our study, and the data of this research were collected from users of OHCs. While the findings provide valuable insights on how different factors influence members’ seeking and sharing health information, the results may not be able to generalize to all types of social media (such as Facebook, Instagram, or Twitter). Further research is needed to examine users’ participating behaviors and motivations for exchanging health-related information on different so cial media platforms.

Fifth, future research could leverage our findings and investigate the possible interaction effects between information exchange intentions, personality traits (e.g., agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, neuroticism, openness), and patient engagement. The results indicate that together the factors were able to explain 65 % of the variance in patient engagement with OHC. Another research avenue to consider is examining additional factors that may enhance the amount of variation in patient engagement explained. For instance. Future studies should include some factors, such as type of health information, satisfaction with personal doctors, and trusting beliefs into the model. Finally, future research should more deeply compare the effects of specific health status (e.g., physical and mental health stability) to articulate whether a lack of physical and mental health stability can exert different effects on in formation exchange intention and its relationship with patient engage ment in OHCs.

## 6. Conclusion

In this paper, we applied channel expansion and SET to explain pa tients’ engagement in OHCs and its impacts. We proposed and empiri cally tested seven sets of hypotheses on the antecedents and consequences of patient engagement in OHC. To examine the hypothe sized relation, we collected data from a national sample of 348 patients who are actively involved in OHCs within the last six months. The results show that perceived channel richness positively influences individuals expectations to receive informational support as well as emotional support, their willingness to share health information, and their engagement in OHCs. The findings also demonstrate that patient health status moderates the impacts of willingness to share and seek informa tion on engagement with OHC. This finding indicates that the impact of information-seeking and information-sharing intentions on participa tion in OHC conversations is not similar between healthy and unhealthy users due to different healthcare needs. Interestingly, the results refute our hypotheses on the relationships between willingness to exchange information (sharing and seeking) and engagement with OHCs. This result highlights that high levels of willingness to seek and share infor mation will not necessarily guarantee high levels of engagement in OHCs. The findings of this study provide insights for the development of patient engagement through OHCs and contribute to the literature on patient engagement, social information exchange, and health informatics.
