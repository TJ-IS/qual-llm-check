## Abstract

Social live streaming services are becoming increasingly popular worldwide, and viewers’ gifting behavior is critical to the success of the business model of these services. However, factors leading to such behavior are not well understood. Drawing on an identity-based motivation model, this study examines how class and relational identities affect viewers gifting behavior and how social density elicited by Danmaku as a significant contextual cue moderates the effects of identities on viewers’ gifting behavior. This study contributes to the gifting literature by revealing the differential effects of class and relational identities on gifting behavior and theorizing about the moderating effects of social density on these effects.

Keywords: Gifting, identity-based motivation model, live streaming, social density

## 1. Introduction

As a form of social media with streaming services, social live streaming services (SLSS) are increasingly popular because of their high interactivity and entertainment value. Live streamers can broadcast various content genres on SLSS platforms, such as games and talent shows [1]. Viewers can build and participate in impromptu channels through co-viewing, where they can interact with other viewers by commenting or sending gifts to their favorite streamers [2]. Twitch, which is one of the biggest live streaming websites worldwide, reported having 3.7 Twitch admitted that China’s live streaming market is “at another level” and grows more aggressively than the rest of the world [4]. Dor (2019) reported that more than 500 million people watch live streams on more than 200 live streaming websites in China and that China’s live streaming market is expected to hit 12.6 billion at the end of 2020.

The success of SLSS in China can be attributed to virtual gifting, which is one of the most successful business models [6] and stimulates streamers’ content generation and viewerstreamer interactions [7]. Most Chinese live streaming websites support gifting services. Gifts have two types: free gifts, which serve as a reward for viewers’ daily sign-in or watching from sales of paid gifts, whereas streamers can earn a proportional income from received gifts [7]. A previous study found that 20% of viewers contributed 97% of all sent gifts [8], indicating that a majority of gifts were sent by only a small number of viewers. This finding is similar to donations on Twitch [9]. Besides, most viewers only send free instead of paid gifts [10]. These findings warrant a scholarly investigation into the factors that lead to viewers’ free gift versus paid gift choices on SLSS platforms.

Although an increasing number of information systems (IS) studies have examined the gifting phenomenon in the SLSS context [1, 6, 11], few scholars have examined gifting in terms of free gift-giving (FGG) and paid gift-giving (PGG) as two different behaviors. Sending free gifts is popular as a cost-efficient way to express support and interact with the streamer. However, viewers who want to be noticed and recognized by streamers tend to spend money on paid gifts [12]. Such different behavioral patterns prompt us to adopt an identity perspective to explore the motivations that underlie the two gifting behaviors and explain the decisions regarding whether to gift and what to gift (i.e., gift selection).

Gifting carries symbolic meanings in personal and social dimensions [13]. Previous studies have found that gift selections are simultaneously affected by the giver’s concerns for the consistency of personal traits [14, 15] and specific social roles developed with the recipient [16, 17]. Therefore, gifts play a role in expressing the giver’s personal and social aspects of identity. Extant literature has explained gifting behavior primarily based on economic and social exchange models that emphasize reciprocity [18-20]. Nevertheless, gifting on SLSS platforms has brought about major changes in giver-recipient relationships and the social visibility of sending gifts, thereby highlighting the identity expression in personal and social aspects as major motivations. Although prior studies have identified the desire to present online identity as a key driver for purchasing digital items [21], a fine-grained examination of different aspects (i.e., personal vs. social) of online identity on gifting remains lacking.

This study draws upon the identity-based motivation (IBM) model to study the motivations for gifting behind a giver’s different identities. The IBM model posits that personal and social aspects of identity matter in motivating behaviors in social contexts [22]. We propose that viewers have two prominent identities. One is relational identity, which is the social aspect of identity developed from a viewer’s desire to build a private relationship with a specific streamer. The other is class identity, which is the personal aspect of identity that highlights social class and is noticeable to the public. The two identities are significant in the gifting context and should be examined simultaneously in the context of SLSS.

According to the IBM model, contextual cues can strengthen or weaken people’s identitybased motivations and affect further behaviors [23]. On SLSS platforms, the social density elicited from Danmaku (i.e., a type of comment that can scroll across a video in real-time) can affirmation. Therefore, social density of a streaming channel is a prominent cue for viewers identity-based motivations because people tend to reinforce identities by taking actions to address identity threats in a specific context [24]. This study considers social density as a prominent contextual cue of SLSS and examines how it influences the effects of viewers’ identities on their gifting behavior.

In summary, we seek to answer the following questions: (1) Do different types of identity on SLSS platforms affect viewers’ gifting behavior differently? (2) How are the effects of the identities contingent upon the social density of a streaming channel?

This study makes the following contributions. First, we decompose the gifting behaviors on SLSS platforms into FGG and PGG based on their underlying motives and practical implications. Second, we adopt the IBM model as a framework and specify relational and class identities as viewers’ prominent identities in the SLSS environment. We examine their common and differing effects on gifting. Third, we investigate the moderating effects of a contextual cue (i.e., social density of a streaming channel) on the relationships between viewers relational and class identities and gifting choices.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1 Gifting behavior on SLSS platforms

Gifting is defined as a behavior of giving an object to another without expectations of a known return [25]. Scholars in various disciplines, such as marketing [13, 14, 18, 26], psychology [24, 27], and economics [25], have studied gifting behavior. IS scholars have also examined gifting in online contexts, such as social networking sites, virtual worlds, and SLSS platforms [11, 20, 28].

Compared with other contexts, gifting on SLSS platforms has several new features. First, the giver-recipient relationship on SLSS platforms is one-sided and parasocial because viewers develop identification with someone they never meet [29]. Gifting is a popular way to maintain relationships with people who are socially connected [26]. In other contexts, the recipient is often someone who has reciprocal relationships with the giver (e.g., friends). Thus, the norm of reciprocity is identified as an important motive, and gifting has been explained from the perspective of economic and social exchange models in the literature [18-20, 28]. For example, Belk (1976) suggested that the giver and the recipient expect a balanced reciprocity in terms of economic gains. Kim et al. (2018) suggested that gifting on social networking sites is a social exchange because people assess the worth of gifting by making a trade-off between benefits happen on SLSS platforms; instead, viewers emotional attachment with streamers may play an important role in our research context [11].

Second, gifting on SLSS platforms is highly visible to social others. Gifting on physical and social networking sites is often limited to a private or small network of givers and recipients [20], whereas gifting on SLSS platforms is visible to all viewers in a streaming channel. As a status are increasingly prominent. Evidence from Hou et al. (2020) indicated that social status display is a significant motive for gifting on SLSS platforms. Findings from Zhou et al. (2019) implied that the giver is likely to seek social status when many viewers are in the channel. Therefore, unlike gifting in other contexts where givers care about the consistency between gifts and personality traits (e.g., creativity) [18, 30], givers on SLSS platforms care more about social status and display it as an important component of self-identity. The social cues (e.g., number of co-viewers) elicited from social others may influence viewers’ gifting on SLSS platforms as well.

Based on the new features of SLSS gifting, on the one hand, SLSS gifting can be motivated by a viewer’s relational identity developed from his/her emotional attachment with the steamers. On the other hand, a viewer’s class identity is significant on SLSS platforms, where gifting is highly visible to social others. Extant studies examined SLSS gifting primarily from a relational perspective (e.g., emotional attachment) [11] and focused on watching experience (e.g., interactivity) [1, 6] but failed to consider the identity-based motivations of givers behind different gifting strategies (i.e., FGG and PGG). Thus, to fill the gaps, we adopt a holistic perspective from the IBM model to explore how a giver’s identity induces different cognitions and gifting choices on SLSS platforms.

## 2.2 Identity-based motivation model

The IBM model offers a social cognition framework by integrating cultural and social identity perspectives and highlights the role of social contexts in triggering salient identities and personal goals that motivate behaviors [22]. According to the IBM model, people are motivated to understand and behave in a way that is congruent with the personal and social aspects of their identities [23]. This conjecture is consistent with the notion of Triandis (1989) that “individuals have both individualistic and collectivistic cognitive elements.” The personal aspect of identity includes traits and characteristics that indicate an individual mindset motivating people to attain desired identity goals and avoiding undesired ones. By contrast, the social aspect of identity is derived from social relationships that indicate a collective mindset; thus, people act to connect themselves with other group members [23].

The IBM model also holds that self-relevant information perceived in the environment influences how people develop their self-concept, and thus their identity is sensitive to social contexts [23]. For example, studies have revealed that self-concept related to class identity tends to develop in contexts where people encounter class disparities with others [39-41] and that race-ethnic identity is more salient and meaningfully distinctive for people whose ethnic group is in the minority [42]. Social contexts can also moderate resulting behaviors by affecting people’s self-goal [43]. For instance, people who link themselves to be relevant others are motivated to enhance themselves when provided with a successful target [44]. Thus, social contexts can significantly reinforce or weaken an individual’s identity-based motivations.

The IBM model has been applied in various contexts, such as health [45], academic performance [46], and consumption behavior [43, 47]. Studies in consumer research have highlighted the importance of identity motivations for gifting behaviors [13, 18, 48]. Schwartz (1967) described gifting as an activity that “imposes identity upon both giver and recipient.” Previous studies found that givers choose gifts consistent with their self-image, whereas sending identity-contrary gifts would bring an identity threat [14, 15]. Likewise, Sherry (1983) suggested that givers seek to confirm their identities in gifting. Kim et al. (2012) found that people purchase digital items to present online identities in a virtual environment. However, although studies have emphasized the role of identity in gifting across different contexts, most of them only conceptualized identity from a personal aspect, such as personality traits [18, 30]. Therefore, the relationships between different aspects (i.e., personal vs. social) of online identity and gifting have not been examined, which justifies the IBM model as a suitable framework for illuminating how identity motivations influence SLSS gifting.

Consistent with the IBM model, Ward and Broniarczyk (2011) argued that social relationships are integral to people’s sense of self and can affect their gifting behaviors. In an online community, the social relationship refers to the interpersonal bonds among individual members [49]. Whereas on SLSS platforms, the relationships are developed mainly between viewers and streamers because viewers are gathered impromptu and it is their interpersonal bonds with the streamer that link them in a live stream [50]. Thus, the relational identity, which indicates the role of fans in the relationship between a viewer and his/her favorite streamer, forms an individual’s social aspect of identity on SLSS platforms. Meanwhile, the personal aspect of the online identity of an individual can differ from his/her offline identity, and the presentation of online identity is restricted by the visual or textual cues available in the virtual space [51]. Thus, the class identity captured by a user’s noble status serves as a limited way of presenting his/her personal aspect of identity on SLSS platforms. In conclusion, drawing from the IBM model, we examine how relational and class identities, as different (i.e., social vs. personal) aspects of a viewer’s online identity, induce SLSS gifting. We also examine how social contexts moderate the relationship between identity-based motivations and different SLSS gifting choices.

## 2.3 Social density

In a crowded live streaming channel, Danmaku becomes an illegible waterfall of text that causes difficulty for the audience to follow the conversation [50]. Thus, viewers may feel a sense of crowdedness because of their cognitive constraints. We propose social density, which is defined as the perceived crowdedness in a channel of interest, as a key contextual cue that affects identity-based motivations. Marketing research has extensively studied how social density affects consumer behaviors [52-54], and some studies have examined similar effects of social size in the online context. For example, Messer et al. (2017) revealed that visitor traffic positively affects online shopping via routes of social pressure (e.g., competition) and social proof (e.g., product quality). Shmargad and Watts (2016) found that a large social size decreases a user’s intention to send gifts because of the anxiety of potential surveillance from friends on social media.

Although prior studies have explored social density as a direct factor in consumer behavior, according to the IBM model, social density can act as a social cue to reinforce or discount an individual’s identity-based motivations. To elaborate, first, the mere presence of others can affect motivations. Zajonc (1965) found that the presence of others can increase people’s arousal level and enhance their prior behavioral dispositions. According to the self-presentation theory, people present themselves to please the audience, and this motive varies depending on the importance of the audience [57]. The presence of identified others can activate a normative value and motivate the target to perform socially desirable behaviors in the group [58]. The “stands for the kind of prestige life, through success and ostentation;” it is an important concept in Chinese culture and is particularly desirable by the Chinese [59]. People who desire to save face have many concerns with how others think of them, and the motivation to save face can affect the importance attached to gifting and the given amount [60].

Second, studies have found that individuals experience a reduction of personal control in a high-density environment [61] and that a dense situation can impede the fulfillment of one’s self-goals [62]. People are likely to perform self-affirmation behaviors to restore their sense of control [63]. Consiglio et al. (2018) found that customers would use word-of-mouth to restore control of their images. Therefore, social density is likely to strengthen a viewer’s motivation for self-affirmation and control on SLSS platforms.

## 3.1 Effects of class identity on gifting

Class identity refers to an individual’s identity based on power and prestige distinction, and people can express the distinction by individualized ideas of dignity [65]. Class identity in the physical world is reflected by an individual’s economic position [41], whereas class identity on SLSS platforms is reflected by a user’s noble status. Noble users have the advantage of showy entrance notices and comments with distinct colors and decorations. People with high-class identity have an analytical thinking style, tend to view themselves as unique, and use conspicuous consumption to build a reputation [66].

On SLSS platforms, high-value gifts produce grand visual effects on a channel, and the visual effects can be “upgraded” when viewers send many gifts [12]. For example, if a viewer sends 999 “love hearts” at one time, then a super “shinning heart” explodes and fully occupies the viewer’s screen. Thus, PGG can be regarded as a conspicuous consumption on SLSS platforms. Previous research found that players attach importance to the visual authority value of virtual items to satisfy their needs to show off [67]. Hou et al. (2020) found that the motive for social status display strongly influences viewers’ intentions to buy virtual gifts. Thus, viewers with a high-class identity are likely to buy many paid gifts to show off and build a reputation on SLSS platforms.

Previous studies have also revealed that free gifts are ordinary products on SLSS platforms, and most givers only send free gifts instead of paid ones [10]. Based on the IBM model, as people attach their class identity to traits different from others [65], the personal aspect of identity is formed, which activates an individual mindset and a separating cognitive procedure. Thus, people tend to avoid taking actions that may bring them undesired identities. Berger and Heath (2007) studied from the identity-signaling perspective and showed that the high-class tend to signal their identities by abandoning preferences shared with majorities. As a result, high-class viewers tend to avoid sending free gifts because it may lower their desired public images. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1a: Class identity is positively related to the number of paid gifts given.

Hypothesis 1b: Class identity is negatively related to the number of free gifts given.

## 3.2 Effects of relational identity on gifting

Relational identity indicates an individual’s identity based on a role relationship with another (e.g., mother-child) [69], which activates a collective mindset and connecting cognitive procedures. Live streamers are similar to television celebrities; viewers can develop parasocial relationships with their favorite streamers and would describe themselves as “fans” [29]. Thus, relational identity on SLSS platforms can be regarded as a fan-celebrity relationship.

People with high relational identity tend to develop an affective commitment to a significant other and desire to enhance their relationships by taking actions [70]. Previous studies found that fans are likely to become passionate consumers who support celebrity jobs. For example, Babad (1987) observed that sports fans endorse their favorite teams in competitions. Lee and Watkins (2016) found that viewers who have parasocial relationships with YouTube vloggers are more likely to be persuaded to buy luxury brands endorsed by these vloggers. Studies on SLSS gifting found similar evidence that viewers’ high emotional attachment to streamers is an important factor for gifting intentions [11]. Kelman (1961) argued that relational identity can be “the desire to appease, emulate, or vicariously gain the qualities of the other.” Given that monetary incentive is the most important source of income for streamers, fans may attempt to be liked by their streamers by spending a lot on paid gifts. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2a: Relational identity is positively related to the number of paid gifts given.

People with high relational identity tend to attach great importance to the nature of personal interaction and intimacy with the target [74]. Given that a role relationship is built through a long-term relationship with their celebrity through various social engagements. For example, Stever (2011) found that fans will spend considerable time reviewing their celebrity’s information, such as photos or videos. In the SLSS context, Hu et al. (2017) found that viewers who highly identify with their streamer indicate a higher continuous watching intention in the channel. Given that free gifts are rewarded by the platforms at no cost, FGG is a convenient and cost-effective way to interact with a viewer’s favorite streamers and show emotional support. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2b: Relational identity is positively related to the number of free gifts given.

3.3 Interaction effects of identities on gifting

Class identity affects people’s thinking styles. The high-class tend to adopt an analytical thinking style that views the world as discrete and highlights the unique attributes of objects [66]. Likewise, people with high relational identity desire to be perceived as unique in the eyes of the target as they seek to develop a close connection with the target [74]. Moreover, the possession of high relational identity can further strengthen the high-class’s desires to stand out from the crowd and impress the streamer. Wang et al. (2019) observed that streamers would shout out the giver’s name when they receive expensive gifts during a live streaming, and some streamers would regularly send thank-you cards to those who send many gifts. Thus, for viewers with high relational identity, the high-class ones are much more likely to approach the streamer and acquire uniqueness through PGG than their low-class counterparts. By contrast, for viewers with low relational identity, the high-class are not so motived to win the streamer’s with low relational identity.

In addition, viewers who want to maintain a long-term relationship with the streamer tend to reward the streamer to “compensate” the streamer’s expended efforts [7]. Higher class identity is positively linked to the concerns of face [59]. For viewers with high relational identity, the high-class ones are less likely to give free gifts in the channel as compared to those low-class ones, given that the free gifts may bring them the risk of being regarded as “freeriders” and thus losing face. That is, the negative effect of class identity on giving free gifts is particularly strong. However, for viewers with low relational identity, they would be less concerned about the norm of reciprocity; thus, the negative effect of class identity on giving free gifts is week.

On the basis of the above discussions, we conclude that the effects of class identify on giving paid gifts and free gifts are contingent upon viewers’ relational identity with the streamer. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3a: An interaction effect exists between relational and class identities on the number of paid gifts given, such that the effect of class identity is stronger for givers who have high relational identity than those who have low relational identity.

Hypothesis 3b: An interaction effect exists between relational and class identities on the number of free gifts given, such that the effect of class identity is stronger for givers who have high relational identity than who have low relational identity.

## 3.4 Moderating effects of social density on gifting

Relational identity involves the self at an interpersonal and collective level [74]. Kim and Kim (2017) stated that people with high fan identification regard themselves as psychologically attached to the attributes of the fan community. Luo (2005) found that people are more likely to comply with the normative expectations of identified others when they are present. In our context, we contend that viewers who regard themselves as fans tend to comply with the norms and values in the channel. Gifting is assumed to be an informal norm to reward the streamer considering that gifts can encourage streamers to generate high-quality content [7]. Hence, the presence of others can strengthen fans’ awareness of group norms, and the effect of relational identity on giving either paid or free gifts should be strong. By contrast, in the absence of many other viewers in a streaming channel, the focal viewer is less urged to comply with the norm of rewarding the streamer; hence, the effect of relational identity on the number of paid or free

In addition, the interpersonal-level self emphasizes the interpersonal bond with a streamer. In a high-density situation, individuals may experience a loss of control and are motivated to restore it through compensatory behaviors [61, 64]. Similarly, in a high-density channel, viewers who want to attract the streamer’s attention would find it more difficult because the Danmaku is flushed quickly and is hard to follow. As a result, fans may perceive losing control and try to restore it by sending expensive gifts. By contrast, when the viewer density is low in a channel, the need for regaining control is limited, and thus the effect of relational identity on the number of paid gifts given is weak.

Based on the above discussion on the different levels of the effect of relational identity on the number of gifts, we posit the following moderating hypotheses:

Hypothesis 4a: The effect of relational identity on the number of paid gifts given is stronger in a high-density situation than in a low-density channel.

Hypothesis 4b: The effect of relational identity on the number of free gifts given is stronger in a high-density situation than in a low-density channel.

Social crowdedness can trigger defensive responses from the victims [78]. O’Guinn et al. (2015) indicated that high-class people tend to show their power and control with large territories. Therefore, in a crowded situation, high-class people may have strong motivations for defending their personal spaces. As paid gifts can appear above the chat area and stay long, high-class people are likely to defend and declare their territories by buying more paid gifts and thus better show their power. By contrast, when the viewer density is low, the need for fighting for territories in the channel is limited. Besides, Xu et al. (2012) found that to buy more distinctive products to reassert their own uniqueness. As high-class people put a high value on their uniqueness [66], the density of a channel can strengthen the effects of class identify on the purchasing of paid gifts. Likewise, as free gifts are ordinary products and inconspicuous, the high-class are much less likely to send free gifts in a dense channel, for they tend to avoid being confused with other audience in a crowded channel. That is, the density of a channel can also strengthen the negative effect of class identify on giving free gifts.

Moreover, face is associated with reputation and social standing; thus, people with highclass identity may attach great importance to winning face and avoiding losing face [59]. Yau et al. (1999) claimed that the Chinese are afraid of losing face because they may feel shame if someone gives them undesirable social feedback. Therefore, the concept of face has a significant effect on gifting. In particular, gift price is a concern, and low-price gifts would cause the giver to lose face [7, 81]. In a high-density channel, given that gifting behaviors are visible to many witnesses, high-class people are strongly motivated to separate themselves and win face from the conspicuous consumption of paid gifts, and they may perceive a high risk of losing face by giving free gifts. Therefore, the effects of class identify on giving paid and free gifts are stronger in a high-density channel than those in a low-density channel. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 5a: The effect of class identity on the number of paid gifts given is stronger in a high-density situation than in a low-density channel.

Hypothesis 5b: The effect of class identity on the number of free gifts given is stronger in a high-density situation than in a low-density channel.

## 4. Research Method

## 4.1 Research context

We scrawled data on the largest live streaming platform in China, Douyu (www.douyu.com), to test our hypotheses. By the end of 2018, Douyu had more than 66,000 active streamers and more than 434 million active users in an average month. Douyu is particularly suitable for testing our hypotheses for several reasons. First, Douyu is a typical live streaming website that supports a gifting system. Dou has a wide range of virtual gifts, such as rockets, airplanes, and light sticks. The visual effect of a free gift (e.g., light sticks) is negligible, whereas a paid gift (e.g., rockets and airplanes) is conspicuous and has an effect that stays long in a channel.<sup>1</sup> Second, Douyu supports fan badges and a noble system for its viewers, which allowed us to quantify our independent variables (i.e., class and relational identities). Third, Douyu allows users to establish their websites with rich information (e.g., social network and updated activities). We collected information on users’ characteristics from their webpages to control for individual differences.

## 4.2 Sample and data collection

Each verified streamer can create a live channel on Douyu, and each channel reveals the genre type and the streamer’s profile at the top of the webpage (see Figure 2). Different streaming genres induce distinct patterns of gifting behavior [10]. We considered two main types of genres on Douyu, namely, video games and talent shows. These genres are consistent with the most popular classification of streaming genres in the literature [76]. We targeted channels of both genres with relatively stable broadcasting activities in October 2018 (i.e., broadcasted over 20 days) to collect valid and real-time data. To ensure our samples are representative under the restrictions of crawling capacity, we randomly selected 40 channels for each genre based on subscriptions: 1) channels with over 10,000 subscriptions that represent rising stars, 2) channels with over 100,000 subscriptions, which represent the minor celebrities, and 3) channels with over 1,000,000 subscriptions, which represent the outstanding channels.

Data were collected between November 1 and 8, 2018. However, given Douyu’s access limitations to personal websites, we performed the main analysis based on data collected on November 1, 2018 (including both consumption and personal data). We observed the broadcast of 23 video channels and 25 talent show channels on that day. We provide a basic description of the channel characteristics in Appendix A. The results of a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test show that the distribution of channel characteristics between our samples and the top 1000 channels ranked by revenues (i.e., received values of all gifts) have no significant differences. Therefore, our samples are representative of popular channels, where the number of givers (mean = 11,433) and received values of all gifts (mean = 459,993) are in the lead.

## 4.3 Variables

The independent variables are the viewer’s class and relational identities. Douyu provides an aristocracy system to users who want to obtain privileges on the platform. By paying a membership fee, a noble user can display a noble medal in his/her profile card, enjoy a welcome animation when entering a channel, and customize the colors of Danmaku. Although previous literature has measured class identity by income, education, and occupation [40], the distinction on power or privileges lays a solid foundation for defining class identity because it underlines how people perceive their class positions [65]. Thus, we used the noble status to measure a user’s class identity on Douyu, where 1 denotes a high-class user with a noble membership and 0 denotes an ordinary and low-class user.

We used the level of a fan badge as a proxy to measure the relational identity of each viewer. Each Douyu streamer can design an exclusive fan badge, which can be placed with the viewer’s nickname and is visible to other audiences in the channel. The badge can only be obtained when a viewer gives the targeted streamer a special paid gift called “Get a Card.” A viewer’s fan badge can be upgraded by sending free or paid gifts or engaging actively in the corresponding streamer’s channel. The badge system has an upper limit per day. A viewer with a high-level badge means he/she has interacted with the streamer for a long time. Therefore, the level of fan badge reflects a viewer’s long-term relationship with the streamer and serves as an indicator for a viewer’ relational identity.

Social density is measured generally by the number of co-visitors in a given place [82]. Zhou et al. (2019) extended the measurement of the presence of others on SLSS platforms by minute to measure the social density of a streaming channel.

## 4.4 Model estimation

We employed the following empirical model to test our hypotheses. The model was adjusted to a natural logarithmic linear regression model because of the large variance of the dependent variables.

$$
\begin{array}{l} L n (N P G G) _ {i j} = \\ \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} C I _ {i j} + \alpha_ {2} R I _ {i j} + \alpha_ {3} C o n t r o l s _ {i j} + \beta C h a n n e l A t t r i b u t e s _ {j} + \\ Z _ {1} C I _ {i j} * R I _ {i j} + Z _ {2} L n (S D _ {j}) * C I _ {i j} + Z _ {3} L n (S D _ {j}) * R I _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i j} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} L n (N F G G) _ {i j} = \\ \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} C I _ {i j} + \alpha_ {2} R I _ {i j} + \alpha_ {3} C o n t r o l s _ {i j} + \beta C h a n n e l A t t r i b u t e s _ {j} + \\ Z _ {1} C I _ {i j} * R I _ {i j} + Z _ {2} L n (S D _ {j}) * C I _ {i j} + Z _ {3} L n (S D _ {j}) * R I _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i j} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Let i=1…N be the index of the viewer and $j { = } I$ …N be the index of the channel. $C I _ { i j }$ and $R I _ { i j }$ are the main explanatory variables, and Controls denotes a set of viewer characteristics, such as consumption expenditures and whether the viewer is a streamer on Douyu. $\mathrm { C I } ^ { * } \mathrm { R I }$ denotes the interaction between two identities. $\mathrm { L n } ( \mathrm { S D } ) ^ { * } \mathrm { C I }$ and $\mathrm { L n } ( \mathrm { S D } ) ^ { * } \mathrm { R I }$ are the interaction terms to model the moderating effect of social density. ChannelAttributes includes several channel attributes as controls, namely, channel type, social density, and the ratio of videos and plays. $\mathcal { E } \tilde { \iota }$ denotes a random error associated with observation $i j .$

A complicating factor in modeling the gifting behaviors is that PGG and FGG are likely to be intertwined, that is, a substitute or complementary relationship between PGG and FGG may exist. Thus, we estimate the model using the seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) method to account for correlated errors across equations [83]. Accordingly, the system consists of two interrelated equations: the first equation (Equation 1) represents when the dependent variable is the number of paid gifts given, and the second equation (Equation 2) represents when the dependent variable is the number of free gifts given. The systems of Equations 1 and 2 are estimated simultaneously to obtain effective estimations.

## 4.5 Heckman correction of self-selection bias

Self-selection bias arises if the noble users differ systematically from ordinary users in paying decisions. In our study, users decide as to whether to join a noble membership or not. This decision is endogenous because economic well-being simultaneously affect a user’ decision to pay for membership fees and future consumptions on gifting. In this case, an effective method to correct the bias is a two-step Heckman correction procedure (also known as Heckit method) [84].

The first step is to estimate the probability of a user joining the noble membership with a Probit model. In our context, we identified the number of observed variables that may affect a user’s propensity to join a noble membership and be included in the covariates Z. We included explanatory variables, such as whether the viewer is a streamer, the monthly expenditures, level of fan badge, numbers of posts, fans, and following on the platform. Specifically, a user’s actual consumption (i.e., monthly expenditure) is an appropriate measure of his/her living standards and economic well-being. It is also considered as a better measure compared with income, for it is less subject to seasonal shocks than income and can be accurately measured [85]. To satisfy the exclusion restriction, variables controlled for network characteristics and updated activities, such as the number of posts, fans, and following, are excluded in the main regression model. The second step includes the inverse Mills ratio (IMR) calculated from the Probit model into the main regression model and obtains unbiased estimations. The first step results of the Heckman correction are provided in Appendix C.

## 5. Robustness Checks

In this section, we perform several robustness checks of the main results. We proceed in three steps. First, we perform a propensity score matching (PSM) analysis as an alternative method to control for potential self-selection bias based on observed variables. Second, we used the spending amount on paid gifts as an alternative dependent variable for the PGG model. Finally, we apply an alternative model specification by considering unobserved channel heterogeneity and autocorrelation of observations in our model. These tests validate that our findings are not biased by omitted variables or model misspecifications.

As the PSM method relies on a strong assumption that the observed characteristics fully explain the selection process and no other hidden biases exist, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by calculating the Rosenbaum bounds [87]. When the Rosenbaum bounds (Γ) (log odds of differential assignment due to unobserved factors) equals to 1, then no unobserved selection effects exist. Therefore, the test statistics indicate how far away the Γ has to be from 1 to nullify the treatment effects identified in the PSM analysis. We found that the treatment effects at Γ = 1.5 remain significant at the 10 percent level in both conditions (DV = NPGG or DV = NFGG). This result suggests that to nullify the effect of noble membership on the gifting behaviors, the unobserved factors would have to affect the odds of selection into the treatment group by at least 50 percent. Therefore, the results are not sensitive to the possible presence of an unobserved confounding factor.

## 6. Discussion and Implications

## 6.1 Discussion of findings

Gifting is an important tool to promote streamers’ active content generation and viewerstreamer interaction [7]. We investigate the effects of a viewer’s class identity and relational that, in the context of SLSS, a viewer’s class and relational identities positively affect the number of paid gifts given, but class identity is negatively related to the number of free gifts given. An interaction effect also exists between relational and class identities on the gifting choices, such that the effects of class identity on PGG and FGG would be stronger if the giver has high relational identity with the streamer. We also confirm that social density is an important contextual cue that moderates the relationships between viewers’ class identity and their gifting behaviors.

##

To elaborate, we find that class identity positively influences viewers’ consumption of paid gifts but negatively influences free gift-giving behavior and that both effects were strengthened under a high-density condition. Given that people can deliver their desired identities to others by making diverse choices [68], viewers with high-class identity tend to distinguish themselves from others by having more conspicuous consumption and less similar behaviors as compared to with others. Moreover, this effect is amplified in a crowded situation where high-class people are strongly motivated to gain status through distance and avoid losing face in front of many other viewers [54]. For the effects of relational identity, gifts can serve as tools to provide both financial and emotional supports. Therefore, viewers with high relational identity are expected to show their emotional attachments and intentions to interact with their favorite streamers by sending paid or free gifts [11].

However, H4a and H4b are not supported. We found that social density cannot leverage the positive relationships between relational identity and gifting behaviors. In terms of PGG, one possible explanation is that although loyal fans may attempt to restore their sense of control and attract the streamer’s attention by consuming many paid gifts, they may also deliberately avoid the severe competition in a crowded situation given a limited budget. For example, for viewers who value the returns of investment on gifting, they may maximize their investments on gifting in a low-density situation, as in a case where there is a low risk the givers would be drowned in the crowd and neglected by the streamer. Thus, viewers’ different preferences on investments and risks may lead to different strategies as to the best time to give gifts (high- vs. low-density condition), hence possibly neutralizing the positive role of social density. Whereas for FGG, because paid gifts are expected by streamers, giving paid gifts instead of free gifts is more likely to become the informal norm in a channel [7]. Consequently, the presence of others would strengthen this norm, leading to the insignificant role of social density on FGG. In short, our findings indicate that social density is more effective in reinforcing the positive effects of the viewer’s class identity but not the effects of the relational identity on gifting behaviors on SLSS platforms.

## 6.2 Theoretical implications

Our research has several theoretical implications. First, we contribute to the IBM model by revealing the differential effects of a viewer’s coexisting identities, namely, class identity from the personal aspect of identity and relational identity from the social aspect of identity, on viewers’ gifting behaviors on SLSS platforms. We theorize that class identity induces individuals’ separating cognitive procedures such that this identity inspires people to take actions to obtain their desired identities but avoid undesired identities during the process. By contrast, relational identity can induce connecting cognitive procedures so that people tend to behave congruently with identified others. By extending the IBM model to our SLSS context, the study advances our understanding of how class and relational identities affect givers’ related cognitions and gifting choices. Extant literature treated online identity as a whole and did not identify different self-goals for online presentation (see, e.g., Refs. [21]). We reveal two salient aspects of online identity in the SLSS context and identify the underlying motivations related to these aspects. We also investigate how relational identity and class identity influence each other when affecting consumers’ gifting choices (H3a and H3b), which supplements prior research.

Second, this study considers FGG and PGG as different gifting behaviors, while most prior studies only examined users’ general intention of purchasing gifts [1, 11]. We examine the regarded as ordinary products, whereas paid gifts are conspicuous products on SLSS platforms. These two types of gifts function differently with respect to presenting identities and achieving self-goals. Therefore, we advance the understanding of viewers’ different gifting choices on SLSS platforms. Moreover, by using a dataset of users who enacted actual purchasing decisions, we avoided the biases commonly associated with surveys in previous research.

Third, we reveal that social density elicited by Danmaku can be a significant social cue that influences viewers’ identity-based motivations and resulting behaviors. Gifting can be affected by different contextual cues, such as the number of people present and their relationships with social others [35]. Although prior studies have found that audience size is positively correlated with a large number of gifts given on SLSS platforms [6, 10], their findings were obtained at the channel level and did not provide the theoretical mechanism to explain how contextual cues influence a viewer’s gifting behavior at the individual level. We fill the gaps by revealing that social density can reinforce the relationships between viewers class identity and gifting choices. Specifically, the moderating effect of social density on class identity is driven by viewers’ motives of seeking recognition and concerns with regard to face. identity-based motivations and gifting behaviors.

## 6.3 Practical implications

The findings from this study have several practical implications for business operators on SLSS platforms. First, this study finds that class identity is an effective indicator of viewers’ PGG behavior. Thus, SLSS platforms can attract people to become noble users by establishing leaderboards, awarding medals, and highlighting class badges with shinning colors. Moreover, platforms can further subdivide the current aristocratic system to add grades and reduce the entry barrier for users. Second, given that social density can significantly promote a viewer’s consumption of paid gifts, SLSS platform operators can encourage viewers to engage actively during live streaming. For example, an SLSS platform can carry out a variety of activities to encourage viewers to build social relationships with other members, or the platform can set a reward system based on users’ records of social engagements (e.g., comments and gifting). Streamers can also select more interesting live topics that can induce discussion and encourage viewers to interact with them, such as playing games together. Third, channel types affect consumer behavior. Our results show that people are more likely to send paid gifts when the channel type is talent shows. Therefore, SLSS platforms can encourage streamers to generate more talent-show-type content.

## 6.4 Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the results are based only on data collected from the Douyu website and thus may not be generalizable for gifting behaviors on SLSS platforms in other countries or cultures. Future studies must be conducted to test the validity of our findings in other live streaming platforms. Moreover, our samples and time duration may not be large enough because of the issues with the availability of data and the limitation of crawling capability. Nevertheless, we have added some robustness checks and provided support to our findings. Specifically, our data were collected from the top and popular channels at Douyu. Thus, verifying whether our conclusions on identities and gifting behaviors remain valid in new and small channels is needed. Future research could include more channels over a longer period of time. Second, we did not control for the time duration of each viewer staying in a channel in our model, as this information was not publicly disclosed on Douyu. Thus, future research may further examine how this time duration of staying may affect a viewer’s gifting behaviors. Third, differences exist between objective and subjective class identities [91]. In this study, however, we could only measure the objective class identity from the noble membership. Future research could combine online and real-world data to control individual characteristics and verify the robustness of our findings.

## 7. Conclusion

Most Chinese live-streaming platforms support FGG and PGG. However, previous studies have failed to distinguish between the two as different gifting choices. Drawing on the IBM model, this study investigates how viewers’ salient and coexisting identities (i.e., class and relational identities) influence their gifting choices. This study also investigates social density elicited by Danmaku as a moderator for the effects of the two types of identities to reveal the significant role of contextual cues. Our results suggest that the two identities induce different effects on viewers’ gifting choices, and social density significantly moderates the relationships between viewers’ class identity and gifting behavior. Thus, SLSS platforms and live streamers should focus on the differential effects of personal and social aspects of identity and pay attention to social density in a channel. This study also offers guidelines for live-streaming practitioners to encourage users’ engagement on the SLSS platform.
