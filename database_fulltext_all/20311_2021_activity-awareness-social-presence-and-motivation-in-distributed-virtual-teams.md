# Activity awareness, social presence, and motivation in distributed virtual teams

![](/api/attachments/Y9JVRBJM/fulltext/images/8a6cc4bea526f5a94bae815fb4d67c0c46b2664cf16b00aa4436a66015fdb22c.jpg)

Russell Haines

Old Dominion University, Department of Information Technology and Decision Sciences, Norfolk, VA, 23529, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Awareness Social presence Virtual teams Social motivation Coordination

## A B S T R A C T

Distributed virtual team members are unable to directly observe each other, making it difficult to evaluate each other’s efforts. The results of an experiment are reported in which activity awareness and feelings of social presence had a significant impact on willingness to work, which in turn were associated with higher feelings of loyalty. This suggests that in many working contexts, awareness practices can increase motivation and perfor mance, in contrast with prior virtual team research that found decreased trust and effort. Designers and users of collaboration technology are therefore advised to consider social motivation effects that might occur in virtua teams.

## 1. Introduction

How can people be motivated to increase their effort when working in a team? When working on a task with others, there are times when one will become immersed in the task and interaction with the others to the extent that outside matters are ignored ([1], p. 38). However, there are times when people working in a team are not as interested in making their best effort. The phenomenon of social motivation has been studied for many years by social psychologists (e.g., [2]) and researchers that study distributed teams (e.g., [3]). This line of research suggests that individuals work harder when they feel their efforts can be identified and evaluated ([4], p. 87).

Unfortunately, identifying and evaluating individual contributions to a team’s objectives can be difficult even in teams working together face-to-face [5]. When team members are geographically dispersed and interact only via computer-mediated communication (CMC), the lack of physical presence can make evaluation virtually impossible [3]. This forces team members to exert additional effort in order to evaluate contributions [6]. Virtual teams research suggests that geographic dispersal can affect the development of relationships within a team to the extent that a large number of teams are unable to organize them selves and complete their assigned task [7–9]. Furthermore, social motivation losses are ostensibly widespread in virtual team contexts, with prior research observing them in electronic brainstorming [10,11] and group decision-making [12].

However, recent studies suggest that higher social presence in distributed teams has a positive effect on satisfaction [13] and team performance [14], and that higher social presence facilitates helping behavior [15]. Furthermore, positive team outcomes can occur when virtual team members understand the reason for others' behavior [16] and when teams develop practices that increase awareness of members activities [17]. Because prior research did not suggest mechanisms beyond lack of physical presence that reduce social motivation in virtual teams, an examination of the impact of social presence on motivation would fill a gap in the literature. Furthermore, there is virtually no research about the impact of activity awareness on social presence in virtual teams. Group process theory suggests that social motivation gains can occur in face-to-face contexts when members are more aware of others’ activities (cf., [18]) because the team can see where adapting their processes would improve outcomes and/or the members could see when the team would benefit from a little more effort $( \mathrm { c f . , ~ } [ 1 9 , 2 0 ] )$ There has been some interest in the effect of activity awareness infor mation on trust in virtual teams in the information systems literature (e. g., [9,26]); however, the potential for activity awareness or social presence to increase motivation and performance has received little attention.

Thus, the research questions for this study are: (1) How does awareness of others’ activities affect one’s feelings of social presence with a team and one’s motivation to work? and (2) How does social presence affect attraction and affiliation to the team? This paper begins with a brief review of empirical studies of social motivation in virtual teams. This is followed by a theoretical foundation, which discusses the propositions of group process theory, the role of awareness in face-toface and virtual teams for coordinating behavior, and the development of hypotheses. Next, the design and results of a survey conducted in a controlled experimental environment are presented. The paper con cludes with a discussion of the results, along with implications for re searchers and software designers.

## 2. Literature review

As part of the development of this research project, a literature search and review was conducted using indexes of peer-reviewed jour nals. The search terms chosen were broadly related to activity awareness in virtual teams, and included terms involving awareness (e.g., activity tracking, status reporting, etc.), social presence (e.g., connectedness, copresence, anonymity etc.), and motivation (e.g., social loafing, perfor mance, satisfaction, etc.). The next step of the review was to read the abstracts of the articles and consider only empirical articles that (1) studied virtual teams (e.g., not learning environments or web site evaluation), and either (2) studied social presence as an independent variable, or (3) studied social presence, motivation, or cohesion as an outcome of awareness. Finally, the reference list of the remaining arti cles was consulted to find additional studies that were not part of the initial search results.

The salient empirical literature is summarized in Table A5, in the Appendix. Several theoretical gaps can be observed. Overall, the awareness literature has few studies of the linkage between awareness and social presence, and only implicitly mentions social motivation as an outcome of awareness. Although the awareness literature is particularly interested in how various aspects of awareness are created, the social presence literature generally takes the processes that would be used to create it for granted. Finally, the social presence literature implicitly assumes a linkage between social presence and motivation by studying its links to trust, satisfaction, cohesion, and performance; however, this linkage has never been explicitly studied. Thus, this manuscript con nects two literature streams in the information systems literature and fills several important gaps in the theoretical chain that connects the two. Group process theory, a well-established theory of motivation and performance in face-to-face groups [4], was used as a theoretical lens to examine the prior virtual teams literature and as a basis for the theo retical foundation of the study.

## 3. Theoretical foundation

Group process theory arises from the observation that, when given a task to accomplish as a team, the effort exerted by the team as a whole is often less than the total amount of effort that the individual members are capable of individually. The so-called process losses are defined as the difference between the sum of the individual team members’ capabil ities and the team’s performance [5]. Process losses consist of (1) co ordination losses, which arise when members’ efforts are not contributed at the best time or not fully used by the team and (2) motivation losses. which arise when team members do not exert their best effort on behalf of the team [4,5].

## 3.1. Coordination losses

Coordination in a team denotes the overall process of managing ac tivities and the dependencies between them [21]. For example, a team could have shared resources and have to schedule when individual members may use them, or they might have a situation where some tasks have to be completed before other tasks can begin. Thus, coordination processes comprise the entire set of activities that are used to manage task and subtask dependencies in teams and are used to ensure that team members know what they need to do and when they need to do it. Without these activities, members’ efforts could be used inappropriately and ineffectively because they duplicate efforts and/or interfere with the efforts of others.

## 3.1.1. Awareness to facilitate coordination

Awareness of the activities of other team members – what they are working on and how those activities relate to each other – is critical for coordinating activities within a team [22]. Using awareness informa tion, one can develop an understanding of what others are doing and use this information to provide a context for one’s own activities [23]. In face-to-face settings, awareness has been shown to be critical for a va riety of contexts, including air traffic [24] and subway controllers [25]. Because one can observe others directly in a face-to-face setting, one can often gather information about others’ activities even though they may not explicitly communicate what they are doing. For example, a retail manager might be able to directly observe that a cashier is engaged on the phone, meaning that manager is aware that the cashier cannot serve customers until the call is complete. Awareness places one’s activities in the context of others’ activities (e.g., the manager needs to close the cashier’s line until the call is complete), provides additional context about the others (e.g., the cashier’s automobile is being serviced after a traffic accident earlier in the week), and can thereby decrease coordi nation losses.

Knowledge workers, though perhaps less directly connected to each other than retail or manufacturing workers, also have awareness needs that, when met, enable them to coordinate their efforts with others. In an office environment, open offices and/or cubicles make obtaining infor mation about other people’s activities relatively easy: one can overhear conversations and see others enter and exit other people’s workspaces. This activity awareness can be used to coordinate activities and decrease coordination losses; for example, a request for information can be timed such that one is ready to ask as soon as the other’s phone conversation ends.

In a virtual team setting where one cannot directly observe other team members in their physical environment, information about the activities of others cannot be obtained by direct observation. When one only interacts with others via CMC, awareness information must be provided explicitly if it is not automatically conveyed by the mediating technology. Referring to the prior example, if the retail manager can only see that a particular cashier’s line is growing but not what the cashier is doing, the manager will not be able to form a realistic expectation of when the line will begin moving. Perhaps worse, the manager can only speculate about why the cashier is not able to com plete his/her work (cf., [16,26]). Indeed, one might think of a virtual team setting as analogous to a face-to-face setting in which another is observable as being bodily present, but whose back is turned such that his/her activities cannot be directly observed. In such a setting, it might be possible to identify another’s contributions as they are produced, but one has a difficult time predicting when those contributions will occur, much less being able to evaluate whether the other’s efforts are adequate.

## 3.1.2. Mitigation of coordination losses via CMC

Distributed virtual team members that communicate via CMC can compensate for coordination losses that arise when others being unable to directly observe them by developing practices that convey awareness information. For example, one can use the presence and status message features of most instant messaging (IM) applications to create and maintain awareness of other team members’ presence or activities [27]. This information can be used by the team to coordinate their activities and ensure achievement of their overall goal [17,28,29]. Providing ac tivity awareness information can also help with team coordination processes in specific ways – for example, improving the performance of a team by enabling a team member to more carefully time when he/she interrupts another team member [30,31].

System designers can explicitly add user interface elements that automatically show information about the activities of others via mediated communication channels, such as presence indicators, activity monitors, or status messages [22,28]. Such user interface elements can make awareness practices easier for users to create and implement [32];

however, user practices for creating and interpreting awareness infor mation must be developed in order for a technology’s intended purpose of decreasing coordination losses to be realized [64]. Thus, there is not a direct link between an awareness-providing technology tool and other factors like feeling connected or motivated. Rather, the technology’s impact is mediated by one’s perceived awareness of the activities of others:

H1. Members of virtual teams with a user interface element that automatically provides information about the activities of other mem bers will have stronger perceived awareness of the activities of others.

In a face-to-face setting, team members often take it for granted that they can observe the activities of others and can be relatively unreflec tive about the importance of this information in decreasing coordination losses. Distributed virtual team members, however, cannot directly observe other team members’ activities, making it important for them to feel like their communication technology can be used to connect the members of their team [33]. A feeling of being connected to the rest of one’s team is widely termed social presence [34]. Prior research has shown that practices that provide activity awareness enable team members to feel like other team members are fully present and available [35]. In addition, qualitative researchers have observed situations in which distributed team members valued activity awareness to the point that they left teleconferencing connections open just so they could hear the sounds of other’s typing [27]. When activity awareness is lacking, it has been shown to decrease understanding of the behavior of others [16, 26] and potentially leads to conflict [17]. Thus, activity awareness is an important prerequisite for feeling like one’s virtual team is a socially present, connected group rather than being an agglomeration of members:

H2. In virtual teams, stronger perceived awareness of the activities of other team members will lead to stronger feelings of social presence.

## 3.2. Social motivation

In addition to coordination losses, which occur when one is not sure when or how to apply one’s efforts for one’s team, group process theory proposes that teams can also experience social motivation losses, which occur when one questions whether to apply one’s best effort [4]. Thus, social motivation losses can also affect performance of distributed teams. For example, a team that is brainstorming is more likely to generate a good idea if all of the members are contributing, but a given member may not necessarily exert the effort to contribute ideas at all [10]. According to group process theory, social motivation losses occur most often when individual efforts cannot be directly observed, and are particularly salient for teams performing conjunctive tasks in which the team’s performance is largely determined by the performance of the weakest person [5]. Social motivation losses are primarily a function of a team member feeling (1) high dispensability and/or (2) low involve ment [4].

High dispensability indicates the team member feels that their effort is not necessary for his/her team’s performance. For example, a member of a brainstorming team might feel that his/her ideas are not worth contributing because others are contributing better ideas. Low involve ment occurs when an individual member does not care whether his/her team performs well or not, and therefore contributes less than he/she is capable. For example, a member of a brainstorming team might not feel loyalty to the team and/or might not feel motivated by the team’s po tential reward, and therefore does not contribute as many ideas as he/ she could. On the other hand, high involvement can lead team members to motivation gains when team members have a high degree of visibility about each other’s efforts – members that would perform poorly when alone will actually work harder as part of a team in face-to-face contexts [19].

## 3.2.1. Mitigation of motivation losses via CMC

When using CMC, prior research suggests that the influence of others is reduced because one does not feel their presence to the same extent as in face-to-face environments ([6,34,35]). In anonymous CMC contexts, the influence of others is reduced even more [36,37]. This lack of in fluence results in lower motivation in CMC decision-making contexts (e. g., [10,38]). As a result, a rather bleak view of virtual teamwork is pervasive. According to research on virtual team development, organi zations should expect to see virtual team members shirking their re sponsibilities and focusing on the failings of other team members, with the result that team members have low trust in each other and their teams are ineffective at accomplishing their assigned tasks $( \mathbf { e } . g . , [ 8 , 3 9 ] )$ In a study of processes that are supposed to improve virtual teams, re searchers found that requiring members to report their activities in a formal weekly status report reduced social motivation rather than motivating members to work harder [9]. The authors suggest that this occurred because the reports “highlighted differential contribution and failure to fulfill these obligations by some team members” ([9], p. 387)

In contrast to this notion, the development of informal awareness practices by virtual team members that increase awareness of the ac tivities of others can create a working environment where members feel like they are able to trust that others are working [17]. Ultimately, awareness forms the basis for making comparisons among team mem bers [6], meaning that, rather than highlighting failures, activity awareness might highlight contributions and motivate virtual team members to work harder in a context that enables trust. As noted earlier, comparison information cannot be taken for granted in distributed vir tual contexts, and must be imputed using the information that has been provided by others or by the mediating technology [23]. When activities are part of that information, group process theory proposes that one would be expected to at least match the efforts of others [4] and perform to a higher standard [2,19] rather than free riding or social loafing. This occurs because other team members can observe and evaluate one’s efforts:

H3. In virtual teams, stronger perceived awareness of the activities of other team members will lead to stronger willingness to work.

In essence, being more aware of the activities of other team members should be expected to increase one’s own effort because of increased social comparison. In a face-to-face context, social loafing can be elim inated when group members work with people that they are close to and are allowed to develop cohesiveness [40]. In distributed teams, mem bers indicated that motivation increased when they received activity information from others, reporting increased feelings of being connected with their team [17].

A case was noted earlier where periodic, mandatory reports led to demotivating effects rather than motivating members to work harder [9]. Another reported case from another organization had a similar observation: members were observed copying emails to supervisors and other colleagues to show “how busy I am” ([41], p. 14). Based on these results, having additional information about the activities of others is likely only one of the factors that increase social motivation. One can expect that social motivation will also be increased when one feels more connected to the other members of one’s team (cf., [26,35,42,43]). In a distributed context, connectedness heightens the virtual social presence of others [8,35], raising evaluation apprehension $( { \bf c } { \bf f } . , ~ [ 2 , 6 , 1 9 ] )$ . In contrast to group process theory’s direct impact of activity awareness on motivation in which one directly compares one’s effort with the effort of others [4], feelings of evaluation apprehension are more about the feeling of having an audience [2,44]. Thus, in a distributed environ ment, feelings of social presence at least partially mediate the link be tween awareness of the activities of others and increased social motivation:

H4. In virtual teams, stronger feelings of social presence are associated with stronger willingness to work.

## 3.2.2. Social presence, motivation, and team loyalty

According to group process theory, feelings of attraction and affili ation toward one’s team increases in face-to-face teams when members feel a sense of community and are satisfied with the team’s performance [4]. Teams using CMC have been shown to increase their feelings of attraction and affiliation over time [45], likely stemming from the introduction of communication practices such as notification of when a message will be read [39], or by the increasing use of relational communication [46]. Earlier, it was noted that such awareness practices encourage a feeling of social presence with others on a work team. Social presence should therefore at least partially mediate the relationship between activity awareness and the strength of one’s desire to remain on the team and/or work with the same team members again (cf., [47]). Thus, feelings of attraction and affiliation to a team, here termed loyalty, are at least partially a reflection of a virtual team member’s feelings about whether they feel like they are connected with the other members of the team:

H5. In virtual teams, stronger feelings of social presence are associated with stronger feelings of team loyalty.

In teams where suspicions arise that others are not putting forth their best efforts, satisfaction with one’s team decreases [48]. In contrast, when team members are willing to do additional work for their team, they are likely to feel that other team members likewise feel obligated to do the same [43]. Working harder to achieve team goals is linked with feelings of attraction and affiliation [45]; thus, team loyalty is at least partially a reflection of a team member’s feelings about whether they, and by extension others, are willing to work harder for the team:

H6. In virtual teams, stronger feelings of willingness to do additional work are associated with stronger feelings of team loyalty.

The research model, which integrates the constructs of activity awareness and social presence in distributed teams into group process theory, is summarized in Fig. 1. A survey conducted in a controlled experimental environment in which virtual teams worked on a medical diagnosis task was used to test the hypotheses. Participants were placed into three different virtual teams over the course of an experimental session, which enabled examination of the hypotheses as the partici pants became more familiar with the technology, task, and work envi ronment. In contrast to prior studies, the experimental setting for this study modeled real life workgroup contexts that are conducive to reducing social loafing [40]: (1) teams should have a specific goal rather than just “do your best,” (2) team members should be free to commu nicate, and (3) team members should know each other and feel they might work together in the future. These conditions can enable the rapid development of roles, relationships, and expectations in teams [49], and should inspire higher levels of performance when members can constructively compare their efforts with others [19]. The ability to monitor and compare efforts with other team members emerges in vir tual teams as a result of awareness practices, and as noted earlier, awareness of others’ activities cannot be taken for granted via CMC like it can in a face-to-face context. The experimental task therefore facilitated conditions in which teams that developed activity awareness practices could experience the hypothesized social motivation gains from their members. The hypotheses are expected to hold across all three virtual teams and time periods; however, other links among the study variables were tested on an exploratory basis to see if those re lationships evolved as team members participated in accomplishing tasks with their first, second, and third teams.

## 4. Research method

Participants were recruited from a first year general education course offered by the business school at a public U.S. Eastern University. All communication during the experiment took place via CMC.

## 4.1. Experimental task

The teams’ task for all three task rounds involved the completion of medical diagnoses for a series of hypothetical patients in a generic “crisis” situation. The medical context was understandable and relatable to the participants, who all had some familiarity with medical diagnosis processes via their own experience and portrayals of medical contexts in popular culture. The experimental context modeled real world medical contexts where diagnosis and treatment involves several different health care providers, such as paramedics, triage nurses, general practitioners, surgeons, and specialists [50–52]. Generally speaking, the medical diagnosis process begins with a paramedic or triage nurse as first responder, who passes information on to other, more specialized pro viders for more specialized diagnosis or treatment.

The diagnosis process was such that a team, working together, could complete the medical diagnosis of a patient with the assistance of rolespecific expert systems. To keep the overall process relatable and involving, a subset of health care providers was modeled and the hy pothetical patients had a relatively narrow set of medical conditions that were either respiratory (e.g., streptococcal pharyngitis) or digestive (e. g., campylobacteriosis). Patients presented primary symptoms of persistent cough or diarrhea, and participants obtained secondary symptoms as part of simulated examinations, which included fever, hematochezia, and/or hemoptysis.

Three medical roles were filled by team members in this study: (1) nurse, (2) doctor, and (3) specialist. The nurse completed his/her job by interviewing a patient and received a patient’s primary symptom and vital signs as output (e.g., persistent cough with a fever). The doctor completed his/her job by entering a patient’s primary symptom and vital signs as received from the nurse, then examined the patient. As output, the doctor received more specific symptoms (e.g., hemoptysis, hematochezia) that were passed on to the specialist. The specialist entered the patient’s primary symptom, vital signs, and specific symp toms, and received a final diagnosis as output, which was the final step in the process. The results of each interview, examination, or diagnosis appeared on the screen after a delay of 15 s. A delay was chosen in order to allow team members some idle time to observe their screens, compose messages, and/or read messages from other team members.

The participants had the same role during all three rounds of the experiment. For example, a participant assigned to the doctor role (1) read doctor-specific instructions during the instructions phase of the experiment, (2) was identified as a doctor during every chat session, and (3) used the doctor-specific expert system during every diagnosing period. The participants were re-assigned to new teams at the conclusion of each diagnosing period but kept the same role. This meant that a participant in the doctor role might work with a different specialist and nurse for Rounds 1, 2, or 3. A team-level performance incentive was given to the participants: the participants in each session whose three teams diagnosed the most patients overall won a nominal cash prize. The cash prizes were distributed at the conclusion of each experimental session.

## 4.2. Experimental procedures

The experiment was completed by the students during normal classroom hours as a class exercise illustrating online collaboration. All sessions were administered by the same person, who was not an instructor of the class. Students received a small candy bar or pencil for participating. No course credit was given for participating, and students could choose to observe another student instead of participating; how ever, all chose to participate. The experimental procedures are sum marized graphically in Fig. 2. First, the participants entered their first name, indicated their informed consent, and filled out a demographic questionnaire. Next, the computer totaled the number of participants in the session and randomly assigned the participants to a role. When the class size was not an exact multiple of three, the remaining participants were given the role of lab technician, meaning some teams had four members. The specialist on four-member teams had to enter the results of a lab test before a patient diagnosis could be completed. Teams with four members were excluded from the analysis – the data for this study include only teams that had three members.

Once the roles were assigned, the participants were given verbal instructions that were also displayed on-screen about the rules of the exercise (e.g., “You are allowed to converse with your team through email or chat messages, so no talking out loud.”), the different roles on a team, the task their teams needed to complete, and how to use the messaging system. Finally, each participant read role-specific in structions: how to use their expert system, the information they needed to receive and from whom, and the information they needed to send and to whom it should be sent. After all of the participants had read these instructions, the administrator answered any questions. Next, they were told that they would meet in a chat room and instructed to use that time to decide on a process for completing a patient diagnosis. The partici pants were then randomly assigned to a team and the team was randomly assigned a treatment. Thus, began the first of three diagnosing rounds, the processes of which are summarized graphically in Fig. 3.

Each round consisted of two separate periods: an organizing period and a diagnosing period. During the organization periods, team mem bers communicated via a chat room in which posted messages were displayed to all team members simultaneously. The first round’s orga nizing period lasted five minutes in order to give team members time to familiarize themselves with their role and how it fit in the diagnosing process. Because the participants were more familiar with their roles and the diagnosing process, a three-minute chat period was used for the second and third rounds’ organizing periods. This shorter time period did not seem to affect the teams in any way, and was chosen because the longer time period led to about two minutes of idle time and off topic chat messages during pilot studies. During the diagnosing period, par ticipants used an email system to communicate. The email system was integrated into the experimental application and was modeled after current web-based email systems. An inbox displayed all of the messages that the participant had received during that period in a list, and the text of a message was displayed below the list when that row was clicked (Figs. 4 & 5 ). The participants composed and sent messages by clicking a New Message button then addressing the message using a drop-down list that contained the names of their team members. All three diagnosing periods lasted four minutes.

Each participant performed the same role as part of three different teams over the course of the experiment, meaning they became more familiar with their role and the processes and communication practices surrounding it as the session proceeded. Team reassignment happened after the teams in the session had finished each diagnosing period. After completing the first post-diagnosing questionnaire, the participants were randomly assigned to a new, second team, which was randomly assigned to a treatment. As new teams, team members met in a chat room to decide on a process for diagnosing patients, then diagnosed a second set of patients. After their second team was finished diagnosing patients, the participants completed the post-diagnosing questionnaire again and were randomly assigned to a new, third team and treatment. Thus, over the course of participating in three different teams, the par ticipants were able to become skilled with using the technology and at performing their own role, while: (1) becoming familiar with different team processes for accomplishing the task and (2) creating and adopting different practices for facilitating awareness of the activities of others.

Throughout the experiment, messages were identified by first names, which were entered by the participants at the beginning of the session when they indicated informed consent. Identification of messages enabled team members to recognize people they had worked with before and might work with again during this experiment or on other class projects. On the first, second, and third teams, each participant always performed the same role, but potentially worked with new people. This meant that a given participant in a given round could be on a team that was the same size as a previous round but with one or more different team members, or be on a team with a different number of members. In this way, the experiment simulated a real-life work environment where an individual becomes an expert at his/her own role, but is working with new members and/or with new steps in the process. This environment is typical in the health care industry as a face-to-face context, and in other industries in both face-to-face and virtual team contexts.

## 4.3. Study variables

Treatment and non-treatment team members had identical email interfaces, except for an on-screen indicator that showed the activities of their team members (compare Figs. 4 and 5). The on-screen indicator changed its status whenever a team member clicked on an object on his/ her screen. For example, clicking on an email would change the status to “Reading message.” The status changed for email composing, reading, and sending as well as for activities involving their expert system (e.g., “Started on Patient” was displayed when the participant began the interview, examination, or diagnosis process). The expert system that a participant used to interview, examine, or diagnose a patient was located below the status display on the left side of the screen.

The scales used for this study were adapted from validated scales when possible. However, some scales were developed specifically for this study by reducing and/or modifying a larger set of items that were developed via card sorting and used during two pilot study sessions. The scales were all Likert-type seven point scales, and randomly mixed together on a single questionnaire page. Scale reliability and validity was analyzed for each of the three rounds separately and is reported in the results section. Activity awareness is defined as the extent to which one feels that one knows when the other people on the team are work ing. Five items were used to measure activity awareness, which were based on the scale developed by [18]; anchored Not At All – To A Great Extent):

1) I could tell when the other people on this team were occupied with work.

2) I knew when the other people on this team were busy.

3) I knew whether or not I should wait before sending a request or infor mation to another person on this team.

4) I was aware of when the other people on this team were doing something.

5) I recognized when the other people on this team were working versus not working.

Social presence is defined as the extent to which one feels connected to others in a virtual team via a mediating technology [33,35]. Four items were used to measure social presence, which were based on the scale developed by [13]; anchored Not At All – To A Great Extent):

1) I felt like the messaging system connected me to the other people on this team.

2) I felt like the people on this team were connected through the message system.

3) The message system linked me with the other members of this team.

4) It seemed like we were linked together as a team.

Willingness to work is defined as one’s feeling that it is reasonable to put forth more effort for the team. Five items were used to measure willingness to work (anchored Strongly Disagree – Strongly Agree):

1) I did not mind working a little harder for this team.

2) It was not a big deal do work a little harder for this team.

3) I felt like it was okay to exert a little more effort for this team.

4) It was reasonable to exert a little more effort for this team.

5) I did not mind working a little bit more for this team.

Loyalty measures one’s feelings of attraction and affiliation, and is defined as one’s feeling of enjoying being on a particular team and wanting to work with members of the team again. Three items were used to measure loyalty, which are derived from the scale developed by Dobbins and Zaccaro [53] anchored Strongly Disagree – Strongly Agree):

1) I feel that I am really a part of this team.

2) I hope to work with the members of this team again.

3) I enjoy being a member of this team.

## 6. Discussion

The hypotheses were confirmed by the experimental results: providing activity awareness information about other team members via a user interface element increased feelings of awareness of the activities of others (H1). Awareness of others in turn was associated with higher feelings of social presence (H2) and willingness to work (H3). Higher social presence was associated with higher willingness to work (H4), and team loyalty was positively associated with social presence (H5) and willingness to work (H6). Thus, it can be concluded that increased awareness of the activities of others increases social motivation and feelings of social presence in virtual team contexts where members can constructively compare their efforts with others. This in turn leads to higher team loyalty.

## 6.1. Limitations

Team members in this study performed their tasks in a context that modeled conditions that are conducive to reducing social loafing [40]. Although these conditions are present in many organizations, team tasks in organizations are often less clearly defined. In such contexts, team processes might take several weeks or months to develop instead of in a few minutes. Furthermore, as noted earlier, some organizational CMC contexts are less constructive when it comes to the comparison of efforts, meaning activity awareness could have a detrimental effect on social presence and willingness to work there. There is also a trade-off between being able to measure the relationships among the study variables at the various time points and the possible confounding effect of having par ticipants answer the same questions several times; meaning researchers that wish to study mature working relationships should consider only administering their questionnaire one time. Finally, the user interface element conveyed relatively little information about the online activities of these virtual team members when compared with some workspace awareness technologies (e.g., [56]).

Clearly, additional research is needed to examine whether and why different results occur under less constructive organizational conditions $( \mathrm { c f . , ~ } [ 9 , 4 1 ] )$ . However, while it is easy to be dismissive of simple tech nologies and experimental contexts, the results suggest this is potentially a fruitful line of research. Very simple activity awareness technologies have been shown to become part of awareness practices that increase trust in more sophisticated, longer term organizational contexts [17], and have been used as the basis for strategic staffing decisions [57].

## 6.2. Team performance

Team performance was measured by the number of patients diag nosed per round by each team, and was examined on an exploratory basis. Any causal assertions here are speculative because team perfor mance and the participants’ responses to the study variables should be assumed to be intertwined. However, some additional opportunities for future research can be identified. The user interface element had no impact on performance for any of the rounds. For Round 1, none of the variables had a significant link with team performance $( | \mathbf { b } | < = . 1 5 9 , \mathbf { p } >$ .05). This can be broadly attributed to the participants’ inexperience with each other, the task, and the technology, and adds to the literature cautioning against using one-shot experimental tasks for distributed virtual teams research.

In later rounds, significant links with performance were observed. When all variables were used to predict performance, only team loyalty had a significant link $\left( \mathtt { p } < . 0 5 \right)$ for Rounds $2 \left( \mathbf { b } = . 3 4 2 , \right.$ , all others $| \mathbf { b } | < =$ .118) and 3 $\mathrm { \nabla { \cdot } } \left( \mathrm { b } = . 4 7 2 , \right.$ all others $| \mathbf { b } | < = . 1 3 7 )$ . If team loyalty is removed as a predictor of performance and instead used as an outcome of per formance, willingness to work has a significant impact on team perfor mance for Round 2 $( \mathbf { b } = . 2 5 9 )$ , and social presence has a significant impact on team performance for Round $3 \left( \mathrm { b } = . 2 5 5 \right)$ . If the paths to team loyalty from social presence and willingness to work are removed, team performance has a significant impact on team loyalty for both Rounds 2 $( \mathbf { b } = . 3 2 9 )$ and $3 \ ( \mathrm { b } = . 3 7 2 )$ . Overall, this tends to suggest a possible causal chain where participants’ perceptions of social presence and willingness to work impact their team’s performance, which in turn impacts their perceptions of loyalty to their team. This is an opportunity for future research.

## 7. Conclusions and implications

The results show that a relatively simple technology can be leveraged by distributed virtual teams into practices that increase activity awareness. Awareness practices might be developed by team members with the goal of improving coordination to improve team effectiveness (i.e., reduction of coordination losses); however, these results suggest that such activity awareness practices can increase feelings of social presence within the team and a willingness to work harder for the team, filling a gap in the virtual teams’ literature. One could speculate that the mechanism by which social motivation gains occur is because team members that are more aware of the activities of others are better able to compare their own efforts with the efforts of others. This motivates them to work harder because they feel other team members are doing the same and/or feel that a little additional effort would have a great benefit for their team. Altogether, these feelings have the effect of increasing loyalty to one’s team.

## 7.1. Implications for tool designers

The user interface tool was somewhat richer than the activity in dicators provided with most IM systems, which typically only provide information about whether the CMC application on the user’s computer is connected with the messaging service and whether the user has recently moved the mouse or pressed a key on the keyboard. Just as prio research has noted that observing that another’s icon changed in the company’s IM application could indicate that the other has begun his/ her workday [17], this somewhat richer tool was used to create awareness of the activities of others. Simply saying that a person is online and/or actively using some aspect of a collaborative workspace, however, does not necessarily directly increase willingness to work or provide a feeling of connectedness; rather, users likely need more in formation about the context in which the other resides (cf., $[ 2 6 , 4 2 , 5 8 ] )$ and these ultimately become part of awareness practices. Beyond simply knowing that the other is or was connected to the mediating application, knowing that the other is at a restaurant, in the office, or at home may have relevance when one determines how much effort the other is making. Similarly, in the experimental context, seeing another’s change of status (e.g., from $\mathrm { \ " { I d l e } ^ { \prime } }$ to “Reading Message”) may be enough to indicate that the other is engaged in team-related work and thereby heighten one’s willingness to work. However, none of this this would occur unless one combined the presence or status indication with other taken-for-granted information, such as knowing from experience that the other would only use a particular mediating application when per forming team-related tasks (cf., [42,59]). Heightened awareness in conjunction with a user interface object seems to be durable over long time periods – participants in the experiment with the tool reported higher levels of activity awareness than participants without it even after working with their third group. This fills a gap in the design literature, and suggests tools for increasing awareness might not be taken for granted over time and still be used as a means for obtaining awareness $( \mathsf { c f . , } [ 5 6 ] )$ , and reaffirms that subtle differences indeed count in the design of collaboration systems (cf., [60]).

## 7.2. Implications for researchers

In the CMC literature, social presence is an often-used construct that has been helpful in assessing the extent to which distributed virtual teams are able to work together using a variety of technologies. This research bridges several gaps in the overall body of CMC research and links it with group process theory by showing that activity awareness can lead to increased feelings of social presence, and that activity awareness can be enhanced through the implementation of a technol: ogy. Teams working in distributed contexts are unable to directly observe each other, and can use only what they receive via CMC to compare efforts [6]. Prior researchers have noted that awareness prac tices are required in order to maintain awareness of others’ “being there” via CMC, and that without these practices, team loyalty declines [35]. The results of this study show that this awareness is likely more specific, meaning users’ awareness of what other people are doing has implications for social presence, willingness to work harder, and one’s feelings about one’s team. Thus, the lack of bodily presence in a virtual team context has additional implications than “Is anyone there?” (e.g., [8,35,39,61]), and extends to “What is everyone doing?”.

Activity awareness depends on being able to identify the members of the team and being able to tie those identities to contributions, at least partially explaining an unanswered question in the anonymity litera ture: why virtual teams working in total anonymity have serious prob lems with not only making decisions [16], but also social motivation (cf., [62]). This adds further support to the notion that a few foundational aspects of awareness are necessary prerequisites to building later, more performance-oriented aspects of awareness such as social presence and loyalty [32].

In conclusion, virtual team researchers have embraced the notion of process losses and have given much attention to the need for awareness to reduce coordination losses [42,63], but have failed to examine how the decreased richness and increased anonymity of CMC contexts in creases the likelihood of social motivation losses. Based on the results of this study, it can be concluded that social motivation losses can be mitigated in distributed virtual teams when awareness practices are adopted that enable users to reliably track the activities of others. Additionally, awareness tools even as simple as the presence feature of IM applications can be used to increase activity awareness, social pres ence, and motivation; team members can observe when another’s IM status changes and use this information not only to determine when the other is available for work, but also as a means for determining how much the other is working and the extent to which one feels connected to one’s team. Given that negative consequences of awareness information were reported in previous research [9,41], future research should examine the factors that encourage users to feel that awareness infor mation presented by a mediating technology can be relied on, and the degree to which users combine such information with contextual in formation to create other aspects of awareness and/or increase trust and performance.
