# Understanding the emotional and informational influence on customer knowledge contribution through quantitative content analysis

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Emotional support Informational support Customer knowledge contribution Emotional asymmetry Curvilinear effect

## A B S T R A C T

Customers’ knowledge contributions are a vital source of business value. This study is one of the few attempts to study the influence of emotional and informational feedback on knowledge contribution. Through quantitative content analysis, we analyzed content data from 2324 users in a firm-hosted online community. We found that informational support (argument quality and source credibility) and emotional support (emotional approval and emotional asymmetry) significantly affect customers’ knowledge contributions. Moreover, we identified an inverted-U relationship between emotional asymmetry and knowledge contributions in initiated posts. This study has implications for both researchers and practitioners.

## 1. Introduction

In the digital economy, customers are becoming vital sources of business value [1,2]. Through firm-hosted online communities, they share with firms and other customers their suggestions and usage experience with products. Dell’s Ideastorm and Starbuck’s My Starbuck Idea are examples of communities that collect customers’ ideas and suggestions about products [3,4]. Customers’ knowledge contributions have been regarded as a main driver of business innovation and growth [5].

However, firms still face challenges from a lack of customers knowledge contributions in online communities [6]. The levels of users participation range from very low to intensely high [7]. This is because knowledge contribution is mostly voluntary [8]. Users are unwilling to spend time and effort unless they receive expected benefits from the community. Understanding the influence factors of users’ knowledge contributions is still a central concern for both practitioners and researchers.

A mechanism for social feedback is the foundation of customer knowledge contribution in firm-hosted online communities [9–12]. Users are embedded in social networks and exposed to various com ments and evaluations from peers [13]. Prior studies have suggested the important role of social feedback in facilitating user knowledge contri bution [14,15]. Ideal feedback from peers can bring users useful information, strong social ties, high status, and pleasurable experiences, all of which may increase their motivation to generate content [16].

Users can receive two types of social feedback from the community: informational feedback and emotional feedback [17,18]. Informational feedback refers to the exchange of product-related information [19]. Receiving informational feedback such as advice or tips can help users to learn more knowledge and generate more content [16]. The more high-quality feedback a recipient receives from other users, the more opportunities the recipient has to learn from others [20]. Users may actively share their knowledge out of reciprocity [21,22].

Emotional feedback relates to emotional expressions in the exchanged messages. Emotional interactions establish emotional bonds among users through their discussions about their moods, emotions, and affects [23]. In online communities, emotion is an irrational element that can affect users’ rational actions [24,25]. Emotional feedback such as mutual understanding and empathy may induce the emergence of collective emotions and enable unintended knowledge-sharing behav iors among users [26,27]. Some studies suggest that receiving positive feedback brings affective benefits (e.g., self-image enhancement and more pleasure) [28,29] that can promote knowledge contribution [30]. However, some scholars consider negative emotional expressions in multiple comments may increase users’ perceived informative value [31], which may also affect their knowledge contributions. There are two kinds of emotional responses from other users that may facilitate users’ contributive behaviors: emotional approval toward users and emotional resonance toward products. Emotional approval reflects recognition by peers that could facilitate users’ intention to contribute [32]. In addition, other users’ emotional resonance toward products could help focal users to evaluate a product better and evoke their more prosocial behaviors [33,34]. On the one hand, the opinions toward the product from other users supporting the focal user’s view may reduce this focal user’s stress in judging the quality of the product [66]. On the other hand, different opinions from peers may also be helpful in reducing a focal user’s ambiguity about products [23]. The benefits from these emotional factors could evoke more prosocial behaviors.

Given the important role of social feedback, firm managers are faced with the following questions: (1) How does informational and emotional feedback influence users’ knowledge contributions? (2) When will users pay more attention to informational feedback or emotional feedback? (3) What are the differences among emotional responses and how do they affect knowledge contribution differently?

Answering these questions requires an understanding of the current research gaps. First, prior studies have mainly identified individual and informational motivations [35–37] while neglecting the influence of emotional feedback [38]. Second, prior work on emotion has focused mainly on the absolute emotion valence of each message, ignoring relative emotional comparisons among users. We used the concept of symmetry and asymmetry [39] to represent two possible results of emotion comparisons: consistent or different. Emotional symmetry re fers to the degree to which other users hold consistent (same polarized) opinions with that of the focal user. Emotional asymmetry refers to the degree to which other users’ emotions toward products differ from that of the focal user (i.e., more negative or positive). Either emotional symmetry or asymmetry could give emotional support to users through discussions of feelings [40]. However, little research has empirically analyzed these asymmetrical effects on customer knowledge contribu tion. Third, previous studies often used the number of replies to measure knowledge contribution [35,41]. However, not all messages from peers are meaningful and helpful [42]. The quality of knowledge is worthy of study. Moreover, compared with replying to existing posts of other users, initiating a new topic to share product experience is more pro active. The extent to which informational and emotional feedback in fluences the two types of behaviors differently remains to be explored.

Social support theory is discussed here to address our research questions. It emphasizes that informational and emotional feedback can be supportive resources for users [43,44]. We identified the factors that might play important roles as informational and emotional support in motivating users to contribute knowledge. Then we examined the effects of these factors by examining and analyzing nine weeks of panel data from 2324 users of a firm-hosted online community. Finally, we deter mined the impact of different emotional responses, especially that of emotional asymmetry. We also showed how informational and emotional feedback influences posting and replying behaviors differ ently. This study will provide insights to managers on improving users knowledge contributions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. User knowledge contribution

Previous researchers have discussed three kinds of motivation for knowledge contribution: intrinsic, pure extrinsic, and internalized extrinsic [35]. Intrinsic motivation focuses on the inherent benefits from an activity [45,46]. Intrinsic motivations of knowledge contribution include joy, efficacy, and commitment toward a community [46–49]. Intrinsic motivations have been studied extensively in online commu nities, and hence, we exclude them and did not focus on them in this study.

Extrinsic motivation indicates goal-driven factors such as rewards and social benefits [50]. Extrinsic motivations include pure extrinsic motivations and internalized extrinsic motivations [51]. Pure extrinsic motivation refers to economic rewards such as pay and careers. Prior studies have shown that monetary rewards can be used to motivate users’ contributions [19,52]. We did not focus on pure extrinsic moti vations because our focused online community does not involve mone tary rewards.

In this study, we focused on internalized extrinsic motivations of knowledge contribution. Internalized extrinsic motivations are essen tially extrinsic motivations, but users can internalize their effects and transform the external regulations of a behavior into internal regulations [53,54]. When internalized, users treat the motivations as consistent with their own goals and consider them as self-regulating behavior rather than external impositions [35]. Therefore, internalized extrinsic motivations can be regarded as a combination of extrinsic and intrinsic motivations. The internalization process can take place when an indi vidual strives to earn external support and recognition from others [55]. Common internalized extrinsic motivations include social motivational factors such as reputation, social interactions, reciprocity, learning, self-image, and own-use value [35,51].

Among the available internalized extrinsic factors, informational, emotional and other social factors have been discussed in prior studies (as shown in Table 1). For informational factors, extant studies have shown that receiving comments from peers can fulfill a user’s knowledge needs and motivate him or her to contribute more knowledge because of the norm of reciprocity [56–59]. For emotional factors, scholars have focused mainly on recognition from others such as the effect of other users’ approvals and votes in favor of the content another user generates [9,35,59–62]. In addition, users may also contribute content because of other social motivational factors such as virtual incentives, status and reputation, positive feelings, and more social ties [19,41,52,63–66].

There are several research opportunities. First, little research has studied the influence of emotional factors on users’ participation, especially the influence of peers’ emotional resonance toward products [38]. The effects of consistent or different opinions toward products from other users on a focal user’s knowledge contributions also remain to be studied. Second, limited attention has been devoted to the differ ences between the effects of informational factors and emotional factors. Third, as noted earlier, prior researchers mainly used the quantity of user-generated content to measure knowledge [35,41,63]. However, higher levels of participation do not necessarily lead to better perfor mance [67]. The quality of contributed knowledge is more meaningful and should be considered instead [11,68].

## 2.2. Social support theory

The social support theory can explain the impact of informational feedback and emotional feedback from peers on users’ knowledge contribution behavior. Social support refers to “the exchange of verbal and nonverbal messages conveying emotion, information, or referral, to help to reduce one’s uncertainty or stress” (p. 154) [69]. It can be regarded as a social resource [70] that enables a person to feel cared for and responded to by other people [71]. Social support is a potential internalized extrinsic motivation of knowledge contribution because users may care about information, recognition and opinions from others and assimilate such peer influence [55]. They may be willing to discuss topics with peers to satisfy their own inner informational and emotional needs.

Social support theory is utilized here to understand the influence of social feedback on users for two reasons. First, social support theory classifies the social influence from peers' feedback into informational support and emotional support [72,73]. Informational support is helpful messages from peers such as recommendations, advice, or knowledge [74]. Emotional support is others’ expressions of concern, comfort, or encouragement [75]. Social support theory has been applied to explain the support from peers in communities of online health discussions, social commerce, and social networks [81–83]. As shown in Table 2, existing studies on social support have shown that informational and emotional support from peers can affect the quality of a user’s relationship with peers [74,84] and her or his perceived social influence [85,86], which can further increase her or his social commerce intention and continuance intention in the community [87,88]. Moreover, the two types of social support have been found to encourage a user’s willingness to share knowledge [89] or co-create value with peers [90] because they can bring users positive feelings such as self-efficacy [91], self-esteem [75] and belongingness [92].

Second, social support can be measured by resources that are pro vided to users in empirical studies or in survey studies by users’ sub jective perceptions of the supportive resources from others. In survey studies, scholars have measured informational support and emotional support by items contained in messages that access users’ perceptions of helpful informational or emotional concerns [74,82,93,94]. In content analysis, linguistic features, post-level features or punctuation features were counted to identify related information or emotions [95–99]. In this paper, we adopted the content analysis method. We analyzed the linguistic features (product-related terminologies and emotions), post-level features (source, type of post), and punctuation features of messages to identify possible informational support and emotional support.

## 2.3. Hypotheses development

## 2.3.1. Informational support

Informational support refers to assistance from others in the form of recommendations, advice, or knowledge [100]. Because users’ knowl edge is a public good, users may not be willing to contribute knowledge unless they receive informational benefits from others [41]. Informa tional support can increase the quality of relationships [82], self-efficacy [75], satisfaction with the community [68], and contributed knowledge [43]. Supportive information does not imply a high number of messages from others—only that the information they contain is relevant and helpful [101]. Compared with quantity, quality has more central cues for users to use to determine informational benefits [23]. The receipt of quality information triggers opportunities for users to learn new things; consequently, its receipt can be viewed as a proxy measure of infor mational support [99].

The quality aspects of information generally include argument quality and source credibility [102–104]. Argument quality is a content-related attribute that plays a central role in informational in fluence. Source credibility is a source-related attribute commonly regarded as a peripheral cue of assessing information quality. Higher argument quality and source credibility can increase users’ perceived informational helpfulness and their intentions to exchange information [105,106].

Argument quality refers to the persuasive strength of arguments in an informational message [80]. The more helpful, valuable, informative, or persuasive the information is, the higher quality users will attribute to them [107]. Quality arguments determine the degree of informational support and can satisfy users’ informational needs [105]. Thus, users will be more willing to contribute either by initiating a new topic or responding to topics by others for reciprocity.

Hypothesis 1a. The argument quality of other users’ messages is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of initiated posts.

Hypothesis 1b. The argument quality of other users’ messages is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of replies.

Source credibility measures the extent to which a message source is perceived to be authentic and credible [42]. It is related to the expertise and trustworthiness of the informant and reflects nothing about the content of the message [104,108]. When users perceive an informant is in a position to know the truth, they will consider the information useful and give it more weight than they attach to other informants’ messages [23]. An informant with high expertise establishes the knowledge structure of products [109]. This person can accurately identify product-related problems and answer questions. Therefore, source credibility will save users’ time in searching for information and reduce its ambiguity. In such a situation, users may be more willing to partic ipate in knowledge exchange.

Hypothesis 2a. The source credibility of received information is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of initiated posts.

Hypothesis 2b. The source credibility of received information is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of replies.

## 2.3.2. Emotional support

Emotional support refers to messages from others that contain emotional concerns such as caring, understanding, sympathy, and empathy [100]. In online communities, knowledge management is no longer entirely a rational process because users can construct knowledge at social levels [24]. Managing and guiding emotional interactions among users become important parts of a focal firm’s user knowledge management because users’ rational behaviors are limited by irrational elements such as emotion [27]. Users are inclined to pursue feelings and emotions in their interactions with others. Emotional interactions can induce the emergence of collective minds and emotions that enable collaboration and knowledge exchange among users [25]. Understand ing the influence of emotional feedback from other users can help a firm in guiding favorable collective emotions, developing collaborative en vironments, and motivating knowledge contribution behaviors.

Online firm-hosted communities are filled with relationships be tween two users or between a user and a product/brand. Therefore, emotion can refer either to users’ emotions toward other users’ behav iors or to opinions toward products/brands. In the two types of emo tions, emotional approval toward contribution behaviors can give emotional support to users by showing recognition or acceptance from peers [40]. In addition, emotional resonance toward products contains consistent or different product-related opinions from other users. Such emotional interactions allow discussions of feelings and product quality, which can reduce users’ uncertainty about products [23]. Therefore, we discussed the influence of the two forms of emotional support on knowledge contribution.

2.3.2.1. Emotional approval toward contribution behaviors. Approval utility may come when peers approve of a focal user’s contributions [110]. When a user receives positive recognition (e.g., praise, appreci ation) from others because of his or her sharing of knowledge, he or she may incur higher participative motivation. Emotional approval implies peer recognition, reputation, and self-efficacy out of high expertise, which should enhance a user’s desire to contribute [32]. In addition, expressions of approval may increase relationship quality [84] and satisfaction [110,111], both of which can instill positive emotions and facilitate contribution behaviors [112].

Hypothesis 3a. Emotional approval toward individual posting behavior gained from other users is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of initiated posts.

Hypothesis 3b. Emotional approval toward individual posting behavior gained from other users is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of replies.

2.3.2.2. Emotional resonance toward products. Online communities have an abundance of messages that convey how users evaluate a product (or brand). Some studies have shown that the valence of review ratings toward products can influence other customers’ perceptions of the helpfulness of reviews [34,113,114]. Other studies have also studied emotional influence on knowledge sharing [115,116]. They found that emotional cues in messages could facilitate users’ knowledge sharing.

However, most studies have focused on the absolute emotional valence of each message, neglecting the relative emotional differences among users. A focal user can express his or her opinions toward products by initiating a post. Then other users can review that post and either agree or disagree with the author. We anticipated that the emotional resonance from other users would influence further knowl edge contributive behaviors. Emotional resonance is defined as “the emotional harmony and/or disjuncture between collective action frames and the emotional lives of potential recruits” (p. 1) [117]. Based on this definition, two resonances are possible: those that are directionally the same and those that are the opposite. We used two measures—emotional symmetry and emotional asymmetry—to represent emotional resonance results.

The concepts of symmetry and asymmetry come from the literature of group behavior research. Jehn et al. (2010) used conflict symmetry and conflict asymmetry to represent the degree to which a member of a group perceives more (or less) conflict than other group members [39]. Similarly, we used the concept of emotional asymmetry to describe the degree to which one focal user’s emotion toward a product/brand differs from his or her reviewers’ emotions (i.e., whether other users’ emotions are more negative or positive than the focal users’). Thus, emotional symmetry refers to the degree to which reviewers hold consistent (the same polarized emotion) opinions with the focal user.

Emotional symmetry measures emotional consistency through comparison of the emotional direction between a focal user and his or her reviewers. Higher emotional symmetry implies a focal user receives a higher proportion of similar opinions toward products from reviewers. Similar opinions could further facilitate users’ altruistic behaviors [118] because they represent a kind of agreement and support from other users. This kind of emotional consistency could ease focal users’ stress [119,120]. Qiu et al. (2012) have suggested that customers are more likely to contribute product-related information when they perceive they are aligned with the opinions of others toward products [121].

Hypothesis 4a. Emotional symmetry toward products between a focal user and reviewers is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of initiated posts

Hypothesis 4b. Emotional symmetry toward products between a focal user and reviewers is positively associated with a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of replies.

When a user receives a review of her or his post, she or he can notice not only the emotional valence of the review, but also the gap or dis tance between her or his own emotional intensity in the post and the emotional intensity in the review. For example, suppose a user praised a product in a post. The emotion score in the post is 2. The post received reviews with emotional scores of [3, 1, -2] toward a product. It is obvious that the first reviewer had a more positive emotion toward the product than the user’s (3 > 2); therefore, the downward emotional distance is equal to 2− 3=-1. The other two reviewers were more negative than the user (1 < 2, the emotional distance was 2− 1 = 1; -2 < 2, the emotional distance was 2-(-2) = 4). Different posts may receive reviews with different emotional scores. Emotional asymmetry is a measure to compute the value of aggregate emotional difference by taking into account the user’s reviews of all posts.

Emotional asymmetry measures emotional difference through com parison of emotional distance between a focal user and reviewers. It can be computed comparatively in either an upward or downward direction. Upward emotional asymmetry shows to what degree reviewers hold more positive views than a focal user (a negative value denotes re viewers hold more negative views). Downward emotional asymmetry is computed as the degree to which that reviewers hold more negative views than a focal user (a negative value means reviewers hold more positive views). The two methods are equivalent in reflecting emotional asymmetry despite yielding diametrically opposite values. We chose to use downward emotional asymmetry.

Emotional valence has been the research focus because of its possible influence on customers’ altruistic behaviors [122,123]. Compared with their reactions to positive information, users tend to be more sensitive to negative information during brand evaluation and decision making [23]. They regard negative information as more useful because more of it implies less ambiguity in categorizing a product as low in quality [33, 124,125]. This is called negativity bias. Therefore, we regard more negative emotions (other than the focal user’s) (i.e., higher downward emotional asymmetry) as a kind of emotional support because they are helpful in reducing users’ ambiguity about products and to a certain degree promote their desires to make positive contributions.

However, as psychological research suggests, “people can get too much of a good thing, experiencing a downturn in good outcomes” [126]. Oishi et al. (2007) suggested that a level of positive emotions slightly lower than the maximum may yield the most successful partic ipative outcomes [127]. Lam et al. (2014) also championed an inverted-U relationship between a positive affect at work and proactive behaviors [128]. Similarly, we anticipated the possibility of a nonlinear relationship between emotional asymmetry and knowledge contribution behaviors. Although some scholars have argued that users give more weight to negative reviews than to positive ones, several studies have made contradictory findings. Sen and Lerman (2007) found customers consider a negative review less accurate than a positive one, at least for some products [129]. When users think a negative review comes from internal motivations but not product-related motivations, they may find the review less useful. An excessive number of positive or negative re views could not bring focal users maximum benefits [130]. Two kinds of phenomena may occur in the online community: First, a user who receives several negative reviews may be willing to delve more into the differing opinions to determine the real problems associated with a product. A user who receives an overwhelming number of negative re views (one reason for high emotional asymmetry) may conclude that a consensus exists about a product’s performance and no further discus sion is warranted. Second, a user who receives one review with an overly negative emotional score (another reason for high emotional asymme try) may consider the review as less useful because of the possibility that it is based on the reviewer’s internal disgust toward the product and not on an objective performance evaluation. Thus, a user may not partici pate in discussions in which the emotional asymmetry is considered too high. We think that emotional asymmetry has a supportive effect only when it is within in a certain range.

Hypothesis 5a. The relationship between emotional asymmetry (down ward) and a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of initiated posts exhibits a curvilinear, inverted-U relationship: Emotional asymmetry has the effect of encouraging a user to contribute more knowledge in the content of initiated posts when the user perceives her or his posts are less negative than the emotions other reviewers express toward products; however, when this emotional asymmetry increases beyond a specific point, its effect becomes negative.

Hypothesis 5b. The relationship between emotional asymmetry (down ward) and a user’s contributed product knowledge in the content of replies exhibits a curvilinear, inverted-U relationship: Emotional asymmetry has the effect of encouraging a user to contribute more knowledge in the content of replies when the user perceives her or his posts are less negative than the emotions other reviewers express toward products; however, when this emotional asymmetry increases beyond a specific point, its effect becomes negative.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Data collection

We collected panel data from Xiaomi’s online MI User Interface (MIUI) community (http://www.miui.com/). Xiaomi was established in 2010 and has been among the top five smartphone manufacturers in China. It repeatedly attributes its rapid growth to customers’ knowledge contributions to product development and improvement. MIUI is one of its software products. Customers in the MIUI community can report product bugs, suggest possible solutions, and discuss their experiences in using the company’s products.

We developed a program to collect the panel data of users. Our sample users were drawn from an elite user group labeled as “inner testing group” within this community; they are known as Mi Fans and considered passionate users of the firm’s software products [131]. Regular users must first apply to become a member of this user group. The firm then selects the users with higher reputation scores and his tories of better participative performance into the group of Mi Fans. These Mi Fans are generally more active and more technically zealous and astute than regular users. They are expected to voluntarily partici pate in testing of the weekly updates of the MIUI system, reporting software bugs, making suggestions about software functions, and answering questions from other users. In 2018, the number of Mi Fans reached 100,000 [132].

Because the user group of Mi Fans can facilitate rapid, iterative software development, the firm tries not only to retain its Mi Fans but also to stimulate them to be even more active. It periodically evaluates their participation, eliminates those with poor records of participation, and selects new members from its backlog of applicants from the com munity of regular users. The firm uses an automated system to evaluate a member’s performance in terms of attendance at discussions about weekly updates of the MIUI system and active interaction with other users. As a result, a large number of members have been removed from the group because their level of participation was inadequate. Therefore, it is necessary to understand the factors that may motivate the knowl edge contributions of this group of Mi Fans.

We have focused only on the social influence of Mi Fans for two reasons. First, Mi Fans are the main contributors of software-related knowledge in the community. They are elite users who contribute more intensely than regular users [133]. They are regarded as more central users within the community than regular users [134]. Up to a third of suggestions about software functions and functionality come from collaborations between developers and these Mi Fans [135]. Sec ond, Mi Fans are more sensitive to social influence than regular users. Recent research about the MIUI community has compared the partici pative motivations of regular users and of special users like Mi Fans who make up the “inner testing group” [134]. It suggests that social factors such as reputation and social relationships only affect special users because these individuals are more willing to interact with others because of their interest in the field. In contrast, regular users contribute less to the community and get less recognition from other users. They may seek answers from the community initially but become lurkers eventually [63]. They are less sensitive to social influence, especially emotional influence. Therefore, we focused exclusively on the contri bution behavior of these Mi Fans because of the importance of Mi Fans to the community and the need to study the effects of social influence on them.

We randomly chose 2515 Mi Fans and tracked their weekly activities from January 7, 2018, to March 13, 2018. Data were collected from nine time-period panels. After eliminating those users with incomplete data, 2324 users remained and were used in this research. These users complete activity history (including 129,167 posts and 1,442,041 re plies) and all reviews of their posts (3,577,020 reviews) were evaluated using content analysis as described in the next section.

## 3.2. Research method

Quantitative content analysis, a research technique for objective quantitative description of content [136], was used to analyze the data. The process includes segmenting content into several units, assigning each unit to a category, and providing numerical values to each category [137]. We used product feature extraction to analyze the product-related information in the content. We established a dictionary to count the product-related terminologies. We then used existing electronic product glossaries from a Chinese search engine platform named Sougou (https://pinyin.sogou.com/dict). Moreover, colloquial words, synonyms, and unique words in our studied community were extracted from official posts and customers’ posts that include product features, brand names, technical terms, product evaluations, and usage experience. Using an tf-idf algorithm in Python, we computed the weight of each word in those posts and sorted out the important technical nouns [138].

In addition, we conducted sentiment analysis to mine emotions in messages. Many research institutions provide well-classified word dic tionaries that are used to perform content analysis of Chinese. Our dictionaries of emotions (including positive/negative adjectives and adverbs) were adapted from the National Taiwan University Sentiment Dictionary (NTUSD) [139] and the Hownet lexicon from the CNKI platform (a Chinese knowledge management platform) [140]. We used punctuation to dissect each message into sub-sentences. We further computed the emotional score—both positive and negative—for each sub-sentence. This is because messages sometimes contain both positive and negative elements [23]. When computing an emotional score, we weighted some adjectives based on the existence of specified adverbs, exclamation points, and private words. By summarizing all the positive and negative scores of these sub-sentences, we obtained net emotional scores for each sentence (as shown in Fig. 2). For example, in this message, “Thank you for your sharing. The software interface is pretty, but it is too expensive.”, the sentence can be dissected into three sub-sentences based on punctuation. The first sub-sentence “Thank you for your sharing” implies the reviewer’s gratitude and approval toward this post. The level of emotional approval of this sub-sentence is 1. The second sub-sentence “The software interface i $; p r e t t y ^ { * }$ is an evaluation toward the software interface (the number of product-related terms of this sub-sentence is 1). The emotional score of this sub-sentence equals 1 because there is one positive word pretty. The third sub-sentence “but it is too expensive” describes the price of the software, which is another product-related attribute. The negative adjective expensive is weighted 1.5 times because of the adverb too $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , the emotion score equals -1.5). Finally, for this review, the total number of product-related terms is 2 (i. $\mathrm { e } _ { \cdot , }$ software interface, price). The total number of emotional approval is 1. The net emotional score of the whole message toward the software is $( 1 + ( - 1 . 5 ) = - 0 . 5 )$ . In this way, we analyzed all messages received by focal users.

## 3.3. Variable measures

## 3.3.1. Dependent variables

Users’ product knowledge includes both product-related experiences and expertise such as knowledge of brands, attributes, technical terms, evaluations, and usage situations [141,142]. Some studies have used product-related terms to measure users’ product knowledge. For example, Klostermann et al. (2018) extracted brand information from user-generated content to measure brand-related product knowledge [143]. Marchi et al. (2011) used the number of unique technical terms about motorcycles in online messages to measure users’ product knowledge [144]. Therefore, we computed a user’s product knowledge contribution as the number of product-related words in the technical discussion posts he or she initiated (PostKnowledge $i = 1 , 2 , . . . , 2 3 2 4 , t$ $= 1 , 2 , . . . 9 )$ and his or her responses to messages (ReplyKnowledge ). This is a reasonable way to identify whether a user is posting quality infor mation in an online brand community.

The product-related words were extracted from the official posts and users’ replies to those posts. The official posts by the firm include the words of product attributes, brand names, and technical terms (e.g., configuration or service of hardware and software). Users’ responses to those posts show their perceptions, evaluations, and experience with the product features introduced. Extracting product-related terms in this way can incorporate professional product terms used by developers and common words (e.g., idioms, abbreviations, spoken words, and phrases) used by users. Using this feature extraction analysis, 7372 productrelated words were selected in the dictionary. Based on this dictio nary, we used a text-retrieval technique in Python language to compute the number of non-repeated product-related terms that appeared in users’ postings and messaged replies. We used the logarithmic forms of product knowledge as dependent variables (lnPostKnowledge<sub>it</sub>, lnReplyKnowledge ).

In the measurement of source credibility, product developers are experts that customers consider credible and trustworthy. In the MIUI community, internal developers are employees assigned to answer cus tomers’ questions. ## 3.3.3. Control variables

Prior studies have suggested the influence of incentives $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ ., repu tation, rewards, self-image) on knowledge contributions [9,145]. It considers official recognition $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ , whether community managers clas sify their posts as elite posts) and peer recognition (i.e., number of responses and the scores of rewards from other users). This reputational score is a kind of extrinsic reward that also reflects a user’s image in the community. We used the logarithmic form of reputational value (lnRe $p u t a t i o n _ { i , t - 1 } )$ as a control variable.

Furthermore, the MIUI community provides each member’s cumu lative online time $( T e n u r e _ { i , t - 1 } )$ . It reflects the duration of users’ partici pation. In community research, empirical results have shown positive [11], negative [63] or insignificant [68] relationships between online time and knowledge contribution. Therefore, we used online time as one control variable.

In addition, a focal user’s own emotion toward products could also influence users’ behaviors. Users’ emotions toward products are a result of their evaluation of product characteristics and their usage experience. Such attitudes may further influence users’ intention to contribute knowledge. When users have negative attitudes toward products, they may be unwilling to participate because of dissatisfaction [146] or they may participate more to express their complaints [147]. To control for such potential effects, we took a focal user’s emotional valence $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ positive or negative) toward products $( S e l f E m o _ { i , t - 1 } )$ as a control variable. When emotional asymmetry values exceeded a standardized score of 0.654, such a relationship became negative.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

Our research provided evidence that informational and emotional support have different effects on the posting and replying behaviors involved in knowledge contribution. The theoretical contributions are as follows.

First, we identified the effects of argument quality and source cred ibility as informational support on knowledge contribution. In the knowledge contribution literature, prior research mainly measured perceived informational benefits by using survey and numerical objec tive data shown directly on websites [35,149]. They lacked under standing of informational content. Some studies of social support used content analysis to measure informational support [95,96]. However, they only considered argument quality and neglected that a credible source of information could also be a kind of informational support through the provision of authentic information to users.

We found argument quality and source credibility as informational support both positively affect users’ knowledge contribution. This is because quality information from others can fulfill users’ informational needs. Moreover, developers can accurately address the problems of customers and improve customers’ satisfaction and value [150]. The two forms of informational support help to decrease users’ perceived product failures. Users tend to reward other users for their assistance by contributing knowledge out of reciprocity [151]. In addition, the coef ficient of argument quality was found to be larger than that of source credibility. This is consistent with the view that argument quality is a central route to informational influence, and source credibility is only a peripheral route [104].

Second, we found different effects of emotional approval, emotional symmetry, and emotional asymmetry as emotional support on knowl edge contribution. For emotional support, prior studies did not consider the potential supportive effects of emotional comparisons with peers. We filled this gap by examining the emotional symmetry or asymmetry

Emotional approval was proven to have significant influence on both posting and replying knowledge contributions. This is because positive feedback from other users reflects their positive judgments (e.g., admi ration and respect) toward a focal user’s behaviors [152]. Users receiving more approval perceive objects, other persons, and ideas more favorably [153]. In addition, emotional approval can give users a perception of identity and belonging through high social acceptance [154]. The benefits from approval can motivate users to contribute more knowledge.

We found emotional symmetry had no influence on either type of knowledge contribution. This suggests that more consistent opinions from reviewers could not motivate focal users to contribute more knowledge. This conflicts with the opinions of Yu and Chu (2007) that affection similarity can produce voluntary contributions [155]. Chen et al. (2019) gave a possible explanation for our observations and re sults. Their findings show that anonymous users tend to express more inconsistent opinions in virtual communities [156]. Luarn and Hsieh (2014) concurred, reporting similar findings [157]. Likewise, in the MIUI community, users do not know whether a user uses a real or assumed name. In such a case, more inconsistent opinions did not imply lower support from others. In a sense, these could be outcomes because of users’ anonymity. In addition, consistent emotions toward products may imply that users behave in an expected manner. Such consensus delivers one-sided information about products, which is difficult for users to use to draw inferences about product quality [158]. Hence, users may not pay as much attention to consistent emotions from reviewers.

Interestingly, we showed that there was an inverted-U-shaped rela tionship between downward emotional asymmetry and posting knowl edge contribution. This implies that when the value of emotional asymmetry is below a specific point, more negative emotions from peers will increase initiated knowledge. When the value increases beyond a certain point, more negative emotions from peers will decrease initiated knowledge. Prior studies only focused on the absolute emotional valence of reviews and neglected the relative emotional differences among users. More negative information implies less product ambiguity, which pro vides more reference values for users [125]. Emotional asymmetry re lates to more knowledge contribution, but only up to a point. This result redirects the prior theories by introducing emotional comparisons. It also challenges the view that positive or negative emotions always produce directionally invariant effects [129,158].

The effect of emotional asymmetry on replying knowledge contri bution was shown to be insignificant. The result could be explained by review orientation. The emotional differences were computed by comparing the emotions between a post and its reviews. It is oriented toward the emotions revealed in posts initiated by the users. Therefore, such a difference can generate a direct influence on posting knowledge contributions but not on replying knowledge contributions.

Third, we found different effects of informational support and emotional support on different types of contribution behaviors. In firmhosted online communities, managers may wonder if users put more emphasis on informational value or on the emotional value of in teractions. By comparing the standardized coefficients of informational factors with those of emotional factors, we found users are more moti vated by emotional support in posting behaviors and by informational support in replying behaviors. For posting behaviors, we found that the additive value of coefficients of emotional approval and emotional asymmetry exceeded that of argument quality and source credibility. For replying behaviors, argument quality and emotional approval were the most influential factors.

These results are reasonable because initiating new posts and replying to existing posts are different behaviors [63,159]. Replying to existing posts is a reactive knowledge contribution behavior in response to other users’ discussions. Reactive behaviors focus on fulfilling basic needs and helping others in response to requests, rewards, and outcomes [160,161]. Acquisition of information is one of the principal motivations for online community participation. Rafaeli, Ariel and Hayat (2005) suggested that motivation to learn new things ranked higher than other motives in knowledge-based communities [162]. Replying to others messages occurs largely to reciprocate for others’ informational help or to request more information in the future. Therefore, it makes sense that quality information is more influential than emotional factors in reactive replying behaviors.

In contrast, initiating posts is a proactive knowledge sharing behavior by creating new topics for discussion. Proactive behaviors focus on seeking feedback, influencing others, and developing social relationships [160]. For such behaviors, understanding one’s own emotions and others’ emotions is an important motivator [163]. Our results revealed that in posting behavior, users pay more attention to others’ emotional responses than to informational support. This may be due to the fact that emotional support can give users vitality and a sense of meaningfulness compared with receiving informational support [164]. Emotional asymmetry can reduce feelings of product ambiguity and free users’ energies to invest in proactive contributive behaviors. Emotional approval tells users they are doing something meaningful. The motivation to initiate proactive behaviors derives more from doing meaningful things than from reducing ambiguity and uncertainty [164]. This could be the reason for our result that the effect of emotional approval is more significant than that of emotional asymmetry.

## 5.2. Practical implications

Our study contains practical implications for firm-hosted community managers. This study reveals that when it comes to the technology artifact design, managers should focus not only on reputation-based or membership-based mechanisms, but also on informational and emotional interactions among users. On the one hand, we found that argument quality and source credibility can increase users’ knowledge contributions. The results show the importance of information quality in social interactions. Community managers should establish channels for users to seek high-quality informational resources. For example, they can use a recommendation system to push high-quality content to user [165]. They can also provide a helpfulness button to achieve content evaluation from users [166]. The evaluation scores of posts can help users to easily identify high-quality content. In addition, employees are credible sources of information to customers. Employee–customer interaction can also play an important role in facilitating users’ contri butions. Community managers can build customer contact and problem solving into their employees’ performance evaluations to encourage more employee–customer interactions.

On the other hand, we found that emotional approval and emotional asymmetry significantly affect knowledge contribution. Our finding that emotional approval positively affect knowledge contribution implies that users pay much attention to peers’ evaluations. Community man agers can add emotional expression functions (e.g., the “like” button) to online communities to visually display positive evaluations from peers [167]. Additionally, the inverted-U-shaped relationship between downward emotional asymmetry and posting knowledge contribution implies that when reviewers hold overly negative emotions toward products/brands, users may not be willing to participate anymore. This calls for greater attention on sentiment monitoring. Community man agers can provide a mechanism to control the degree of inconsistent emotions among users. For example, a recommendation algorithm can be designed to avoid presenting posts that are overly emotionally distant to a user.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single firm–hosted community. The community includes topics about bug re ports, new function suggestion, resource sharing, and casual chitchat. We did not differentiate topics in this study because we focused on the knowledge contributive behavior of users on all topics. In future research we can study the factors that influence users’ contributions on a certain topic. Moreover, analyses with multiple firm-hosted online communities are needed to generalize our findings.

Second, we used only objective behavioral data and consequently cannot capture the effects of potential intrinsic motivations on users knowledge contributions. Some scholars have suggested that extrinsic motivations (e.g., virtual incentives) may decrease the effects of intrinsic motivations. Users, especially elite users, are more likely to pursue vir tual points and attribute their knowledge contributions to obtaining virtual rewards but not intrinsic benefits [36]. However, we cannot explore interactive effects between extrinsic motivations and intrinsic motivations in this study because of limitations inherent in empirical data. In the future, lab experiments can be conducted to identify the influence of potential intrinsic motivations and the interactive effects between different types of motivations. Moreover, it would be better to combine subjective data and objective data to mine the inner thoughts of users in future research. In this way, the omitted variable bias can be further reduced.

Third, we only focused on the knowledge contribution behavior of Mi Fans because they are the main contributors in the community [131]. However, regular users may behave differently in the community. They mainly aim at seeking software-related solutions in the community and may be less emotionally affected by other users [134]. More research can be conducted to understand the contributive motivations of regular users and how social influence affects Mi Fans and regular users differently.

## 6. Conclusion

Customers have increasingly become co-creators of value with firms by contributing knowledge to their products or services. Prior literature has not paid adequate attention to the emotional antecedents of knowledge contribution. We used quantitative content analysis, including sentiment analysis and product feature extraction analysis, to explore the influence of social interactions on users’ knowledge contri butions. Based on social support theory, we found significant effects from informational and emotional support on knowledge contributions. We further examined the different effects of emotional approval and emotional asymmetry on knowledge contributive behaviors.

Specifically, we identified an inverted-U relationship between emotional asymmetry and knowledge contributions in initiated posts. This research contributes to the literature on knowledge contribution by identifying the different effects of informational interactions and emotional interactions as well as the curvilinear effect of emotional asymmetry. This study can also provide insights for managers on how to motivate users to contribute more knowledge in similar firm-hosted online communities.
