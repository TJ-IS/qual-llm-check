# The strategic role of CIOs in IT controls: IT control weaknesses and CIO turnover

Wanyun Li <sup>a,</sup>\*, Soon-Yeow Phang <sup>b</sup>, Ka Wai (Stanley) Choi <sup>a</sup>, Shuk Ying Ho

<sup>a</sup> Research School of Accounting, The Australian National University, Australia

<sup>b</sup> Department of Accounting, Monash University, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Sarbanes-Oxley Internal controls Information technology control weaknesses (ITCWs) Remediation CIO turnover

## A B S T R A C T

Extending the literature on information technology control weaknesses (ITCWs), we investigate the strategic role of chief information officers (CIOs) in maintaining adequate internal controls and remediating ITCWs. Drawing on institutional theory and sunk cost effect, we develop three hypotheses. We adopt an archival approach and obtain CIO turnover and ITCW data for 890 distinct firms, finding that: (i) ITCW disclosures may not increase the likelihood of CIO turnover, (ii) CIOs’ backgrounds are associated with the likelihood of turnover, and (iii) CIO turnover increases the likelihood of subsequent ITCW remediation. Theoretically. we extend the literature by investigating the strategic role of CIOs in remediating ITCW. Practically, we find that common practices by firms to retain CIOs in ITCW situations are ineffective for ITCW remediation. Firms are encouraged to reconsider these practices.

## 1. Introduction

The early 20o0s witnessed maior corporate scandals related to internal controls, including those involving Enron, Tyco International, and WorldCom. In response to these scandals, society raised questions about the integrity of financial reporting [1,2]. Accordingly, the provision of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX) Section 404 mandates public firms in the United States (US) to establish internal controls, including infor mation technology (IT)–related controls [3–5], and to report on the adequacy of such controls [3–5]. Despite this effort, material IT de ficiencies and flaws remain a problem. Recent scandals involved Equi fax<sup>1</sup> in 2017 and British Airways<sup>2</sup> in 2019. Firms with material IT deficiencies and flaws lose legitimacy [6,7], as stakeholders become pessimistic about their data processing integrity, system access and se curity, and system structure and usage [2,8,9]. To respond to SOX, in formation system (IS) researchers (e.g. [5,9–11],) have focused on enhancing the design and implementation of IT to ensure adequate IT controls in digital businesses. For example, the compliance and moni toring of IT controls and remediation activities are starting to become automated through robotic process automation and machine-learning solutions. Despite this effort, IT deficiencies and flaws remain common in the era of digital business because of the massive volume of data, complex digital technology, and hyper-connectivity across firms.

Disclosures of material IT deficiencies and flaws impair a firm’s legitimacy because they hinder the quality and usefulness of the information produced in financial reporting and thus provoke stakeholders to question the appropriateness of the firm’s corporate governance [2,4, 12]. Therefore, firms must act to restore their legitimacy affected by internal control–related problems. For a management (not IT) scandal, the literature on corporate governance suggests that dismissing execu tives is an effective way to restore firm legitimacy [6,13], given that more than 70 % of management scandals involve chief executive offi cer/chief financial officer (CEO/CFO) intentional misconduct to achieve their personal interests [14]. If the CEO/CFO remains, this may break the trust between the firm and shareholders, as shareholders are con cerned about CEO/CFO integrity. In contrast, when the CEO/CFO is dismissed, this reflects the firm’s strong intention to implement changes to their systems and minimize the likelihood of recurrence [7,15]. The dismissal of the CEO/CFO can appease shareholders. In addition, their dismissal can resolve the misalignment of the interests between CEOs/CFOs and shareholders [13,16]. There have been business ex amples of such actions. In June 2019, Sealed Air dismissed its CFO, Bill

Stiehl, amidst a Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) inquiry into its accounting irregularities.<sup>3</sup> More recently, in May 2020, Luckin Coffee fired its CEO, Jenny Qian, in relation to an internal investigation of sales frauds.<sup>4</sup>

In contrast, a firm may not easily fire a chief information officer (CIO) in an IT scandal. Similar to management scandals, the dismissal of CIOs helps restore shareholders’ trust in a firm because some share holders consider CIOs accountable for IT scandals [17]. However, IT scandals are more likely to be caused by technical issues in IT design or processes, rather than CIOs’ pursuit of personal interests. A recent report shows that the top causes of more than 3813 IT incidents worldwide include imperfect software development, poor technology base or infrastructure, lack of technical competence, and faulty hardware [18]. Apparently, none of these incidents were caused by intentional mistakes by CIOs. Thus, the problems underlining IT scandals can hardly be fixed by simply dismissing CIOs. Instead, because CIOs hold intrinsic knowl edge of firm systems [19,20], dismissing CIOs may cause disruptions to firms’ IT operations. As shown by Say and Vasudeva [17], CIO turnover response to data breaches is ineffective in reducing the reoccurrence of data breaches. Therefore, the nature of CIO dismissal in an IT scandal and the nature of CEO/CFO dismissal in a management scandal have subtle differences.

While the literature has discussed the strategic role of CIOs in the IT control environment (e.g., [5,21–23]) and considers the remedial ac tions after IT deficiencies and flaws (e.g. [24–26]), scant attention is devoted to CIO career outcome as a consequence of IT scandal. As such, little is known about the opportunities raised by CIO dismissal for firms facing IT control weaknesses (ITCWs). This motivates us to examine the likelihood of CIO turnovers in firms’ situations of IT deficiencies and flaws, and the effect of CIO turnovers on subsequent remediation. To this end, we first follow SOX and refer to material IT deficiencies and flaws as material ITCWs in financial reporting. Aligned with “Auditing Standard No. 5—An Audit of Internal Control Over Financial Reporting” issued by the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board [PCAOB] [27], we define ITCWs as significant deficiencies or a combination of deficiencies in IT controls that create a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement in a firm's financial reports (either due to error or fraud) will not be detected or prevented on a timely basis. Examples of ITCWs include unrestricted access to programs and data by IT and accounting personnel, incompatible applications and platforms, inadequate IT support staff, and a lack of IT controls over third-party vendors [28]. We then formulate our two research questions as follows:

RQ1: How do ITCW disclosures affect CIO turnover?

RQ2: How does CIO turnover affect subsequent ITCW remediation?

We draw on institutional theory in this research. Institutional theory focuses on the role of the social structures—including schemes, rules, norms, and routines—in which firms operate and gain their legitimacy [29,30]. Legitimacy is a state in which a firm’s behavior aligns with its institutional environment [13,31,32]. It is associated with stakeholders acceptance that a firm’s behavior is appropriate, given existing norms and laws. When a firm is perceived as legitimate, stakeholders support the firm, and this support enables the firm to access external resources for further development [33,34]. Therefore, when a firm’s legitimacy is compromised, it is incentivized to restore its legitimacy [13,35]. In restoring a firm’s legitimacy, ITCW remediation is important because it signals to stakeholders an improvement in the quality and usefulness of financial reports [6,12]. Accordingly, we draw on sunk cost effect to investigate whether retaining the CIO or introducing a new CIO can increase the likelihood of a subsequent ITCW remediation. Specifically, we argue that the continuing CIO is prone to deny any negative feedback, and subsequently cause delays in remediating ITCWs. In contrast, we consider the new CIO to hold an open mind and to pinpoint and correct system flaws in enforcing ITCW remediation.

We develop three hypotheses. First, we formulate H1 and develop an empirical model to examine the association between ITCW disclosures and CIO turnover. We find that firms with ITCW disclosures are more likely to retain their CIOs than are firms without ITCW disclosures. Second, to gain a better understanding of why firms retain their CIOs despite ITCWs, we incorporate the effect of CIOs’ expertise on their turnover after ITCW disclosures. We show that CIOs with IT expertise are less likely to be dismissed than CIOs with business expertise. Third, we develop H3 to investigate how CIO turnover affects ITCW remedia tion. The results show that firms that experience CIO turnover are more likely to remediate their ITCWs than those that do not replace CIOs. Overall, our findings suggest that firms commonly retain (rather than dismiss) their CIOs following ITCW disclosures, and particularly retain CIOs with IT expertise, which is actually not an effective strategy for ITCW remediation.

This article leads to theoretical and practical contributions. Theo retically, previous research on chief executives’ turnover as a response to the illegitimate action of internal control–related problems focuses on CEOs and CFOs (e.g., [4,6,36]), and their foci may not be IT-related controls. Scant attention has been devoted to IT problems and CIOs, whose primary responsibility is to lead a firm’s IT development. Adding to this, we examine how ITCW disclosures affect CIO turnover. While previous studies focus on the strategic role of a CIO in IT resource management and IT risk management (e.g. [19,37,38],), this article extends this examination to the subsequent ITCW remediation that contributes to the maintenance of adequate IT controls. Moreover, this article contributes to previous research on types of CIOs and their expertise (e.g. [20,37,39],) by showing that the chance of a CIO being dismissed is affected by his or her expert areas. This finding contributes to the corporate governance literature by indicating that a firm should consider the expertise of executives before it dismisses an executive to restore firm reputation in the situation of a scandal. Practically, this article advises CIOs to be mindful of the reliabilities of the IT-related financial reporting process, which affects their job security. This article also provides relevant information to firms and regulators on how to enhance ITCW remediation and the reliabilities of the IT-related financial reporting process.

This article is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the theoretical background and develops the hypotheses, while Section 3 describes the sample selection and research design. Section 4 presents the main results and additional analyses, and Section 5 discusses the contributions, limitations, and directions for future research. The final section provides the conclusions.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses development

## 2.1. ITCW and executive turnovers

Corporate governance provides a framework to ensure that an institution manages its stakeholder relationships in a fair and trans parent manner by ensuring board and executive accountability. The CEO, as the most senior executive, is directly accountable to the stake holders for all activities of the firm. Specifically, the CEO is responsible for establishing the leadership and vision of an internal control environment. In doing so, the CEO brings together other chief executives, including the CFO, CIO, and chief digital officer (CDO), to contribute to the implementation and assessment of adequate internal controls [40]. The CFO holds the primary responsibility for supervising the financial unit, including financial planning, managing financial risks, and finan cial reporting. The CFO is directly accountable to stakeholders for the implementation and assessment of internal controls—especially those controls specific to an account balance, class of transactions, or appli cations (but not related to IT) [40]. A 2018 report by

PricewaterhouseCoopers [41] finds that 44 % of CEO and CFO ousters are caused by lapses in ethical conduct, and 35 % of ousters are a result of poor financial performance.

In addition to the CEO and CFO, the CIO and CDO play vital roles in representing a firm’s legitimacy to stakeholders. They have the primary responsibility of IT governance to ensure alignment between the firm’s IT and business strategy, including implementation of adequate IT controls and planning for IT recovery from possible disasters [42,43]. Specifically, the CIO focuses on the management of IT systems and functions, creation and enforcement of strategies and policies, and establishment of an executive-level interface between the IT department and non-IT departments [42]. Meanwhile, the CDO focuses on the use of digital technology, such as mobile applications, social media, and virtual goods, to digitalize the business [43]. However, currently, many firms CIOs perform the job of the CDO, and there are not many firms with two separate titles (CIO and CDO); thus, this article does not separately consider CIOs and CDOs. For simplicity, we refer to both the CIO and CDO as the CIO.

In the case of ITCWs, firms can restore legitimacy by dismissing an executive who is accountable for ITCWs and by recruiting a new one to address ITCWs. Thus far, the literature has examined CEO and CFO turnovers when firms have ITCW disclosures. To elaborate, an ITCW damages the quality and usefulness of the information produced in financial reporting—such as greater earnings estimates [44], more ma terial financial misstatements [45], and lower management forecast accuracy [2]. Therefore, as required by SOX, ITCWs should be disclosed in financial reporting to awaken stakeholders’ awareness of the poten tial for material misstatements and earnings management [3–5]. Accordingly, ITCW disclosures lead stakeholders to doubt the firm’s IT governance. Financial reporting is an important topic in accounting and this explains why prior research related to ITCWs in financial reporting has merely focused on CEO and CFO turnovers $[ 6 , 7 ]$ . Although a key responsibility of CIOs is to lead the design and implementation of IT controls [42,43], little is known about how ITCW disclosures affect CIO turnovers. In sum, prior studies on ITCWs might have narrowly focused on CEOs and CFOs and overlooked CIOs.

Beyond the context of CIO career outcomes, the literature discusses the importance of CIOs in IT governance and control environments $[ 5 ,$ 21,22]. For example, Li, Lim, and Wang [23] compare a pair of 110 firm-year observations (i.e., 110 observations with effective IT controls and 110 observations with IT flaws) between January and December 2005, and find that firms with CIO positions or longer-tenured CIOs are more likely to have effective IT controls than are their peer firms. Hai slip, Masli, Richardson, and Sanchez [6] compare a pair of 411 firm-year observations $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ 411 observations with effective IT controls and 411 observations with IT flaws) between 2004 and 2008, and find that firms that add or promote a CIO to the board level are more likely to enhance the effectiveness of IT controls. Taken together, the literature has empirically shown the importance of CIOs for IT governance and control environments, yet is silent on CIO turnover in the case of ITCWs. In the following, we hypothesize the relationship between ITCW disclosures (or remediation) and CIO turnover.

## 2.2. ITCW disclosures and CIO turnover

Prior IS research uses institutional theory to investigate firms’ stra tegies to defend legitimacy in response to accusations of illegitimate behavior [4,6,43]. Legitimacy is important to firms because stake holders expect legitimate firms to act appropriately within prevailing norms and laws, thereby becoming more trustworthy [33,34]. As such, legitimate firms can leverage this trust to improve their access to scarce external resources [33,34]. In contrast, the loss of legitimacy, or even a threat of the loss of legitimacy, can disrupt the flow of such resources into firms because stakeholders tend to disassociate themselves from firms with impaired legitimacy and consequently withdraw their re sources from these firms [13,31].

ITCW disclosures weaken firms’ legitimacy because ITCWs affect the capturing and processing of data in financial reporting. As a result, ITCW disclosures are associated with low quality and low usefulness of the information produced in financial reporting, as reflected in greater earnings estimates [44], more material financial misstatements [45,], and lower management forecast accuracy [2]. Consequently, stake holders penalize firms that disclose ITCWs by restricting their resources in such firms, such as by requiring higher audit fees [46,47] and higher cost of equity [48,49]. Therefore, firms always take decisive actions to defend or repair their legitimacy after ITCW disclosures.

According to institutional theory, one common way that firms take such actions is through changes in their leadership structure—particu larly through chief executive turnover [2,19,30]. Chief executives are the symbol of a firm’s legitimacy—that is, stakeholders credit (blame) chief executives for a firm’s legitimacy (or lack thereof) [30]. As such, when a firm experiences a loss of legitimacy, the dismissal of current chief executives can itself represent the firm’s intention to fix the current problem, thereby helping restore legitimacy [2,19,30]. In cases of ethical scandals or financial frauds, the CEO and CFO, as the most accountable executives for these illegitimate conducts, are usually removed. For example, when a firm faces the loss of legitimacy because of financial restatements, the firm tends to dismiss the current CEO and CFO [13]. Similarly, after disclosures of earnings manipulation, firms are more inclined to replace the incumbent CEO [50].

In this article, we extend the logic of using executive turnovers to restore firms’ legitimacy to ITCW disclosures. ITCWs are related to IT. CIOs may bear the culpability for ITCWs and become a candidate for dismissal because their core responsibilities are related to IT governance [5,21,22].<sup>5</sup> Regarding IT governance, CIOs have two key responsibilities: (i) to ensure that executives allocate sufficient resources to IT governance and (ii) to manage people and systems in IT functional units for quality IT controls [22,42,51]. As such, the CIO is accountable for ITCWs.

Among all executives, the CIO takes care of how IT controls are prioritized in the firm’s agenda, breaks down complicated technical solutions into actionable steps that other chief executives and board members can understand and discuss, and communicates strategic IT concerns faced by the firm [22,42,52]. Nowadays, because digital technology drives the business strategy in almost all industries, CIOs play a significant role in conveying to chief executives and board members the potentials and risks associated with digital technology with regard to growing the business [22]. On some occasions, the CEO and CFO provide recommendations to the CIO on designing adequate IT controls that match the business strategy [22,53,54]. Following such recommendations, within the IT functional units, IT controls are then modularized into specific activities performed by people or systems, such as physical security, logical access, software development, incident management, and technical support [9]. In addition, the CIO leads and structures these units, establishes IT priorities in the implementation of IT controls, and oversees the IT functional units to deliver such controls as necessary [22,42]. Therefore, the CIO is accountable for any ITCW disclosures. Drawing on institutional theory, we argue that, when firms lose legitimacy because of ITCW disclosures, they are more likely to dismiss the incumbent CIO. Taken together, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Firms with ITCW disclosures are more likely to dismiss their CIOs than are firms without ITCW disclosures

## 2.3. Moderating role of CIO expertise

As mentioned in Section 1, institutional theory considers the deeper and more resilient aspects of firm structure (such as rules and norms). Firm structure acts as authoritative guidelines to help firms gain legiti macy from stakeholders [29,30]. Gaining legitimacy is particularly important for firms involved in scandals. Institutional theory suggests that executive turnover is a way to enable a firm to gain (or regain) legitimacy when the firm is involved in a scandal [6,13]. Specifically, the theory implies that, when a firm has ITCW problems, the firm can choose to dismiss its CIO to restore its legitimacy among stakeholders. However, in practice, dismissing the CIO is likely to be costly and disruptive to the firm, as CIO dismissal may slow down ITCW remedi ation. We anticipate that a firm will dismiss its CIO to restore legitimacy only when CIO dismissal is unlikely to cause any harm to the remedia tion process, and, therefore, the types of expertise that a CIO possesses are likely to play a role in determining CIO turnover following any IT scandal.

The literature classifies CIOs into two types: those with strong business expertise and those with strong IT expertise [37,39]. CIOs with strong business expertise are strong in applying their knowledge to maximize the economic return of their IT investment and to align IT development with firms’ business models [37,39]. These CIOs are good at dealing with negotiations and resolving conflicts with IT teams, and managing project risks [20,37]. In contrast, CIOs with strong IT exper tise emphasize synergies between IT and business objectives, the quality of IT development, and effectiveness in implementing IT controls [37, 39].

The types of expertise that a CIO possesses are likely to determine CIO turnover following IT scandals. To elaborate, CIOs’ business expertise is more relevant than CIOs’ IT expertise when the IT scandal is related to IT investment. For example, Commonwealth Bank of Australia was hit by allegations of aiding money laundering by deploying a fleet of intelligent deposit machines in 2018. The bank was accused of not assessing the risk of the machines being used for money laundering or counter-terrorism financing. Although acknowledging the significant progress in IT and operation capacity led by its incumbent CIO, David Whiteing, the bank was set to seek a new CIO who could strengthen risk controls over IT investment [55]. Conversely, when the IT scandal is related to technical issues, such as data breach. CiOs’ IT expertise is essential to deal with technical problems, rather than CIOs’ business knowledge. For example, after Equifax’s data breach, the firm retained Jamil Farshchi, who oversaw its information security, and even elevated him to board level [56]. ITCW is a technical problem; thus, we believe that CIOs with strong IT expertise can work closely with their IT teams, with the aim of promptly remediating ITCWs. The dismissal of CIOs with IT expertise would otherwise impede ITCW remediation [57,58].

Taken together, in the face of ITCWs, firms are more likely to dismiss CIOs with business expertise than CIOs with IT expertise:

H2. In the face of ITCWs, CIOs with business expertise are more likely to be dismissed than are CIOs with IT expertise.

## 2.4. CIO turnover and ITCW remediation

In the examination of ITCW, it is important to study which factors lead to ITCW remediation. ITCW remediation is a situation in which a firm discloses an ITCW in financial reporting as required by SOX and then, later, the same firm discloses that IT controls are deemed adequate, thereby suggesting that the previous ITCWs no longer exist [59]. ITCW remediation is important because ITCWs present significant threats to the quality and usefulness of financial reporting, and ITCW remediation should mitigate those threats. In addition, ITCW remedia tion restores stakeholders’ trust in financial reporting, which subsequently improves the firm’s legitimacy [60]. As evidenced by prior studies, firms that initiate remediation plans to restore legitimacy receive a lower penalty from stakeholders, such as smaller decreases in their stock price [61], fewer audit fees [62], and lower interest rates [63], than do firms that do not take action to restore legitimacy. Therefore, ITCW remediation is practically important to firms.

Given that this article focuses on the strategic role of CIOs, we examine whether CIO turnover can help ITCW remediation. We use sunk cost effect as the theoretical frame to develop our prediction. According to sunk cost effect, people continue a failing decision as a result of previously invested resources (sunk costs), such as time, money, and effort, because of their desire to avoid admitting their prior mistakes [24,64,65] or their fear of being blamed for wasting previously invested resources [66,67]. As such, people are reluctant to terminate or even revise their previous incorrect decisions [64,65,67]. In business, sunk cost effect is often exhibited in project escalation. For instance, CEOs and CFOs are reluctant to de-commit from a failing project course, such as poorly performing acquisitions [68], impaired asset investment [69], and failing research and development projects [70,71]. Sunk cost effect can be applied to both business and IT projects. Keil and his colleagues conduct a series of field studies that draw on sunk cost effect to inves tigate the escalation of IT projects [72–74]. Recent studies show that IT managers escalate failing projects, such as IT systems in airports [24], electronic government systems [25], and networking projects [26].

According to sunk cost effect, people often maintain commitment to their prior decision, even as they face problems in the decision, because they feel that acknowledging these problems means admitting their prior mistakes [64,65,67]. Therefore, these people may choose to hide the problems or create alternative explanations to justify the problems [69]. Given that retained CIOs were leaders in developing the system that contains ITCWs, sunk cost effect may guide them to avoid critically pinpointing the IT deficiencies and flaws created by their teams.

In contrast, incoming CIOs do not have such psychological barriers because the IT deficiencies and flaws in the current system were not built under their leadership; thus, they are likely to hold an open mind to identifying deficiencies in the current system. In addition, incoming CIOs may offer new perspectives to firms’ IT governance and control systems. Before proceeding to ITCW remediation, because incoming CIOs have just joined the firm after ITCW disclosures, they must reevaluate IT governance and diagnose misalignment between IT con trols and business strategies [49,57]. One output document is the remediation plan, which will be reviewed by other executives. Given that incoming CIOs do not reveal any “sunk cost” behavior, they are likely to effectively scrutinize the misalignment between IT controls and business strategy, and explain how remediation will be implemented and adhered to in practice [57,75]. An effective remediation plan can raise awareness of the laggard executives and gain their support.

Taken together, we anticipate that, when firms replace the current CIO with a new CIO, the firm is more likely to remediate ITCWs. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H3. Firms that replace their CIOs are more likely to remediate ITCWs than are firms that do not replace their CIOs.

## 4.2. Multivariate analyses

## 4.2.1. Test for H1

Panel A of Table 5 presents the regression results of model (1).

Contrary to our expectation, we find that the coefficient on ITCW is negative and marginally significant $( \mathrm { c o e f . } = - 0 . 6 4 7 , p = \ 0 . 0 2 9 )$ , suggesting that firms with ITCW disclosures are less likely to experience CIO turnover than are their counterparts. The marginal effect (dy/dx) im plies that firms that disclose ITCWs have a 6.5 % lower likelihood of CIO turnover than do firms without any ITCW disclosures. This finding is contradictory to our prediction, and H1 is not supported.

To probe this issue further, we perform a propensity score matching (PSM) analysis for model (1). Following Achleitner, Günther, Kaserer, and Siciliano [82], we conduct a difference-in-difference match based on kernel weights. We associate with the outcome (CIOChg) of firms with ITCW disclosures a matched outcome given by a kernel-weighted average of the outcomes of all firms without ITCW disclosures. This yields a matched sample of 6488 firm-year observations. We then run a logistic regression with the same independent and dependent variables as specified in model (1) and measure the conditional probability of experiencing a CIO turnover, given an ITCW disclosure. We control for other variables in model (1). The results are presented in Panel B of Table 5. We find that the coefficient on ITCW remains negative and marginally significant (coef. $= - 0 . 6 7 6 , p = \ 0 . 0 6 6 )$ . Overall, the results in Table 5 contradict H1, suggesting that ITCW disclosures are nega tively associated with CIO turnover.<sup>12</sup> One possible explanation for this finding is that, when firms experience ITCWs, they tend to retain their current CIO because he or she has tacit knowledge to fix the ITCWs.

## 4.2.2. Test for H2

H2 predicts that firms with ITCWs are more likely to remove CIOs with business expertise than CIOs with IT expertise. Panel A of Table 6 presents the regression results of model (1) using the sample of CIO with IT background (ITExp = 1). The coefficient on ITCW is negative and significant (coef. = − 1.426, p = 0.048), suggesting that firms with ITCW disclosures are less likely to experience turnover of CIOs with IT expertise than are firms without ITCW disclosures. The marginal effect (dy/dx) implies that firms that have disclosed ITCWs have a 14.9 % lower likelihood of turnover of a CIO with IT expertise than do firms without any ITCW disclosures. Panel B of Table 6 presents the regression results of model (1) using the sample of CIOs with business backgrounds (ITExp = 0). The coefficient on ITCW is positive yet nonsignificant (coef. = 0.432, p = 0.665), suggesting little effect of ITCW disclosures on the turnover of CIOs with a business background.

We then compare the magnitude of the effect of ITCW disclosures on CIO turnover in the two groups [78–80]. As shown in the final column of Table $^ { 6 , }$ the effect of ITCWs on the turnover of CIOs with IT expertise is found to be significantly lower (diff. = − 1.858, p = 0.045) than that among CIOs with business expertise. That is, in cases of ITCW disclo sures, the likelihood of turnover of CIOs with IT expertise is lower than that of CIOs with business expertise. Thus, the results of Table 6 support H2.

## 4.3. Additional analyses

## 4.3.1. Placebo test

It is important to ascertain whether the positive association between CIO turnover and ITCW remediation (H3) is driven by factors not related to IT controls, but by factors correlated with non-IT internal controls. To address this concern, we conduct a placebo test by changing the reme diation of ITCW (Remedy) to the remediation of non-IT internal control material weaknesses (RemOther) as the dependent variable in model (2). In our sample, 196 firm-year observations (39.4 %) remediate at least one non-IT internal control material weakness in year t + 2, while 301 firm-year observations (60.6 %) do not. According to Table 8, the co efficients on CIOChg are positive yet nonsignificant before PSM (coef. = 0.216, p = 0.675) and after PSM $( \mathrm { c o e f . } = 0 . 3 2 6 , p = 0 . 5 6 7 )$ . The results suggest that CIO turnover has little effect on the remediation of non-IT-related internal control material weaknesses, which provides some evidence that the positive association between CIO turnover and ITCW remediation is unlikely to be driven by other factors.

## 4.3.2. Effect of time lag between ITCW disclosures and CIO turnover on results

H1 examines whether ITCW disclosures lead to subsequent CIO turnover. In the main test, we select a one-year time gap—that is, ITCW disclosures at year t and CIO turnover at year t + 1. In this test, we vary the time gap to see if the result remains the same. We increase the time gap by one year—that is, ITCW disclosure in year t and CIO turnover in year t + 2. We code a binary variable CIOChgLag that equals one if there is CIO turnover in year t + 2, and zero otherwise. We re-run model (1) while replacing CIOChg with CIOChgLag. Panel A of Table 9 shows the regression results without PSM. We find that the coefficient on ITCW is negative yet not significant $( \mathrm { c o e f . } = - 0 . 1 5 7 , \ p \ = \ 0 . 6 2 9 )$ . We then compare the confidence interval of the coefficient on ITCW in this cur rent analysis with that in the main analyses (as shown in Table 5, Panel A). The 95 % confidence interval of the coefficient on ITCW in the current analysis is (− 0.792, 0.478), and is (− 1.226, − 0.067) in the main analysis. The two confidence intervals overlap, providing evidence that there is no significant difference in the magnitude of these two co efficients. Panel B of Table 9 shows the regression results after the PSM, where CIOChgLag remains negative, yet is nonsignificant (coef. $= - 0 . 0 4 9 , p = 0 . 6 9 7 )$ . Taken together, the results in Table 9 suggest that the effect of ITCW disclosure on CIO turnover is concentrated in the first year after the disclosure.

## 5. Discussions

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

Our study contributes to theory in three ways. First, it extends the literature on institutional theory by investigating the relationship be tween CIO turnover and the illegitimate action of failures concerning IT controls (i.e., ITCWs documented in financial reporting). ITCWs are attracting increased attention in the era of digital business. Our data show that firms that disclose ITCWs are likely to retain their current CIO, particularly when their CIOs possess IT expertise. Our findings differ from the findings of previous studies on CEO/CFO turnover after ITCW disclosure [4,6]. What causes the difference in associations between ITCW disclosure and CEO/CFO/CIO turnover? One plausible explana tion is that, although ITCW disclosures represent IT deficiencies and flaws that cause material misstatements in financial reporting, stake holders always pay close attention to such misstatements, while over looking the cause (ITCWs). CEOs and CFOs, rather than CIOs, are accountable for the quality of financial reporting, and SOX emphasizes the role of CEOs and CFOs in financial reporting, yet provides little guidance on CIOs’ responsibility. Therefore, stakeholders seldom realize that CIOs should be held accountable for ITCWs. As such, after an ITCW disclosure, CEO/CFO turnover, rather than CIO turnover, is more commonly implemented by firms to defend their legitimacy. In the era of digital business, given the complexity of digital technology and IT infrastructure, CIOs hold tacit knowledge about systems and IT gover nance in their firms. Firms consider the retention of their CIO's tacit knowledge a necessity with regard to remediating ITCWs; thus, they

retain the CIO.

Second, our study adds to the literature on the strategic role of CIOs in IT governance. While previous studies focus on CIOs’ job in managing IT resources or IT risks, they dedicate less attention to their role in ITrelated issues concerning financial reporting. However, the emergence of digital technologies, such as big data, cloud computing, and mobile accounting, is transforming methods of financial reporting. IS re searchers acknowledge the significance of digital technologies in financial reporting processes (e.g., [83–85]). To bridge this gap, our study is one of the earliest to investigate the CIO’s role concerning IT controls over financial reporting. Our findings suggest that, as opposed to firms’ common practice of retaining an existing CIO after ITCWs, replacing the current CIO increases the likelihood of ITCW remediation. This is largely because incoming CIOs do not have psychological barriers (i.e., sunk cost effect) to identifying ITCWs and bring new perspectives to IT controls.

Third, our study extends the literature on CIO turnover by providing initial empirical evidence linking CIO turnover to the types of CIO expertise: business expertise and IT expertise. While problems related to managing IT resources or IT risks may result from intentional mistakes or external environment factors, an ITCW has less causal ambiguity and can be directly attributed to technical issues in IT design or processes. In other words, CIOs’ IT expertise is more related to ITCWs and more relied on to remedy ITCWs than is their business expertise. As shown by our findings, CIOs with IT expertise are more likely to be retained than are their counterparts when dealing with ITCWs. We call on future research to assess the effects of other IT scandals on the turnover of different types of CIOs.

## 5.2. Practical contributions

Our study offers a number of practical implications. First, our find ings shift CIOs’ foci from operational efficiencies and product in novations to IT-related financial reporting reliabilities. According to the managing director of the US CIO program for Deloitte LLP, Khalid Kark,

CIOs are often tasked to focus on the operational efficiencies of the business, and the recent COVID-19 pandemic has guided them to shift their foci to fostering innovation and developing new services for their firm sustainability.<sup>14</sup> The observations by Khalid Kark align with the survey conducted by Harvey Nash and KPMG, which surveyed 3352 CIOs around the world, with 63 % indicating that investing in IT projects that generate revenue is their top priority, and only 37 % indicating IT system reliability as their most important consideration [86]. The CIO foci are somewhat driven by their compensation packages. About 68 % of CIO pay (e.g., bonus, stock, and options) is tied to the financial outcome of IT projects.<sup>15</sup> Considering these practitioners’ surveys together, CIOs focus more on digital innovations with revenue genera tion, and less on delivering reliable IT systems. Ironically, digital in novations cause 27 % of IT disruptions [86]. Our findings convey an important message to CIOs—CIOs may think that ITCWs are not a problem that originates from them, and thus feel less accountable for ITCWs. However, when there are IT-related problems related to finan cial reporting, CEOs may find that CIO turnover promotes successful remediation of ITCWs, and CIOs may lose their jobs. Therefore, CIOs efforts in enhancing IT-related financial reporting reliabilities may not lead to enhanced CIO compensation packages, yet may help retain their job. Our findings advise CIOs not to overlook operational reliabili ty—particularly the reliabilities of the IT-related financial reporting process.

Second, our study provides firms with actionable suggestions regarding IT control management and practice. The best way to manage IT control is a conundrum faced by board members of many firms [2]. According to a recent report by [87], about 40 % of initial public of fering firms in the US disclosed at least one material internal control weakness in their financial reports in the first nine months of 2019. Among all types of material internal control weaknesses disclosed, ITCWs ranked at the top [87]. In practice, a board tends to retain their CIO, yet dismiss their CEO/CFO, to defend their legitimacy when faced with ITCWs. Based on our data, less than 13.5 % of firms dismiss their CIOs after an ITCW disclosure, which is lower than the 19.2 % and 22.4 % of firms that dismiss their CEOs or CFOs after an ITCW disclosure, respectively. In other words, after an ITCW disclosure, a common practice of firms is to retain their CIOs. A board has many reasons to be reluctant to dismiss CIOs. First, as discussed in Section 2, board members are concerned about the loss of CIOs’ intrinsic knowledge of the firm’s IT systems. Second, board members expect more from CIOs in digital in novations that generate revenues than in safeguarding IT systems. Furthermore, some firms overlook the significance of the risks associ ated with the use of digital technology in financial reporting, which affects their recovery approach with regard to such risks. However, with regard to remediating ITCWs, our findings show that replacing the current CIO with a new one is crucial to the ITCW remediation process. Based on our data, about 76.6 % (90.9 %) of firms that do not dismiss (do dismiss) their CIOs after an ITCW disclosure successfully remediate the firm’s ITCWs. These figures inform board members that their common practice—of retaining CIOs after ITCW disclosure—is less effective at resolving ITCWs.

Moreover, regulators, such as the European Securities and Markets Authority, recognize the significance of digital technologies in financial reporting processes. These regulators issue new guidance to help firms reform their financial reporting in the era of digital business.<sup>16</sup> Our findings provide regulators (e.g., the SEC and PCAOB) with evidence that a board may not hold CIOs accountable for material misstatements in financial reporting caused by ITCWs. Given that the CIO’s role is gaining importance in the development of high-quality IT controls with the emergence of digital business, regulators should provide further guidance on the CIO’s role in IT controls for the benefit of stakeholders.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This article has several limitations. First, in addition to ITCWs, SOX identifies two further categories of deficiencies and weaknesses: IT de ficiencies and significant IT deficiencies. These categories refer to the design or operation of IT controls that adversely affect management or employees in the normal course of performing their assigned functions to prevent or detect misstatements on time [12]. Unlike for ITCWs, the AuditAnalytics (SOX 404 Internal Control) database does not contain records of IT deficiencies or significant IT deficiencies. Future research may adopt a case study approach to investigate how these two categories lead to CIO turnovers. Second, there may exist other factors that simultaneously affect CIO turnover, ITCW disclosure, and subsequent ITCW remediation. To address the endogeneity issue, we conduct two PSM analyses, as discussed in Section 4.2, to test the robustness of our results. Although it is not possible to completely rule out endogeneity, the additional PSM analyses provide partial evidence that endogeneity may not be an issue. Future research is warranted to examine whether any other factors may influence the association between ITCW disclo sure and CIO turnover, as well as the association between CIO turnover and ITCW remediation. Third, unlike CEO/CFO data, CIO data are less available in subscribed databases. In future research, researchers may adopt a survey or interview approach to collect qualitative information on CIO turnover, such as the reason behind the turnover, to extend this line of research. Finally, apart from CIOs, firms may choose to dismiss a CEO/CFO in an IT scandal or dismiss a CIO after ITCW remediation. Future research can explore how firms’ situational factors (such as ex ecutive tenures and influence of the board of directors) influence which executives are dismissed and when this occurs.

## 6. Conclusions

The global network of commercial activities in the era of digital business creates challenges for firms in ensuring adequate IT controls and gaining legitimacy among stakeholders. We undertake this study to explore the strategic role of CIOs in the situation of ITCWs. Our empirical results establish that CIO turnover after an ITCW disclosure promotes ITCW remediation, yet firms often retain their CIO, rather than hiring a new one, when faced with ITCWs. We provide firms with in sights regarding ITCW disclosures and subsequent ITCW remediation, and, together with prior studies, offer suggestions to improve the maintenance and remediation of adequate IT controls within the realm of digital business.
