## A B S T R A C T

This paper examines the enactment of formal controls in information system development (ISD). Specifically, we investigate process guidance and goal importance as enactments of behavior control and outcome control, respectively. We hypothesize the direct and interaction performance relationships of these control enactments, arguing process guidance exhibits a curvilinear (inverted U-shape) and goal importance exhibits a positive linear relationship with performance, Our finding of a U-shaped interaction relationship indicates that when goal importance is high, higher performance is achieved by either low or high process guidance. Our work bolsters the growing idea that control enactment is a salient consideration in ISD.

## 1. Introduction

The effective control of information system development (ISD) poses a special challenge due to the complex and nonroutine nature of the tasks [1–3]. Acknowledging the simultaneous existence of multiple control modes in practice (e.g., behavior, outcome, informal control), information system (IS) scholars have extensively studied the portfolios and interactions of these different control modes [4,5]. Given the mixed results in these studies, more recent research has begun to explore various processes through which control modes are enacted [6,7]. This control enactment research is enhancing knowledge by expanding the understanding of the array of possible enactment processes and their performance consequences [6,8].

Control enactment focuses on the how rather than the what. where the how refers to the interaction between the controller and controlee through which selected controls (the what) are implemented or pro moted [6,9]. This line of research indicates that the prior inconsistent results in studies of control modes may be partially due to differences in control enactment processes which influence control effectiveness [10]. This control enactment work has distinguished different enactment styles, which can be either more authoritative or more enabling [6]. Even though both styles intend to ensure controlee compliance, an authoritative style is designed top-down and often enforces compliance, while an enabling style is collaborative and therefore allows controlees to better deal with contingencies.

Our research aims to extend this emerging understanding of control enactment regarding formal controls (behavior and outcome) enacted in enabling styles. We investigate a multi-department IS branch of a state government in the United States. The particular conditions of this context, as will be further explained later, resulted in behavior control being enacted through process guidance and outcome control being enacted through goal importance. These formal control enactments are manifestations of a control style that was more enabling than authori tative. Specifically, behavior control was accomplished by allowing each department manager (controller) to establish processes to shepherd their projects and guide the ISD professionals (controlees). Controllers then prudently evaluated conformity to provide behavior guidance; thus, the defined processes were used as a means to communicate, co ordinate, and provide learning-based guidance to controlees. Therefore, we call this enactment of behavior control process guidance, to represent its distinction from authoritarian behavior control enactment that relies on centralized surveillance and enforcement. In our examined context, outcome control was accomplished by delineating the importance level of different goals (e.g., reducing client “errors in use” is more important than maintaining budgeted cost). As such, the performance goals in outcome control were directional and signaled priorities to controlees without rigidity in time or scale. This promoted collaborative commu nication between controller and controlee to provide direction and discretion (autonomy) to the controlee. We call this enactment of outcome control goal importance.

Our analysis of control enactment builds on the work of scholars who have acknowledged that organizations frequently have a portfolio of controls [3,4] and that these controls interact in affecting performance $[ 1 1 , 1 2 ]$ . Therefore, while we first investigate the formal controls (behavior and outcome control) enacted in enabling styles, we also address an additional research gap regarding the interaction of formal control enactment processes. Mapping to the unique control enactment of our examined context, our specific research question is:

How will behavior control, enacted through process guidance, and outcome control, enacted through goal importance, affect con trolees’ performance both directly and jointly?

We examine the effects of these enactment processes from the perspective of controlees who receive the message from the controller. In this way, our examination departs from prior work that has primarily focused on the controller side of the control dyad [13,14]. Our interest in the controlee perspective of control enactment stimulated us to join a growing stream of research that argues the enactment of formal controls shape controlees’ performance through their motivation [7,15]. This literature indicates that behavior control can potentially have curvi linear effects, exhibiting a mid-range optimal level [16]; therefore, we likewise theorize a curvilinear relationship between process guidance and performance. Most importantly, we theorize an interaction with a curve flip—arguing that with high goal importance, the process gui dance–performance relationship becomes U-shaped, as compared with an inverted U-shaped when goal importance is low.

Overall, our investigation contributes to the IS control theory liter ature, opens pathways for future research, and provides insight for practice. We examine novel, enabling-style enactment of behavior and outcome controls and their interaction. In doing so, we respond to calls to investigate control enactment [6], the curvilinear relationships of controls [16], and the motivational influence of controls [12].

## 2. Theory background

## 2.1. Control theory, control modes, and control enactment

Control is defined as the processes and practices by which controllers “seek to align controlees capabilities, activities, and performance with controllers' goals and aspirations" [[17]. p.3]. The literature has distinguished two broad categories of controls: formal control modes, which rely on prescribed processes (behavior control) or performance standards (outcome control), and informal control modes, which rely on people or socially based mechanisms [5,15,18]. Our investigation, aligning with the work of many other IS control scholars [19,20], fo cuses on formal controls, primarily because they are under the direct and responsive control of management unlike informal controls, which are socially embedded over longer periods of time [10].

To account for the inconsistent results with regard to the perfor mance effects of different control modes, control scholars have begun to explore the salience of control enactment [19,21]. For example, Tiwana and Keil [8] argued that the inconsistent results in the literature are partially explained by the distinction between attempted and realized levels of control. The level of realized control is the controlees’ per ceptions of the extent to which control modes are enacted by the controller. In this work, a prominent aspect of enactment is control style [19,22,23]. Control style is the manner in which the controller interacts with the controlee and has been generally categorized as authoritative or enabling [6,23]. Wiener, Mahring, Remus and Saunders [6], building on Adler and Borys [24], identified that an enabling style is character ized two distinguishing features: repair and transparency. In repair, an enabling style anticipates breakdowns and appreciates collaboration between the controlee and controller to repair them. In transparency, controllers provide controlees with contextual information, under standing about the controls, and feedback on performance.

While there is increasing research interest in examining the enact ment of controls [6,7], there is limited research that explicitly theorizes and empirically tests the direct and interactive performance conse quences of different control enactment processes. Accordingly, our research aims to extend the emerging understanding of formal controls that are enacted in an enabling style. As further described below, we investigate behavior control enacted through process guidance and outcome control enacted through goal importance.

## 2.2. Behavior control enacted through process guidance

Behavior control has traditionally been enacted by regulating con trolees’ activities through formalized rules and procedures [25,26], setting behavioral boundaries to control risk and reduce errors [3,27], and by controllers closely monitoring controlees’ adherence to the procedures with sanctions for diversions [2,28]. As such, behavior control provides explicit direction (through procedures) to controlees and motivates their effort [15,29].

Several IS studies have included the enactment of behavior control in their analyses. For example, Venkatesh, Rai and Maruping [2] examined how IS project managers’ project-related knowledge mitigates technical IS project risk by facilitating the enactment of process controls in their IS projects. Chua, Lim, Soh and Sia [10] mentioned the enactment of behavior control in an authoritative style by mandating the adoption of a certain process-modeling notation. In contrast, behavior control can also be enacted in an enabling style; for example, through weekly meetings to “reach and recurrently re-establish consensus” and “to inform stakeholders and obtain their view on the project” [23, p.350]. To date, research has only begun to examine the multitude of possible ways to enact behavior control.

Contributing to this area, we investigate behavior control enacted through process guidance. In our investigated context, process guidance was implemented in an enabling control style—having features of both repair and transparency. While process guidance has the characteristic levels of intensity found in traditional behavior control, it is manifested in an enabling style in that the controlling function is achieved through a substantive level of communication between the controller and con trolee, a prudent level of process conformity is continuously evaluated, and controlees receive learning-based corrective action guidance. These attributes conform to the features of an enabling style—with high repair and transparency [6]. More specifically, aligning with the general con cepts of repair, process guidance allows both controlee feedback and deviations from the process guidance—owing to its nature as “guidance” and to not being centrally surveilled and enforced. Aligning with the general concepts of transparency, process guidance provides context transparency because controllers explain the rationale for the controls to the controlees. The communication of repair and transparency, addi tionally, provide rich learning opportunities for controlees [24].

## 2.3. Outcome control enacted through goal importance

Outcome control is typically founded on the goals desired by the controller [8]. In uncertain ISD environments, goals are frequently ambiguous and sporadically shift [1]. The notion that goals, as a con trolling device, can direct activity has been identified in the IS literature as controlled flexibility by Harris, Collins and Hevner [1]. Harris and colleagues researched software development under uncertainty and argued that emergent outcome control shapes project-scope boundaries, without specific performance outcomes. In their work, emergent outcome control—which limits solution options—steers (directs)

development rather than focusing on the evaluation of results, as done by traditional outcome control. IS studies have also begun to include the enactment of outcome control in their analyses. As one example, Choudhury and Sabherwal [4] suggested that in outsourced projects, a shared understanding regarding underlying project motives (trans parency) can be developed to enact outcome control in IS outsourcing contracts. In general, our understanding of the possible ways to enact outcome control remains incomplete.

Contributing to this area, we investigate, as previously mentioned, outcome control enacted through goal importance. Lacking the specificity of a traditional performance target, goal importance provides direction and signals priorities to controlees without rigidity in time or scale. In our investigated context, goal importance was implemented in an enabling control style—having features of repair and transparency. While goal importance signifies the desired level of effort and the di rection of the effort, it was manifested in an enabling style in that the controlling function is achieved through collaborative communication between the controller and controlee, periodic monitoring of perfor mance progress, and controlees receiving performance feedback. These attributes conform to the features of an enabling style—with high repair and transparency [6]. More specifically, aligning with the general con cepts of repair, goal importance allows controlees discretion to respond to real-world contingencies and to provide feedback to refine goal importance levels. Aligning with the general concepts of transparency, goal importance provides context transparency because controllers define and explain the rationale for the importance levels of different desired goals to controlees.

## 2.4. Performance consequences of controls

The IS control literature has examined the influence of formal control modes and control enactments on a broad array of performance di mensions at various levels of analysis [e.g. 2,30,31]. The IS control modes literature has examined the effects of selected control modes on ISD project performance in general [8,30] as well as specific perfor mance dimensions such as project efficiency and quality [e.g. 3,32]. While earlier studies provided empirical support for the performance-enhancing effects of single control modes [8,27,33], the literature has produced partly inconclusive or even contradictory re sults, for example, with regard to the interaction of different control modes [6]. To address the inconsistencies in the control modes litera ture, control scholars started to show keen interest in examining control enactment [19,21]. While the IS control enactment literature is more limited, it has examined the effects of control transmission on project performance [34] as well as the enactment of formal controls in pro moting informal control [7,32]. Recently, Remus, Wiener, Saunders and M¨ahring [31] made the first empirical test of the integrated IS project control theory and found that control enactment is a stronger predictor of individual outcome than control modes.

Our study examines the performance of ISD professionals (con trolees) in client satisfaction which is the degree to which they meet the full spectrum of client requirements and desires [e.g. 35]. Client satis faction not only relates to the ISD performance (on-time, within budget, and meeting project objectives), but also responds to the variability and ambiguity found in ISD performance targets, because clients are aware of the changes experienced. Importantly, to develop generalizable the ory, we build our hypotheses about the general performance of controlees.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. Behavior control enacted through process guidance and performance

Our first hypothesis examines behavior control enacted through process guidance. We follow a widely acknowledged but limitedly examined notion in the literature that behavior control can have an inverted U-shaped relationship with performance [36]—that is, too much control can cease to be a good thing [16]. The underlying logic of these nonlinear arguments is that controlee autonomy is constrained at high levels of behavior control, thereby reducing motivation and consequent performance [37,38]. In this view, behavior control gener ally benefits performance, but at high levels controlees perceive such control as excessive and unfair, and therefore, their motivation to make efforts is constrained [39]. Drawing upon these insights, we argue that (a) the effects on behavior control depend on its enactment and that (b) both too little and too much process guidance by the controller will be detrimental to controlee performance.

To more deeply understand this curvilinear relationship and the ef fects of control enactment, we join a growing set of ISD scholars who argue that the way in which controls are enacted influences the moti vation of controlees [7,15]. In addition to the anticipated positive per formance gains of formal controls, motivation plays a role in determining controlees’ responses and discretionary efforts. Impor tantly, different enactment processes may have different effects on motivation and resultant performance [40,41].<sup>1</sup> In general, an enabling style, in comparison with authoritative style, is anticipated to exhibit higher controlee motivation [19,21]. Even though an enabling style of process guidance exhibits higher communication between the controller and controlee, has ongoing conformity evaluations, supports corrective actions, and promotes learning, we contend that it still retains an inverted U-shaped relationship with performance.<sup>2</sup>

Accordingly, with increasing behavior control enacted through process guidance two resulting effects are simultaneously in play: increasing direction from explicit procedures, which increases motivation and consequent performance, and increasing constraints, which decrease motivation and consequent performance (see Fig. 1). With low process guidance, performance is low because there is a lack of specified pro cedures that could provide direction and motivate controlees [24]. As the level of process guidance increases, performance increases because controlees’ behaviors are increasingly specified by explicit direction from the procedures [27,29]. The explicit direction from process guid ance motivates controlees as they are more confident in knowing what to do [7]. However, as process guidance is further increased and a large number of procedures are defined and evaluated, even in an enabling style, controlees are likely to experience significant constraints on their autonomy, with concomitant decreases in motivation [42,43]. The result of these divergent influences is a curvilinear relationship in which performance initially increases with increasing levels of process guid ance due to the increases in direction (and motivation) with limited constraints, but the gains are offset at some point by reduced motivation due to increasing constraints on autonomy. Hence:

Hypothesis 1. In the ISD context, the relationship between behavior control, enacted through process guidance, and performance exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship.

## 3.2. Outcome control enacted through goal importance and performance

Our second hypothesis examines outcome control enacted through goal importance. While the notion of goal importance is new to the control literature, the view in the goal setting literature is that goal importance is generally, but not always, positively related to performance [44–47]. In the IS literature, outcome control, which is based on performance targets, is also generally reported to have positive performance effects [e.g., 8]. According to theories of motivation, goal importance will have a positive relationship with controlee performance to the extent that it is internalized by controlees and integrated into their own sense of priorities [40,41,46,47]. This internalization is analogous to the level of realized control, as explained by Tiwana and Keil [8], in the IS control literature. We anticipate that goal internalization and thus goal motivation is enhanced by an enabling style of enactment [23]. There is a great deal of research evidence to support that higher out comes follow from active commitment toward a goal due to higher in tensity and persistence of effort [e.g., 46,47]. Thus, aligning with the preponderance of prior literature, we expect that outcome control enacted through goal importance on a specific goal, to the extent that goal importance is internalized by controlees, has a salutary effect on performance on that specific goal. ## 3.3. Interactive effects of process guidance and goal importance

Our third hypothesis is that outcome control, enacted through goal importance, and behavior control, enacted through process guidance, have interactive effects on the performance of controlees. We argue that both low and high, but not mid-range, process guidance by the controller will be beneficial to controlee performance when goal importance is high. The reason for this curve flip is that the motivational effect of high goal importance, in comparison with low goal importance, shifts the moti vational benefits and autonomy constraints of process guidance.

To more deeply understand this curvilinear interaction relationship, we consider the combined motivational effects of the two control en actments. This includes motivation from goal importance and the divergent motivational effects from process guidance, as depicted in Fig. 1. As a foundation, we contend that under a low level of goal importance this interaction follows Hypothesis 1—an inverted U-shape. However, we argue that the relationship flips to be a U-shape when goal importance is high.

Our argument for a U-shaped relationship is built by examining the mid-point and high and low extremes of process guidance conditional on high goal importance. At some mid-range level of process guidance, as Hypothesis 1 suggests, the controlees receive an optimum level of pro cess guidance from the controllers—not too little or too much. In this situation, we contend that the effect of goal importance is limited because the level of process guidance is well-suited for the motivation of controlees and any higher or lower motivation from goal importance has little influence on the total motivation level of controlees [42,43,48].<sup>3</sup> Extending from this central point, we next argue how high goal importance can mitigate the negative impact of the too-much (or too-little) effect of process guidance on controlees’ performance outcomes.

With high goal importance and low process guidance, performance will be high. This is because the combination of high goal importance and little process guidance results in a high level of goal motivation with little constraint on possible behaviors. Motivation from goal importance is generally accepted as influential in achieving high levels of perfor mance [42,43]. Notably, this combination allows controllers to motivate controlees to explore novel strategies that often result in higher per formance outcomes (Locke and Latham [47]).

When goal importance is high and process guidance is high, we likewise argue that performance will be high. While the explicit pro cedures of high process guidance decrease motivation due to autonomy reduction [49], we argue that the motivation of high goal importance offsets the decreased motivation due to autonomy reduction [7]. Importantly, this offset allows the performance gains of more explicit direction (from procedures) to be revealed. In this situation, higher levels of process guidance, particularly in an enabling style, motivate controlees by providing clarity on the legitimate procedures to achieve the important goals [2,50].

Overall, we argue that outcome control, enacted through goal importance, and behavior control, enacted through process guidance, have interactive effects on controlees’ performance. More specifically, we argue that the motivational effects from high goal importance shift the influence of the divergent motivational and demotivational effects that result from process guidance. This shift under high goal importance results in the demotivation from autonomy constraints dominating the relationship at low to moderate levels of process guidance and the motivation from explicit direction dominating the relationship at mod erate to high levels of process guidance (the opposite of the expectation under low goal importance). Therefore:

Hypothesis 3. In the ISD context, outcome control, enacted through goal importance, and behavior control, enacted through process guid ance, interact in their relationship with performance such that (a) when goal importance is low, process guidance exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship with performance and (b) when goal importance is high, process guidance exhibits a U-shaped relationship with performance.

## 4. Methods

To test our hypotheses, we use a proprietary dataset collected through a detailed survey performed in 2008 and 2009. The investigated context is an IS branch of a state government in the United States at a time when the technology landscape was changing. This organization contained ISD professionals (controlees) supporting all agencies of the government of the state (e.g., Bureau of Motor Vehicles, Department of Revenue) and was organized into different departments by agency. There was a large array of ISD projects, including new IS initiations, upgrades, and terminations. All the IS department managers reported to a central IS Group Director, a former business executive who was in the early stages of implementing business principles in a government context.

In this environment, the department managers (controllers) inten tionally established directional goals, as previously described. The de partments had more than one goal, and these goals were distinguished by varying levels of importance across the departments based on the distinct agency (client) desires. Although these goals were formally rolled-down to projects, they were treated as “soft” directional targets rather than “hard” requirements, that is, they were accepted as flexible. However, these goals nevertheless had a distinct importance (or prior ity). We view this goal importance as the enactment of outcome control. Regarding behavior control, the ISD professionals (controlees) had a variety of training, capability, and prior experience in ISD project management, six sigma, and lean techniques. This diversity led top management to accept varied forms of project management processes, which means that there was not a mandated, standard development methodology to enforce controlee behavior across the IS branch nor were there centralized review requirements. This situation resulted in each department manager establishing processes to shepherd their projects and guide the ISD professionals. We view this process guidance approach as the enactment of behavior control because the department managers (as controllers) defined a project management process as a means to communicate, coordinate, and provide guidance to the ISD professionals (as controlees).

The control enactments exercised in our context are more enabling than authoritative, largely because of the legitimacy concerns of top down control in this government organization [19].<sup>4</sup> IS technology was substantially changing and uncertain at the time, such that top managers took the view that traditional, fixed, top-down control speci fications would be perceived to be illegitimate by the ISD professionals. Overall, behavior control, processes with varying levels of guidance, and outcome control, goals with varying levels of importance, were simul taneously in play, thereby enabling our investigation of whether and how they interact in affecting performance. It should be noted that informal control was relatively uniform due to the hiring practices of the state with regard to ISD professional roles (these roles are not political appointments). Nonetheless, our empirical models control for informal

control.

Importantly, within the IS branch, the process of delivering new ISs and software was rhetorically called an “improvement activity” to be sensitive to the political and cultural environment. This nomenclature was adopted by the organization because new ISs largely meant termi nating old, often highly manual, systems and reducing the number of employees as the new systems were more efficient, which influences our survey question design, but is not a critical aspect of our hypotheses. The task was to improve the ISs of the state government. Questionnaires were delivered by e-mail to 770 ISD professionals, and 447 were returned, for a response rate of 58.1 %. The high response rate helps reduce the concern of nonresponse bias. Not all respondents provided full data, so our hypotheses were tested on 329 observations.

As discussed previously, our investigation takes the view that con trollers influence controlees by enacting controls that influence con trolees’ behavior and motivation toward accomplishing projects that satisfy government agencies. Our variables are therefore designed to measure the enactment of formal controls from the perspective of ISD professionals (controlees). More specifically, process guidance and goal importance are measured as perceptions of the controls received by the responding ISD professionals. For example, outcome control as goal importance is the reported goal importance of the ISD professionals, which we view as internalized, or realized, control [8]. Similarly, the realized level of process guidance is what influences motivation and motivates the action of the ISD professionals.

## 4.1. Independent variables

Behavior control enacted through process guidance is measured using five items, which are generally based on the work of Aiken and Hage [51] and Cardinal [25], with adaptation to our specific program man agement context (see the supplemental material). Our items address the level of process guidance perceived by the controlee as enacted by the controller (this process guidance was enacted in an enabling style, as previously described). To establish the psychometric soundness of our measure, we conducted exploratory factor analysis. This analysis sug gests that the items load on one factor, supporting their unidimension ality [52] and reliability, as indicated by the Cronbach alpha of 0.82 and average variance extracted (AVE) of 0.88, respectively.

Our measure of outcome control enacted through goal importance is adapted from prior research [18]. This measure captures the controlee's understanding of a specific goal’s relative importance as communicated by the controller. We examine the goal of client satisfaction because it is highly achievable by direct actions in ISD. This achievability has been related to motivation in prior goal research [e.g., 53]. Thus, the client satisfaction goal aligns with our hypothesizing using self-determination theory (for a further discussion of goal selection for outcome control, see the theory boundary conditions information provided in the supple mental file of this manuscript). Client satisfaction refers to the IS orga nization’s internal customers, which are government agencies rather than end-users (i.e., service recipients)<sup>6</sup> . To obtain a relative importance measure for this goal, our survey instrument examines the four key goals of the organization: cost, quality, client satisfaction, and end-user customer satisfaction. The survey respondents were asked to indicate the importance of the four goals by allocating 20 points across the goals (see the supplemental material). Our measure is the percentage of the 20 points allocated to the client satisfaction goal. Our method forces re spondents to choose among alternatives, thus revealing their priorities. This method of quantification has been shown to be a more robust approach in survey measurement than absolute importance ratings [54].

## 4.2. Dependent variable

Our indicator of performance is client satisfaction, measured through a survey response on a five-point Likert scale. Analogous to goal importance, our survey instrument includes four performance di mensions: cost, quality, client (internal customer) satisfaction, and enduser customer satisfaction. We focus our hypothesis testing on client satisfaction performance to align with the client satisfaction goal, as mentioned previously. This one-to-one alignment follows the arguments that control mechanisms are “directly tied to specific organizational goals” and that goal attainment implies performance [55, p. 560]. Since individual employee performance, as evaluated by the client govern ment agencies, is difficult to accurately collect in our studied context, we resort to perceived department performance as rated by the controlee as a proxy for controlee performance. We take this view because 1) the literature acknowledges that the lower level subjects play a substantive role in composing higher level constructs—that is, the functional rela tionship between these levels is additive [56] and 2) assessing perfor mance at a higher organizational level (e.g., project, team, or department) can mitigate the recognized bias that individuals exhibit in assessing themselves $[ 5 7 , 5 8 ] . ^ { 7 }$ This view also aligns with the control enactment literature. where the performance outcome at the individual level is considered to be highly correlated with the performance at a higher organizational level (e.g., project, team, or department) [2,31]. Specifically, the respondent controlees were asked to rate their depart ment performance over the past year from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” based on the item “My department currently meets our customer requirements.” In this setting, each respondent was respon sible for meeting the requirements of their clients, who were in a single government agency. With this narrow scope of focus, we take the view that the respondent controlees possess insight into the performance of their IS departments. Additionally, we discuss the limitation of this proxy in the limitations section.

## 4.3. Control variables

We consider a set of control variables that could provide alternative explanations of performance, influence survey responses, or influence the reaction to the formal controls (e.g., experience and knowledge may influence the response to controls). These control variables include a proxy for informal control (socialization), education, employment duration, goal difficulty, leadership effectiveness, and respondents’ age and gender.

## 4.4. Analysis

We model the responses of the ISD professionals as independent observations controlling for department fixed effects (see Table 2). The nested nature of our data might suggest multilevel modeling (e.g., hi erarchical linear modeling or random effects). However, scholars have indicated that the parameter estimates and standard errors may be biased when the sample size is small, for example, less than 30 groups [60], and our sample size was less than the required threshold. None theless, we were able to run an independent observations model with department fixed effects. This approach allowed us to partial-out the department variance, as the process guidance and goal importance levels were, in large part, shaped by each department. Note that our reported analysis used an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression pro cedure that provided robust errors, and the independent variables in the interaction were mean-centered to ease interpretation. All the variance inflation factor values were less than 3.2, indicating that multicollinearity was of limited concern [61].

Overall, outcome control, enacted through goal importance, and behavior control, enacted through process guidance, interact in their relationship with performance and this interaction is critical to understanding the determinants of performance.

Furthermore, we conducted numerous sensitivity checks (which are available in the supplemental material), increasing the confidence in our findings.

## 5. Discussion and implications

The intent of this research is to enhance both theoretical and prac tical knowledge regarding the enactment of formal controls in ISD contexts. Specifically, we analyze process guidance as an enactment of behavior control and goal importance as an enactment of outcome control. While the notion of goals directing project professionals attention during the ISD process is not new, to our knowledge, this is the first examination of goal importance as an enactment of outcome con trol. Process guidance as an enactment of behavior control is also new, but can be understood as an enabling style of traditional process formalization. Most importantly, our research extends the primary focus on control mode interactions to the interactive effects of enactment processes within control configurations. We find general support for our hypotheses, although we only find an inverted U-shape for process guidance in the presence of low goal importance. Our results indicate that behavior control, enacted through process guidance, of a moderate level diminishes the additional benefit of outcome control, enacted through goal importance. However, most interestingly, our results also indicate that with high goal importance performance is enhanced by either low or high—but not moderate—process guidance. ## 5.1. Implications for theory

Overall, our investigation contributes to the IS control theory liter ature by examining novel, enabling-style enactment of formal controls in an ISD context, considering curvilinear relationships, and by theo rizing about the motivational influences of these control enactments in their interaction. Our investigation extends research on the enactment of formal control modes, adding to the research by Srivastava and Teo [62] and Wiener, Mahring, Remus and Saunders [6]. Our work adds support to the contention that IS control scholars need to widen and deepen their scope of examination into the various possible ways in which control modes can be enacted, thereby opening rich pathways for future research.

First, we contribute to the emerging work on the enactment of con trols by examining enabling-style enactments that are applicable in ISD contexts [63]. This contribution directly responds to calls for enactment investigations [6]. Goal importance extends Harris, Collins and Hevner’s [1] notions regarding emergent outcome control, as well as controlled flexibility, yet it is distinct in that stakeholders define performance priorities rather than constraining solution options.

Second, we are among the first (to our knowledge) to investigate the joint effects of specific enactments of formal controls while considering curvilinear relationships. Our analysis results present a provocative insight: outcome control, enacted through goal importance, influences the performance effects of behavior control, enacted through process guidance, more substantially at the extremes of behavior control, and the moderating influence of outcome control weakens in the mid-range of behavior control. While the broad literature has suggested that con trols and governance approaches [36,64] can be curvilinear, this phe nomenon is understudied and has substantial effects in our analysis in that the traditionally anticipated optimality at a mid-range level is the low performance point if goal importance is high. Our work further demonstrates not only the research relevance of control portfolios [4], but also the interactive effects of the enactments of control modes within these portfolios [6,62,65]. As such, our research responds to calls fo examinations of control portfolio configurations and enactment [6].

Third, extending the work of a growing list of scholars in ISD [7,15] and management control [66–68] and following the call from Sihag and Rijsdijk [12], our theorizing examines the motivational aspects of con trol enactment processes. Adding to this limited prior work, our argu ments suggest that the micro-motivational influence of macro-control enactment on ISD professionals is crucial to understanding performance.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

This research provides novel implications for IS organizations operating in uncertain contexts in which goal importance may be a legitimate way to enact outcome control. Specifically, there appears to be a performance-enhancing “balance” between behavior control, enacted through process guidance, and outcome control, enacted through goal importance, that requires careful consideration of the motivational consequences on ISD professionals. Indeed, there is a “sweet spot” of moderate process guidance where the addition of goal importance ceases to materially influence performance. Yet this level of process guidance is not the performance optimum if goals are internal ized as important. Our results suggest that IS organizations seeking to enhance performance on a specific goal within a given level of process guidance may want to communicate the specific goal’s importance with watchful consideration of the achievability of the goal to build commitment and internalization. Reciprocally, top managers of an IS organization may want to specify the level of process guidance with consideration of existing goal attributes. Our results suggest a provoc ative prescription: that with high internalized goal importance, highe performance is achieved by either low or high rather than the moderate

level of process guidance.

Our analysis indicates that controllers should seek a deep under standing of controlee response to controls. An interesting implication of our findings is that very high levels of process guidance are only bene ficial if ISD professionals have internalized the goals as important (for more information, see the theory boundary conditions in the supple mental file). Further, our arguments indicate that the perception of autonomy is an important motivational factor regarding process guid ance. We believe, as others have suggested [e.g., 21,69], that such perceptions, as well as acceptance (internalization), of goals can be shaped by management communication and persuasion, namely, through the interactions of controllers and controlees. In other words, the ways in which controls are enacted is a salient consideration in ISD contexts.

## 5.3. Limitations and research opportunities

While we believe our work enhances knowledge of control enact ment, the present study also has limitations that provide future research opportunities. First, the data collection was carried out in a large gov ernment IS organization. This is an advantage as the nature of govern ment IS work tends to result in a high level of commonality among other environmental attributes which aggravate multi-company studies. Nonetheless, our results from a single organization should be interpreted with caution, and future research should more broadly explore the ef fects in other contexts. Second, our study, as many before, has imperfect measures. Our measurement of control enactment is based on the con trolee perspective, and thus, our data are unable to capture dyadic controller-controlee perspectives and transmission loss information that could build richer theory regarding enactment. Additionally, as previ ously mentioned, our measure of controlee performance is a proxy, and future work can improve on this aspect of our work. Third, this study has a risk of method bias because of the use of self-reported survey data. While we followed survey design guidelines, performed several statis tical procedures, and found literature supporting that quadratic effects in a regression equation are unlikely to be artefacts of method variance (see the supplemental material), we recommend that data collection from multiple sources should be undertaken in future work. Fourth, our dependent variable is a single item measure, although it is a forced ranking with the strengths thereof. Future research should consider improved measurement of dependent variables that explicitly align with the goals that the controls target to achieve. Fifth, although we provide evidence that is consistent with our theorizing, our dataset did not allow empirical testing of the causal mechanisms we theorized. Future work on testing these mechanisms is highly encouraged.

We encourage future studies of control to use the entirety of self determination theory [40,41], as doing so may provide a more nuanced understanding of how controllers influence controlees, as well as the response of controlees to controls and various enactment pro cesses [see: 7,15]. More specifically, SDT examines the conditions sup porting an individual’s experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which are argued to result in motivation and thus enhanced performance, persistence, and creativity [40,41]. Beyond our arguments of autonomy, we believe that standardized procedures, as found in behavior control, may foster perceptions of competence and that informal controls may foster perceptions of relatedness. We, therefore, encourage research that assesses controller intentions, control enact ment, controlee perceptions (underpinning motivation), and the resul tant performance.

We encourage studies of other forms of control enactment and their influence on ISD professionals’ motivation. Post-bureaucratic organi zations in which knowledge-based, professional work is the norm [70]; agile organizations operating in highly dynamic environments; and or ganizations using temporary or loosely connected staff members may develop more creative control approaches and novel control enactment. We believe that an improved understanding of control enactment in today’s diverse ISD work environments is important—theoretically, socially, and practically.
