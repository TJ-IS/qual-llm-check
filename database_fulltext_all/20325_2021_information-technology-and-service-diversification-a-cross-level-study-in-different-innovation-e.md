# Information technology and service diversification: A cross-level study in different innovation environments

Yang Lei <sup>a</sup>, Yue Guo <sup>a</sup>, Yiying Zhang <sup>b,</sup>\*, Waiman Cheung

<sup>a</sup> Southern University of Science and Technology, China

<sup>b</sup> China Europe International Business School, China

<sup>c</sup> Chinese University of Hong Kong, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Service diversification Information technology Innovation environment Affordance theory TOE framework

## A B S T R A C T

Service diversification (SD) enhances firms’ operational performance. Although the literature shows information technology (IT) helps firms with their SD strategies, the effects of specific IT need further investigation. More over, the effects of the macro environment are overlooked. This study proposes a cross-level model, which is examined using data of 690 logistics firms across 23 Chinese cities. The results show that the four types of IT that are widely used in the logistics industry and the innovation environment are positively related to SD. However, the innovation environment negatively moderates the relationships between two of the four types of IT and SD.

## 1. Introduction

Business diversification is a corporate strategy for launching new products or product lines, offering new services, or entering new mar kets [1,2]. Studies have found that business diversification strategies can enhance a firm’s performance by effectively utilizing current employees, facilities, skills, and other resources to expand into new markets [3,4]. However, as numbers of operations departments, markets, products, and services increase, coordination across departments becomes much more complex, and a business diversification strategy sometimes leads to serious failures [1]. Thus, the question of how to achieve effective co ordination to support business diversification has attracted the attention of scholars from a range of disciplines.

IS literature suggests that information technology (IT) may help firms tackle this increased complexity by improving the efficiency of information flow and facilitating both internal and external coordina tion [5]. Firms with more IT have greater abilities to coordinate re sources [6], and this is crucial for successful business diversification [7]. Prior studies of the effects of IT on business diversification have pro vided useful insights [5], with most of them examining the influence from an aggregate IT perspective. However, according to IT affordance theory, different types of IT have distinctive functions, leading to different impacts. As such, the effects of diverse types of IT on business diversification can vary. Empirical findings from an aggregate IT perspective may not provide concrete suggestions for a firm’s IT investment, as the firm’s limited resources will affect its choice among the available types of IT. Therefore, it is necessary to examine the effects of IT on business diversification at a more granular level.

Business diversification can be classified into service diversification (SD) and product diversification [2], and a large amount of literature has investigated unspecified business diversification (e.g [8]) or product diversification (e.g [9]). The relatively small number of studies devoted to SD contrasts sharply with economic reality, as 70% of many advanced economies’ gross domestic product is generated by the services sector [10]. Previous studies have debated whether the successful drivers of product innovation are the same as those of service innovation [11], given that the characteristics of new services are different from those of products in terms of “intangibility, co-production with customers, simultaneity, heterogeneity and perishability” [12]. Compared with product diversification, SD involves internal administration and coor dination among departments that is more complex, and more external communication with customers and suppliers [13]. In particular, firms need to coordinate more information and knowledge resources when offering new services [14]. Therefore, in this paper, we concentrate on the influences of IT on SD.

According to the institution-based view (IBV), the social institutional environment also influences a firm’s strategic choices [15]. Previous studies have confirmed that the external macro environments in different cities have unequal effects on local firms’ strategies [16], and that support gained from the environment affects firms’ productivity and innovation engagement [17], which are crucial to SD. Therefore, we expect that the macro environment will also influence a firm’s SD.

In addition to its direct impact, we argue that the macro environment may play a moderating role that affects the influence of IT on the SD of a firm. Specifically, a city with a better innovation environment can provide firms with better IT infrastructure [18], better IT human capital [19], and greater IT knowledge resources [20]. For example, firms with better IT human capital can implement customized IT for an existing business more efficiently [18]. In addition, in a more innovative envi ronment, firms may receive greater government support in the form of information programs and training opportunities that facilitate their use of IT. Thus, it is reasonable to investigate whether the effects of IT on SD are moderated by the external macro environment. A multilevel perspective is likely to provide a more comprehensive picture of SD. However, to date, little is known about the interactive effects of the macro environment with IT on SD. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to fill these gaps by investigating the following questions:

RQ1. How does the use of different types of IT affect firms’ SD?

RQ2. How does the macro environment affect firms’ SD and the re lationships between different types of IT and SD?

To answer these questions, we propose a cross-level conceptual research model based on the technology–organization–environment (TOE) framework. This theoretical framework has been used extensively in the IS field to study the influences of technological, organizational, and environmental contexts on firms’ decision making. Therefore, the TOE framework can help us explore the effects of IT and the macro environment on firms’ SD. In addition, IT affordance theory, which ar gues that different types of IT have different effects because of their distinctive features, is adopted here as the theoretical lens through which to explain the different effects of specific types of IT.

Our study focuses on the logistics industry, where firms offer intan gible third-party logistics services rather than selling tangible products. Because of the rapid global development of e-commerce, logistics firms must provide a full range of integrated services to meet a wide range of demands [21]. SD is thus viewed as an important organizational strategy for logistics firms to remain competitive. In this study, based on semi-structured interviews with industry experts (see Appendix A), we identified four types of IT that are widely used in the logistics industry to help firms with distinct capabilities to address challenges with the implementation of SD. We refer to these types of IT as enterprise systems (ES) for internal coordination capability, logistics information platforms (LIPs) for external coordination capability, the Internet of Things (IoT) for data acquisition capability, and big data analytics (BDA) for data analytics capability. As such, we investigate the effects of the four types of IT on a firm’s SD. We also take into account the important macro environmental factor of city innovation environment (CIE), which has been found to have a substantial effect on firms’ productivity and creativity [17,22]. Our cross-level model is proposed and tested using data from 690 logistics firms in 23 major Chinese cities, and the results show that the four types of IT are all positively related to SD and that CIE positively affects SD. Surprisingly, the interactive effects of CIE with both IoT and BDA on SD are significantly negative.

This paper makes several important theoretical contributions. First, we expand the literature on the relationship between IT and business diversification by exploring the different effects of four types of IT. In addition, owing to the different characteristics between tangible prod ucts and intangible services, we enhance the business diversification literature by investigating the effects of the four types of IT on SD. Second, we enrich the TOE framework by adopting a multilevel perspective on the environmental context and exploring the interactions of technological and environmental contexts. Finally, this study con tributes to the innovation environment literature. We identify the double-edged effects of CIE on firms, and specifically its positive direct effect on SD and its negative moderating effects on the influence of IoT and BDA on SD.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Service diversification

Diversification is a firm’s strategy for expanding its scope along with its business or geographical dimensions [5], referred to as business diversification and geographical diversification, respectively. Business diversification includes both product diversification and SD, depending on the firm’s business type [2]. SD can be defined as the extent to which a firm operates in multiple lines of service business.

Previous strategy studies suggest that there is a positive relationship between SD and a firm’s performance. First, service-diversified firms benefit from enhanced market power, such as the adoption of predatory strategies or the establishment of favorable reciprocal arrangements with other firms [23]. Second, SD helps firms reduce capital costs [24] and offers insurance for investors [25]. By expanding their business portfolio, firms can also make better use of resources and capabilities, thereby benefiting from economies of scope [3,4]. Third, diversified firms enjoy tax advantages by transforming inter-firm transactions into intra-firm transactions [26]. Fourth, SD yields a portfolio effect, which enhances firms’ risk-enduring abilities [27] and increases their market valuation [28]. Lastly, expanding the total package of services they offer helps firms to attract new clients and serve existing clients better through bundled services [29]. Therefore, it is important for practi tioners and academics to understand how firms can carry out successfu SD.

## 2.2. Antecedents of service diversification

## 2.2.1. Information technology and service diversification

A stream of studies has explored the influence of IT on firms' business diversification. Business diversification is positively related to IT in vestments [30,31], because it requires coordination and control of various resources, such as proprietary technologies, market information, management expertise, and specialized assets [14]. In addition, firms with business diversification have a higher demand for IT [5,32], which serves as an effective coordination and control mechanism at moderate levels of business diversification. Similarly, IT improves the perfor mance of business-diversified firms by reducing the cost of coordinating business resources [9,33]. Ray et al. [8] found that IT capital moderates the relationship between a firm’s assets and business diversification. Leon ´ et al. [34] identified a positive relationship between information communication technology and business diversification for small and medium enterprises. Gomez ´ et al. [35] observed that business diversi fication leads to greater internal coordination costs, thereby creating incentives to enhance IT capabilities.

However, most studies have focused either on product diversification or on unspecified aggregate business diversification (as summarized in Table 1), and understanding of the relationship between IT and SD is limited and inconsistent. Notably, a subsample analysis conducted by Shin [9] indicated that IT has a positive impact on business diversifi cation for manufacturing firms only, and not for service firms. Liu [5] claimed that SD incurs greater IT demand than product diversification, as IT has more influence on coordination and control activities in service firms. Given this lack of consensus on the impact of IT on SD, it is worth examining SD separately.

In addition, although previous studies have explored the effects of a firm’s aggregate IT on its business diversification (e.g. [5,32]), ignoring the different effects of specific types of IT may constrain our under standing and limit our capability to provide concrete suggestions for firms’ IT investment strategies. Therefore, it is also vital to investigate the effects of specific types of IT on SD.

## 2.2.2. External environment and service diversification

Previous studies have examined the direct impact of the external macro environment on SD [36,37]. The IBV, a relatively new perspec tive, explains that the national institutional environment may signifi cantly influence a firm’s strategic choices [15], including its business diversification strategies, in different respects. Using data from six Western European countries, Wan and Hoskisson [2] found that the munificence of the home country environment is an important compo nent in the study of business diversification strategies. Boschma and Capone [38] found that firms in liberal market economies are more likely to move into new industries. In addition, the macro competitive environment has been observed to influence product diversification directly, in that macroeconomic growth positively affects product diversification, whereas foreign competition reduces product diversifi cation [39].

Compared with the large number of studies on the direct effect of the macro environment on business diversification, there have been few anecdotal studies of its crucial moderating role on the relationship be tween the antecedents of business diversification and business diversi fication itself. For example, Li et al. [36] found that the positive relationship between political connections and business diversification is moderated by the level of regional institutional development. More over, the literature on the business value of IT suggests that the external environment moderates the effects of IT on firms (e.g [40,41]). For instance, Chen et al. [42] found that environmental hostility weakens the effect of IT on business process agility.

However, to our knowledge, very few empirical studies have exam ined the moderating effect of the external macro environment on the relationship between IT and SD. Therefore, the interactive effect of the macro environment with IT on SD requires further investigation. In response to this call [43], our study extends the literature by investi gating not only a direct effect, but also a moderating effect of the macro environment on the relationship between IT and SD.

## 2.3. IT affordance theory

IT affordance theory suggests that different technologies have different effects because of their distinctive features [44]. In the field of IS, affordance, sometimes referred to as functional affordance, repre sents the potential for goal-oriented action afforded to a specific user or community [45]. In other words, IT affordance can be understood as the capability of the technology as much as the choices of individuals or organizations about how to use that capability [46]. Previous IS studies have used IT affordance theory as a theoretical lens to facilitate our understanding of the relationship between IT and organizational prac tices [47]. According to IT affordance theory, potential goals are determined by IT features [48]. In the present study, based on five in-depth interviews with industry experts, we propose that the four types of widely used IT in the logistics industry, ES, LIP, IoT, and BDA, afford firms’ various capabilities (as illustrated in Fig. 1). Some exem plary quotes from the expert interviews are provided in Appendix B.

First, ES refers to a group of large-scale enterprise software programs used to support business processes and information flows within orga nizations. Such systems afford logistics firms with improved internal information coordination capability [49] by managing a large amount of transactional data within the firm. For example, a warehouse manage ment system (WMS) helps firms manage daily operations in the ware house and optimizes inventory decisions. Second, LIP refers to platform technologies integrating various functions that provide logistics firms with greater external coordination capability. LIP can enable a firm to integrate its supply chain by facilitating transactional data exchange between organizations, which involves both manufacturers/distributors and logistics providers [50]. Third, IoT refers to IT techniques that help firms establish an extensive network of interrelated computing devices, and mechanical and digital machines [51]. These technologies afford logistics firms with data acquisition capability by enabling automatic, real-time data collection; for example, when a radio-frequency identi fication (RFID) tag item passes through an RFID reader, firms can automatically collect product information. Fourth, BDA refers to any IT technique that helps firms analyze and extract insights from large-scale data (i.e. big data) and thus affords the focal firms with data analytics capabilities [52]. For example, logistics firms apply data mining tech niques to analyze extensive data on transition points, moving times, and loading docks to achieve improved hub terminal capacity. In sum, four types of IT work together to increase firms’ data acquisition and ana lytics capability, and their internal and external resource coordination capability, thereby significantly reducing the complexity of imple menting SD.

In addition, it is noted that each type of IT consists of different IT techniques, and that some IT techniques in different IT types may be used together (e.g. IoT-enabled ES). However, as we focus on IT-afforded capabilities, such overlaps in usage do not undermine the main argu ments made in this article. Taking a specific example to illustrate, the WMS (which belongs to the ES) is sometimes integrated with RFID (which belongs to the IoT), and at other times is used independently. Considering these situations, the RFID-enabled WMS enhances both internal coordination capability and data acquisition capability, while the WMS without RFID enhances only internal coordination capability.

## 2.4. The technology–organization–environment framework

The TOE framework conceptualizes the factors influencing firms strategic decisions in three categories: technological, organizational, and environmental contexts. The technological context includes the technology-related factors that influence firms’ decision making and the business value of IT [53]. The organizational context is the influence of a firm’s profile characteristics, such as its size and scope [54]. The envi ronmental context is the significant influence on a firm's decisions of external factors beyond its control. It is worth noting that the TOE framework does not identify specific factors, as these should be deter mined according to the research setting and research questions.

In our study, the technological context includes four types of IT. To capture the influence of the environmental context, we use the CIE, which represents local government support for innovation. In a more innovative city environment, logistics firms have access to more external resources and financial support to improve their coordination capability and to achieve SD. Firm size and firm scope are taken as the organiza tional context, following the approach adopted by many previous TOE studies (e.g. [54]). Thus, the TOE framework provides a solid but adaptable theoretical ground on which to develop our conceptual model.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

Drawing on the TOE framework and IT affordance theory, we propose a cross-level research model to investigate how four types of IT influence SD, taking into account the innovation environment (as shown in Fig. 2). The model includes both firm and city levels. At the firm level, four types of IT (ES, LIP, IoT, and BDA) are hypothesized to influence logistics firms’ SD. The relationships between SD and organizational contexts, specifically firm size and firm scope, are also developed. At the city level, one important environmental factor, CIE, is considered. It is hypothesized that CIE enhances SD and strengthens the relationship between IT use and SD.

## 3.1. Technological context and service diversification

## 3.1.1. Enterprise systems

ES comprises a group of large-scale integrated packaged systems with embedded industry best practices that enables a firm to efficiently integrate and process a large amount of internal information, thereby improving its internal process capability [55]. For example, enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems facilitate integrated and real-time planning of internal resources [56]. WMS can save product informa tion automatically and continuously, thereby increasing operational efficiency [57]. In short, ES enhances managerial capabilities by facili tating internal information transparency [58] and coordination [59]. When SD creates internal coordination requirements, such as sharing resources and processing information [7,30], ES improves a firm’s abilities to meet these demands by coordinating its internal resources, which helps the firm to achieve SD. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1. The use of ES is positively related to logistics firms’ SD.

## 3.1.2. Logistics information platform

LIP improves a firm’s external information integration process through its ability to connect with other parties [60]. It makes it easier for logistics firms to cooperate with suppliers [61], thereby reducing the costs of activities with their supply chain partners. With fewer costs and better external coordination ability, firms are in a better position to achieve SD. Specifically, LIP provides multiple functions to integrate cross-functional applications, create connectivity across a range of complementary uses, and facilitate the exchange of supplementary in formation between logistics firms [55]. As a result, LIP enables logistics firms to promote their current business or provide new services [62], and improves the experience of both customers and suppliers, thereby enhancing product adoption and diffusion [63]. Thus, increased use of LIP is expected to be positively related to the likelihood of successful SD of logistics firms. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2. The use of LIP is positively related to logistics firms’ SD.

## 3.1.3. Internet of things

IoT benefits firms’ SD by facilitating accurate, real-time supply chain visibility, agility, and adaptability [64]. Prior studies suggest that IoT technologies can directly improve the design of new products and ser vices [65]. Here, we posit that IoT provides logistics firms with several advantages to enhance SD. First, IoT technologies enhance firms’ effi cient use of resources, increase inventory efficiency, and reduce shrinkage and misplacement by tracking inventory and sharing infor mation [66]; such an efficient operation process helps firms tackle the complexity associated with SD. Second, IoT technologies improve firms environmental adaptability by extracting data from the environment [67], allowing market information to be collected and integrated simultaneously. With such information, firms can not only improve their current services but also provide new services to meet customer needs [68]. Thanks to these benefits, IoT enhances firms’ data acquisition capability and is expected to contribute to SD. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. The use of IoT is positively related to logistics firms’ SD.

## 3.1.4. Big data analytics

BDA allows organizations to exploit and anticipate emerging busi ness opportunities [52]. This enables firms to be proactive and forward-looking in the market and to stay one step ahead of competitors by providing new services. Previous studies document that the analysis and deployment of big data enables firms to gain insights into customer requirements, engage with customers on an appropriate level, and generate financial benefits [69], all of which are fundamental for SD. In addition, the use of organizational big data allows firms to extract business intelligence from petabyte-scale data almost in real time [70]. These BDA-enabled data analytics capabilities make it possible for firms to coordinate with customers more efficiently, which helps them to develop new services to meet customers’ needs. In summary, BDA can help firms to find a promising new market while reducing entry costs. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4. The use of BDA is positively related to logistics firms SD.

## 3.2. Environmental context

According to the IBV, the macro environment may significantly influence a focal firm [36,37], and a better macro environment can provide easier access to external resources. As CIE is positively related to local government support, it can positively impact firms’ SD. For example, government funding for research and development (R&D) improves firms’ productivity and en courages long-term engagement in R&D [17]. Furthermore, government financial support enhances collaboration between firms and research institutes and positively influences innovation outcomes [22]. Likewise,

In addition to the direct effect of the macro environment on SD, previous studies have shown that a hostile domestic environment moderates the impact of a firm’s attributes on SD [72]. Thus, we posit that CIE plays a moderating role in the relationship between IT use and SD. An environment that supports innovation has been identified as a moderator of many antecedents of innovation behavior at the firm level; such an environment will influence practices, procedures, policies, and the ways things are done [73], which is a prerequisite for SD. At the macro level, the literature also suggests that the external macro envi ronment may have an interaction effect with IT on firms (e.g. [18]). Thus, we believe that such an effect can be generalized to the relation ship between IT and SD.

CIE has a supportive effect mainly because it offers firms broader infrastructure [18], better human capital [19], and greater knowledge [20]. When firms have more resources, they can make better use of the capabilities afforded by IT and achieve more SD. For example, custom izing standard IT offerings and adapting them to existing businesses is a complex and challenging process, and firms with better human capital can complete this process more efficiently [18]. Similarly, firms in a better innovation environment receive more government support, which facilitates their use of IT to generate value; as a result, the IT tends to have a stronger reinforcing effect. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 6. CIE positively moderates the relationship between (a) ES, (b) LIP, (c) IoT, (d) BDA, and SD.

## 3.3. Organizational context

Previous studies have identified several relationships between organizational factors and business diversification [74,75]. In studies of innovation, firm size is one of the most widely used organizational measures, and is critical to firm performance [53,76]. Larger firms have more knowledge and financial and human resources, which are posi tively related to business diversification [74]. Therefore, we expect a positive relationship between firm size and SD.

The firm scope is another organizational factor that may influence firms’ decisions. Unlike previous studies that view firm scope as the geographical extent of the business [53,54], we consider a firm’s scope in terms of its market variety. In a niche market, third-party logistic firms usually have different resources and IT requirements. For example, transportation of perishable goods needs cold chain technology, while transportation of medical materials requires high sanitary standards. As such, the more industries a firm operates in, the greater its capabilities for coordinating resources and dealing with complexity. Such capabil ities are important for achieving SD. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 7. Firm size is positively related to logistics firms’ SD.

Hypothesis 8. Firm scope is positively related to logistics firms’ SD.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

City-level environment data and firm-level data were collected in 23 major Chinese cities, including four municipalities and 19 prefecturelevel cities. We used a range of sources, including self-report survey and public data. Macro environment data were collected from the offi cial annual report published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, and firm-level data were collected using a questionnaire. The survey used quota sampling, which is a standard method of ensuring the same number of respondents in each city [77,78]. An online survey was designed, and a professional survey company carried out the data collection. Logistics firms were first contacted by telephone to confirm their willingness to take part in the research; 1,447 firms agreed to participate. Then, an online link to the questionnaire was sent to the appropriate respondents in each firm. The survey company imple mented strict quality control during the data collection process, and after completion of the questionnaire, follow-up phone calls were made to ensure that each respondent had taken the survey seriously. In addition, a second round of data collection was carried out for those who did not reply in the first round.

## 4.2. Construct operationalization

As indicated in the literature, content validity assesses whether an instrument adequately and exhaustively contains all the items necessary to represent the concept being measured [80]. In order to establish content validity, we created a panel of three experts, all of whom are specialists in both supply chains and IS, and asked them to rate the relevance of each question/item in the questionnaire on a scale of 1–4. The proportion agreement method known as the content validity index is used to estimate content validity [81]. This index is computed as the number of content valid experts who assign a rating of either 3 (quite relevant) or 4 (highly relevant) divided by the total number of experts [82]. In this case, the value of the index was greater than 0.9, which supports the content validity of our questionnaire.

Here, we summarize the operationalization of the constructs (Ap pendix C provides details of the measures used). The measures of SD are based on the business count approach, a method widely used to measure levels of business diversification [83]. Participants were asked to state whether their firms provided certain types of services, and the total number of services provided by a firm was used as the value of SD. Note that, in the strategic management literature, most studies use the en tropy measure or Herfindahl index to measure SD. However, these measures require information about the market share (in terms of sales value) of the products/services offered by the firm. Such measures are suitable for public firms that are obliged to publish details of their financial situation; however, the samples in our study are mostly private firms whose financial data are confidential. Thus, the entropy measure was not appropriate for this research.

The measures of the four types of IT are based on the characteristics of each technology. Following Trainor et al. [84], an index was devel oped for each technology. Respondents were presented with a list of modules (functions) for each technology and asked to indicate whether their firms use the corresponding technologies. We aggregated a single score for the number of modules used to measure the use of the tech nology, in line with the approach used in previous research (e.g. [85]).

ES use was measured according to how many different ES are used in a firm. The more systems used, the greater the internal information integration. For example, a WMS facilitates warehouse information integration and a transportation management system (TMS) facilitates transportation information integration within organizations. LIP use was measured by the number of functions used by a firm on the LIP to coordinate with its supply chain partners. As a logistics platform always integrates multiple functions, the use of LIP can be measured from a functional perspective [86]: The more functions that are used in the LIP, the stronger the firm’s external information capability. IoT use was calculated as the number of IoT technologies a firm has. Firms with more IoT facilities have stronger data acquisition capability. BDA use was captured by the number of data source types used by a firm. Under the BDA architecture [87], data sources are critical for a firm’s ability to generate insights in different fields. The more types of data a firm ana lyzes, the higher its data analytics capability.

Concerning the measure of CIE, previous studies suggest R&D ex penses as a proxy for innovation input [88]. In this regard, dos Santos Ferreira [89] used the government’s regional R&D expenses as a proxy for the regional innovation environment. We therefore used the R&D expenses of the local government to measure CIE. The unit is RMB 100 million (roughly equivalent to USD 14.02 million). Finally, for the two organizational factors, we use operating income as a proxy for firm size, and the measure of firm scope is defined as the number of industries in which a firm operates.

Table 3 shows the descriptive statistics and correlation matrix for all the variables in our study. The correlations between each variable are below 0.7, which suggests that common method bias is not a major concern [90]. Moreover, the variance inflation factors among variables are all below 2.5, which indicates no serious multicollinearity issues.

## 6. Discussion

Drawing on the TOE framework and IT affordance theory, this paper used a cross-level model to investigate the effects of specific IT use on SD, taking into account the multilevel effects of CIE. Most of our hy potheses are supported, and our results confirm the positive effects of IT use on SD. Four types of IT (ES, LIP, IoT, and BDA) that are widely used in the logistics industry all have positive and significant effects on SD.

Our results also confirm that CIE is a crucial predictor of a firm’s SD. However, it is surprising that CIE has different moderating effects on the relationship between use of specific types of IT and SD. The interactive effects of CIE with ES and LIP on SD are not significant; however, CIE negatively moderates the relationships of IoT and BDA with SD. We suggest that this difference can be explained in terms of technology life cycles, according to which technologies differ in development time and costs, cost recovery schedules, and methods of generating profits pro portional to the costs and risks involved. Compared with mature tech nologies, such as ES and LIP, IoT and BDA are relatively new to the logistics industry.

CIE can heterogeneously moderate the effect of mature technologie and emerging technologies for two reasons. On one hand, the rareness advantage of different technologies is in play. Emerging types of IT (e.g. IoT and BDA) have a greater rareness advantage in a low-innovation environment than in a high-innovation environment. In a low-macro innovation environment, there is less competition among firms involving emerging technologies, as few firms have adopted those technologies. Therefore, in cities with low levels of innovation, the rareness of emerging technologies generates an extra advantage for those who adopt them. However, ES and LIP have been more thoroughly developed, and any firm can take advantage of these mature technolo gies. This leads to the insignificant moderation effect of the innovation environment on such techniques.

On the other hand, the different moderating effects of CIE may be caused by different learning processes influenced by the availability of external knowledge support. In a low-innovation environment, firms have little access to outside knowledge support, and they must learn for themselves how to use emerging technologies. Such self-directed learning can benefit these firms compared with firms in a highinnovation environment that rely on existing external knowledge [96]. This leads to more significant advantages in using emerging technology in a low-innovation environment. Mature technologies, in contrast, are highly familiar to users, and firms using them do not need to rely on outside knowledge support. Therefore, with mature technologies, there is no difference in benefit across innovation environments.

In addition to the perspective provided by technology life cycles, the effects of different capabilities afforded by the four types of IT may also be heterogeneous in different innovation environments. As illustrated in Fig. 1, BDA and IoT afford data-oriented capabilities, whereas ES and LIP afford coordination-oriented capabilities. With data-oriented capa bilities, firms in a low-innovation environment can better understand their customers for the purposes of diversifying their services, because the market is relatively small compared with a high-innovation envi ronment. Specifically, given the same level of BDA and IoT use by firms (the same level of data-oriented capabilities), firms in a low-innovation environment can acquire and analyze data from a larger proportion of the local market and customers, which may help them to achieve SD. However, regardless of the innovation environment, coordinationoriented capabilities are crucial for firms seeking to integrate both in ternal and external resources, and this explains the insignificant effect of CIE on the relationships between ES and LIP and SD.

It is also interesting to find differences between self-operated logis tics firms and logistics agency firms in the additional analysis. In addi tion to those findings that are consistent with the main study, we see that BDA leads to SD directly for logistics agency firms but not for selfoperated logistics firms. One explanation for this finding lies in the distinctive characteristics of the two types of firms; logistics agency firms are more likely to be related to customers directly, a situation in which better data analytics capability can help them achieve SD. In addition, the results of the additional analysis show that CIE is more important for self-operated logistics firms than for logistics agency firms. We posit that, because logistics agency firms with fewer fixed assets often provide services across a wide geographical area, the local inno vation environment where the firm is based may not have a strong in fluence. On the other hand, self-operated logistics firms tend to provide geographically concentrated services, thus relying more on support from the local government.

Lastly, it is surprising to find in the additional analysis that CIE has a negative interactive effect with firm scope on SD. Although this result is consistent with previous findings that the effects of firm characteristics on firm performance depend on the institutional environment [97], the negative interactive effect indicates that the firm scope in a low CIE will generate greater SD. As with the self-learning explanations proposed above for the negative interactive effects of CIE with IoT and BDA, we suggest that in low CIE with less outside support, a firm operates in more industries and expends more effort. This may cause the firm to develop greater capabilities for achieving SD, thereby leading to more significant advantages of firm scope in a low-innovation environment.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

Our study has several important theoretical implications. First, and most importantly, it expands the literature on the relationship between

IT and SD. Although previous studies have investigated this relationship and provided useful insights, most have adopted an aggregate IT perspective [5,32]. A lack of research on the effects of specific IT types has made it difficult to decide how firms can strategically allocate re sources to specific types of IT, given that resources are always limited. Furthermore, most previous studies on the effects of IT on SD have taken a single-level perspective, which cannot adequately depict the situation of a firm within the macro environment. Our study contributes to this line of research by examining the effects of specific types of IT while taking the macro environment into account. It is thus among the first to investigate the interactive effects of different types of IT and the macro environment on SD from a multilevel perspective.

Second, this study contributes to business diversification research by focusing on SD. Previous studies have reported positive effects of IT on product diversification or unspecified aggregate business diversifica tion. However, providing services is often more complicated than selling products, not least because services rely more on information and knowledge [14] and are consequently more IT-enabled and digital-based [98]. Therefore, it is necessary to differentiate SD from product diversification when investigating the effects of IT on SD. Our paper fills this gap by confirming the positive relationship between IT and SD.

Third, our study contributes to the TOE framework by adopting a multilevel perspective on environmental context and exploring the in teractions of technological and environmental contexts. Most TOE studies have investigated technology, organization, and environment independently. However, the significant interactive effects of two technologies (IoT and BDA) with CIE suggest that the technological and environmental contexts of TOE are not independent. Furthermore, the results of our additional analysis suggest that environmental factors have interactive effects with organizational context factors, which further supports our claim that the constructs within the TOE framework are not independent. This indicates that future studies should explore the interactive effects among technological, environmental, and orga nizational contexts when they adopt the TOE framework as a theoretical foundation. In this way, our study enriches the TOE framework and identifies avenues for future research.

In addition, this study contributes to the literature on innovation environment. Previous studies have mainly documented a positive effect of the innovation environment on firms, while a few have identified a negative effect. This study is pioneering in its identification of the double-edged effects of CIE on firms’ SD, showing that, in addition to its positive direct effect on SD, CIE has an indirect negative effect on the transmission of emerging technologies to firms’ SD. This extends our understanding by revealing the side effects of CIE and opening up ave nues for future research in this area.

Finally, the differences between self-operated logistics firms and logistics agency providers revealed in the additional analysis represent another contribution of this study. This finding suggests that the rela tionship between specific IT and SD is heterogeneous for these two types of firms. It is likely that this is a result of the differences between self operated logistics firms and logistics agency firms, and it reflects the comparatively low level of importance placed by such characteristics on BDA and CIE. Therefore, future studies focusing on the logistics industry should take these differences into account and seek to identify further differences between the two types of firms.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Our study has practical implications for managers and policymakers. Focusing on the logistics industry, our results confirm the positive effects of multiple types of IT; therefore, firms can achieve SD by increasing their use of different techniques. Moreover, the moderating influence of CIE on the relationship between IT use and SD suggests that managers pursuing SD should simultaneously consider the macro environment and the type of IT used. For emerging technologies, such as IoT and BDA, in particular, the negative interactive effects of CIE on their impact on SD suggest that firms in lower-innovation cities could benefit more from emerging technologies. These firms may be reluctant to adopt emerging technologies because of the lack of outside technological and financial support, which can lead to higher costs. However, we strongly encourage such firms to invest in emerging technologies, as they will achieve a greater advantage in pursuing SD in a low-innovation envi ronment. In addition, firms in a low-innovation environment are advised to expand their market variety to serve more industries. In doing so, they are more likely to improve their capabilities, thereby enhancing thei SD.

The results of our additional analysis indicate that managers of selfoperated logistics firms should prioritize investment in ES, LIP, and IoT if resources are limited. However, we posit that the effect of BDA on SD is still important, as the correlation between a firm’s BDA usage and SD is significantly positive $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 5 , \rho < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Therefore, if self-operated logistics firms have sufficient resources, managers are still advised to invest further in these areas.

Policymakers should note that the negative moderating effect must not reduce government motivation to improve the innovation environ ment of cities. As the main effect of the environment on SD is positive, the government should continue to improve the innovation environment for firms. The negative impact of the innovation environment on the relationship between emerging technologies and SD requires policy makers to think about improving the innovation environment without losing the benefits of using technology. For example, firms could be supported to learn about technology for themselves, rather than the government simply providing them with knowledge. This would allow firms in high-innovation environments also to benefit from the learning process.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations that should be addressed in future work. First, our research is based on data from a single industry, the logistics industry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, although the services provided by these firms may differ from those of other industries, the challenges of SD are the same for different types of firms, and we expect that our findings on the positive effects of IT use on SD and the influence of the macro innovation environment can be applied to other service-based industries. Future studies should examine whether our findings hold in different industry settings.

Second, although the types of IT examined in our study are positively related to the SD of logistics firms, they may be more widely used in the logistics industry than in other industries. Therefore, future research into the effects of specific types of IT in other sectors should consider other forms of IT. Similarly, future studies should explore whether other types of IT used in the logistics industry affect a firm’s SD. For example, in the interviews we conducted, one of the industry experts noted that some logistics firms use automation technologies, such as warehouse robots and self-guided vehicles. Our sample of 690 firms included only four that use warehouse robots and 20 that use self-guided vehicles, which suggests that this type of IT has not yet been widely adopted. However, as automation technologies continue to mature, we strongly suggest that future studies explore their effects on SD.

Third, as our study finds that the CIE has varying effects, future studies should aim to identify the optimal levels for CIEs, thus providing more accurate guidelines for government and practitioners. Because our results are based on data from a sample of Chinese firms, their gener alizability to other countries or cultures may be limited. and future studies should take cultural differences into account.

Finally, this study is based on a cross-sectional dataset. Although it provides insights into the relationship between specific IT use and SD, it does not examine the long-term effects of technologies. Therefore, future studies should collect longitudinal panel data to explore this topic further.
