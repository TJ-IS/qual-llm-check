# The path to people’s responses to native advertising in social media: A perspective of self-presentational desire

![](/api/attachments/3SVBSUS9/fulltext/images/3ad6c57667fa5f33ae5d56c237cbbc3a3695986c30861fb18f18dd85f808c3a6.jpg)

Yanwu Yang , Ting (Lisa) Gao \*

School of Management, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Address: Luoyu Road, Wuhan, 430074, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Advertising responses Advertising acceptance Advertising engagement Self-Presentational desire Native advertising

## A B S T R A C T

Recently, anecdotal evidence has suggested that native advertising in social media can be considered another important arena for people to build desired self-presentational images and online identities. Thus, it calls for an empirical analysis on people’s responses to native advertising from a perspective of self-presentational desire (SPD). This study proposes a conceptual model to examine the role of SPD on people’s responses to native advertising, i.e., advertising acceptance (AC) and advertising engagement (AE), in social media. The model was validated using survey data collected from a large social community. The results indicate that SPD is a crucial determinant of native advertising responses. Moreover. AC partially mediates the relationship between SPD and AE. This work contributes to our understanding of advertising responses by extending the theory of selfpresentation to the context of native advertising and generates several critical managerial insights for socia media providers and advertisers.

## 1. Introduction

In the past decade, social media has become an important channel of advertising communications with potential consumers for global and local businesses [1]. Advertisers increasingly dispense a large share of their online marketing dollars toward social media advertising. Ac cording to Interactive Advertising Bureau (2019), Internet advertising revenues of U.S. hit a record high of \$107.5 billion in 2018, where social media advertising grew 31% from 2017 and accounted for 27% of that pie. Facebook gained 98.5% of its total revenue from social media advertising in 2018 [2]. By 2021, social media advertising revenues will surpass \$30 billion in the U.S. alone, according to the estimates from Business Insider Intelligence, based on the historical data from the Interactive Advertising Bureau and PwC, as well as IHS [3].

In recent years, native advertising has flourished on popular social media platforms. Native advertising is a newly created advertising form, which is identical to original contents (i.e., posts by friends) with respect to format and style [4,5], and allows people to perform a variety of actions (e.g., likes, comments and shares) visible publically or within a circle of friends [6]. Fig. 1 presents several illustrations of native advertising on Facebook, Instagram, and WeChat. For example, from the “Chic Me” advertisement (shown in the left picture of Fig. 1), the recipient can observe how many times and who liked, commented and shared the ad; meanwhile, Facebook encourages her to participate in the social interactions around this advertisement. Social media platforms deliver native advertising in a customized way. Hence, for an individual user, native advertisements she receives reflect the label that social media platforms put on her. As a result, in order to construct and maintain desired online identities, social media users are more willing to receive and have strong incentives to engage with native advertisements and participate in interactions among them over advertising topics. The following quotes posted on major social media forums succinctly portray such thinking:

I would like to receive the XXX ad in my Facebook feeds. I will share and comment on the XXX ad in my Facebook feeds, then my friends will know I am also in the zone.

Some of my Facebook friends even posted screenshots of the XXX ad they received.

In the extant literature on advertising responses as shown in Table 1, researchers have attempted to explain people’s responses (e.g., the willingness to accept, attitudes, and engaging behaviors) to advertising by identifying potential antecedents: (1) consumers’ motives (e.g., [7,9, 10]) and psychological characteristics (e.g., [11,12]), (2) advertising perceptions including perceived informativeness (e.g., [9,13,14]), perceived entertainment (e.g., [15,16]), perceived ease of use (e.g., [17]), perceived intrusiveness and obtrusiveness (e.g., [18,31]), perceived relevance (e.g., [19–21]), perceived value (e.g., [22]), etc., (3) advertising content including ad creativity (e.g., [23]), personali zation (e.g., [24]), persuasiveness (e.g.,[26]), etc., (4) social norms (e.g., [27]), (5) advertiser trust (e.g., [28,29]), and (6) media interactions (e. g., [16,30]). These prior studies provide valuable knowledge for un derstanding advertising responses in various settings. However, the above-mentioned phenomenon associated with people’s responses to native advertising in social media characterizes a distinct motive that closely relates to personal image building and impression development. Hereafter, we call it self-presentational desire (SPD for short) in the rest of this paper. SPD might implicitly interrelate with the socializing motive [7,9], in that it comes into play in the context of social connec tions. However, there is a great line in between SPD and socializing motive. More specifically, social connection motive describes people’s needs to connect, interact, and share with their friends, which is essentially orthogonal to the desire of self-presentation because the latter is concerned with how they may look or what others might think, believe, and feel about them. Altogether, theories employed by prior studies on advertising responses fail to explain the above phenomenon. The present study is intended to fill this gap by examining the potential effect of SPD on people’s responses to native advertising in social media. To the best of our knowledge, relatively less effort has been made in this direction.

In this study, we propose a conceptual model examining whether SPD is a major driver for people’s responses to native advertising, i.e., advertising acceptance (AC) and advertising engagement (AE), in social media. In particular, AC is referred to the strong willingness to accept and positive attitudes toward advertisements [10,32]; and AE represents behavioral intentions and interactions with advertisements [9]. Although prior research has investigated the effects of SPD on commitment and trust in online games [33], electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) [8], and intentions to purchase digital items in virtual com munities [34], no study we are aware of has established and tested the path through SPD and its antecedents to advertising responses. More over, this research highlights that native advertising can be considered another important avenue for people to build self-presentational images and online identities. Note that there are generally various ways for people to fill their SPDs in a social media environment, such as posting messages and pictures, group discussing, and interacting with others around native advertisements. In this research, we exclusively focus on how native advertising can be used to fill people’s SPDs in the context of social media. Our conceptual model was tested on survey data collected from a large social group with hundreds of members with a special in terest on outdoor activities and travels. Our findings support the asser tion that SPD is a crucial determinant of people’s responses to native advertising in social media. These findings yield critical implications for both research and practice.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 pro vides theoretical background for this study. Section 3 develops our research model and hypotheses. Next, we describe research design in Section 4 and present results in Section 5. Finally, we discuss the major findings of this study and implications for research and practice, and conclude this work with potential avenues for future research in Section 6.

## 2. Theoretical background

We first introduce self-presentation theory (SPT) and its applications in social media advertising, which is the main theoretical foundation of this study. Then we describe uses and gratification theory (UGT), and constructs derived from social influence.

## 2.1. Self-presentation theory

Self-concept is one of the most popular concepts in social psychology. People desire to present their identities before concentrating on their goals or works that may bring them together [35]. In other words, effective social interactions among people are impossible to occur unless desired identities of their own have been constructed, which are observed and underscored by Goffman [36]. In general, self-identity can be used by people to specify who they are (i.e., distinctiveness) and to locate themselves relative to other people (i.e., sameness) [37]. In the physical (or offline) world, an identity can be described by a set of at tributes such as demographics (e.g., age and gender) and physically expressed factors (e.g., fabric clothes). Self-presentation theory [39], built on Goffman [36]’s theories of identity and social performance, explains how people want others to see them and the extent to which their own self-presentational images will influence others and are influenced by others. Self-presentation occur in a wide range of situations, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , in a small circle of friends, in mar riage, and in public, etc. People always have substantial concerns on their social images, which make self-presentation ubiquitous in daily lives. These social impressions are maintained through a process called impression management, i.e., consistently performing a series of goal-oriented coherent and complementary behaviors [40]. In effect, people can achieve beneficial results from self-presentational impres sions. Thus, in general, people strategically control how they are perceived by others [41,42], i.e., selectively providing information about themselves and carefully packaging this information, in order to construct and maintain desired social images and create a desired implication on others [43]. Nonetheless, self-presentation aims not to deceive or harm others through superficial, deceptive, or manipulative activities, but to interact and communicate with them effectively and adequately [44,45].

In the physical world, self-presentation happens along with in teractions among people. The Internet provides another venue for peo ple to express alternate selves. While in the cyber-space, an individual has to construct her identity and profile before building relationships with others. Indeed, self-presentation is the key prerequisite condition for connecting and interacting with others in social media environments. Besides, online social media sites are designed to encourage people to construct accurate self-presentations of them, although they perform to varying degrees [46]. For example, Facebook provides ample opportu nities for users to engage in self-presentation through a set of activities including posting photographs, profile construction, and messaging postings [47,48]. As observed by prior studies (e.g., [48,49]), possible and ideal self with hidden self-aspects might be presented in online so cial media sites. More specifically, in more anonymous cyber-spaces (e. $g _ { \cdot , }$ chat rooms), people tend to present ideal selves; however, on less anonymous platforms (e.g., dating sites), personality traits in their profiles usually reflect actual selves [50].

Although self-presentation and impression management can be governed by a variety of motives, the SPT posits two basic motives that are sometimes related to each other [34,45]. First, an individual can construct a personal identity and use it to associate with admirable or similar persons. Second, people are intended to accomplish interper sonal goals and gain rewards by influencing responses of others to themselves. As a matter of fact, both of the two motives are strengthened in social networking environments. The former concerns relationship development and social capital accumulation embedded in social re lationships [51]. That is, self-presentational image helps people find similar others; they thereby obtain a better social image that may make them look and feel better, by linking themselves to admirable or similar others. The latter is related to beneficial consequences of social capital embedded in social networks [52]. In other words, people can make others like them and obtain more or less influences on others to achieve their goals, by utilizing network positions they hold (i.e., centrality) [43].

## 2.2. Uses and gratification theory

Uses and gratification theory explains how people select and consume media content to satisfy a set of their needs [53]. The basic premise of the UGT is that people are aware of their needs and desires, and capable of seeking media and identifying their sources of satisfac tion that lead to the ultimate gratification.

The UGT has two important characteristics, compared to other media theories. First of all, the UGT is need-oriented, i.e., people’s (both util itarian and nonutilitarian) motives drive them to actively seek gratifications in selected media. In other words, there are intrinsic mo tives for selecting and using media content that go far beyond in strumentalities such as usefulness and availability [54]. Intrinsic motives refer to internal gratification, such as entertainment and socialization. This makes the UGT a valuable theoretical framework fo examining gratifications that social media provide to people [14]. Another vital characteristic of the UGT is that, it highlights that people control their media decisions, thus are not passive but active decision makers. In this sense, the UGT has a specific relevance to social media in that the latter in particular encourages active interactions among peo ple, as well as between people and media. Therefore, a widely extensive literature (e.g., [14,55,56]) has applied the UGT to explore how and the extent to which social media consumptions fulfill people’s various needs and desires. Among them, SPD is a major motive for people to use social media, as observed in prior studies (e.g., [42,47,48]).

## 2.3. Social influence

People’s behaviors have been identified as a major way to control self-presentation [39,57], which is conceptualized as social influence [58]. The social influence model focuses on how an individual interacts with social groups in a long-term relationship (i.e., socialization), where three processes are identified, namely, compliance, identification and internalization [58]. More specifically, compliance represents the adherence to rules or norms $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ , a set of behavioral requirements) of the social system; identification reflects an orientation to the role of social system member that is an important part of self-definition; and inter nalization reflects an orientation to values of the social system that the individual personally shares. In a social system, individuals (or mem bers) will live up to implications of shared values and maintain the integrity of their personal value framework, via this process of accepting influence. Kim et al. [34] argued that, in virtual communities, compli ance and internalization explain the importance of self-presentational norms, and identification is associated with social group involvement.

## 2.4. The linkage of this research to the state-of-the-art literature

In spite of a plethora of literature on people’s responses to social media advertising (e.g., [14,23,27,59–62]), as we show in Table 1, the potential effect of self-presentation has not been explored. Self-presentation is a major motive for people to adopt social media (e. g., Facebook) and the prerequisite condition for their relationships inception and development. Hence, self-presentation strongly affects people’s activities on social media [48,63,64] and personal information disclosure [65,66]. For instance, the desire for online self-presentation has a positive effect on eWOM [8,67] and also acts as a key motive for purchasing digital goods in virtual communities [34], as illustrated in Table 2. Although these studies provided valuable cues to our under standing of self-presentation and its subsequent outcomes, there is still to explore its role in people’s responses to native advertising in social media. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first effort in this direction.

## 3.1. Self-presentational desire and advertising acceptance

Desire is “trying to get”, according to action-based theories of desire [69]. That is, a desire is a matter that an individual has been disposed to act in order to fulfill it [70]. Concerning SPD, following Kim et al. [34], it is defined as “the extent to which an individual wants to present her or his preferred image” within a social group of interest or in a social media community. SPD is the focal explanatory construct in this study.

According to the Oxford English Dictionary, acceptance is referred to “the action of consenting to receive or undertake something offered”. In this study, following the extant literature (e.g., [24,30,71]), we define AC as the extent to which an individual is willing or consenting to receive and adopt advertisements or other forms of marketing commu nications related to native advertising delivered on a social media platform. In other words, AC represents a positive attitude toward advertising [10,32]. AC is a response construct we focus on in this study.

The SPT states that people are motived to present themselves in order to get desired or positive perception from others. People always have incentives to construct online images because positive self-presentation could enhance their happiness [47]. The need for self-presentation is an influential antecedent of social media adoptions [42,48]. Following the excitation transfer theory [72], people’s positive feelings obtained from social media and advertising engaging experience can be transferred to the presented advertisements. That is, people’ positive attitudes toward social media may lead to a positive attitude toward advertising delivered on that media [73].

In the meanwhile, there exists a reciprocal relationship between people and social media platforms (called the reciprocity norm) that requires people to return some benefits for other benefits they receive [32], which reflects social exchange theory (SET) [74]. The SET states that people usually evaluate social exchanges in terms of costs and re wards from subsequent behaviors. In particular, the willingness to accept advertisements or other forms of marketing communications can be framed as a quid pro quo that they accept in return for social media website’s free services [32]. In particular, social media platforms pro vide a functional space for people to freely construct their presentational images, develop relationships, etc. This provokes an individual’s innate desire to repay a favor (e.g., accepting social media advertising). That is, accepting advertisements and other forms of marketing communications is one of the important and symbolic actions manifesting people’s gratifications from social media adoption.

Moreover, native advertising provides a new arena for people to build their images. That is, people can use native advertising to satisfy their SPDs. In addition to message-related factors such as informative ness, entertainment, relevance, and usefulness [18,23,24,71], the utility of native advertising in social media also comprises a part of social value. Social value of native advertising is related to people’s needs for self-presentation. Thus, in a similar vein to the influence of SPD on social media adoptions, which can be easily explained through the UGT framework, people show favorable attitudes toward native advertising because it can fulfill their desires for self-presentation.

Furthermore, SPD motives an individual to seek out novel informa tion, accept and try innovative items such as new products and services [8]. Basically, native advertising is about informing potential consumers with relevant information about product or services that might address their needs $[ 7 5 , 7 6 ]$ . In this sense, an individual with a strong SPD will also express a strong willingness to accept native advertising information.

Thus, the desire for constructing and maintaining a positive image is a strong motive for acceptance of native advertising in social media. Hence, we test

Hypothesis 1 (H1). SPD has a positive effect on people’s acceptance of native advertising in social media.

## 3.2. Self-presentational desire and advertising engagement

Compared to other media vehicles, social media highlights a novel concept called engagement, which explains how and where people interact with others, firms such as branders and advertisers, and social media platforms. Similarly, native advertising emergent recently in so cial media allows participations from potential consumers and active interactions among them, by integrating branded information in the format and design of media content [12]. Compared with other adver tising forms, native advertising is less intrusive and more persuasive, thus obtains more consumers’ attentions and engagement [77], which, in turn, lead to better advertising effectiveness [6,78,79]. In this study, following the extant literature on engagement (e.g., [25,80]) and the format of native advertising popularized in social media [81], we take people’s actions exhibiting sentimental values such as likes, shares, and comments, as a manifest embodiment for AE. AE is another response construct we focus on in this study.

Native advertising provides a hot spot that facilitates social in teractions among people by allowing them to interact with one another through instant communication techniques. Similarly, from the view point of the UGT, people’s SPDs can be fulfilled through engagement with advertising on social media platforms. SPD has a direct effect on people’s actions in social communities [80,82]. In particular, the desire for online self-presentation has a positive effect on eWOM [8], and acts as a key motive for purchasing digital goods in virtual communities [34]. In this sense, people with strong SPDs are more likely to engage with native advertising, which may raise better word-of-mouth effects through active interactions among them. Besides, consumers tend to actively commingle brands and their self-presentational images [40]. In short, people’s interactions and engagement with native advertising will be noticed by her friends, which definitely help building self-presentation. The fulfillment of SPD through AE will enhance rela tionship development [51], enable peer acceptance of self-identity and image [48], and boost their self-esteem and well-being [83].

Social media platforms elicit each individual’s identity in order to deliver the right advertisements to the target consumer. Such that, advertising contents specifically delivered to people reflect appraisals from an important stakeholder $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ social media platforms), which accounts for an integral part of reflected appraisals [68]. In this sense, the personalized advertising delivery entitles people to obtain scores on self-presentation from the social media platform. Thus, in addition to careful packaging of personal information and related online behaviors, engaging with personalized advertising contents can be considered another avenue for people to build self-presentational images. In other words, people might also obtain some positive feelings (e.g., satisfac tions) from interactions over topics related to native advertisements with their friends. For example, when an individual receives an adver tisement about luxury goods reflecting her desired social status, she might tend to share or $\mathbf { \vec { \omega } } ^ { \mathrm { s } } \mathbf { l i k } \mathbf { e } ^ { \mathbf { \vec { \nu } } }$ the advertisement. In the meanwhile, her engaging actions are visible in a circle of her friends, which certainly help construct social images and creating a desired implication on others.

In summary, people’s SPDs can be fulfilled through engagement with native advertisements on social media platforms. Hence, we test

Hypothesis 2 (H2). SPD has a positive effect on people’s engagement with native advertising in social media.

## 3.3. Advertising acceptance and advertising engagement

Both the two constructs, namely, AC and AE, are considered as important response indexes for social media advertising. In this study, we make a distinction between AC and AE. That is, the former is referred to a positive attitude toward advertising, while the latter is viewed as a type of behavior related to advertisements.

In the field of social psychology, a series of systematic work has been conducted to explore the correspondence between attitudes and subse quent behaviors, which proved that people’s attitudes guide later be haviors [84]. Similarly, the consistency between attitudes and behaviors has been widely recognized in the literature of consumer behaviors [85]. That is, consumers with a positive attitude are likely to take favorable actions. As observed by prior studies on advertising $( \mathbf { e } . g . , \ [ 9 , 8 4 ] )$ , people’s attitudes toward advertising are more positive, they would more likely take behavioral responses favorably to advertising. Hence, with no exception, attitudes influence behavioral responses in a similar vein in the context of social media advertising [62].

Of most importance, native advertising in social media distinctly emphasizes the social dimension of advertising. In an ethnographic study, Ritson and Elliott [86] pointed out that the solitary subject dominant in traditional advertising research failed to explain social cultural settings that contextualize advertising related behaviors. They found that AC forms the basis of AE (i.e., a wide variety of advertising-related interactions among people). Hence, we test

Hypothesis 3 (H3). AC has a positive effect on people’s engagement with native advertising in social media.

## 3.4. Antecedents of self-presentational desire

Involvement has been defined as “an unobservable state of motiva tion, arousal or interest toward an associated product” [87,88]. Social networking involvement, also known as degree centrality [89], de scribes an individual’s group involvement in relationships and in teractions with others within a social group, which is positively related to the amount of emotional support and opportunities available to her. For an individual, the more relationships and interactions with others, she should have a higher desire to present herself in a social group (e.g., a circle of her friends or a group of special interest). Thus, the more involvement with a certain group, an individual should show a stronger desire to accept interests of this group [90], and follow and adapt to group behaviors [91].

Group norms are referred to perceptions and opinions of others that whether or not an individual should perform a behavior [92,93]. Group norms are related to compliance and internalization in social influence, which reflect an individual’s adherence to rules and orientation to values of a social system [94]. Social networking norms, imposed on an individual by a certain group or a social community, are embedded in local relationships, which can be measured with clustering coefficient [95] that describes the extent of connections between her or his friends in an egocentric network that contains people with direct relationships with her. That is, for an individual, the more connections in her egocentric network lead to stronger forces of norms $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ compliance and internalization), which, in turn, result in greater desire of people to present themselves in social media. Hence, we test

Hypothesis 4a (H4a). Social networking involvement has a positive effect on SPD in social media.

Hypothesis 4b (H4b). Social networking norms have a positive effect on SPD in social media.

## 4. Research methodology

In this study, we focus on people’s behaviors related to SPD and advertising responses on social media platforms.

## 4.1. Data collection

The reported study chose a social group where people share a com mon interest on outdoor activities and travels on WeChat, due to the following reasons: 1) most members of this group had ever met each other physically and become WeChat friends, 2) they were highly active in group communications, media and advertising engagement, for example, native advertisements about sportswear, sneakers and travels equipment were frequently shared in this group and triggered heated discussions on numerous occasions, and 3) this group had a total of 285 members, which forms a natural boundary for networking measures and is large enough to support our study. WeChat moments has launched native advertising in 2015, which is the equivalent of Facebook adver tising on the timeline. WeChat moments advertising includes videos and pictures, looks like a post in a circle of friends, and is charged by impression (see the right picture of Fig. 1).

We collected the data through Web-based survey to test our research model. This survey consisted of three parts: demographics of partici pants, scales of constructs identified in this research, and identification information. Moreover, we also collected networking data through processing historical conversations among members of this group and built a graph in which each node corresponds to a member. The his torical records of conversations in the WeChat group are publically observable to every member. In the meanwhile, for an individual, there are several ways to indicate one or more specific persons as receivers of her message in the social media group. Certainly. if she wants to mention a certain member of the group, she can directly include the real name or nickname of the person who wants to speak to in her message. Besides, she can also simply use the user tag followed by the nickname of the person to be notified $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ @Nickname). Note that several persons can be tagged at the same time in her message. In the context of WeChat group, the @ symbol allows an individual to tag any member of the group in her message. In addition to the explicit indication of the message receivers, we can also tell implicit clues of who-speak-to-who in the group con versations, typically in the case of replying (or responding) to someone’s message (or topic), regardless of whether or not the replier is mentioned in the message or the topic issued. For example, Emma issues a con versation by sending a message “good morning” in the group, Lisa re plies “This made my day, and it hasn’t even really started yet”. In thi case, we believe that Lisa speaks to Emma. Since a coauthor of this paper has joined this group for five years, we can access such information stored in her smartphone. We manually collected and went through the historical records of conversations in the WeChat group to identify whether a particular person (i.e., the sender) spoke to a member of the group (i.e., the receiver), by using the following criteria: 1) the sender mentions the receiver’s real name or nickname, 2) the sender uses the user tag @ to indicate the receiver, and 3) the sender sends a reply as responses to the messages or the topics previously issued by the receiver in the WeChat group. Note that we didn’t count in cases with group notice or $\ "  \ @ \mathsf { a l l } ^ { \boldsymbol { \mathsf { \prime } } }$ to notify all members of the group. We then inputted a $ { \mathbf { n } } \times  { \mathbf { n } }$ matrix (where $\mathbf { n } = 2 1 2$ denotes the number of participants in the survey) into a social network software called UCINET where the con version graph was built automatically. There is an edge between two members if they communicated (either unidirectionally or bidirection ally) at least once during a five-month observation period (from June 1 to October 31, 2018). We calculated social networking involvement and norms using functions implemented in the UCINET.

Note that in the third part of the survey, participants were asked if they volunteered to provide their nicknames used in the group. The participants had the right to choose 1) providing their nicknames and finishing the survey, 2) canceling and leaving before the submission of the survey if they don’t want to provide such information, or 3) sub mitting the survey without providing nicknames. Then, we related the survey data with the measures of social networking constructs based on their nicknames. In the case without the nickname, we had to drop the corresponding survey. As a matter of fact, it’s unrealistic to obtain complete information for building social networks of all participants. The “who-speak-to who” network provides a convenient proxy for computing social networking metrics, although its edges do not neces sarily indicate friendships among participants. In our case, the chosen group was quite active online and organized offline outdoor activities regularly, thus most members have got acquainted with each other and become WeChat friends. In order to validate the two social networking antecedents, in the survey we also measured scales of social involvement and social norms that correspond to social networking involvement and norms, respectively.

We sent a survey invitation message with a URL to each member in this group, which can be clicked or pasted in a browser to access the questionnaire. In order to encourage participation, participants would achieve a reward (i.e., a certain amount of e-cash) after finishing the survey. At the beginning of the survey, several samples of native ad vertisements were shown to each participant in order to check whether she had a fair idea on the stimulus (i.e., native advertising). Screening questions were asked each participant in order to filter those familiar with native advertising into the study. For example, each participant was required to answer the question regarding whether he or she had ever received the WeChat moments advertising before, we rejected the survey if the answer was “no”. We also excluded the survey if partici pants completed their questionnaires too quickly or if participants answered “strongly disagree” or “strongly agree” to all questions. A total of 245 responses were collected over four weeks, yielding 212 complete and valid responses for subsequent analyses. We used Power 3.1 [96] to perform a power computation. The result shows that the minimum sample size needed for this study is 107 participants in the setting with effect size as 0.15, alpha value as 0.05, and power value as 0.95. Regarding the nonresponse bias, we compared the early (those who replied during the first week) and late participants (those who replied during the last week) by a t-test. The results suggest that there were no statistically significant differences in demographics.

## 4.2. Operationalization of key constructs

In this study, we derived our measures for three constructs that have been applied and validated in prior studies, including SPD, AC, and AE. We adapted scales for SPD from Kim et al. [34] based on our research context. Our measure for AC was adapted from scales used in advertising empirical studies [24,30]. To measure AE in social media, we adapted scales from Lee et al. [25], Khan [80] to our context.

The measurement items were derived from the English literature, while the survey was conducted in a Chinese social media context. Several rounds of forward translation (English to Chinese) by two PhD students and backward translation (Chinese to English) by another two PhD students were performed to ensure the precise interpretation of items. In the pre-test process, we invited 12 PhD students who have prior experiences in WeChat moments advertising to evaluate the measure ment items. Then, we placed items in proper sequence, refined unclear and wordy sentences to pursue items clarity, and several items were reworded to improve ambiguous expressions. Afterward, we also con ducted a pilot test in a small WeChat group including 35 members to examine statistical properties of the survey. We adopted participants suggestions to drop redundant items. Cronbach’s alpha values and factor analysis of the results indicated that all constructs exhibited acceptable reliability levels.

The questionnaire related to the three constructs employed the seven-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 7=strongly agree), and the two social networking constructs (i.e., social networking involve ment and social networking norms) were calculated from the conversation graph built from historical records of the group of special interest.

Moreover, this study employed two social networking metrics for group involvement and norms in social media, as described in Section 3.4. More specifically, as common in the literature on social network analysis [25,80], social networking involvement is instantiated as a measure of degree centrality, i.e., the extent to which an individual connects to others within a social network and social networking norms are instantiated as a measure of clustering coefficient, which describes the extent to which friends of an individual (i.e., ego) are connected with one another. A complete list of measures is presented in Table 4.

Note that we adapted scales of social media involvement developed in Kyle et al. [88] and social media presentational norms from Taylor and Todd [92]. Similarly, the questionnaire related to social media involvement and presentational norms employed the seven-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree, 7=strongly agree). The two constructs met the criteria for reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Statistical technique

The theoretical model has multiple stages, which indicates that it is of necessity to employ a structural equation modeling technique capable of simultaneously testing multiple relationships between constructs used in this study. We tested the hypotheses in our research model using the partial least squares (PLS), a component-based structural equation modeling analysis approach in SmartPLS 3.0. PLS is an appropriate choice for our analysis because it has no restriction on sample distri bution (the data for this study have a non-normal distribution, as indi cated by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests) and sample size, and can process constructs measured by a single item (in our case, the two social networking metrics, i.e., social networking involvement and norms were calculated from a conversation graph). In terms of homoscedasticity, we conducted the homogeneity of variance test, and the result showed that the data satisfied homoscedasticity. In addition, the linearity of the dataset was verified via the matrix scatter plots. In the following, we first verify the reliability and validity of the mea surement model, and then examine hypotheses.

Discriminant validity was demonstrated using three methods: For nell and Larcker [99]’s criterion, cross-loadings of the items [100], and the heterotrait–monotrait (HTMT) ratio [101]. First, to assess the discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE for each construct should be larger than correlations between constructs (i.e., the off-diagonal elements in Table 5a) [99]. All constructs met this requirement. Second, as shown in Table 5b, for each construct, the respective items had higher loading values on its own than on other constructs. Third, Table 5c illustrates that all HTMT ratios were below 0.85. Hence, the discriminant validity was established.

We tested our data for the common method bias using three methods. First, following the analytical procedure by Liang et al. [102], we included in the PLS model a common method factor. The results indi cated that the average substantive variance was 0.796, while the average common method-based variance was 0.002 (Table 6a). The ratio of substantive variance to method-based variance was 398:1. Moreover, most method factor loadings were insignificant. Second, Harman’s single-factor test was employed to examine whether there exists a common method bias [103] by making exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factory analysis (CFA) [104]. We run EFA to examine whether a single factor can explain a majority of covariance in the dependent and independent variables. The results show that no single factor was responsible for the total variance. Following Homburg et al. [105], we also conducted a CFA with a common method factor to examine the fit of the measurement model by comparing the full model with all constructs (multi-factor) with a single-factor model [106]. In the CFA approach, the hypothesized model has a single factor that includes all manifested items, and method biases exist if the single factor model fits the data well. Table 6b indicates that a one-factor model did not fit well (CFI = 0.740, RMSEA = 0.208), while the full model fitted the data satisfactorily (CFI = 0.994, RMSEA = 0.022). Third, we included a common method factor in the structural model, where all items were loaded [105]. We assigned all loadings of the method factor the same value and specified the method factor being uncorrelated with other constructs. The results demonstrate that the average of the substantive variance and the method-based variance was 0.314, which suggests that the model was stable after including the method factor (Table 6c). Therefore, we contented that common method bias was unlikely to be a concern in this study.

## 6. Discussion and implication

## 6.1. Discussion of results

This research generates several important findings. First of all, SPD is a significant factor determining people’s responses to native advertising (i.e., AC and AE) in social media. Overall, this finding is in line with the results of prior studies on relationships between self-presentational motives and social media adoption [48,82] and those on people’s desired impression formation [108]. In other words, this finding con firms the influence of self-presentation on people’s online behaviors including media adoption and consumption. However, compared to the fact that social media can be viewed as a complementary context (i.e., the cyber space) to the physical space for relationship development, self-presentation in native advertising reflects a business-based aspect for people to mingle with others and brands. That is, our finding points to a novel economic value of social media. Moreover, of interest, SPD shows a dual effect on people’s internal (i.e., psychological acceptance) and external $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ behavioral engagement) responses to native adver tising. This indicates that, besides advertising engaging behaviors visible to others, people are also concerned about the potential consequence of psychological attitudes $( \mathrm { i . e . , A C ) }$ that might be invisible to others, with respect to their self-presentational images.

Second, AC partially mediates the relationship between SPD and AE. In particular, people with strong SPDs take native advertising as op portunities to fulfill their social needs, thus tend to take positive atti tudes toward advertisements delivered there. Consequently, they are more likely to participate into related social interactions. This can be explained by the motivation-attitude-behavior framework and the attitude-behavior consistency [109]. This finding is consistent with prior studies on advertising attitudes [14,62] and those on subsequent behaviors such as clicking and purchasing after exposure to advertising [9,18]. These prior studies focused on either influences of consumers motives on advertising attitudes or relationships between advertising attitudes and behavior intentions in the context of social media adver tising. However, this study pays close attention to a novel advertising form, and explores how advertising attitudes affect engaging behaviors around native advertisements. One exceptional research conducted by Lee et al. [12] explored which type of native advertising perception (i.e., nonintrusive or manipulative) is a better predictor of consumers’ atti tudinal and behavioral responses. But they looked into different ante cedents from ours, and did not examine the relationship between attitudinal and behavioral responses to native advertising.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

The present study makes several important contributions to the research literature by understanding the path for people to positively respond to native advertising in social media, from the perspective of the SPD and its antecedents.

First, this study adds to the broader line of research on advertising responses (e.g., acceptance and engagement). Our research validates and illustrates the importance of SPD as a critical driver for people’s responses to native advertising in social media, both empirically and theoretically. The role of SPD has largely been ignored in the advertising research. Little is known about how people’s desire for image building and relationship development affects their responses to advertising. Thi study bridges the literature gap. Specifically, we explore advertising responses from a novel perspective, i.e., SPD, taking native advertising recently flourishing in social media as the research subject, which has not been addressed before. Our research outcome also provides insights into the theoretical frameworks widely employed in the advertising research. On the one hand, we postulate that, SPD, which truly captures the distinct feature of native advertising, should not replace but com plement other motives in the UGT framework. On the other hand, in addition to message-related antecedents such as informativeness, entertainment, relevance, and usefulness reported in prior studies (e.g., [18,23,24,71]), our study reveals the utility of native advertising in social media for social uses, which are important to understanding consumers’ attitudes and behaviors after advertising exposures. In this sense, it also expands the horizon of the technology acceptance model (TAM) in the advertising research, which might be an interesting

prospect to explore in the future.

Second, this study contributes to the SPT by explaining SPD and its antecedents (i.e., social networking involvement and norms) in the context of native advertising in social media. Previous research have investigated how self-presentation and online identity affect social media adoption (e.g., [42,48,82]), why self-presentation profiles and perceived identity verification help motivate people’s satisfaction and knowledge contribution [35], and how interactivity in online game community enables better on self-presentation [33]. Nonetheless, this research empirically analyses SPD and its antecedents, and emphasized a different set of consequences of self-presentation, namely, people’s attitudes $( \mathrm { i . e . , A C ) }$ and behaviors $( \mathrm { i . e . , A E } )$ toward native advertising as a new avenue. This discloses the potential and perspective of self-presentation in the advertising space, thus adding a novel antenna for the SPT.

Moreover, this work also makes contributions to the literature related to social influence theory. In a prior study, Kim et al. [34] identified antecedents of SPD from a perspective of social influence. Nonetheless, we believe that social influence theory has a natural connection with social networks. Particularly, the two social networking metrics of involvement and norms used in this research, to some degree, correspond to two social influence antecedents identified in their work. We empirically examined Pearson correlations between social media involvement and social networking involvement $( \rho = 0 . 7 9 0 , \rho < 0 . 0 1 )$ , and between social media self-presentational norms and social networking norms $( \rho = 0 . 9 5 0 , \ \ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Thus, it is acceptable to replace social media involvement and presentational norms with social networking involvement and norms, respectively. In effect, social networking metrics have a potential advantage over ordinary survey measures in that, the former is calculated from networking relationships and thus more objective, while the latter is prone to many errors such as mis-reporting.

Third, this research additionally enriches the extension of the motivation-attitude-behavior framework and the attitude-behavior consistency, by highlighting the role of AC in mediating the relation ship between SPD and AE. This study is the first to test the integrated framework in the context of native advertising from a totally different perspective (i.e., SPD). Moreover, this illuminates the positive rela tionship between the two types of advertising responses, namely, AC and AE. Researchers in information systems have often analyzed the influ ence of advertising attitudes on consequent purchasing intentions, rather than an exclusive focus on advertising. However, we posit that it’s a meaningful effort to explore further how the two types of advertising responses influence subsequent behaviors such as purchasing, review ing, and peer recommendations.

## 6.3. Practical implications

In additional to theoretical contributions, this study also provides critical insights for advertisers and social media providers. The main finding of this study indicates that SPD is a major predictor of people’s responses to native advertising in social media. Of most interest, this research proves that SPD can be transferred from the adoption of social media to social media advertising, which illustrates the economic value of social media.

First of all, social media providers are suggested to stimulate people’ desires for self-presentation. To this end, social media platform should provide a rich set of expressive artifacts for people to achieve their self presentational goals, and make these self-presentational artifacts more easy to use and more convenient to obtain. Of most importance, this research emphasizes that native advertising is another important avenue for people to build self-presentational image and online identity. In light of this, it is highly worthwhile to design advertising schemes suitable for social media users to control information and influence their impres sions formed by others.

Second, this research also sheds light on two aspects, which corre sponds to two identified antecedents of SPD. Essentially, networking measures of involvement and norms employed in this study manifest the quantity of ego (i.e., the subject)’s local relationships and those among alters (i.e., neighboring friends). Thus, from the aspect of social networking involvement and norms embedded in local relationships, social media providers should facilitate relationships development among individuals in various circles of friends, either by designing mechanisms (e.g., whether and how the list of friends and postings are visible to others in various communication contexts) and tools (e.g., recommendations and functions related to group of special interest), or by running certain operations (e.g., creating hot topics or organize social events around which people can communicate and make conversations).

Third, a salient finding of this research suggests that there exists a strong mediatory effect of AC on the relationship between SPD and AE. In this sense, both social media providers and advertisers need to sup port more media-rich advertising contents and more personalized advertising delivery, and provide more options of actions in advertising artifacts. For advertisers, they are suggested to design more attractive, entertaining, and conversational advertisements for potential con sumers to engage with firms (or branders) and interact with one another.

Nowadays, Internet giants such as AOL and Google have incorpo rated user profiles in order to provide personalized information services. Therefore, without loss of generality, managerial insights generated from this research might also provide valuable cues for media providers over a broader spectrum of advertising forms. In particular, Web portals and search engines are strongly suggested to consider stimulating peo ple’s desires for self-presentation and strengthening participating ele ments in advertising schemes in order to obtain positive advertising responses.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

Despite the valuable findings of this study, we acknowledge that our research is subject to several shortcomings. First, we took members of a group of special interest on outdoor activities and travels as subjects. It would be interesting to further test the robustness of our results on other groups with different interests. Second, this research examines how native advertisements displayed in the WeChat moments can be used to fill people’s SPDs in the context of social media (i.e., WeChat in general). In the meanwhile, the two social networking metrics were calculated on the “who-speak-to who” network built from the historical conversions in a large social group, which provided a convenient proxy for describing relationships among people in social media. In order to validate our results, we plan to further investigate the behavioral consistency across WeChat in general, WeChat group and moments. In addition, in the present study, the two social network metrics were calculated on a binary-valued undirected graph of conversations in the group. The binary-valued undirected graph provides an ideal base for testing, implementing and interpreting social networking measures conve niently. However, it is of prominently interest to examine our concep tual model on a valued directed conversation graph in a social media group. Third, our results were derived from a Chinese social media platform. It is a promising perspective to validate our model on other social media platforms in other country settings. A fourth limitation might be that, this research collected information about social networking relationships through online communications. Intuitively, two individuals with good offline relationships tend to develop corre sponding relationships online, and vice versa. That is, the latter, to a large degree, reflects the former. Thus, we believe that online relation ships among people are another major predictor of their responses to social media advertising. Further research would attempt to test our concept model by integrating online and offline behaviors in a cyberphysical space.

Besides future research triggered by limitations discussed above, we plan to investigate other factors (e.g., social context and subjective distance) that might have potential effects on advertising responses in social media. Another interesting perspective is to explore the role of SPD in determining advertising responses in other forms of advertising media (e.g., display advertising and search advertising) in experimental settings.
