## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: mobile ICT ICT Productivity GDP IT productivity paradox

## A B S T R A C T

This paper examines the influence of mobile Information and Communication Technology (ICT) on national productivity by separating ICT factors into wired and mobile infrastructures. Using a unique data set that in cludes global wired and mobile internet speeds, we find that mobile ICT plays an important role in increasing national productivity in developing countries; however, we find no significant impact of wired ICT for either developed or developing countries. This work contributes to the literature by providing deeper insights into the impact of ICT on global productivity by specifying the ICT factors (which are combined in previous research) as wired and mobile ICT.

## 1. Introduction

Mobile Information and Communication Technology (ICT) has become a critical factor in economic growth [1,2]. In particular, mobile ICTs such as smartphones and LTE networks have accelerated the global diffusion of ICT by providing enhanced information accessibility and mobility. As a fundamental infrastructure, mobile ICT has also promoted the development of Internet of Things (IoT) technology by providing network connections to devices without limitations of place [3–5]. Shapiro and Hassett [6] reported that in the US, investment in mobile network upgrades (e.g., 3 G to 4 G wireless networks) has increased the use of smart devices and mobile applications and spurred job creation. Furthermore, mobile ICT allows ubiquitous access to the internet, which increases firms’ labor productivity [7], customer relationships [8], and employee autonomy [9].

There are different patterns of internet penetration between devel oped and developing countries. In developed countries, which have strong backbone networks, the internet has mainly been distributed through wired ICT initially (e.g., cable/fiber-optic networks and com puters) and then reinforced by mobile ICT (e.g., 3 G/4 G networks and smartphones). However, many developing countries, which have un derdeveloped infrastructures, have spread the internet mostly through mobile networks to avoid high setup costs and heavy investment [10]. In this respect, mobile networks are cheaper and easier to install, providing rapid service penetration [11]. As such, many developing countries have bypassed fixed phone lines in favor of mobile technology due to the lack of infrastructure required for reliable connections [10].

As a major competitor to wired communications, mobile communi cations in developing countries are used more widely to access internet and communication services in areas of physical health, local culture, civility, and the economy [12]. Ghyasi and Kushchu [13] state that m-government, which is governmental information and services relying on mobile technologies, is favored in developing countries where the number of wireless communication users is greater than the number of wired communication users. Through wireless applications, developing countries can bypass more costly and time-consuming investments in fixed-wire telecom infrastructures in adopting new technologies, which is very important to economic growth in developing countries [14].

The importance of internet speed for economies has increased in the past decade because high-speed internet access can provide substantial economic benefits, such as increased innovation and productivity in business, better access to services, improved healthcare, more efficient energy consumption, and improved education [15]. The schema sug gested by Little [16] shows that enhanced broadband internet speed and penetration can increase productivity by reducing costs and saving time, spurring innovations in product development and sales procedures, increasing job creation by building new infrastructures and adding services, and benefiting consumers by increasing the quality of communication and providing more information through online ser vices. Governments play a key role to improve the countries’ internet speed and adoption. For example, in the last few decades, the govern ment of the Republic of Korea has established policies and programs to support ICT development [17]. As a result, the country now boasts the fastest internet in the world [18], and ICT has become one of its major businesses [19]. The federal government of Canada launched “Connect to Innovate” to bring high-speed internet to 300 rural and remote communities. The program’s coordinators expect to create new jobs and businesses by encouraging internet-based innovation [20].

As shown above, mobile ICT has played a critical role in recent economic development and internet speed is becoming an essential part of it [16]. However, to examine the “productivity paradox of IT” that questions the contributions of IT to national productivity [21], existing studies so far have investigated the influence of ICT on economic pro ductivity without distinguishing wired and mobile ICT. For example, Dewan and Kraemer [22] examined the returns from ICT investments by considering ICT and non-ICT as inputs to national output, measured by a country’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP), and comparing the differences between developed and developing economies. Dedrick, Kraemer, and Shih [23] extended this work by investigating how human capital, openness of the economy, and telecommunication infrastructure affect the relationship between ICT and economic growth. They also examined the impact of ICT factors such as internet penetration, cellular pene tration, and cost of telephone subscriptions on the national productivity of developed and developing countries. However, they did not examine the contribution of ICT to national productivity separately between wired and mobile ICT.

In this study, we extend the prior literature by investigating whether wired and mobile ICT contribute to national productivity differently in developing and developed countries by employing internet speed for wired and mobile internet as a proxy of wired and mobile ICTs. By analyzing international data on wired and mobile internet speeds from 2011 to 2016, we find that mobile ICT plays an important role in increasing national productivity in developing countries. However, we do not find a significant impact of wired ICT in either developed or developing countries. The results demonstrate that when investigating the “productivity paradox of IT,” it is important to consider heteroge neity in the type of ICT (e.g. wired vs mobile) rather than simply considering ICT as an aggregate factor.

This study contributes to the literature on ICT effects of the national productivity in two ways. First, this paper extends current findings on the influence of ICT on national productivity by the separation of ICT factors into wired and mobile infrastructures. It provides deeper insights into the impact of ICT on global productivity by specifying the ICT factors (which are combined or individually considered in previous research) as wired and mobile ICT. Second, we use a unique data set that includes global wired and mobile internet speeds. While the existing ICT variables such as telephone subscription and internet penetration mea sure the quantity of internet use [23,24], the internet speed captures the quality of internet use in business, education, and public services [15,18, 20]. Therefore, internet speed data allow us to examine the impact of the quality of ICT on global productivity and also compare the difference in the results by using quantitative ICT variables and qualitative ICT variables.

## 2. Literature review

In this literature review, we discuss past studies by classifying them into three categories (Categories I, II, and III) and distinguish them from our study (Category IV) as shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.1. The impact of ICT investment on national productivity (Category I)

With the development of ICT, the industry and the economy have substantially benefited from reduced production costs, improved effi ciency in resource allocation, and information services [25,26,2,27,28].

Prior studies investigated the returns to ICT investment from the perspectives of industry-level and firm-level productivity. These studies found the evidence of positive and significant returns from IT capital investment (see, e.g., [21,29–31]). While the link between ICT contri butions to productivity can be better captured at the industry-level and firm-level, the external validity beyond the narrow domain of these studies was questionable. Specifically, it was an open question as to whether the industry and firm-level findings would automatically translate into the economy-wide level

Addressing this issue, the initial stream of economy-level research has examined the contribution of ICT toward the economic growth of a single country and provided mixed results [32–34]. Broadening the focus from a single country to multiple countries, subsequent studies on the impact of ICT investment on country-level productivity have also reported mixed results. Specifically, some studies found that ICT in vestment plays a positive and significant role in improving economic performance and national productivity [35–41], while other studies found that the productivity effect of ICT investment is negative and significant [22,27,42–44].

Some studies also focused on the long-term effect of ICT on national economies by considering ICT as one of the general purpose technolo gies [45–48]. These studies generally found that the long-term effects of ICT investment are much larger than its short-term effects, consistent with studies that document the positive impact of ICT on country-level productivity. The mixed evidence of cross-country studies suggests that the effect of ICT investment on economic growth may occur un evenly between developed and developing countries due to differences in the level of ICT use and the factors shaping that use [22,43].

## 2.2. Differential impact of ICT between developing and developed countries (Category II)

Earlier studies on ICT and national productivity across multiple countries used data up to the early 2000s when it was just the beginning of the rapid diffusion of ICT in developed countries only and docu mented a positive and significant effect of ICT investment on produc tivity gains for developed countries but not for developing countries [22, 42,44,49]. Moreover, some of the later studies document that the pos itive evidence for ICT on economic growth is mostly driven by the USA with little evidence of its effect in other countries [45,50,51]. These studies suggest that insignificant productivity gains from ICT investment in developing countries could be driven by the lack of complementary infrastructures or a business environment to reap the benefits from ICT investment, and the lack of experience with ICT as there is a significant lagging effect for developing countries to capitalize on ICT [22].

Subsequent studies provide different results by utilizing more recent data that were not covered in earlier studies. Employing more recent and comprehensive data from 1994 to 2007, Dedrick et al. [23] found a positive and significant effect of ICT investment on productivity gains in both developed and upper-income developing countries. Dedrick et al. [23] explained that upper-income developing countries have increased their IT capital stocks and gained experience with the use of ICT during their sample period. On the other hand, Cardona et al. [45] and Niebel [38] showed the productivity effect of ICT investment is positive and significant, but the positive effect is uniform among developing, emerging, and developed countries suggesting that the influence of IT technology becomes more certain regardless of the level of economic development.

Overall, prior studies provided mixed evidence of the impact of ICT on economic growth between developing and developed countries. The mixed empirical evidence is largely attributable to different empirical models and the use of data sets covering different countries and time periods. Furthermore, most of these studies were based on data before mobile ICT emerged as a dominant part of ICT and thus do not examine the differential effect of fixed and mobile ICT on productivity gains.

## 2.3. The emergence of mobile ICT and its impact on economic growth (Category III)

Mobile communication technologies have developed rapidly over the last few decades. By 2002, the number of mobile phone subscribers had overtaken the number of fixed lines [52]. Particularly, in developing countries like sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and some areas in Asia, mobile broadband appears to be growing rapidly while fixed broadband is growing steadily, relative to developed countries.

In response to the fast-growing penetration of mobile service, re searchers have paid attention to the importance of mobile ICT and its impact on economic growth. However, only a handful of studies provide comprehensive evidence on the effect of mobile ICT on productivity gains due to the lack of comprehensive country-level mobile ICT data.

One strand of studies examined the economic impact of mobile phones particularly at the small firm-level in a single or a few developing countries such as Brazil, India, Uganda, and Ethiopia. These studies showed a positive effect of mobile phones on consumer and producer welfare [53], agricultural production and marketing decisions [54], and firm productivity [55].

Another strand of studies also documented a positive association between mobile ICT penetration and productivity by focusing on developing countries in sub-Saharan Africa with the rapid diffusion of mobile ICT and underdeveloped fixed ICT infrastructure [1,56,2,57].

Recent studies began to provide more comprehensive cross-country evidence. These studies used mobile phone subscriber data before 2010 and found strong effects on economic growth and production ef ficiency from investments in mobile telecommunications in developing countries [58–61]. These findings suggest that developing countries can leapfrog by focusing on mobile ICT investment rather than fixed ICT investment. However, they are limited in that they examined the effect of mobile ICT in developing countries only or they focused on produc tion efficiency, not on economic growth. Moreover, mobile phone sub scriber data used in recent studies may be limited to capture the aggregate effect of mobile ICT on productivity gains.

Overall, despite the enormous expansion of mobile ICT, there is a void in the literature with no systematic evidence that to what extent, mobile ICT has contributed to global economic growth. Our study aims to fill out the void by investigating whether and how fixed and mobile ICT can affect developed and developing countries differently depend ing on their type.

## 2.4. The impact of internet speed on economic growth

High-speed Internet became an essential component of the quality of ICT affecting various industries including the entertainment industry, social media industry, healthcare industry, and educational service in dustry, as well as a host of other industries. Prior studies have shown that broadband penetration has material economic impacts. However, features other than penetration, such as internet speed are also impor tant determinants of the effects of ICT [62].

Internet speed is a good aggregate measure to capture the qualitative aspect of fixed and mobile ICTs because enhanced internet speed pro vides more convenient internet access and allows various internet ap plications. [15,18,20]. Consistent with this notion, an increasing number of countries consider high-speed internet access as a key infra structure that brings competitive advantages and promises future eco nomic growth [63]. Increasing broadband internet speed and penetration contribute to enhancing the efficiency by saving time and costs, stimulating innovation in business, bringing new jobs, and increasing customer satisfaction [16]. Besides, based on the rapid development of mobile communication technology in the past decade, the internet speed of mobile communication has been rapidly increasing, which leads to the development of related IoT technologies such as smart devices and mobile applications and then stimulate job creations [3–6].

Despite the increasing importance of internet speed on increasing productivity of business and quality of life [15], academic studies on speed are sparse and they failed to provide conclusive evidence. For example, using broadband speed data in the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries during the period 2008–2010, Rohman and Bohlin [64] found a significant and positive relationship between broadband speed and economic growth and sug gested countries could increase long-term prosperity by adopting more advanced networks. Similarly, Lobo et al. [65] recently showed that the unemployment rate in countries with fast internet speeds was signifi cantly lower. However, a study by Ford [66] using more recent data from 2013 to 2015 does not show the positive impact of broadband speed on economic growth.

Using Internet speed as a way to improve the quality of internet service, prior literature has provided evidence that ICT increases firmlevel productivity in various industries. Moreover, mobile ICT helps firms improve labor productivity, enhance customer relationships, and foster employees’ autonomy. Previous literature has not examined the differential effect of the quality of mobile ICT and wired ICT using internet speed on productivity in developed and developing countries.

## 2.5. Research gap (category IV)

Prior studies have examined the impact of ICT investment on na tional productivity. Some scholars divided countries into subgroups such as developing and developed countries and examined the differ ential impact of ICT investment on national productivity. Other studies have divided ICT into wired and mobile ICT and have examined whether there is a differential effect on national productivity. However, most of these studies were based on mobile phone data and thus did not consider other elements of mobile ICT and the overall quality of mobile ICT at the aggregate level. Using Internet speed as a measure to capture the quality of internet service, prior studies have provided evidence that ICT in creases firm-level productivity in various industries. Moreover, these studies found that mobile ICT helps firms improve labor productivity, enhance customer relationships, and foster employees’ autonomy.

In this study, we contribute to the literature by examining the effect of ICT (1) by dividing it into wired and mobile ICT, on national pro ductivity (2) between developing and developed countries (3) using an aggregate quality of ICT based on the internet speed data. To the best of our knowledge, no study has investigated the issue by considering these three dimensions at the same time. This study fills this void by investi gating whether and how the quality of both mobile and wired ICT measured by internet speed influences national productivity between developing and developed countries.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Data and variables

After removing countries with missing data, we obtained data for 32 countries—18 developed and 14 developing—during the period from 2011 to 2016 (Table 1). Following Dewan and Kraemer [22] and Dedrick et al. [23], we labeled our sample countries based on “advanced economies” and “emerging markets and developing economies” from International Monetary Fund’s (IMF’s) country classification [67] as developed countries and developing countries, respectively. Because high-speed mobile network infrastructures $( \mathrm { e . g . , 3 G , 4 G } ,$ and LTE) have recently spread around the globe, our research covers the six most recent years to capture the impact of ICT infrastructure on national economies. The final sample period ends in 2016 because of the limitation of capital data, which was available only through 2015. With reference to Dedrick et al. [23], we add investment in 2015 and depreciate capital stock by five percent to create the capital stock in 2016.

## 3.2. Empirical models

Following Dedrick et al. [23] and Dewan and Kraemer [22], we use an intercountry production function to investigate the impact of ICT investment on country-level productivity,

$$
G D P _ {i t} = f (I C T _ {i t}, N I C T _ {i t}, L _ {i t}),\tag{1}
$$

where the output $\mathrm { G D P } _ { i t }$ is the annual GDP and the inputs are ICT capital stock (ICT ), non-ICT capital stock $\left( \mathrm { N I C T } _ { i t } \right)$ , and annual total labor hours employed (L ) in country i and year t.

We adopted the Cobb-Douglas production function, which is widely used to study the effects of ICT on productivity. Our empirical model uses panel data covering 32 countries over a six-year period (2011–2016):

$$
\log G D P _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \log I C T _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \log N I C T _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \log L _ {i t} + \lambda_ {t} + \nu_ {i} + \in_ {i t},\tag{2}
$$

where $\lambda _ { t }$ is a year fixed effect that controls for factors that impact the economic growth of all countries over time, while $\nu _ { i }$ is a country-specific fixed effect that controls for observable and unobservable differences across countries that are constant over time. $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the random error term in the equation representing the net influence of all the unmeasured factors on the output.

To study the effect of the wired and mobile ICT on national pro ductivity without the influence of ICT capital, we first replaced logICT with wired internet speed and mobile internet speed variables on the following equation:

We estimate pooled ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models and fixed-effect regression models with two-dimensional standard error correction. To alleviate the influence of aggregate time series trends from the panel data we include year fixed effects in the regression model. Lastly, because standard errors of the residuals can be correlated with year-specific or country-specific factors [23] we estimate OLS model by clustering the standard errors at the year and country-levels to mitigate both dependences simultaneously [69].

Endogeneity is an important concern in the investigation of the relation between wired and mobile ICT and the GDP because the wired and mobile internet speeds can be jointly determined by the GDP. While the country-level fixed effect estimation help mitigate concerns that the relation between wired and mobile ICT and the GDP is caused by timeinvariant unobservable factors, it does not necessarily mitigate endo geneity arising when an omitted time-variant factor leads to an increase in both ICT and the GDP. To mitigate this concern, we employ an instrumental variables method. To implement the instrumental variables method, we conduct a two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression analysis where we carry out a first-stage regression of the endogenous variables on the instrumental variables that are correlated with the explanatory variable but have no direct effects on the dependent vari able. Specifically, we regress the wired and mobile internet speeds on four instrumental variables: land size, population density, infrastruc ture, and urban population density [52,70,4,2,16]. Broadband penetration rate, mobile cellular penetration rate, population density, and urban population density were adopted based on Little [16] and Rohman and Bohlin [64].

$$
\begin{array}{r l} W _ {i t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \log I C T _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \log N I C T _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \log L _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} S I Z E _ {i t} + \beta_ {5} P O P _ {i t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {6} I N F _ {i t} + \beta_ {7} U R B _ {i t} + \lambda_ {t} + \nu_ {i} + \epsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

$$

where SIZE is the total area of each country measured by square kilometers; POP is population density which is population divided by land area in square kilometers; INF is each country’s quality of trade and transport infrastructure based on the survey conducted by the World Bank; URB is the urban population which represents the percentage of the population who live in the urban area. We get these data from the World Bank database.

Then, we compute the fitted values of wired and mobile internet speeds from the first stage regression and use the fitted values to replace the actual value of wired and mobile internet speeds in the second stage regression model. That is, the second equation is used to evaluate the impact on GDP by using the predicted values for wired and mobile internet speeds which are estimated in the 1 st stage regression.

$$
\log G D P _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \log N I C T _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \log L _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \widehat {W} _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} \widehat {M} _ {i t} + \lambda_ {t} + \nu_ {i} + \in_ {i t},\tag{6}
$$

where $\widehat { W }$ is the predictive values of wired internet speed from the firststage regression, and M <sup>̂</sup> is the predictive values of mobile internet speed from the first-stage regression.

## 4. Empirical results

## 4.1. The impact of ICT capital on economic growth

Before investigating the impact of wired and mobile internet speed on economic growth, we replicated Dewan and Kraemer [22] and Dedrick et al. [23] using data from 2011 to 2016 to examine the effect of ICT capital investment on GDP for developed and developing countries. Dewan and Kraemer [22] argued that ICT capital in developed countries was positively associated with national productivity, but the relation ships between ICT capital and national productivity in developing countries were not significant. However, Dedrick et al. [23] found that in upper-income developing countries from 1994 to 2007, ICT capital was also positively associated with national productivity at a significant level.

## 4.2. The impact of wired and mobile ICT on economic growth

To test the differential effect of wired and mobile ICT on national productivity, we turn to the impact of wired and mobile internet speeds on GDP. Table 4 presents the regression results for internet speed vari ables (wired internet speed and mobile internet speed). We first estimate each variable separately by adding them in regression models (1) and (2), respectively. Then, we estimate wired internet speed and mobile internet speed jointly in the regression model (3). The regression model in Panel A does not control for ICT capital to eliminate the possibility of a correlation between ICT capital and internet speed variables, but the model in Panel B includes ICT capital to examine how controlling for infrastructure investment impacts our measures of ICT quality.

The results in Panel A show that when we do not control for infra structure spending wired internet speed did not have a statistically significant effect on productivity in either developed or developing countries. However, mobile internet speed is significantly and positively associated with productivity in developing countries $( p < . 0 1 )$ in all specifications. Furthermore, the results in Panel $\mathbf { B } ,$ which includes ICT capital, show that mobile speed had a statistically significant impact on economic growth. This suggests that the results are not driven by capital spending on ICT, but instead are due to the quality component of ICT that we proxy for with internet speeds. Moreover, the results show that enhanced mobile internet speed contributed to increasing GDP in developing rather than developed countries. Developing countries tend to have poor wired internet infrastructures and lower capability to invest in expensive wired networks. Thus, they seem to rely more on mobile internet infrastructure, which is easier and cheaper to install nationwide rapidly [11]. Our results suggest that the proliferation of mobile speed then increases national productivity more significantly in developing countries than in developed countries.

It is possible that the results in the fixed-effects panel data models are driven by differential preexisting trends for countries. For instance, one may estimate a positive spurious relationship if countries with high mobile internet speeds experienced higher economic growth relative to their counterparts. To mitigate this concern, we carried out a series of falsification tests. Instead of using GDP data from 2011 to 2016, we applied GDP data in a different time period (2005–2010) to the analysis. The regression results in panel C demonstrate that both wired and mo bile internet are insignificant for all countries, including developed and developing countries. Thus, the falsification tests provide the evidence that the results in Panels A and B are robust in that they are not spurious due to preexisting trends.

Furthermore, to mitigate endogeneity concerns, we also carry out the 2SLS regression and report the estimation results in Table 5. Panel A shows the result of the first stage regression. The model is used to esti mate the wired and mobile internet speeds and the predicted values are applied to the second regression as independent variables. Panel B demonstrates that the result of the second stage regressions is robust to control for the endogeneity of the wired and mobile internet speeds. The 2SLS results are larger in magnitude than those in Table 4 Panel A and B and provide strong support for the presence of a positive and significant relationship between mobile internet speed with GDP in developing countries.<sup>1</sup>

## 4.3. The impact of ICT infrastructures on economic growth

In this section, we test whether and how our primary findings are affected when we additionally control for the effect of various ICT var iables. Specifically, we add four additional ICT variables to our main regression model: fixed phone penetration (FPH) which indicates the percentage of fixed telephone users by the population, mobile cellular phone penetration (MOB), fixed broadband internet penetration (BRD), and internet penetration (INT) which represents the percentage of internet users by the population.<sup>2</sup>

Table 6 presents the comparison of test results between developed and developing countries with various ICT variables. First, each ICT variable was tested individually from estimations (1) to (7) in Table 6. While none of the ICT variables are significant for developed countries, mobile internet speed and mobile phone penetration are significant in developing countries. The statistically significant effect of mobile phone penetration on economic growth in developing countries coincides with the results from Lee et al. [2] and Ward & Zhang [81]. These results suggest that both types of mobile technologies have contributed to the growth of GDP in developing countries. However, when we jointly es timate all ICT variables in estimation (8) only mobile internet speed shows a statistically significant suggesting that mobile internet speed is a more dominant mobile ICT factor than mobile phone penetration in economic growth in developing countries.

Overall, the findings in Table 6 is consistent with the result in Ta bles 4 and 5, which demonstrates that wired internet is not a significant driver of economic growth for either developed and developing coun tries. Instead, our measure of mobile internet quality (speed) does seem to be an important contributor to economic growth in developing countries.

## 5. Discussion

This paper investigates how the quality of wired ICT and mobile ICT using internet speed differently influences national productivity between developed and developing countries. As shown in Fig. 3, the wired and mobile speeds of the most developing countries are still lagging behind the advanced countries. Our result shows the importance of mobile ICT in the economic growth of developing countries (Table 7).

This suggests that the traditional ICT measures such as ICT investment alone should not be the only factor in assessing the impact of ICT on the developing economy. Rather, the mobile ICT factors such as internet speed and mobile communication networks should be consid ered more than the fixed ICT factors. Further, it is important to consider the new technological developments and trends when assessing the ef fect of ICT on economic growth.

We contribute to the literature in two ways. First, we analyze the effect of ICT on national productivity by using a comprehensive measure of ICT by dividing it into wired ICT and mobile ICT. Developed countries introduced mobile networks after the implementation of strong wired networks based on well-established national infrastructures. On the other hand, developing countries, which lack such infrastructures, have relied on mobile networks to distribute the internet and to use them as a foundation for economic development. Past studies have primarily focused on ICT infrastructure (e.g., phone and internet) without its separation into wired and mobile networks. However, this approach does not consider the potential offsetting effects between wired and mobile ICT on national productivity and thus may distort empirical re sults. By dividing ICT into wired and mobile, the present research ad dresses this issue by showing that mobile ICT influences the productivity of the developing countries but has no impact on developed countries productivity. Our results are consistent with the prior studies doc umenting leapfrogging using mobile phone penetration data before 2010 [58–61]. Because enhanced mobile networks form the foundation of advanced ICT fields such as IoT and cyber-physical systems (CPS), mobile ICT can be an important indicator of ICT growth potential in developing countries.

Second, we use the internet speed as an indicator of ICT. Past research has adopted ICT data such as ICT investment [ICT capital stock] [22], technology possession [patents and new products] [71], and ICT infrastructure [telephone/internet subscriptions] [23,24]. Among these variables, telephone/internet subscriptions can be used as a quantitative measure of ICT. However, the variables do not consider the qualitative perspective of the internet. Internet speed data enable us to measure the impact of the quality of ICT, because increased speeds allow people to increase the convenience, number of applications and web-based con tents. As a result, increased internet speed improves the quality of business, education, and public services [15,18,20]. By using the internet speed data to capture the quality of ICT, we examine the impact of qualitative improvements in ICT on national productivity by the incorporation of the quantitative aspects of ICT.

## 5.1. Implications

Our result shows the importance of mobile ICT in developing coun tries. There are practical implications for firms’ managerial decisions, academic research, and country-level policies. Our study provides several important managerial implications. First, our result suggests that the quality of mobile ICT should be considered as one of the important factors when a multinational enterprise (MNE) wants to expand its business into a developing country. For example, Zhu and Jeon [72] argued that ICT helps firms transmit knowledge across countries effi ciently. They also pointed out that teleconferencing or image trans mission technologies not only reduce communication costs but also increase the distribution of tacit knowledge. Their results suggest that if a firm has a plan to establish a subsidiary in developing countries, the transfer of knowledge from the firm to its subsidiary is critical to make the business successful [73]. Furthermore, the positive effect of mobile ICT on the GDP implies that the local subsidiary of MNE may benefit from the increased GDP which is often positively correlated with average market demand. Consequently, the quality of mobile ICT can be an important factor for MNE’s investment decisions. Similarly, institu tional investors and financial analysts may also consider the quality of mobile ICT when they make an investment decision or a stock-coverage decision in developing countries.

Second, Kearney [74] stated that one of the most important factors in choosing foreign markets to invest in is the country’s technological and innovation capabilities. Foreign direct investment (FDI) involves “establishing warehouses, manufacturing facilities, and other perma nent or long-term organizations abroad” [75]. Gholami et al. [76] argue that “ICT contributes to economic growth indirectly by attracting more foreign direct investment” (p.11). Based on the findings in our paper, foreign direct investors should consider the quality of mobile infra structure more than the wired infrastructure when making a direct in vestment in developing countries. To connect IoT devices and systems, the infrastructure of mobile ICT is essential. When a company that produces IT-related products and services wants to expand its business to emerging markets, it can be important to include mobile ICT indicators such as mobile internet speed in its analysis of the level of mobile infrastructure and the feasibility of business success in the countries.

Lastly, unlike domestic mergers and acquisitions (M&A), crossborder M&A brings greater advantages to the acquiring firm through global diversification [77] and creates value for the acquiring firm [78]. Being the most popular method of foreign direct investment (FDI), cross-border M&As account for more than 85% of foreign direct in vestment [79]. Economic growth in acquiring countries increases the value of capital available for cross-border acquisitions. Economic growth in target countries is also an important determinant of successful cross-border M&As because it increases the short-term profitability of potential target firms after the acquisitions. Given that high-quality mobile ICT improves the country’s GDP which would subsequently affect the target firm’s profitability positively, a manager who considers to acquire a company in a developing country needs to evaluate the country’s mobile ICT status and prospect rather than general ICT infrastructure in choosing the target country. Further, cross-border M&As in the ICT industries have been surging in the past years facili tated by the growth of the Internet. This also has an important impli cation to firm managers considering cross-border M&As to create new markets in developing countries.

In an academic context, academic researchers who have used tradi tional ICT factors such as telephone lines, mobile phone users, and internet users [1,24] can extend their studies on the consideration of the influence of mobile and wired internet as well as the quality of the internet on their research models. The results of this study contribute to research that examines the IT productivity paradox. Our results suggest that mixed evidence in prior literature might have been partly driven by not examining the effect of ICT between wired and mobile ICT sepa rately with different impacts between developing and developed coun tries. For example, the moderating effect of ICT infrastructures on the relationship between ICT investment and national productivity [23] can be examined separately by adding the wired and wireless internet speeds as types of infrastructure that improve the quality of information services. Additionally, the research focusing on the role of ICT in the na tional well-being [24] can be extended by applying the lens of the speed of wired vs. wireless in developed vs. developing countries.

Our results suggest that the positive effect of mobile ICT is significant in developing countries while it does not have a significant effect in developed countries. According to Levi-Bliech et al. [80], the key ben efits of mobile ICT at the organizational level are “working remotely, operating in virtual space, efficient employee interaction” (p309), resulting in increasing flexibility, enhancing performance, and reducing costs. Our results provide an important implication on how firms or organizations interact differently between mobile ICT and wired ICT in developing and developed countries. Furthermore, our results may be useful to make decisions on whether to focus on wired ICT or wireless ICT to reap the benefits of ICT in firm management.

Additionally, policymakers in underdeveloped countries can use these findings to support ICT investment in mobile network infrastruc ture to efficiently boost their economies. There could be many limita tions on the ICT investment in those countries due to a limited budget and underdeveloped ICT infrastructure. Our results suggest that the policymakers should primarily focus on mobile ICT to optimize the ICT investment.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research directions

Because of data availability, our sample covers only six years. Thi relatively short period might not be long enough to capture and compare the full impact of mobile ICT. Therefore. future work could extend the number of countries and the time frame of analysis. The fact that mobile ICT is meaningful only in developing countries does not mean that developed countries should avoid investment in mobile ICT. Currently, mobile technologies are mainly limited to the telecommunication in dustry, but they will contribute to national economic growth by being actively combined with other advanced information technologies such as IoT and CPS. Therefore, future research can also examine how the synergy between mobile ICT and advanced ICT trends changes the patterns of contribution to productivity.

## 6. Conclusion

Mobile and wired ICT may have a heterogeneous effect on developed and developing counties. Thus, results and policy recommendations that combine the two measures could be misleading. For instance, they could give inaccurate information to developing countries considering in vestments in ICT, where mobile internet infrastructure rather than wired internet infrastructure boosts the economy. Therefore, it is necessary to analyze the effects of ICT divided into wired and mobile to more accu rately measure the influence of ICT on national productivity. By adopting a lens that distinguishes wired and mobile ICT, we find that mobile ICT plays an important role in increasing national productivity in developing countries. However, we do not find a significant impact of wired ICT for either developed or developing countries. These results show that the increased quality of internet services has contributed to the increase in the developing countries’ GDP through mobile ICT, which can be installed easily and cheaply as compared to wired net works. Our results are also consistent with the notion that developing countries prefer a mobile ICT to wired ICT because of a lack of civil infrastructure that is critical to set up a wired ICT.
