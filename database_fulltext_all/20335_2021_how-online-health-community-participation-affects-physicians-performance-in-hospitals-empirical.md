# How online health community participation affects physicians performance in hospitals: Empirical evidence from China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Multi-channel strategy Channel effect Online health communities Performance Online and offline

## A B S T R A C T

There have been heated ongoing debates about the pros and cons of the online channel to traditional services in hospitals. We examine the digital channel’s effect on healthcare from the trust-transfer and medical service continuity perspectives by focusing on key indicators of performance: outpatient visits, operational efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, patient loyalty, and satisfaction and analyzing 250,344 physician–patient in teractions from an online health community (OHC) and a well-known hospital in China over the period from January 2010 to December 2018 through three studies. Our work offers managerial insights on performance improvement by adopting a multi-channel strategy in the emerging era of “Internet plus healthcare.”

## 1. Introduction

For the first time in 2015, “Internet plus healthcare” was written into the Chinese government work report to promote the development of online health services, and physicians can spend their free time on thirdparty platforms, such as GoodDoctor (www.haodf.com) to provide pa tients with high-quality and comprehensive medical services [1]. As the integration of traditional health services and information technology matures, online health service is expected to fundamentally restructure the healthcare ecosystem by leveraging rich digital information and advanced artificial intelligence technologies and address challenges, such as lack of access and high costs of medical services in China.

Online health communities (OHCs) where physicians can provide online health services have created additional touchpoints where phy sicians can interact with patients and extend their services from hospi tals to the online channel. Different from traditional medical services in hospitals, OHCs have their unique characteristics in terms of their ser vice patterns, profit model, and their transformation of offline health care [2]. In particular, OHCs provide a) diversified services, including online written consultation (mainly by text, photo, and voice), tele phone consultation, video consultation, and outpatient registration services; b) pricing flexibility—physicians are free to price their services and the platforms set a profit-sharing mechanism where physicians will be paid if patients order their services. Therefore, physicians can gain “sunshine income” by providing high-quality online services; and c) reputation mechanism, including recommendation, thank-you notes, virtual gifts, star ratings from patients, and “best physicians” of each year evaluated by the platforms. These forms of reputation help physi cians build their brand and eventually increase sales and patients’ loy alty [3].

Despite the concept of using OHCs to overcome distance and time barriers was brought up many years ago [4], the adoption rate of OHCs remains low in hospitals (less than 50%), especially in medium- or low-ranked ones. Some health service providers still focus on offering services in traditional offline hospitals [5]. The main obstacle to adop tion comes from hospital managers who are not optimistic about the influences of OHCs on their hospitals [6]. The potential benefits and drawbacks of online health services to medical services in hospitals have sparked considerable ongoing debates among policymakers, healthcare organizations, and physicians, but little empirical research has been done to investigate the digital channel’s effect on healthcare. With the rapid development of online health services, it is necessary to provide healthcare decision-makers, such as hospital managers with a clear picture of the influences of OHC participation on traditional hospitals

Previous research has studied the relationships between online and offline channels [7], relationships between digital and physical products [8], and the impact of multi-channel marketing campaigns on sales [9]. These studies reveal both positive and negative spillover effects of the online channel on the offline one [10–12], which results in an ongoing debate on whether e-commerce investments create economic value [13, 14]. These results do not apply in the context of expert services. Health service, where service content and quality can vary substantially across channels and patients engage in multiple visits to obtain a continuous and satisfactory outcome, is different from retail [15]. Little is known about the channel’s effect in healthcare. In addition, most of these existing studies are conceptual and anecdotal [16,17], or based on survey data [17,18]. Existing studies primarily focused on exploring OHC’s internal operation mechanisms [20–23]. A key research chal lenge is studying the impact of online services in OHCs on offline per formance in traditional offline hospitals.

Driven by practical problems and the theoretical gaps, our research makes an initial effort in using objective, public data to gauge the impact of the online channel on offline performance, especially in healthcare. Based on the trust-transfer theory that is widely used in the crosschannel context and the special characteristic of health service s—medical service continuity, we aim to reveal the channel effect in healthcare and provide decision-makers with guidance on multi-channel strategies, addressing the following question in healthcare: How do physicians’ online health services affect their performance offline? Specially, we focus on outpatient visits, operational efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, patient loyalty, and patient satisfaction, which are important performance indicators in practice and have been examined in existing studies. In addition, we answer the question of how does physicians offline performance varies depending on their involvement in online health services on OHCs?

To answer the above-mentioned research question, we follow phy sicians’ activities on an OHC and their performance indicators in a hospital. Using objective secondary panel data collected from the OHC and the hospital from January 2010 to December 2018, we apply Pro pensity Score Matching-Difference in Differences (PSM-DID) and indi vidual–time-fixed effects panel data model to address the endogeneity concerns. We find strong support for the hypothesis that participating in the additional online channel significantly improves a physician’s off line performance, with a higher number of outpatient visits, less waiting time, higher patient satisfaction and loyalty in the offline channel. Furthermore, a high level of involvement in the online channel is more important in improving offline performance than a mere online pres ence. This empirical study fills the gap of empirical studies on a deep understanding of channel effects in healthcare and contributes to the literature on OHCs, outcome of hospital information technology adop tion, and continuity of medical care. Based on our findings, we provide practical insights to health service providers in the era of “Internet plus.”

## 2. Research background

## 2.1. Debates on “Internet plus healthcare”

The “Internet plus healthcare” is a new application of the Internet in the medical industry. It includes online consultation, electronic pre scription, remote consultation, remote treatment and rehabilitation, and other forms of health and medical services with the Internet as the carrier and technical means. The Chinese government has issued a series of policies and regulations to actively promote the development of “Internet plus healthcare” and the multi-site practice policies further facilitated the fast development of “Internet plus healthcare.” Although the physicians’ multi-site practice has been progressing slowly due to hospital managers’ concerns about physicians’ work in their primary place of practice in hospitals, online platforms allow physicians to provide medical service using their free time and make physicians multi-site practice possible [24].

Policies related to physicians’ multi-site licenses enable physicians to maximize their service capacity and further promote the development of online health services [25]. Online health services cover all segments of the healthcare field supported by the Internet, and the value of the in dustrial chain is redistributed with the penetration of new technologies and business models [25]. From the patient’s perspective, online consultation, disease information search, communication with physi cians, chronic disease management help solve many basic health prob lems. Online health platforms can effectively help save time and improve efficiency through making various functions, such as outpatient registration, queuing, and test report querying available online. From the physicians’ perspective, they can use online health services to improve their work efficiency. Online consultation and patient feedback can also help improve their popularity and income. From the hospital’s perspective, on the one hand, online health services are conducive to information construction and operational efficiency. On the other hand, they help expand the scope of services and greatly improve the brand influence.

Although online health services have developed quickly in recent years, there are still a large number of physicians and hospitals that take a wait-and-see approach, and some are even skeptical. The primary concerns of hospitals include: a) the high work intensity may lower physicians’ efficiency in traditional hospitals as “one’s energy is limited” [6], especially physicians also need to conduct scientific research in addition to intense clinical work in China [26] and b) online health services may exacerbate patients’ difficulty in accessing medical ser vices in traditional hospitals [6]. Adding an online channel to the traditional one may bring potential channel conflicts and result in one channel cannibalizing the sales from the other [27]. However, advocates argue that as an important supplement of physicians’ diagnosis and treatment services in hospitals, these third-party platforms with services using their spare time could help physicians build brands and improve their reputation and popularity, which may help attract more patients to hospitals [28]. The provision of online services helps reach new patients, and the potential spillover effects may result in increased purchases and decreased costs [29]. Patient motivations in the online and offline channels are often different, and the multi-channel strategy is often useful in matching respective patients [30]. In addition, the online and offline combination can greatly improve physician-patient interaction efficiency in hospitals [31].

With the rapid development of online health services, it is necessary to provide healthcare decision-makers with a clear picture of the in fluences of online platforms’ participation on traditional hospitals.

## 2.2. The trust-transfer theory

The trust-transfer theory refers to a cognitive process in which a person’s trust in an entity can be derived from his trust in a related entity [32], and perceived similarity and entitativity are two representative factors used in capturing the relationships between the source and the target [33]. Perceived similarity, often referred to as perceived fit or consistency in other studies, drives people to form similar perceptions regarding these parties [34]. Meanwhile, perceived entitativity, also known as a business tie or perceived integration, drives people to believe that the source and target share certain external cues [34]. Therefore, given the internal and external relationships, the perceptions of people toward one entity can be transferred from the experience with the other entity.

Some studies have examined the trust-transfer process in the crosschannel context in other contexts, such as banking [35] and mobile brokerage services [36]. These studies reveal that consumers’ trust in offline services significantly influences their perceptions regarding their online counterpart, including perceived satisfaction and perceived extent of future use [35], whereas consumers’ trust in online services has a positive effect on their trust in offline services belonging to the same brand [36]. Trust can influence usage behavior [37] and is an important condition of a successful commercial transaction [38]. Given the context of the present study, a patient considers many factors, such as time, cost, and effort when deciding whether to go to a hospital for face-to-face service. The trust in a physician’s online services (entity A) can be transferred to the physician’s offline services (entity B). Therefore, the trust built online may motivate patients to visit physicians in hospitals.

## 2.3. The medical service continuity

Based on prior studies, the continuity of medical service can be explained from different perspectives [39,40]. The continuity of medical service includes: a) information continuity where relevant information can be effectively transferred and shared; b) service continuity where the whole treatment process is complete; c) interpersonal relation continuity where service providers and patients understand each other and build trust; d) experience continuity where the service experience for the whole service process is coordinated and uninterrupted; and e) geography continuity where the provision of medical service is not influenced by patients’ geographic location.

Information technology can be used to support medical service co ordination and continuity [41], and thus influence performance [42]. Patients often face gaps in the information after they leave hospitals [43]. The “Internet plus healthcare” helps strengthen the interconnec tion and intercommunication of various information systems to achieve the continuity of medical services [44]. Although online health services cannot fully meet the needs of patients as they cannot provide treat ments [45], online platforms connect physicians and patients in advance and supplement the services in hospitals effectively [45] by providing daily consultation and follow-up consultation. For example, after Yanda hospital in the Hebei province in China established a cooperative rela tionship with Chunyu Yisheng in 2017, the medical service continuity has improved tremendously, with the average daily number of new patients increased by 200 and patient satisfaction increased significantly [46].

There exists diverse research on the different purchasing stages with different triangulations of the underlying process. A three-stage model, including a pre-purchase stage, a purchase stage, and a post-purchase stage has been proposed [47]. During the pre-purchase stage, including information seeking and consultation, service information can be compared more easily through the online channel [48]. During the purchase stage, the notion of risk perceptions affects consumers decision-making [49]. With the introduction of the online channel, the occurring risks changed [50]. Online channel experience helps patients get to know the physicians and reduce perceived risk. During the post-purchase stage, follow-up services are provided. In healthcare, online health services provide convenience to patients for the pre- and post-purchase stages and offline services should mainly focus on providing purchase stage services. Both transfers “from online health services to offline treatments” and “from offline treatments to online health services” help promote medical service continuity.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Prior studies on channel effects were mainly conducted in the retail setting and revealed both positive and negative results [10–12]. However, these results do not apply in the context of expert services (e.g., health services) for three reasons. First, service contents are different across channels in healthcare, thus online services cannot completely replace offline services in hospitals. Online health services cannot fully meet the needs of patients as they cannot provide treatments [45]. Second, service quality is directly related to the health or safety of pa tients, thus patients are likely to go to the hospitals for face-to-face services, especially when they feel that they have a serious disease. Due to this perceived serious disease, some even visit several physicians for second opinions. Third, higher continuity is associated with higher-quality health care [51]. Patients with a close, continuous rela tionship with a specific physician are more likely to receive the rec ommended care [52]. Therefore, health service, where service content and service quality can vary substantially across channels and patients engage in multiple visits to obtain a continuous and satisfactory outcome, is different from retail [15]. Based on the characteristics of health services, we propose a positive spillover of the channel effect based on the trust-transfer and medical service continuity perspectives.

Our study extends this stream of research on channel effect by first studying the impact of online service on outpatient service demand by collecting data from an OHC and a hospital in China. The current research extends a prior work that examined the channel’s effect on OHCs [7]. However, the prior study did not use real offline data to measure offline service demand, which may lead to biased results. Second, we enrich the existing literature on channel effect by investi gating the impact of online service and other important indicators concerning hospital performance, including operational efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, patient loyalty, and patient satisfaction. The conceptual model of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

By reviewing the literature on multi-channel strategy adoption, we find that the multi-channel strategy has several benefits, including exposure to new consumers, increased sales, enhanced brand awareness, and consumer convenience [53]. Consumers with multi-channel behavior show a higher purchase frequency and loyalty compared with those with single-channel behavior [54]. However, these studies do not recognize how one channel influences the other. Sellers are facing challenges associated with multi-channel consumer behavior [55]. There have been concerns that online activities may cannibalize offline business opportunities, and consequently can have a negative impact on profits [10]. While this issue is faced by all multi-channel sellers [11], it can be argued that this effect is more pronounced with regard to the Internet because the Internet has no geographical boundaries and sellers are unable to limit shoppers’ access to it [12]. In this study, we aim to investigate the channel effect in healthcare, especially the spillover ef fects of online channel use in the offline channel.

## 3.1. Online health services and outpatient visits

The information search, interactivity, information-intensive, and convenient nature of the Internet play a vital role in consumers’ offline purchase decision-making process [56]. Unlike other goods or services, such as books and restaurants, the stakes are high for medical services for both the physicians and patients. In the last few decades, the asymmetric information in the medical field led to a great waste of social resources and was also an important cause of the current tension be tween physicians and patients in China. The emergence of OHCs in troduces a completely different type of information channel: a) the mandatory individual information disclosure on OHCs brings more transparency to the public. Patients typically face uncertainties before purchases, so they often seek information from the Internet [57]. Pa tients search the Internet for information about physicians frequently [58], and a wealth of information help decrease uncertainty and pro mote decision-making; b) communications between physicians and peers and the feedback from peers are important references for the evaluation of physicians’ quality. The online channel helps reduce pa tient concerns of safety and quality and thus increases sales, especially at the early phases of sales [59]; and c) direct interaction with a physician helps the patient acquire the targeted advice or treatments. Although online service helps patients to some extent, services online and offline are not homogeneous [7] in healthcare, thus online services cannot completely replace offline services in hospitals. Online health services cannot fully meet the needs of patients as they cannot provide treat ments [45]. Online health services improve medical service continuity and increase patients’ choices offline. Therefore, we believe that there is a positive influence of online health services on outpatient service demand.

H1. Physicians who provide online health services on OHCs will have more outpatient visits in hospitals.

## 3.2. Online health services and operational efficiency

The operational efficiency measures a firm’s efficiency in trans forming various operational resources (e.g., number of employees) [60]. Patients’ waiting time is used as an important indicator of a hospital’s operational efficiency [61]. The online channel improves information transparency [62] including physicians’ working hours in hospitals. In addition, many physicians on OHCs also provide outpatient registration function for patients, which allows both physicians and patients to track available appointments and thus makes face-to-face treatment a quicker and more efficient process. Patients can make appointments in advance, which helps reduce the uncertainty of making appointments on-the-spot. Another benefit the online channel offers over the tradi tional channel is the capability for the physician–patient interaction and acquaintance in advance of the face-to-face appointment. A physician knows the patient’s condition in advance and can make a more efficient diagnosis in the hospital and arrange the treatment plan in advance to reduce the wait time, which may shorten the amount of time the patient spends in the hospital. When patients make an appointment in advance, they do not have to wait in line for a long time, which saves time and ensures timely treatment [63]. Physicians know patients’ conditions in advance through the appointment information and online service com munications, which help them make diagnosis efficiently and correctly. This leads us to our second hypothesis:

H2. Physicians who provide online health services on OHCs will have higher operational efficiency.

## 3.3. Online health services and resource utilization efficiency

The concept “efficiency” is used to explain variations among health services providers in their use of services, such as costs, length of stay, or procedures, to produce a hospital visit [64]. Using online services, physicians get to know patients’ conditions in advance. For some pa tients in remote areas, they may have done basic tests in local hospitals, and these test results help physicians in diagnosing the patients and there is no need to repeat the tests, especially for patients with chronic diseases. Duplicate testing is one of the main culprits for excessive waste in the healthcare system, and health information sharing across hospi tals can reduce healthcare waste [65]. Test results can be shared across channels and recorded on the platform using online services. Using a multi-channel strategy, the medical service continuity can be improved, which can minimize the duplication of tests, examinations, history taking, and procedures [66]. Before going to hospitals, patients know what test items may be required through online services, whether they need to take samples, whether fasting is required, and what issues they need to be aware of [67]. Patients are probably already aware of the key treatments and disease information by communicating with physician in advance, participating in the disease diagnosis and treatment process. This helps avoid inefficient resource utilization. This leads us to our

third hypothesis:

H3. Physicians who provide online health services on OHCs will have higher resource utilization efficiency in hospitals.

## 3.4. Online health services and patient satisfaction

Patient satisfaction is concerned with the degree to which the ex pectations of a patient are fulfilled by the health services [68]. Using online and offline service channels concurrently, service providers can adapt to consumers’ needs and shopping patterns [69]. A prior study revealed that service qualities in different stages have different impacts on patient satisfaction [20]. Multichannel services give patients more choices that help improve their satisfaction [2,70] and provide sellers with many opportunities to gather information about consumers’ needs and, therefore, adapt not only to the content of the information provided to the consumers but also to the communication style [71]. For example, physicians have already known patients’ conditions by communicating online in advance and the communication efficiency in hospitals can be improved greatly. Patients enjoy higher satisfaction when their needs are met over a wide variety of medical services [2]. Service quality is directly related to the health or safety of patients and can be improved dramatically by integrating the online channel with the offline one. For example, the online channel provides physicians’ information and feedback from peers, which help decrease physician–patient informa tion asymmetry. As an important supplement, online services provide convenience for information gathering and follow-up treatments. This leads us to our fourth hypothesis:

H4. Physicians who provide online health services on OHCs will have higher patient satisfaction in hospitals

## 3.5. Online health services and patient loyalty

Loyalty is a positive propensity for a firm or brand [72], and re-visit intention is used as a surrogate for patient loyalty in the healthcare field [68]. Multi-channel consumers have shown increased purchase fre quency and increased loyalty compared with single-channel ones [62]. It also helps sellers understand consumers’ decision-making process and then improve retention by tracking their behavior across channels [73]. A multi-channel strategy can provide consumers with a convenient and lovalty-building experience across a number of channels [74] and improve service continuity associated with higher-quality health care [51]. Patients with a close, continuous relationship with a specific physician are more likely to receive the recommended care [52] and have a higher loyalty. Online health services help extend services from the hospitals, especially for patients who need long-term treatments. A stable and long-term physician–patient relationship and interaction help patients gain better treatment and improve medical service continuity [44]. Under multi-channel services, the harmonization increases the switching barrier (e.g., switching cost and past behavior) for consumers [75], and a higher switching barrier reduces intentions of seller transfer [76]. This leads us to our fifth hypothesis:

H5. Physicians who provide online health services on OHCs will have higher patient loyalty in hospitals.

## 3.6. Level of involvement in online health services

The differences in the ways physicians use the OHCs may lead to differences in performance. The level of involvement influences the extent of interactions between physicians and patients on OHCs, and helps them to get to know each other. When a physician and a patient have a strong connection, affectivity and compulsoriness will play major roles in their interaction process [77], which help physicians serve pa tients conscientiously [78]. The mere online presence does not neces sarily yield significant performance improvement and that the frequent use of the Internet with the integration of the existing channels could be the key to achieving spillover effects [12,47]. For example, although some physicians have opened online health services, they do not check in frequently or use platform resources efficiently. Based on the data, we collected from the Good Doctor, more than a third of physicians have not logged into the Good Doctor over the last three days. Therefore, we investigate the impacts of involvement level with online health services on offline performance.

High involvement in online health services helps physicians in the following ways. First, it improves the richness of available information (physician’s information disclosure, communications between physi cians and peers, feedback from peers, and direct interactions with physicians), which help improve medical service continuity and increase patients’ choices offline. Second, it allows physicians to get to know patients’ conditions better in advance with adequate and real-time in teractions online, which helps minimize the duplication of tests, exam inations, history taking, and procedures. Third, for physicians with high involvement, patients are probably already aware of the key treatment and disease information by communicating with them in advance, which helps physicians make more efficient and accurate diagnosis. Fourth, knowing patients’ conditions online in advance through OHC helps improve the communication efficiency in hospitals, decreases physician–patient information asymmetry, and fulfills patients’ needs. Fifth, multi-channel strategy can provide consumers with a convenient and loyalty-building experience across channels [74]. High involvement in an OHC allows physicians to keep a close, continuous relationship with patients, which helps improve patient loyalty. A high level of involvement in the online channel is more important in improving off line performance than a mere online presence. Hence, we propose Hy pothesis 6 based on Hypotheses 1–5:

H6. Physicians with a high level of involvement in online health services on OHCs will have a higher performance in outpatient visits (H6a), operational efficiency (H6b), resource utilization efficiency (H6c), patient satisfaction (H6d), and patient loyalty (H6e) in hospitals.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Institutional knowledge

## 4.1.1. OHC—the Good Doctor

One of the major data sources we use to understand the offline im pacts of online actions is the Good Doctor (www.haodf.com). The Good Doctor is a major physician–patient interaction platform that provides diverse services in China. Its prominence was the reason we chose the Good Doctor for this study. The Good Doctor was launched in 2006 and currently is the largest and most professional OHC in China. More than 582,000 physicians’ information is presented and more than 200,000 physicians actively work on the Good Doctor. Three main services are provided by physicians to serve patients: written (text-photos) consul tation, telephone consultation, and outpatient registration service.<sup>1</sup> The multiple services provided is another reason for choosing the Good Doctor. Via the Good Doctor site, patients can choose services based on their needs. There are more than 3 million unique site visitors and nearly 300,000 patients seeking help every day. Moreover, this platform pro vides comprehensive information about each physician, including authenticated individual information and patient feedback, which is important for this study.

## 4.1.2. Hospital

We also collected data from one of the top-tier comprehensive hos pitals in China. It has more than 7000 employees with around 1000 physicians. The Good Doctor is the main platform for physicians from this hospital to provide online consultation services and few physicians from this hospital have participated in other OHCs, which is helpful for us in getting reliable results. At present, physicians from this hospital have provided online services to nearly 200,000 patients, the total homepage views have reached about 17.53 million, and patient satis faction is 96% (8058 positive comments and 189 negative comments). At the end of 2018, 589, 278, and 106 physicians from this hospital have provided written consultation, telephone, and outpatient registration service on the Good Doctor, respectively.

## 4.2. Data collection

We combined data from the Good Doctor and the hospital. We developed a program to crawl data on the Good Doctor. In order to examine the outcomes of OHC participation, for each physician who worked in the hospital, we collected his or her activities on the Good Doctor from January 2010 to December 2018 and calculated the vari ables on a monthly basis and obtained the following data:

## 4.2.1. Time for the initiation of online services

We observed and recorded the OHC start time (to provide online health service) for each physician, namely, whether and when the physician started to provide online health services (written consultation or telephone consultation).

## 4.2.2. Cumulative contribution

The cumulative contribution is calculated based on a series of the physician’s activities, including publishing medical articles, answering patients’ questions, and updating information regularly and timely. We collected each physician’s cumulative contribution on the Good Doctor to measure the physician’s involvement level.

## 4.2.3. Physician’s individual information

Medical title, gender, education, returnee, and department were collected.

## 4.2.4. The entire physician–patient interaction contents

On the Good Doctor, the entire interaction process between a physician and a patient has been recorded, including the patient’s questions, the physician’s replies, and the patient’s feedback (satisfaction and gratitude expression).

From the hospital, we collected the following physicians’ data from January 2010 to December 2018 and calculated the variables on a monthly basis:

## 4.2.5. Daily outpatient records

For each physician, we obtained all patients he has treated in the hospital.

## 4.2.6. Patient-hospital interaction time points

For each patient, we collected various time points when she was in the hospital, including registration, tests (i.e., computed radiography, computed tomography, magnetic resonance, and ultrasound B), picking up medications, and discharges from the hospital.

## 4.2.7. Source of registration

Source of registration, namely, whether a patient registered in the hospital or via the Internet. A physician’s online actions are more likely to have significant effects on patients with online medical information seeking/consultation experiences, and these patients are more likely to register via the Internet [7]. As it is difficult for patients to get an appointment with a specific physician via the registration windows in hospitals in China, this measure helps us identify the relationship be tween online health services and offline performance more accurately. Therefore, by collecting the registration source for each patient, we were able to include only those outpatient records with registrations made via the Internet.

We matched physicians from the Good Doctor and the hospital by their names and departments.

## 4.3. Measurement

Fig. 2 shows an example of a physician’s homepage on the Good Doctor and Fig. 3 shows two physicians with a comparison on whether they work online on the Good Doctor. We conducted two empirical studies to investigate offline spillovers from online participation.

Based on the data sources and empirical methods, three datasets were created. In Study 1, data from both the Good Doctor and the hospital were combined to examine the influence of online participation on offline performance, including outpatient visits, operational effi ciency, resource utilization efficiency, and patient loyalty using PSM DID. In Study 2, data collected from the Good Doctor were used to investigate the relationship between online participation and patient satisfaction with offline services using a t-test. In Study 3, data from both the Good Doctor and the hospital were collected to analyze the impact of involvement level with online health services on offline performance using individual-time fixed effects panel data model.

The dependent variables in Study 1 are outpatient visits, opera tional efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, and patient loyalty. We used the dataset from the hospital to calculate outpatient visits, opera tional efficiency, resources utilization efficiency, and patient loyalty. ## 4.3.1. Outpatient visits

We calculated the number of outpatient visits for Physician i in the hospital at time t (OffP<sub>it</sub>).

## 4.3.2. Operational efficiency

Patients’ waiting time is widely used to measure a hospital’s oper ational efficiency [61]. Thus, we used the average lag between regis tration time and treatment time for Physician i at time t in the hospital (Register\_wait ), the average lag between registration time and departure time (measured by the time of prescription pick up) for Physician i at time t in the hospital (Total\_time ), and the average lag between treat ment time and payment time for Physician i at time t in the hospital (Pay\_time ) to measure operational efficiency.

## 4.3.3. Resource utilization efficiency

Duplicate testing is one of the main culprits for excessive waste in the healthcare system and is widely used to measure a hospital’s resource utilization efficiency [65]. Thus, we added up the average number of tests done per patient in the hospital for Physician i at time t (No.Test ) to measure the resource utilization efficiency.

## 4.3.4. Patient loyalty

Retention, often measured by purchase frequency, can represent customer loyalty [62]. Thus, we reviewed registration records in the hospital for all patients to calculate the average number of revisits per patient for Physician i at time t in the hospital (Revisit ) as a measure of patient loyalty.

## 4.3.5. Patient satisfaction

As the Good Doctor allows patients to connect with their physicians using the “sign up after receiving service in hospitals" function three times for free after their hospital visits (see Fig. 6), we calculated the length of gratitude expressions from the patient-physician communica tion and whether patients have sent virtual gifts or thank you-notes to their physicians, which are used to measure patient satisfaction in a prior study [20]. The average length of gratitude expressed for Physician i at time t on the Good Doctor (Word\_Thanks ) and whether Physician i at time t has received thank you-notes or virtual gifts from their patients on the Good Doctor (Action\_Thanks ) were used to measure patient satis faction. Table 2 summarizes the definitions and measurements of the variables in Study 2 (other variables refer to Table 1).

The independent variable in Studies 1 and 2 is whether Physician i has provided online services, and the independent variable in Study 3 is Physician i’s involvement level in online health services. For each physician, we measured his or her cumulative contribution (calculated based on a series of the physician’s activities, including publishing medical articles, answering patients’ questions, and updating the in formation regularly and timely) at time t on the Good Doctor to measure the physician’s involvement level. The definitions and measurements of the variables in Study 3 are shown in Table 1.

We investigate the outcomes for participating in OHCs by a) comparing the differences in outpatient visits, operational efficiency, resources utilization efficiency, and patient loyalty between physicians who provided online services (treatment group) and those who did not (control group) based on data from the Good Doctor and the hospital and b) comparing the differences in patient satisfaction between patients who have consulted with physicians before their hospital visits (treat ment group) and patients who went to the hospital directly (control group) based on data from the Good Doctor only.

The control variables used in the models, include medical title, gender, education, and returnee. Tasks in different departments have vastly different levels of complexity and interdependence which may lead to different physician behaviors and performance [79]. Therefore, we also controlled the department that the physician worked in.

As the time a physician started providing online health services may be different for different physicians in the treatment group (see Fig. 7), we matched Physician i in the treatment group to the physicians in the control group based on data at the time (i.e., Physician i started providing online health services), namely, we used an unbalanced PSM-DID model. Medical title, gender, and department were used in selecting the matching sample in the PSM. We used the following equation as the PSM-DID model:

$$
\text { OffPerformance } _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} G _ {i} \times T _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} G _ {i} + \beta_ {3} T _ {i t} + \varphi Z _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{1}
$$

Let $i = 1 , . . . , N$ indexes the physician. OffPerformance captures offline performance, including outpatient visits, operational efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, patient satisfaction, and loyalty. $T _ { i t }$ is a dummy variable that indicates if the period is before $( T _ { i t } = 0 )$ or after the time that Physician i began to provide online health services $( T _ { i t } = 1 ) . G _ { i }$ is the treatment dummy that indicates whether Physician i is in the treatment group $( G _ { i } = 1 )$ or control group $( G _ { i } = 0 ) . \beta s$ are coefficients to be estimated. Z is the control variable.

We used the following equation to estimate the individual–time fixed effects panel data model:

$$
\text { OffPerformance } _ {i t} = \dot {\beta_ {0}} + \dot {\beta_ {1}} \text { Contribution } _ {i t} + \varphi^ {\prime} Z _ {i t} + \gamma_ {i} t + u _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{2}
$$

where t is time-fixed effects and u is individual-fixed effects.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

This study provides empirical evidence on the impacts of the online channel on a hospital’s offline performance as reflected in its physicians performance, including outpatient visits, operational efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, patient loyalty, and patient satisfaction. We address the endogeneity concerns posted by self-selection by using nine years of data and establishing a robust quantitative relationship between online actions and offline performance. Overall, based on three studies, we find a physician who provides online health services has a higher number of outpatient visits, less waiting time, higher patient satisfaction, and loyalty in the offline channel. Therefore, Hypotheses 1, 4, and 5 are supported; Hypotheses 2 and 6 are partly supported; and Hypothesis 3 is not supported.

We find strong support for the hypotheses that participating in the online channel significantly improves a physician’s service demand and patient satisfaction in the hospital in the offline channel. These results are consistent with the trust-transfer theory and continuity of medical care, namely online channel use helps patients get more information on the physician and establish trust, and thus generate demands and improve satisfaction. We also find the significant impacts of online health services on operational efficiency in hospitals. However, we do not observe a significant influence of online health services on resource utilization efficiency. The reason may be because a patient’s condition may change from one visit to another and it may need a re-test to detect the changes [65]. This may be because physicians often exhibit “defensive medicine,” where they tend to overuse tests in order to fend off future litigations [81]. Our results indicate that a high level of involvement in the online channel is more important in improving performance than mere online presence.

## 6.1. Implications for research

Our work makes contributions to theory along four dimensions. First, the literature on the benefit of the online channel is conceptual and anecdotal [16,17], or based on survey data [18,19]. Our research makes an initial effort to use objective secondary data to gauge the impact of the online channel on offline performance. Moreover, prior studies mainly focus on channel effects in the product or other service fields [82]. They focus on such fields where products in online and offline channels are the same, such as books [83]. Studies on this subject are insufficient in service fields, especially healthcare. The impact of the online channel for physicians or hospitals in general still has not been fully investigated. From the medical service continuity perspective and based on the differences in medical services in online and offline channels, our results show that there are spillover effects in the offline channel of online channel use. Services on OHCs are a complementary resource for patients and an effective way to maintain good phys ician–patient relationships. Our study contributes to the literature on channel effects in healthcare.

Second, OHCs have become very popular in recent years. However, existing studies on OHCs mainly focus on exploring user behavior online [70]. To the best of our knowledge, the offline outcomes for physicians participation in OHCs are hitherto unexplored. Our research serves as the first attempt to empirically examine the offline impacts of a physi cian’s activities online and contributes to the emerging literature on OHCs by investigating an important and timely question: What are the influences of OHCs on traditional hospitals from the performance perspective, including outpatient visits, operational efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, patient loyalty, and patient satisfaction. Our research using a panel dataset directly from a large OHC platform and an offline hospital discovered cross-channel effects in healthcare.

Third, this paper contributes to the literature on the outcome of hospital information technology adoption by focusing on the “Internet plus healthcare” pattern which is the application of information tech nology in the medical field. Existing studies have extensively investi gated the effects of health information technology in hospitals. However, they mainly focus on the outcomes of health information technology systems such as electronic health records and computerized physician order entry use [84]. To a greater extent than ever before, communicating electronically and on the go is how people connect [85], but few studies have been conducted to examine the adoption of tele communications technologies and their effects in healthcare. Our research explores potential uses of electronic communication such as social media to seek performance improvement in healthcare.

Fourth, the literature on the key issues involving continuity of medical care includes hospital utilization [86] and coordination of care [87]. Moreover, the relationship between patient satisfaction and con tinuity of medical care has gained academic interest [88]. However, these studies mainly focus on the offline environment and generally examine the continuity of medical care by recognizing whether it is the same physician that provides services on the patients’ return visits [89]. Our study contributes to the literature on outcomes of continuity of medical care by extending physicians’ services to different environ ments. We find that using the online channel to improve the continuity of medical care help improve patient satisfaction significantly.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

Our research also provides several important implications for man agement. First, we provide practical insights to physicians and hospitals in the era of “Internet plus.” Numerous articles in the popular press have claimed that there are many potential benefits and drawbacks of the online channel. However, because of the lack of empirical evidence that supports these claims, some hospitals and physicians are still not convinced of the value of the online channel and thus are reluctant to migrate to the online channel. This is the reason that most physicians are not fully engaged in online activities. Our findings regarding the sig nificant positive impacts of online health services on offline outpatient visits, operational efficiency, and patient satisfaction suggest that hos pitals can improve their performance by encouraging their physicians to work on OHCs.

Second, hospitals can also establish their own OHCs as part of their service offering and integrate online platforms in daily patient man agement. The Internet helps increase the reach and capacity of hospitals. By joining on the Internet, the information asymmetry between physi cians and patients can be greatly reduced. The medical service conti nuity (consultation, treatment, and follow-up service) could be enhanced, and thus improving patients’ experience and satisfaction.

Third, although many physicians do participate on the online channel, their use of the online channel is still at its infancy and thus has little discernible impacts. By considering the involvement level in the online channel, we find the effects of online channel participation become significant when it is fully used. Therefore, physicians should make full use of the online channel in their daily work. In addition, physicians can encourage their patients to use online services to improve the continuity of medical care, thus improving patient satisfaction and loyalty.

Fourth, our findings have direct implications for the managers of OHCs. Having a large number of physicians can be an important success factor for OHCs. Because of the synergy between the online channel and offline hospitals, platform operators could actively establish cooperative relationships with hospitals, attracting more hospitals to join OHCs. Moreover, the functions of the online platforms should be enhanced to serve physicians and patients better. Examples include introducing more types of services and reminding and encouraging physicians to serve patients online regularly and timely.

Fifth, our study has practical implications for policymakers. Our results provide a reference for policymakers to guide and standardize policies in healthcare to promote the development of “Internet plus healthcare” such as policies on Internet hospitals and multi-site practices.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research directions

Although this research has highlighted several notable findings and contributions, we acknowledge some limitations. First, although the Good Doctor is the main platform for physicians to serve patients online in China, some physicians may also work on other OHCs and we did not observe physicians’ activities on other OHCs. Second, we could not match patients online and offline as their information is hidden for privacy. Therefore, whether a patient has searched or communicated with physicians is unknown. Third, we cloud not exclude the case that physicians who participate in OHCs may encourage patients to go to their hospitals. Fourth, we only obtained data from an OHC and a hos pital, and the results need to be cross-validated on other OHCs and hospitals. Fifth, we measure patient satisfaction based on the interaction contents on the Good Doctor, and one potential issue of using this design is the loss of patients who are dissatisfied after their hospital visits and may not follow up online anymore. Future research could consider the above questions in the extension of our work. In addition, although we have measured the spillover effects of online participation from the perspective of physicians’ individual performance, we still cannot answer the question of the online channel use on the overall perfor mance of a hospital. Future studies can answer this question by con ducting empirical studies at the organizational level.

## 6.4. Conclusion

Using objective secondary panel data collected from an OHC and a hospital from January 2010 to December 2018, this study provides empirical evidence of the impact of the online channel on a hospital’s different indicators of performance as reflected by its physicians’ per formance, including outpatient visits, operational efficiency, resource utilization efficiency, patient loyalty, and patient satisfaction. Overall, we find strong support for the hypotheses that participating in the additional online channel significantly improves a physician’s perfor mance. Although there is evidence indicating that the mere online presence does not necessarily yield significant performance improve ment, a higher-level involvement in the online channel is found to significantly improve patients’ loyalty. Our study fills an important gap in the literature by providing a deep understanding of the impacts of online platform participation on offline performance. We address the endogeneity concerns due to self-selection by collecting nine years of data and establishing a robust quantitative relationship between online actions and offline performance.
