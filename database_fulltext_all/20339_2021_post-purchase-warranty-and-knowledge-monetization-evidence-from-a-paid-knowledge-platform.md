# Post-purchase warranty and knowledge monetization: Evidence from a paid-knowledge platform

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Post-purchase warranty Free return policy Information richness of extant reviews Paid-knowledge economy

## A B S T R A C T

Knowledge trading through online paid-knowledge platforms (i.e., knowledge monetization) has enjoyed tremendous growth in recent years. As experience goods, knowledge products often suffer from low sales due to information asymmetry. Therefore, some platforms have launched free return policies as post-purchase war ranties to reduce uncertainty and stimulate sales. The impact of free returns on knowledge product sales is explored. Using data from a Chinese paid-knowledge platform, the results suggest that a free return policy negatively influences sales. Also, this relationship is weakened by information richness of extant reviews. The findings contribute to the literature and help platforms design optimal return policies.

## 1. Introduction

The development of information technology enables retailers to sell a wide variety of products in online environments, including both physical products (e.g., electronics and clothes) and digital media products (e.g., music and video). Knowledge is also an emerging digital media experi ence product in recent years, which can be traded online, and it is usually called knowledge monetization. The estimated economy scale of knowledge monetization in China had reached 1.28 billion USD by 2018 [89], and it is expected to reach 3.46 billion USD in 2020 [37]. There fore, knowledge monetization is becoming a major and rigorous market. A knowledge provider named Yuanshen Yan in a MOOC platform received more than one million RMB (≈ 150,000 USD) in 30 days in September 2017 [36]. Nevertheless, not every knowledge provider is so successful. In Zhihu Live, the leading paid-knowledge platform in China, nearly 40 % of the Lives (a Live is similar to a speech) gain less than 1000 RMB (≈ 150 USD) in the first month after their launch. Accordingly, one common issue for paid-knowledge providers is how to monetize knowledge, i.e., encourage others to buy their knowledge.

Selling knowledge products online is not an easy task for several reasons. First, in past decades, people have been accustomed to obtaining information and/or knowledge from the Internet for free. It takes time and effort to switch people’s knowledge consumption habits from a free model to a paid model. Second, knowledge products are highly customized, and they lack standards of practice with a broad application to products. There is a wide variety of knowledge product patterns, such as recorded videos, which may last several hours (e.g., MOOC), paid Q&A (e.g., Zaihangyidian, Baidu Wenka), paid subscrip tion (e.g., iget and Himalaya), one-to-one consulting services (Zaihang), recorded short audio clips, which last 30–40 minutes (e.g., Zai hangyidian), or live broadcasting with consumers’ interactions (e.g., Zhihu Live). These product patterns are different in product models (e. g., length, consumer interaction, and product forms), quality control of knowledge providers, and paid models. Third, in e-commerce, infor mation asymmetry between sellers and consumers is a well-recognized critical issue. The issue is an even more challenging task for experi ence products like knowledge products, because quality of experience products cannot be evaluated by objective measurements. Consumers may not fully know whether an experience product will fit their needs and preferences until they gain some experience of using it [59]. How ever, unlike in conventional brick-and-mortar stores, consumers cannot touch-and-feel products in online stores. Therefore, as a typical kind of experience product, it is considerably more difficult to resolve all un certainty and evaluate quality of knowledge products, particularly in online environments.

To dispel the above concerns, some platforms have launched postpurchase warranties to relieve the tension and stress of selling knowl edge products online and stimulate consumers’ willingness to buy. Postpurchase warranty plays a remarkable role in marketing and customer relationship management. It provides various benefits to both the re tailers and consumers, such as preventing loss of consumers and nega tive word-of-mouth (WOM), revealing product problems, and promoting product images and sales [46,72]. Free return policies are the most common and representative post-purchase warranties, which allow customers to return products when they are not satisfied and get a full refund under certain conditions. Therefore, this study seeks to unravel the factors influencing knowledge consumers from the perspective of post-purchase warranties, with a particular focus on free return policies. An effective product return policy can help reduce consumers’ risk, improve customers’ satisfaction, promote new products, and enhance retailers’ long-term profitability through customer reactions (e.g., trust, WOM, commitment, and repurchase intentions) [11,41,58]. Return policies are used for diverse products; in particular, lenient return pol icies are often observed in products that are sold in remote environments (e.g., the Internet and telephone) and/or experience goods [2,32].

Although the free return policy brings several advantages, handling returned products is an expensive task [2]. Extensive research efforts have been devoted to investigating the effects of free return on retailers performances; however, results have been inconclusive [38]. Some studies show that a free return policy will promote sales [30,80,83], while others indicate that it has a negative effect on retailers’ long-term profitability [47,71]. Further, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, little attention has been paid to knowledge products in the literature. Dealing with returns of knowledge products is quite different from dealing with returns of physical products. First, the delivery costs and packing fees are extremely low. The delivery can be accomplished through the Internet, and this only costs Internet fees. Therefore, the free return of knowledge products will not generate extra costs for retailers. Second, from the customers’ point of view, knowledge products can be easily returned by clicking online buttons. They do not need to repackage the products and send them back to retailers through post offices or express delivery. Such an easy return process may trigger a higher return probability with knowledge products. Third, as a kind of digital products, knowledge products usually can only be experienced a limited part of the products before return, while physical products can be fully experienced. The limited trial of knowledge products becomes an obstacle to completely eliminate the information asymmetry. Therefore, the effects of a free return policy might differ between knowledge products and physical products. Consequently, this study seeks to answer the following key research questions: 1) Can the free return policy promote the sales of knowledge products in online market places? 2) How does information richness of extant reviews interact with the free return policy?

To explore the effects of free returns on knowledge product sales (note that sales in this study refer to the final sales, which exclude the returned sales). This work conducts an empirical study on the Zhihu Live platform, the most popular online paid-knowledge platform in China. In total, 5882 Lives (including Live information, speaker information, transaction data, and online reviews) were collected for analysis. A linear regression model is applied in empirical analysis, and propensity score matching (PSM) is introduced to address the endogeneity issue. There are two main findings obtained from this study. First, the free return policy has a surprisingly negative effect on the sales of knowledge products. Second, the information richness measured by the number of online reviews will weaken the adverse effect of free return policy.

These findings provide insights for managers of knowledge product sales platforms to help them design a better free return policy. Knowl edge product providers will benefit from more effective marketing strategies. More broadly, digital media product platforms could also enjoy benefits from the findings. From the theoretical point of view, this study significantly contributes to information system and marketing literature. First, while the extant literature primarily focuses on physical products (e.g., [22,47,58]), this study is the first attempt to empirically explore the effect of free return policies on the sales of knowledge products. Second, this study further investigates the effect of the inter action between the free return policy and information richness of extant reviews. Third, this study utilizes expected confirmation theory (ECT) to provide a new explanation to the negative effect of free return policy on sales, which is different from factors suggested in the extant literature [30,33]. The implications and contributions are elaborated in detail in the discussion section.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on product return policies, introduces signaling effect of product return policy, and ECT with their applications. Section 3 de velops hypotheses, followed by the variable constructions in Section 4. Section 5 presents the research model and the econometric analysis. The study is concluded in Section 6 by revisiting the findings, and elabo rating the managerial implications and theoretical contributions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Product return policies

Managing product returns is an expensive and challenging task, especially for products that have relatively short life cycles [2]. Prior studies largely focus on physical product returns, and the return costs may include packing fees, delivery fees, restocking fees, and operational costs [11,33,70]. According to Douthit et al. [23], the associated costs of handling returns equate to about 4% of a retailer’s sales. Therefore, when designing return policies, it is crucial for retailers to consider both the benefits and the costs. The extant studies of product returns can be classified into two categories: 1) analysis of the effects of return policies; and 2) optimization of return managements from the retailers’ point of view.

A number of studies have previously investigated the effects of product return policies through various dimensions (e.g., profitability, demand, loyalty, return probability, and repurchase behaviors); how ever, the literature is inconclusive [38]. A positive effect of lenient re turn policies on consumer demand and retailer profitability has been widely documented. In terms of customer satisfaction, the relationships between product return experience and subsequent purchase behaviors have been widely explored. For example, Mollenkopf et al. [58] indicate that previous product return experience could directly influence con sumer loyalty. More specifically, if a product is easy to return, con sumers are more likely to repurchase [22]. Similar conclusions are drawn from the work of Bower and Maxham [11], which suggests that consumers who receive free returns will universally increase their repurchases, and vice versa. The results of Griffis et al. [26] demonstrate that an effective returns management process can positively and significantly influence customers’ repurchase behavior. Further, although many studies reveal that more lenient return policies result in an increase in product return rates, its positive effect on sales will offset more than the negative effect on return costs, and then generate positive profitability [30,80,83].

However, the negative effects of lenient return policies are also extensively discussed. For example, some research indicates that lenient return policies decrease the average order value and result in an increase in the order frequency and return probability [30,47]. Therefore, Lantz and Hjort [47] suggest that it is not necessary to provide free return policies because this may have a long-term negative influence on re tailers. Similarly, the results of regression analysis by Hjort and Lantz [30] indicate that free return policies can provide short-term benefits through repeat purchases, but retailers will not gain profits from such policies due to the high costs of processing returns. Hsiao and Chen [33] observe that restocking fees may be nonmonotonic in product quality; therefore, the positive effects of a lenient return policy on social welfare and customer satisfaction are ambiguous. The work of Shang et al. [71] provides empirical evidence from eBay data to demonstrate that the value of a free return policy to consumers is often overestimated, especially for consumer electronics. Therefore, a general lenient return policy is not always optimal, and retailers can consider providing customized return policies in accordance with consumers’ different levels of online purchase experience.

Another important research stream of product returns is to optimize return management, and a series of theoretical studies and analytical models can be found in the operations management and supply chain literature. Most of such research puts the emphasis on tactical and operational aspects of return management from the retailers’ point of view, such as designing optimal return policies [33,70] and optimizing pricing strategies [2,41,62,74]. In addition, it has been often observed in practice that some consumers opportunistically take advantages of lenient return policies. They often purchase products (e.g., jewelry, party dresses, and wedding suits) with no intention to keep them, and return them after short-term consumption. Clearly, such opportunistic behavior may result in negative effects of free return policies on re tailers, and a growing number of researchers are beginning to address this issue (e.g. [17,22,33,70]).

## 2.2. Signaling effect of product return policy

Product return policies are particularly crucial in remote purchase environments and for experience goods. Under such circumstances, in formation asymmetry becomes more problematic. With an aim to reduce information asymmetry between sellers and consumers, Spence [73] proposed the signaling theory, which suggests that observable entity at tributes could serve as a signal of quality. The signaling theory has been successfully applied to a wide variety of scenarios, including e-com merce [56,84], Internet finance [14], electronic WOM [1], and invest ment decision support [29]. The three core components of the signaling theory are signalers, receivers, and the signal itself. Initially, signalers choose appropriate entity attributes as signals and send them to re ceivers. Receivers observe and interpret the received signals, and they may do so in diverse ways, and then make their decisions. There exist several commonly used quality signals in the literature, such as price [44,67,84,87], brand/retailer reputation [3,65], historical sales [86], advertisement [43,61,85], WOM $\left[ 1 5 , 1 6 , 1 9 , 4 5 \right]$ , and post-purchase warranties (e.g., product return policy) $[ 9 , 1 0 , 5 9 , 6 6 ]$

By employing the signaling theory, some extant studies demonstrate that product return policies can be interpreted as a quality signal and can affect a consumer’s quality perceptions [4,44,59]. For example, the works of Mann and Wissink [53,54] show that a money-back guarantee (MBG) mechanism provides an effective means to reduce customer purchase risk and helps better match consumer needs and product quality; Moorthy and Srinivasan [59] apply the signaling theory to indicate that MBG can be used as a valid quality signal by high-quality firms to guide uninformed consumers; in the paper of Biswas and Bis was [8], the signaling role of full refund policies is found to be more salient in the e-business environment than in conventional brick-and-mortar retailer stores, due to the lack of touch-and-feel and store ambiance cues. However, in reality, different signal receivers may interpret the same signal in different ways [1,18,73]. Sometimes re ceivers may weigh the signals in accordance with their characteristics and product characteristics, generating inconclusive feedback on the same signal. In contrast to the above findings, different interpretations of lenient return policies are observed in some recent studies. For instance, in two studies, Bonifield et al. [9] investigate the interrela tionship between return policy leniency and retailer quality. Due to the distinctive characteristics of products and retailers, inconsistent signaling roles of return policies are observed in different scenarios. More specifically, a positive relationship between retailer quality and return policy leniency is identified in nonconsumable product cate gories; however, such a relationship does not exist in consumable product categories. Similarly, Hsiao and Chen [32] reveal that product quality is not necessarily positively associated with return policy leniency.

## 2.3. Expectation confirmation theory

Product return is a post-purchase behavior. If consumers are not satisfied with the purchased product, they are likely to return the product and ask for their money back. In other words, if consumers perceived performance after using a product or service does not match their initial expectations, this results in expectation disconfirmation and will lead to a product return behavior. The initial expectation of con sumers can be formed after interpreting different received signals. Signaling theory plays a crucial part in explaining the form of pre-usage expectations. It is obvious that return behaviors will have a negative impact on product sales; therefore, it is crucial to investigate the factors that potentially influence product return behaviors in the emerging field of online knowledge markets.

In the literature, ECT [6,63,64] provides a theoretical lens to explain how satisfaction forms and post-purchase behavior $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ product return in this context) resulted through the expectation-confirmation process. The ECT is widely used in consumer behavior literature to examine the relationship among pre-purchase behavior (i.e., expectation), satisfac tion, and post-purchase behavior (e.g., repurchase [64], continuance intention $[ 6 , 5 0 , 5 1 ]$ , customer royalty [42], and complaining [82]). ECT, originally proposed in the work of Oliver [63,64], posits that satisfaction is a function of ex-ante expectations and confirmation, and this, in turn, influences repurchase behaviors [64,78]. Initially, customers form a belief or expectation of a product or service prior to purchase by expo sure to the available information. After a period of product/service exploration and consumption, customers gain ex-post perceptions about its performance. If a product or service outperforms the initial expec tation, a positive level of confirmation is perceived, and satisfied cus tomers are more likely to form a repurchase intention. Otherwise, if a product or service falls short of expectation, dissatisfied users will form a negative attitude toward repurchase.

The original ECT ignores the fact that the initial expectation may evolve over the time [6,7]. After gaining consumption experiences, the post-purchase expectation of customers may be different from their pre-purchase expectation. Therefore, the work of Bhattacherjee [6] ex pands the original ECT by taking the post-purchase expectation (i.e., represented as usefulness) into consideration, and integrates the widely used technology acceptance model (TAM) [21] to identify the motiva tions underlying continuance intention of information systems (IS). After this pioneering study in applying ECT to the IS discipline, a number of subsequent studies further extend or replace perception variables with different underlying theoretical explanations and adapt ECT to a wide range of application contexts, including e-commerce [34, 42,82], information technology [7,25], social networking sites [51], and education [39].

It is worth noting that, in the framework of ECT, the relationship between expectation and satisfaction is complex. The original ECT [63, 64] posits that pre-purchase expectation influences customer satisfac tion through two ways: 1) the direct effect: according to adaptation level theory [28], expectation forms the baseline for customers to evaluate products or services, and a high expectation tends to enhance conse quent customer satisfaction; 2) the indirect effect: the expectation and perceived performance jointly determine the level of confirmation, and confirmation positively influences customer satisfaction. When these effects interact, inconclusive results may be obtained, evidence can be found in [76] and [6]. In the IS literature, the level of confirmation is often measured directly by using a single value [76]. In so doing, such measurement does not capture the absolute level of expectation and perceived performance, and the direction of confirmation is somehow ignored. Having recognized this, some studies (e.g. [12,78]) respectively measure expectation and perceived performance to derive the level of confirmation in a more accurate manner. The effects of positive and negative confirmation (also termed disconfirmation) are investigated, respectively. Furthermore, the polynomial modeling and response sur face methodology are employed to model the nonlinear relationship among expectation. perceived performance, and confirmation.

In summary, prior research efforts on product return policies mainly focus either on the analysis of policy effects or on policy optimization. In most cases, the returned products are regarded as physical products that are normally associated with high return costs. However, research on return policies of digital media goods (e.g., information goods and knowledge products) remains very limited. Obviously, the costs of handling digital media product returns may be much lower than with physical goods because there are no extra packing and delivery costs. Therefore, there is a pivotal need to re-examine the impact of lenient return policies for digital products on sales and revenues, and the cur rent study aims to fill this research gap.

## 3. Hypotheses development

This work primarily employs signaling theory and ECT to build the conceptual framework. Initially, a free return policy can be interpreted as a high-quality signal, as evidenced in previous studies [8,44,59]. Such a quality signal could influence a customer’s pre-purchase expectation of quality in knowledge products. According to ECT, the expectation and perceived performance jointly determine a customer’s satisfaction. If a customer is not satisfied with the purchased product, he or she is likely to return the product and claim a refund. The product return then obviously contributes to the negative effect on the sales of knowledge products. Unlike a free return policy which is supported by sellers, extant reviews (i.e., WOM) are written by previous consumers without any conflicts of interest. Therefore. it could be treated as more reliable. Also, the information embedded in reviews further provides more detailed information about products, thereby customers could gain a better understanding of products. Hence, the effect of free return will be moderated by the richness of information embedded in extant reviews. Hypotheses are described and explained as follows, and the proposed research framework is depicted in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. The effect of free returns

People evaluate the expected utility of product to make the purchase decision. The utility is comprised of the costs (e.g., price) and the po tential benefits brought by a product. The benefits of a product are positively correlated with its quality. Hence, the perceived quality of the product is crucial to making a purchase decision. In e-commerce, con sumers are not able to touch and feel the quality of the product as they do in conventional brick-and-mortar stores. Instead, they can only observe the product attributes and infer the quality of a product accordingly. Therefore, the attributes of products play the signaling role of quality [56; 84]. In an online transaction, the retailer is a signaler, while the consumer is a signal receiver. The retailer sends the signals to the consumer by showing attributes of the product and of the seller himself, and then the consumer makes a decision based on the signals he or she receives. The common attributes include product price, de scriptions, rating, post-purchase warranty, the retailer’s reputation, and others.

In the literature and practice, it is well recognized that a free return policy signals that the retailer stands behind the product quality [4,9,44, 59] and that retailers are confident in their product quality; otherwise, they may be faced with the high costs of handling returned products. Therefore, in order to attract consumers and emphasize product quality, high-quality retailers often launch free return policies to distinguish themselves from low-quality retailers. This may be beneficial for high-quality retailers to generate incremental sales and retain

competitive advantages.

In the context of knowledge products, a free return policy may also be interpreted as a strong signal of high quality by consumers. It has been pointed out in Biswas and Biswas [8] that consumers may rely more on such quality signals as cues in online shopping environments than in conventional brick-and-mortar stores, due to the absence of other cues. Moreover, knowledge products have fewer quality signals than search products due to the lack of product attributes. The work of Bonifield et al. [9] suggests that consumers may expect a high quality when they find a lenient return policy (e.g., free return). As a result, consumers would have a higher expectation of quality for knowledge products if speakers provide a free return policy.

According to the aforementioned literature on ECT, there are two ways for the pre-purchase expectation to influence a consumer’s satisfaction and further post-purchase decisions [6,12,63,64]: 1) the direct way in that a high initial expectation tends to enhance consequent consumer satisfaction and 2) the indirect way through confirmation, which implies that a high initial expectation negatively affects consumer satisfaction. When these two opposite effects coexist and interact, the relationship between pre-purchase expectation and consumer satisfac tion becomes complex, and inconclusive results are found in the literature.

Compared with physical products, the costs of handling returned knowledge products are extremely low. However, a knowledge product is a type of experience product with high customization, which makes for a marketplace where universal standards of quality are impractical. Therefore, more speakers prefer to provide a free return policy regard less of their product quality, because they believe this policy may attract more consumers without any extra costs. From the consumers’ points of view, free return policy is interpreted as a signal of quality, and their expectations will be increased when they received this signal. This will result in an increasing deviation between pre-purchase expectation and perceived performance. When the deviation becomes too large to be tolerated, the negative effect of the post-purchase confirmation (indirect path of influence) would outweigh the positive effect of the pre-purchase expectation (direct path of influence) [12]. In other words, the high pre-purchase expectation will cause disconfirmation, which results in consumers’ dissatisfaction. In addition, as aforementioned, consumers can easily return knowledge products at an extremely low cost; therefore, unsatisfied consumers are more likely to boost the returns of knowledge products to avoid losses [40]. Accordingly, the final sales of knowledge products with free return supported will be lower than that of nonfree return ones, and hypothesis H1 is proposed as follows:

H1. Applying the free return policy negatively influences the sales of knowledge products.

## 3.2. Moderating effect of information richness of extant reviews

The effectiveness and impact of online reviews on consumers’ pur chasing decisions have been confirmed by many extant studies (e.g., [13, 15,16,24,45,55,57]). Online reviews are usually written by previous consumers who do not have conflicts of interest with either sellers or potential customers. Hence, potential customers can utilize more impartial judgments of a product or service by reading extant reviews to reduce the uncertainty and alleviate the information asymmetry prob lem before making purchases. This is especially true for experience products whose product attributes are subjective and heterogeneous; therefore, it is relatively difficult and costly to quantify product quality prior to interaction with the product [60].

Online reviews for different types of products exhibit different characteristics. Reviews of experience products tend to be more sub jective, often hinging on individual differences in perception of con sumption experience, whereas reviews of search products provide more standard or concrete product specifications [49,60,79]. Taste plays a crucial role in evaluating experience products, and consumers are often confident in their own tastes and subjective evaluations; for example, a movie often attracts reviews from consumers who either love it or hate it [60]. As a result, experience goods often have extreme review ratings that include sentiments that are unique or personal to the reviewer. However, such content is not uniformly helpful to the purchase decision, because consumers may interpret the extreme ratings just as a simple difference in taste.

Therefore, this study employs review volume (i.e., refers to the number of cues) to be the proxy of information richness. A greater number of reviews indicate a higher degree of information richness, because consumers can gain more information from a wide variety of viewpoints.

When all information comes from sellers, customers have to rely on signals extracted from this information (e.g., free returns) to make purchasing decisions. However, such reliance might be reduced when other reliable information is available, such as extant reviews. WOM will help to reduce product uncertainty by providing additional information. Rich information gained from extant review content provides customers with a more comprehensive and diverse understanding of the knowledge product. The more information provided in online reviews, the more details customers access about the products. As a result, the role of free returns as a quality signal could be weakened. On this basis, information richness of extant reviews may weaken the impact of free return policy on the sales of knowledge products. Accordingly, the hypothesis H2 is proposed as follows:

H2. Information richness of extant reviews negatively moderates the impact of the free return policy on the sales of knowledge products.

## 4. Data and variables

## 4.1. Research setting

In recent decades, people have become accustomed to sharing and exchanging knowledge for free (e.g., via Yahoo answer, Stackoverflow, and Quora). Powered by the proliferation of social commerce, the knowledge-sharing economy has enjoyed tremendous growth in China since 2016. A number of knowledge-sharing platforms (e.g., Zhihu Live, ZaiHangYiDian, and Himalayas), which allow users to trade their knowledge in various forms (e.g., slides, audio, and video), have been launched, and Zhihu Live (https://www.zhihu.com/lives) is one of the leading platforms. Up to December 2017, more than 5 million customers had purchased knowledge products (i.e., named Live) in Zhihu Live, nearly 3000 users had been served as knowledge sharers [77].

In Zhihu Live, users can host online broadcasting Lives to share their knowledge, skills, experiences, and/or insights with others. Fig. 2 pro vides some screenshots of Zhihu Live. Potential consumers can browse available Lives by category. Fig. 2(a) lists several categories, and Fig. 2 (b) lists the top-ranked Lives within the Internet category. Each Live has a home page that provides basic introduction of the Live (see Fig. 3). If a registered user is interested in a Live, he or she can participate in it as a listener by purchasing a ticket. The ticket price is determined by the speaker (i.e., the knowledge sharer). In the end, listeners are encouraged to rate the purchased Live and write online reviews. As shown in Fig. 2 (c), the basic information of a user and a summary of his or her social activities (e.g., the number of followers and followees, and the number of voted up and thanks responses received) is provided in the user profile.

To address the issue of information asymmetry, Zhihu Live launched a free return policy on April 24, 2017. A speaker could choose the option of a free return for his or her Lives. When a listener purchases a Live that supports free return, the listener can choose up to 15 voice messages by themselves to evaluate this Live. If the listener is not satisfied with the chosen voice messages, he or she can apply for a product refund within seven days from the date of purchase. Note that, it is different from the traditional free trial or free sampling strategies. Traditional free trial strategies are often conducted before the purchase, for digital media products, consumers are allowed to try out a small part of a product (e. $g _ { \cdot , }$ 30 s of a song, preview of a movie, and the first chapter of a book), and then make their purchase decision. However, the product trial in this study is conducted after the purchase, and listeners could have different product return intentions after conducting the free trials. In other words, although free trial and free return policies both allow consumers to experience products with the aim of reducing information asymmetry, free return policy requires consumers to make the payment in advance and introduces extra risk to consumers (e.g., the consumer cannot claim a full refund afterwards). In principle, consumers of Zhihu live can get a full refund under the free return policy if they meet the following conditions: 1) claim the refund within 7 days of purchase (for ended Lives) or Live ends (for ongoing Lives), and 2) listen to no more than 15 voice messages. However, in practice, some risks can still pre vent consumers from successfully claiming their refunds. For example, some consumers may not carefully read the free return policy and are not aware of the free return conditions, some consumers may forget to claim the refund within 7 days, or some consumers may have listened to more than 15 voice messages. Therefore, compared to free trial or free sampling policies, a free return policy indeed introduces extra financial risks for consumers. As illustrated in Fig. 3, each home page contains information about the Live and the speaker, including the Live title, opening time, whether it supports free return, review volume, average rating, brief descriptions of the Live, the price, cumulative sales up to date, the number of questions answered during Live (only applies to ended Lives), name of the speaker, brief descriptions of the speaker, whether the speaker is a verified expert, and other information.

## 4.3. Variables

As this study is seeking to investigate the effect of free return policy on sales of knowledge products, the dependent variable is the final sales of a Live (Sales) (note that returned sales have been excluded in the final sales), and therefore, it could better measure sellers’ actual perfor mance. Since all transactions with their purchasing timestamp were collected, it is possible to calculate daily sales for each Live. Note that Live purchase transaction data cannot be observed if a consumer has returned it. Hence, the transaction data used in this study are the final sales, and sales in each period are calculated to investigate the effects of the free return policy. Every three days from the launch day of a Live is defined as a period in this study. For example, if a Live started on November 1, 2020, then the first period for this Live is from November 1 to November 3, and its second period is from November 4 to November 6.

These four attributes are measurements of a speaker’s reputation in the Zhihu community. This study takes all four into account to calculate the index of the reputation of a speaker. They are standardized respectively at first, and then their average value is derived to represent the reputation. ## 5.1. Propensity score matching

Although the grouped summary statistics indicate a negative effect of free return on sales, a potential endogeneity is the heterogeneity of Live quality between free return–supported Lives and nonfree return Lives. It is possible that the nonfree return Lives might have a significantly higher quality than the supported ones. Such quality heterogeneity between these two groups may lead to the differences between their sales. This study aims to identify the effect of free return policy, and the above assumption may generate biased results. Therefore, it is important to control the difference caused by the quality heterogeneity of Lives.

To address the above issue, a matching technique is performed, which allows identifying the causal relationship [68]. The nearest-neighbor matching technique is employed, which is a type of PSM method. This method selects the closest case as the control case of each treated case [31]. As a result, a group of control cases can be created. In this study, a case is regarded as treated if a Live speaker decides to ensure a free return. Therefore, the treatment occurs at the time that a Live is being created. The control cases are those Lives that do not support free return but are quite similar to the treated Lives that are created at the same time.

## 5.2. Econometrics model

To investigate the effect of free return and the moderating role of online reviews, a regression model is proposed. Although a panel data has been constructed in this study, there still exists a critical issue in applying the fixed effect panel data model. The variable FR which in dicates whether a Live supports free return is a time-invariant variable, and therefore it will be absorbed by the Live-level individual fixed effect. To address this issue, this study proposes the model shown in Eq. (1) and applies the cross-sectional regression model to each time period.

$$
\begin{array}{l} L n \big (S a l e s _ {i, t} \big) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} F R _ {i} + \beta_ {2} F R _ {i} * C u m u R e v _ {i, t} + \gamma_ {1} C u m u R e v _ {i, t} + \gamma_ {2} P r i c e _ {i} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {3} H i s t S a l e s _ {i, t} + \gamma_ {4} V R a t i o _ {i} + \gamma_ {5} Q u e s t i o n _ {i} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {6} T i t l e L e n g t h _ {i} + \gamma_ {7} D e s c L e n g t h _ {i} + \gamma_ {8} R e p u t a t i o n \\ \quad + \pi_ {1} M o n t h _ {i} + \pi_ {2} C a t e g o r y _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In $\mathbf { E q } . \left( 1 \right) , \varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is the random error. Since Lives in different categories might have different market sizes, the category fixed effect (Categeory ) is included in the model. The classification of Live category is provided by Zhihu Live. In addition, a set of monthly dummies (Month ) are included to capture the time-fixed effect since the whole Live market might be changing over time. These dummies indicate the month when the Live i starts.

## 5.3. Main results

The results of the first two periods are initially reported as repre sentative periods. Considering consumers cannot leave review com ments before a Live starts; in the first period (from the opening day to the third day), the explanatory variable CumuRev therefore cannot be derived. However, some Lives could attract reviews in the second period (i.e., from the fourth day to the sixth day); therefore, the variable CumuRev only from the second period is included in the study. Since the Live could be pre-ordered several days before it starts, the variable of cumulative historical sales is also included in the model of the first period.

We only report results for the first two periods for the following two reasons: 1) the first period is the most special period because no review was available before a Live starts, which provides a perfect setting for us to investigate the role of free return by eliminating the influence of re views and 2) the second period is expected to be similar to the remaining periods. All factors (such as reviews) that appear in the future periods have already appeared and captured in the second period. Our results of long-term analysis reported in Appendix A also support out conjunction.

One critical issue in econometric analysis is the multi-collinearity among independent variables. Correlation coefficients of these independent variables presented in Tables 5 and 6 do not indicate multicollinearity. One notable thing in Table 6 is the relatively low correla tion coefficient between historical sales (HistSales) and the number of reviews (CumuRev). A possible reason is the extremely low percentage of posting reviews. According to the summary statistics in Table 2, only about 5 % of buvers have posted reviews. In our dataset, there are 1265 Lives (i.e., accounts for about 25 % of total Lives) that do not attract any reviews at the beginning of the second period. Therefore, the variance of historical sales cannot be fully reflected by the number of accumulative reviews, and the correlation coefficient between them is low. ## 5.5.2. The effects of free return in different scenarios

To further investigate the effects of free return policies in different scenarios, two dimensions are introduced in this study. The first one is the market concentration ratio. It measures the degree of competition of the market. Higher market concentration ratio indicates lower degree of competition in the market. For example, a market with a high concen tration ratio may include some superstar speakers that dominate the sales in this market, while the rest of the speakers attract low sales, and the whole market experiences a relatively lower degree of competition. One Live category is defined as a market in this study because different segments of customers have different preferences on different Live cat egories. Gini index of the daily sales of Lives within the category i at day t (Gini Index) was calculated to represent the market concentration ratio at day t for category i. This enables matching of the market concentra tion ratio for each Live on its starting day. The second dimension is the speaker’s reputation in the Zhihu community (Reputation), the definition and calculation of Reputation have been introduced in Section 4.3. Lives

The results are largely consistent for these two periods, and both of them show interesting findings. When the market concentration ratio (i. e., Gini index) is at the medium/low level and speaker’s reputation is at the medium/high level, the coefficients of the free return are insignifi cant. It indicates that the free return only exerts the effect when the market is at a lower level of competition (i.e., market concentration ratio is high) and/or the speakers are low-reputation users. According to the definition of the market concentration ratio, in a high competition market (i.e., low market concentration ratio), there is no superstar speaker who will dominate the sales of the market. Thus, there exists no strong signal to guide the consumers. Instead, consumers tend to rely on various signals to choose Lives. However, due to the mixture effects of different signals, the effect of a single signal becomes difficult to identify and observe. More specifically, both FR and reputation act as quality signals in this situation, and the effects of reputation might be opposite to that of free returns. Thus, it is difficult to observe the effect of free returns in higher reputation groups in the lower concentration market. In contrast, when the reputation is low or the market concentration ratio is high, except those Lives provided by superstars, consumers might primarily rely on the free return signal to seek high-quality Lives.

## 6. Conclusions and discussions

Drawing on the signaling theory and ECT, this study investigates the effects of free return policies, which are representative post-purchase warranties on the sales of knowledge products and the role of infor mation richness of extant reviews in moderating those effects. Data used in this study were collected from the leading knowledge-sharing plat form, Zhihu Live, in China. A linear regression model was applied in empirical analysis, and PSM and IVs were introduced to address the endogeneity issue. There are two main findings. First, allowing free returns surprisingly harms the sales of knowledge products. It will decrease the sales by roughly 80 % during the initial sales period. Furthermore, the negative effect only occurs when the market has a high concentration ratio and/or when speakers have low reputations. Second, information richness of extant reviews (i.e., WOM) negatively moder ates the impact of free return policies on sales.

This study provides several theoretical and practical contributions. From the theoretical point of view, the findings of this study contribute to information system and marketing literature. First, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study represents a pioneer to explore the effect of free return policies on knowledge products, whereas prior studies mainly focus on physical product returns (e.g., [22,47,58]). One important difference between physical and knowledge products which are knowledge products is the return cost. For physical products, even if retailers support free return, customers still need to spend time on packing and shipping the returned product. However, knowledge products can be easily returned by clicking online buttons. Therefore, consumers of knowledge products might be more likely to return. In addition, knowledge products usually can only be experienced a limited part of the products before return, while physical products can be fully experienced. The limited trial of knowledge products becomes an obstacle to completely eliminate the information asymmetry. Such ef fects are quite different from that of physical products [30,33,47,71,80, 83]. This study sheds light on the future investigation on the effects of free return policies on different types of products due to their heterogeneity.

Second, the interactions between a quality signal and extant infor mation such as WOM are investigated, while prior literature only pays attention on one aspect. The free return policy is considered to come from the seller side (i.e., speakers in this study), while WOMs come from the perspectives of previous consumers. Either the effect of free return [22,47,58] or the effect of WOMs [16,24,45,55,57,75] has been fully examined, respectively. However, the interactions between these two have rarely been considered. In this study, the significance of the interaction effect suggests that the information richness of extant re views does moderate the effect of the free returns. The results provide a possible explanation to the argument of Shang et al. [71] that the effect of the free return policy is often overestimated.

Third, ECT is applied to provide a plausible way to explain the negative effect on sales. The extant literature suggests factors such as high costs of processing returns [30] and restocking fees [33] that cause the negative effect. In this study, an online survey was performed, and the results suggest consumers’ expectation on product quality increases with the introduction of free return policies. Higher expectation might lead to lower satisfaction for given true quality. As a result, free return policies exert a negative effect on sales. Therefore, this study provides a new explanation for the negative effect of free return policies on sales.

This study provides several practical implications. First, although the free return policy seems to be useful to attract potential customers, both platforms and content providers (i.e., speakers) should also be careful in applying this policy because it may raise customers’ expectations regarding the quality of knowledge products and ultimately harm sales. According to the obtained results in different scenarios, speakers with a higher reputation might be less influenced by the free return policy, but still cannot benefit from it. Second, if speakers have to apply free return policies due to regulations made by platforms or the government, they should present as many online reviews as possible to diminish the negative impact generated by free return policies.

This research inevitably also bears several limitations. First, although this study applies the matching technique and IVs to address the endogeneity issue and explore the causal relationship, more effective causal inference methods (e.g., experiments) are restricted due to the availability of data. Second, this study is currently incapable of identi fying the effect of social influence due to the data availability. It also suggests that an important direction for the future would be to collect social network data to address this issue.

## Appendix D. A topic modeling analysis

To further dig into the content of reviews, a topic modeling algo rithm named Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) is employed to identify topics embedded in reviews for each Live. All reviews, including reviews posted after the second period, are used to train the LDA model. Each review is a document. The authors first tokenized reviews using the jieba package, which has been a widely adopted package for Chinese toke nization, and then removed all stop words according to the stop words list provided by the Harbin Institute of Technology. In topic modeling, one important parameter is the number of topics. The perplexity score is usually applied to measure the performance of an LDA model [35]. A lower perplexity score represents better performance. Therefore, the perplexity scores of the LDA model are computed and plotted in Fig. D1 for different topic numbers, ranging from 2 to 15. ## Appendix E. Sentiment analysis

We further explore the role of reviews’ sentiment. The sentiment score for each review is computed by employing a pre-trained Chinese text sentiment classify package named Senta (https://aistudio.baidu. com/aistudio/projectdetail/215814). Senta implements sentiment clas sify model provided by Baidu, which is one of the best Chinese natural language processing companies worldwide. A sentiment score between 0 and 1 is computed for each text. A higher score indicates this text is more likely to be positive. If the score is greater than 0.5. this review will be classified as a positive one. Senta provides five models, namely, Bag of Words (BoW), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Long Short Term Memory (LSTM), and Bidirectional Long Short Term Memory (Bi-LSTM). All of these five models are applied to this study. We first calculate the average sentiment score of reviews for each Live (AvgScore) and rerun the main model with PSM matched sample in the second period. The results are shown in Table E1. We also replace the average score with the proportion of positive reviews (Pos itiveRate), and the results are presented in Table E2. According to the results, the effect of free return is still negative and significant across all models, which supports our hypothesis H1. However, for H2, only re sults for PositiveRate that are derived from LSTM and Bi-LSTM are consistent with our main results. Consequently, there is no strong evi dence showing that the sentiment of reviews moderates the relationship between free return and sales.
