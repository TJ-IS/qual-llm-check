## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Security incentives Prospect theory Security policy compliance Phishing Framing

## A B S T R A C T

This study examined the effects of financial incentives on security policy compliance. Participants were recruited for a computerized in-basket job simulation and randomly assigned to one of three groups: (1) control group with no financial incentives for compliance; (2) positive frame (gain) group where participants could gain up to \$5 for compliance; (3) negative frame (loss) group where participants were awarded \$5, but would lose a portion with each compliance failure. Incentives increased individuals’ vigilance toward phishing emails and decreased likelihood of clicking on links. with the negative frame incentive having the strongest effects. Incentives worked better for non-contextualized emails.

## 1. Introduction

Humans remain the nexus of vulnerabilities that results in informa tion security breaches in organizations [1], with risk behaviors that include setting poor passwords, clicking on malicious links, and trans ferring unencrypted confidential data to portable devices. Properly administering an active security governance program is key to ensuring the security of organizational information [2] and it is imperative that these programs ensure that employee behaviors in this context support the goals of organizational security. The bedrock of organizational ef forts to improve information security continues to be enacting and enforcing information security policies and measures designed to cajole employees into positive security practices. However, literature in the field confirms universally poor compliance with security policies across organizations, with a range of reasons for noncompliance cited, including lack of self-efficacy [3], cognitive overload [4], behavioral propensity for careless behavior [5], and general apathy for security [6]. The question of how to motivate employees to follow information se curity policies remains unanswered, and a major challenge in organi zations. Few significant improvements have been realized despite a range of interventions that have been designed and implemented to improve employee security compliance, from implementing information security policies [7], to security education training and awareness (SETA) campaigns [8], to online monitoring and punitive measures [9].

In fact, the volume and severity of data breaches and other security events have actually increased. The limitations of standard security awareness warnings and programs have been well documented (see [10–12]): consequently, organizations are realizing that awareness programs may be an ineffective and unproductive use of corporate funds [13,14].

Researchers have also tried to address security noncompliance using interventions from the motivation literature to increase compliance in users. The dominant theoretical frameworks applied by researchers to examine information security compliance have been protection moti vation theory (PMT; [15–17]) and deterrence theory [18–21]. These theories suggest that users make rational security decisions by cogni tively weighing the relative gains and losses associated with their choices within a decision calculus. They assume that users will respond rationally to perceived security threats in the environment, and to sanctions imposed for noncompliance. Users are expected to internally regulate their behavior based on both an understanding of security threats and the consequences of risky behavior. However, in daily work activities, users may minimize the risks associated with their behavior, rationalizing noncompliant behavior [22–24], and may assume that the costs of compliance outweigh perceived benefits. It has been argued that many extant security interventions fail because the threats are not personally relevant to employees [25]. We argue that the limited effectiveness of security interventions is due to a failure to properly incentivize user behavior. We attempt to alter user behavior and in crease compliance by changing the security decision calculus. Drawing on principles of behavioral economics, we propose using extrinsic re wards (i.e. financial incentives) to improve compliance.

This paper presents the results of our research in which we examine the role of monetary incentives in changing user security behavior. Specifically, we look at the threat of clicking on phishing links and that of entering information on phishing webpages; we further investigate the effectiveness of interventions that may address this threat. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 discusses the previous theories and interventions used to influence cybersecurity behavior; Sections 3 and 4 present the research hypotheses and the research design; and Section 5 details our results, followed by a dis cussion of the results, study limitations, and future research.

## 2. Background

Phishing attacks are an especially important and challenging infor mation security threat, which results in USD 5.3 billion in annual global losses to more than 40,000 victims [26]. Successful phishing can result in identity theft and financial loss [27] and may also negatively impact organizations in several ways, such as loss of reputation and produc tivity loss [28]. Research in the information systems (IS) area has focused on various roles of phishing messages, recipients’ personal characteristics, and the impact of awareness campaigns on phishing vulnerability [29–36]. The literature on security compliance behaviors (sometimes termed “behavioral security" research or “InfoSec" by authors) is dominated by PMT, fear appeals, and deterrence theory, which emphasize perceived threat, fear, and punishment, respectively. We discuss this in greater depth below.

PMT [16,15] provides a framework for understanding how in dividuals process information about threats and subsequently cope with the danger posed by those threats. PMT underscores the notion that individuals will take calculated (utility maximizing) action toward their self-interests, such that they avoid or mitigate the pain associated with threats. PMT has been applied to the security behavior of individuals in the context of information security and privacy [25,37–41,94,95]. PMT has also been further contextualized as the “technology threat avoidance theory” (TTAT) by Liang and Xue [42]. PMT is a behavior change pro cess model intended to describe outcomes resulting from (and subse quent to) some type of stimulus (e.g., a fear appeal) that communicates a threat and a recommended response to that threat. However, its primary use in the information security literature has been as a theory of behavior, where it is used simply to understand how individuals perceive an existing threat, without concern for a particular behavior change mechanism [43,44].

Research involving fear appeals has been met with doubt and disenchantment as to their effectiveness in security policy compliance [45]. When used for information security threat warnings, we believe that the conventional fear appeal rhetorical framework is inadequate [46,47]. The principle behind the use of fear appeals is that if fear emotions are elicited by highlighting the unpleasant consequences of their actions, users are more likely to take the recommended action in order to reduce fear. Over time, users may develop mechanisms to resist the impacts of fear appeals. To appeal to the self-interests of their audience, fear appeals must achieve a sufficient level of personal rele vance (or “issue involvement”) with the individual, lest they be ignored and otherwise rendered ineffective [48,49,41]. However, rather than being focused on threats to the physical self, as seen in healthcare contexts, fear appeals in the information security context have been mostly focused on threats to one’s things (data, information, and sys tems), and may suffer from a lack of perceived personal relevance and urgency [25]. Conventional managerial cybersecurity methods, espe cially those focused on fear appeals, are inadequate and in need of revision.

Deterrence theory is rooted in the analysis of human behavior developed by the early classical theorists, Cesare Beccaria and Jeremy Bentham [24]. Simply stated, deterrence theory states that people do not commit crimes when they are afraid of being caught and punished. They are more likely to avoid engaging in rule-breaking behaviors when the punishment is certain, immediate, and severe. Deterrence theory has been used to model security behavior in the IS literature [18,20,21], where researchers have focused on the element of personal choice based on rational conscious or non-conscious assessments of the relative gains (“pleasure”) from violation, as compared with the perceived loss (“pain”) resulting from the sanctions or punishment. In the past two decades, a number of studies have applied deterrence theory to explain negative IS behaviors, such as computer abuse [18,21], information security policy violations by employees [50,24,51–53], internet usage policy violations [54], and illegal copying of software [55]. Unfortu nately, the results of these and other studies have been mixed [38,56], with inconsistent effects of perceived sanction severity and certainty on behavioral intention [57,58] and despite the two decades of research, IS-specific theories of deterrence have not fully matured [59].

The ineffectiveness of security programs lies squarely in the gap between user motivation and compliance, or intention and execution. Acquisti and Grosslags [60] showed that while most users profess a strong desire to have and maintain secure systems, they also show a surprising willingness to divulge personal information for as little as \$0.25. Christin et al. [61] provided minimal financial incentives to users willing to download unknown executables. Results demonstrated that, in direct opposition to their stated preferences, most users do not attach an economic value to the security of their systems: 70 % of all partici pants in the study understood that running unknown programs could be dangerous, yet all chose to do so once paid. In addition, users often feel that the effort needed to comply with security policy outweighs the re wards associated with compliance. Beautement et al. [62] coined the term the “compliance budget” to refer to the reasonable amount of effort that employees are willing to expend to keep the organization safe. Further, tasks related to compliance require users to tap a limited amount of resources that are needed for self-control, thereby depleting the reserves for future activities (cf., [93]). Functionality often over shadows security in terms of prioritizing the tasks at hand.

([63], p. 133) argues that: “users’ rejection of the security advice they receive is entirely rational from an economic perspective. The advice offers to shield them from the direct costs of attacks but burdens them with far greater direct costs in terms of effort.” Botnet masters and attackers generally seek financial returns for their efforts. As economic agents, they are primarily driven by profits. While agencies realize financial rewards (or a limitation of security costs and losses) with increased user security hygiene, the individual user realizes no direct economic benefits. Christin [61] notes that modeling attacker behavior is easier than defender (user) behavior since “attackers show much stronger economic rationality than defenders.” Despite the critical importance of information security to enterprises – whether corporate, university, public-sector agency, or entire financial markets – little or no personal motivation is offered to individual users to adopt more secure online behaviors. The current cost/benefit security calculus holds little personal relevance or urgency for the individual user/employee, who realizes no tangible direct personal benefits from secure practices.

Because individual employees are not effectively motivated by traditional efforts to influence their security behaviors, the search con tinues to fill the gap between user intention and security policy compliance. We ask the question, what is the role of financial in centives? How can we explore this potential lever of human behavior in this context, which has not been sufficiently explored? Research on behavioral economics has grown exponentially in the past decade and offers several critical insights for information security. First, to under stand the choices that people make, it is important to understand the user’s perspective. Often, behavior that is seen as irrational to an impassionate observer is not perceived as such by the actor. In an in formation security context, the trade-off between security threats and productivity benefits may be viewed by users differently than by secu rity experts. Second, to change behavior one must identify and create proper incentives. People respond to a host of different incentives, including financial, emotional, and social outcomes [65,66]. The chal lenge is to identify the right incentive for the specific context. In some instances, social incentives, such as reciprocity, guilt, and bonding may be more powerful than financial rewards, depending on the context and magnitude of the reward.

The goal of this research is to explore whether providing an incentive can motivate users to comply with security policies. We applied financial incentives to “flip the switch” to increase user moti vation to act safely. Offering financial incentives is analogous to cor porations investing in wellness programs as a way to manage corporate healthcare costs; incentive programs could prove an economical way to reduce breaches and overall security costs. Beyond encouraging secure behaviors, incentives directly and positively acknowledge the impor tance of the user’s personal decisions to the security of the overall network. Some companies, although not many, have started to incen tivize employees’ information security behaviors [67]. For example, one employee in General Motors told us that the company encourages its employees to forward any suspicious emails to the HR department and once it is confirmed as phishing email, the employee receives bonus [68]. Anecdotally, the rewards motivated employees to pay more attention to potential risks existing in emails and presumably reduced the chance that they respond to a phishing email. Although the mone tary rewards are only applied to email-related security behaviors, they can be easily applied to improve information security performance in other areas. For instance, bug bounty programs have been used exten sively to identify software bugs by many corporations for both em ployees and the community at large [69–72].

Financial incentives have long been assumed to affect people’s behavior and performance. Behaviorists and economists argue that financial incentives exert a strong influence on worker effort, persis tence, and performance. The assumptions have been supported by research. In a review of organizational research, [92] found a positive relationship between financial incentives offered to workers and per formance. Pay-for-performance systems have generally been successful in improving the quantity of performance, if not the quality [73,74]. Camerer and Hogarth [75] found that the positive effects of financial incentives are strongest for tasks in which effort and performance are closely linked. However, there are limits to the effectiveness of financial incentives, as demonstrated by research in behavioral economics. Spe cifically, for money to be an effective motivator, it: (1) needs to be large enough to motivate users to change their behavior and (2) cannot sup plant or replace more powerful social or personal incentives currently operating [76].

Another factor that should be considered when examining the motivational effect of incentives is the framing of outcomes, specifically as losses versus gains. Prospect theory [91] is a descriptive theory of decision-making under uncertainty that explains when people will be risk seeking or risk averse. Prospect theory research suggests that po tential losses exert a stronger influence over people’s judgments and actions than potential gains. A key principle of prospect theory is that the way in which people frame an outcome affects their actions. Judg ments are influenced by two key concepts: reference-dependence and loss aversion. Reference-dependence refers to the fact that decision outcomes are evaluated relative to a reference point, often the status quo. Outcomes above the reference point are seen as gains, whereas outcomes below the reference point are seen as losses. People attach subjective values to gains and losses, such that gains are associated with positive value, and losses with negative value. People are maximally sensitive to change near the reference point (greater value is attached to the amount of change from the status quo), and losses are weighed more heavily than gains. That is, the pain of losing \$100 is greater than the joy of gaining \$100 (Jim Furyk, the golf star, once said that the losing hurts more than winning feels good [77]). An interesting implication of this gain/los asymmetry is that financial incentives may be more effective when people perceive themselves as acting to prevent losses than to secure gains. For example, workers provided with a \$500 bonus but told they will lose a portion of that bonus for each instance of noncompliance may be more motivated to comply than workers offered an equivalent amount of money for each instance of compliance up to \$500. Both groups of workers have the same possible payoff, but the loss frame group psychologically loses the chance to earn the entire \$500 (their reference point) and is motivated to avoid this loss, whereas the gain frame group’s motivation is to acquire the \$500. The former group has the mindset of avoiding incremental losses, while the latter has the mindset of incremental gains. According to prospect theory, motivation may be higher in the loss frame group. Our study design tested the application of this theory in the information security context.

## 3. Research Hypotheses

The extrinsic motivation and behavioral economics literature suggest that compliance behavior should increase when strong incentives are present and contingent on correct behavior. Farahmand and Spafford [78] have conceptually explored the application of Kahneman and Tversky’s [91] prospect theory to the problem of insider security de cisions, but they conceptualized the individual’s response to framing as an internal cognitive function; they explored it mathematically, rather than empirically with experimental manipulations of the framing al ternatives. The literature in behavioral economics, including prospect theory, informs us that individual decisions can be and will be influ enced by financial incentives, in general, and we specifically hypothe size that this influence applies to the context of security policy compliance. Therefore, we offer the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. Providing financial incentives for compliance will in crease the likelihood that people will comply with information security policies related to phishing.

Prospect theory argues that people are more sensitive to losses than gains, and thus incentives delivered within a loss frame should lead to greater compliance than incentives delivered within a gain frame. This has been mathematically modeled in an application to information se curity risk-taking behaviors by insiders by Farahmand and Spafford [78]. We suggest that prospect theory will apply to the compliance – noncompliance decision calculus in the context of information security policies, such that threats to lose financial assets will be more im pactful than promises of financial rewards. Accordingly, we hy pothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. Compliance with information security policies regarding phishing emails will be higher when participants are told that noncompliance reduces their rewards (negative or loss frame) than when compliance increases their rewards (positive or gain frame).

## 4.2. Procedures

This study was designed to test whether financial incentives attached to information security behaviors will lead to greater compliance with information security policies on phishing. However, participants were told that the purpose of the study was to validate a selection tool that was designed for a local company to select managers. The selection tool is an in-basket job simulation task that required participants to role-play as a manager of a fictitious company named “Super Signs.” Participants were told they would receive a sequence of (21) emails and that they need to determine as a manager the priority of the emails and respond to them using their judgment. They performed the task using desktop computers in a computer lab. Participants were told that the researchers would examine their responses to the emails and assess whether they are a valid predictor of one’s management ability. Participants were asked to perform their best on the task. The true purpose was not disclosed to the participants until the experiment was concluded.

Before starting their in-basket task, all participants were required to complete the company’s information security training, delivered through a document that outlined various security policies, including password setting and protection, handling emails and suspicious links, use of cloud drive, use of thumb drives, etc. The two sections, “security concerns about web links in emails, instant messages, social media, or other means” and “issues regarding the cloud drive” are excerpted below, since they are most relevant to the purpose and the design of the current study:

4. Security concerns about web links in email, instant messages, social media, or other means.

For business or personal email, be especially careful when clicking on links in email messages. Some scams are in the form of embedded links in emails. Once a recipient clicks on the link, malicious software is installed on the user’s computer. It is not a good idea to click on links in a Facebook or other social media page. Be wary of websites that ask for sensitive/ private information.

Do not click the web link unless you know what it connects to and you trust the person who sent the email to you. You can try holding the mouse pointer over the link and look at the bottom of the browser window to ensure that the actual link (displayed there) matches the link description in the message.

## 7. Issues regarding the cloud drive.

At the end of the security policy document, several practice questions were provided that participants answered and then self-checked their answers with the keys provided on the back. Through this process, we not only mimicked real organizational environment, but also ensured that all participants obtained basic information security knowledge before they started working on the tasks. This way, we controlled the level of information security knowledge, which is a potential factor that influences individuals’ information security practices. The participants were asked to follow the policies whenever applicable during the inbasket job simulation task. After this training process, participants in the two incentive groups (please see 4.3.1 for more details about the experimental conditions) were offered opportunity to earn extra rewards if they comply with the policies and it was explained to them in details how the amount of reward would be determined based on their compliance behaviors. The participants kept the training document during the task so that they were able to review the document whenever needed.

Next, participants engaged in the in-basket task that lasted for one hour. Each participant was provided with a username and password to log in to an email account which was located on our laboratory server. The 21 emails were sent to their email accounts in a pre-designed sequence. For the in-basket test, participants were provided with a description of their managerial role, their company title, and an orga nizational chart. They were instructed that they needed to determine priorities, organize their work schedule, and respond to emails accord ingly. Randomly embedded within the string of emails they received three phishing emails which instructed participants to click on embedded links that open websites that we had designed. The websites invited respondents to enter their company password and other personal information.

After the in-basket task was completed, demographic information was collected through an online survey. At the conclusion of the experiment, all participants were fully debriefed with the real purpose of the study, told about the three fake phishing emails, and provided an swers to any questions they may have had. No participant expressed any concerns. After the debriefing, participants were also asked to recall if they suspected any of the emails they received as phishing attempts other than the three fake phishing emails we had designed. All partici pants receive a \$20 gift card (regardless of whichever condition (explained below) they were in, or how well they performed).

## 4.3. Manipulations

## 4.3.1. Incentive condition

Our manipulations included three incentive conditions: 1) financial incentive within a positive (gain) frame. (2) financial incentive within a negative (loss) frame, and (3) no-incentive control group. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the three groups. The financial incentive manipulation tests the hypothesis that people will be more likely to comply with policy when provided an incentive. The gain/loss frame manipulation tests the hypothesis that people are more sensitive to losses than gains, and hence more motivated to act to prevent losses.

All participants were recruited to participate in the study in exchange for a \$15 gift card. Upon arrival at the laboratory, they were randomly assigned to one of the three incentive conditions. The participants in the two incentive groups were offered opportunity for extra rewards based on their compliance with information security policies. They were told that during the in-basket simulation they would encounter situations where the company’s information security policies should be followed. Participants in the positive-framing incentive group were told they would gain extra compensation (up to \$5) for complying with the company’s information security policies. Specifically, they would receive an extra 3 dollars if no more than two violations were detected, 4 dollars if one violation; and 5 dollars if zero violation. Participants in the negative-framing incentive group were told that they would also be given a \$5 bonus for complying with the company’s information security policies, but that they would lose a portion of that bonus if/when they failed to comply with the policies. Specifically, they would lose 1 dollar of reward if one violation was detected; 2 dollars if two violations; and all the 5 extra dollars if three or more violations. For participants in the control group, the importance of following the company information security policies was addressed and they were asked to follow the pol icies wherever applicable during the task; there was no mention of additional compensation.

## 4.3.2. Fake phishing emails

To test our hypotheses, we included three fake phishing emails which contained clickable links, and examined the participants’ ability to detect these as phishing emails. We recorded how the subjects handled both phishing and genuine e-mails. Metrics included: (1) whether and when they opened the emails, (2) whether or not they clicked on the links embedded in the emails, and (3) whether they entered information on the phishing webpages.

The three emails varied in their level of contextualization. Two emails were designed to be contextualized for the work role assigned to the participants and one was not contextualized. The two contextualized emails purportedly came from a staff member inside the organization and requested participants to (1) activate a file storage account on the company’s cloud drive (referred as “cloud drive” below) and (2) answer a customer survey from one of the company’s vendors, the name of which mimicked the name of a genuine company, Acu-Rite (referred as “customer survey” below). The cloud drive email is the most contextu alized among the three phishing emails because cloud drive is a part of the company system that was introduced at the beginning of the study in both the task description and information security training. In addition, among the genuine emails, there is one email asking participants to complete a task using the company cloud drive. The non-contextualized email asked participants to “check out” the pictures from a recent company picnic event (referred as “picnic picture” below). Appendix 1 presents screenshots of the three fake phishing emails. We varied the extent of contextualization because previous work has shown that sus ceptibility increases with contextualized emails [30].

The phishing webpages were fictitious, and we only recorded whether participants entered information or not; we did not save the content of what they entered. Appendix 2 presents screenshots of the three fictitious phishing webpages.

## 4.4. Analytical approach

To determine whether incentives and email condition influenced susceptibility to phishing we used repeated measures binomial logistic regression within a generalized estimating equation (GEE) framework. GEE is an extension of generalized linear models that enables regression analysis for non-normally distributed data. The dependent variables in our primary analyses were dichotomous (e.g., click/no click; yes/no entered information; yes/no detected) and, thus, a binary logistic regression framework is appropriate. In our 3 (incentive $g _ { \mathsf { r o u p } } ) \times 3$ (email) design, email was a repeated (within-subjects) measure, which requires that the correlation among responses for a given participant be taken into account. Failure to account for correlated responses may inflate the standard errors of regression coefficients in repeated mea sures data. GEE methods estimate unbiased regression parameters by specifving a correlation matrix that accounts for the form of withinsubject correlations of responses on dependent variables [79]. They are particularly useful when response variables collected over time or within-subjects are not normally distributed, as is typically the case of counted or choice behaviors [80]. Thus, GEE was used a variant of generalized linear modeling, particularly appropriate given the nature of our data.

To fit a GEE model to data, users need to specify (a) the distribution of the dependent variable, (b) a link function that transforms the dependent variable to a vector of parameter estimates in an additive model (i.e., $, \mathbf { y } = \beta \mathbf { 0 } + \beta \mathbf { 1 } \times 1 + \beta \mathbf { 2 } \times 2 + \beta \mathbf { 3 } \times 3 . . . ) .$ , and (c) the cor relation structure of the response variable [79]. Given that our primary dependent variables had two possible outcomes (e.g., click/no click), a binomial distribution was specified. The standard linking function for binary dependent variables is the logit link [79], which was used in our analyses. Finally, we specified an “exchangeable” correlation structure, which assumes nearly equal variances and nearly equal covariances among responses (i.e., compound symmetry). This structure is recom mended when there is no logical ordering to the observations within persons [79], as is the case for our data.<sup>1</sup> Examination of the var iance–covariance matrices in our analyses indicated reasonable support for compound symmetry assumptions.

GEE methods are common in life sciences and epidemiology research and becoming more prevalent in organizational science studies, including laboratory studies involving repeated measures (e.g., [81]). An example of a similar application of GEE in IS research is a study by Pinsonneault et al. [82] examining the effects of health information technology on patient care outcomes.

## 6. Discussion

We added to our understanding of user security compliance by exploring the role of financial incentives in improving people’s security behaviors, specifically on complying with policies regarding phishing emails. Our results indicated that incentives can indeed improve security behavior, particularly when a loss frame is employed. Compared with participants in the no incentive condition, those in the negative frame condition were less likely to click on phishing links and when they became suspicious of links were more likely to refrain from clicking. These findings are consistent with the rationale for Hypothesis 2, such that presenting incentives with a loss framing induces loss aversion, which motivates employees to comply with the policies so as to avoid losing money [91]. We hypothesized that both incentive conditions would improve security compliance and that a loss frame would produce stronger compliance than a gain frame. We find support for incentives structured within a loss frame, but not within a gain frame. Further, we failed to find consistent differences between the loss and gain frames.

It is important to note that the improvement in security under the loss frame was found in the case of non-contextualized (picnic pictures) and less-contextualized (survey request) and presumably easier to detect phishing emails, where it appears that the financial incentive increased participants’ vigilance for phishing emails. When phishing emails were highly contextualized, incentives showed no effect on susceptibility. In each of the phishing emails, there were multiple cues that participants could have used to make judgments about the authenticity of the emails. For example, the sender of the cloud drive email was someone whose name was not on the contact list and the email address indicated that it was from an external email system. In addition, the content of the email was clearly against the security policies of the fictitious company. For the customer survey email, although we borrowed the name of a genuine company (Acu-Rite), one could tell from the domain of the email address (“acuurite.com” instead of “acu-rite.com”) that the email was not from a genuine company account. For the picnic picture email, the sender was again not on the contact list. Furthermore, participants could have al ways hovered the mouse over the link to inspect the address. We assume that if individuals follow the policies and fully use these cues, they should be able to detect the emails as phishing. However, among the three phishing emails, providing incentives showed an effect on increasing people’s compliance with information security policies only in the survey and picnic picture emails. One possible explanation is that the lower contextualization of these emails made them easier to be detected.

The cloud drive email is most highly contextualized among the three emails because the participants were introduced at the information se curity training session to the cloud drive system used by the company, and received a genuine email that required them to perform a task using a file that was accessed through the cloud drive. The high contextuali zation made the cloud drive email difficult to be detected, and all three groups had about the same level of susceptibility for the cloud drive email. Also, the cloud drive task was purportedly a measure of one’s management skills, participants may have been more focused on accomplishing their “jobs.” As a result, the cues failed to raise red flags. This indicates that incentives may be more effective for detecting phishing emails that are relatively easily distinguishable. In fact, studies have found that the effects of incentives on individuals’ performance vary across the complexity of tasks (e.g., [83,84]). It is proposed that with the increase of complexity, more skills or resources are required to accomplish the task. When there is a natural limitation of skills, the gap between task complexity and skills increases, and additional effort is not sufficient to solve the task. In contrast, when extra effort does result in a change in behaviors and outcomes, incentives show a stronger effect [75].

Avoiding clicking links is the first and the most important step in avoiding successful deception by phishing emails, as many phishing emails contain links which upon clicking will automatically install ma licious software on the computer. In other cases, the major purpose of phishing attempts is to collect personal information. In such cases, it will be important that people make correct decisions at the second step by not submitting any personal information on the webpage. In the current study, we designed all three phishing emails with links that aim to collect personal information, so that we could examine how incentives affect participants’ likelihood of submitting information. We found no statistically significant differences in submission rates across groups for the three emails. However, one interesting finding is that, compared with those in the positive group, participants in the negative group were more likely to submit information once they click on the links. This suggests that incentives, serving as a buffer from falling for phishing, might work best at the first step when the incentives are introduced with a loss framing when the user makes that initial decision, but failed to have further effects beyond the first step. By contrast, when positive framing is adopted, people may be more susceptible to deception when initially evaluating the legitimacy of emails, but more vigilant during subsequent steps. This suggests that the mechanisms of the effect of incentives might be different when people are introduced with the re wards with a loss versus a gain framing. One potential explanation is that people in the negative group might be more cautious initially when they make decisions because they are motivated to avoid losses but are more confident once initial decisions are made. However, the results need to be interpreted with caution because they are not statistically significant. Further studies are needed to fully examine the mechanisms of different framings.

## 7. LIMITATION AND FUTURE RESEARCH

The current study adopted an “In-Basket” job simulation task, in which participants were told that the purpose was to validate a tool developed to hire managers and were instructed to try their best on the task. This deception was designed to mask the true purpose of the study and the tasks closely mimic the actual working environment. However, this also introduces a potential confound such that the participants may react to the phishing emails according to their job duties rather than their correct judgment regarding the phishing emails. That is, they did not click on the links or enter information because they believed it was not their responsibility or it was not work-relevant. However, our results regarding the suspicion rates suggested that incentives do have an effect on increasing individuals’ likelihood to question the picnic picture email, as there are significantly more participants in the two incentive groups than in the control group that suspected the email as phishing and did not click the link.

In addition, although our results provided some support for the effect of financial incentives on improving people’s responses toward phishing emails, researchers have criticized the use of extrinsic incentives for several reasons. Behavior that occurs in the presence of a reward is unlikely to be sustained when the reward is withdrawn. This suggests that once financial incentives are instituted, they must be maintained to ensure desired results. Prolonged use of financial incentives can be costly to organizations, so changes in behavior need to be strong enough to ensure a positive return on investment. Psychologists and behavioral economists have also identified unintended consequences of financial rewards; rewards may increase target behaviors while reducing other desirable behaviors (e.g., quality), or may inadvertently increase un desirable behaviors (e.g., cheating) [85]. Other psychologists argue that financial incentives undermine intrinsic motivation, which is seen as essential for prolonged effort and interest in an activity. Intrinsic moti vation refers to actions that are inherently interesting or enjoyable, whereas extrinsic motivation refers to actions that lead to outcomes that are separate from the activity itself (e.g., money, praise [86]). Intrinsic motivation is a powerful source of motivation that sustains engagement and effort over extended periods of time without the need for “external prods, pressures, or rewards” ([86], p. 56). However, it is most relevant for explaining behaviors that are exploratory, curiosity-driven, or associated with play. Workers can hardly be seen as intrinsically moti vated to follow security policies; security-related tasks are not designed to be intrinsically interesting or rewarding, and thus require some type of extrinsic motivation. The challenge is to prevent or mitigate the negative and unintended consequences of extrinsic motivation noted above.

A related opportunity for further investigation is potential for iden tifying the post-decision cognitive process of individuals who received the negatively-framed message and who choose to respond to the mes sage, rather than ignore it. Do such individuals re-calibrate their internal assessment of the risk when engaging in the subsequent behavior? Though difficult to measure and assess, it would be insightful to gauge the message recipients’ perceptions and beliefs about the relative risk after they receive the negatively-framed message and consider the ac tion and its consequences as they proceed. Perhaps, a neurophysiolog ical investigation such as Warkentin et al. [87] could reveal the nature of this possible cognitive process.

More creative methods, such as psychological nudges [90] in the form of pop-up messages or other subtle communications that may in crease the effectiveness of financial incentives need to be explored. Also, to sustain the desired security behaviors after the financial incentives are removed, textual or graphical nudges may stimulate thoughts that provide a subtle influence on security-related choices and may reinforce the motivation to engage in the desired behaviors. Future research should explore the use of nudges as well as explore this technique both as an initial intervention, as well as a maintenance intervention.

## 8. Conclusions

Our study examined the effect of financial incentives on individuals compliance with information security policies and the results found that incentives introduced with a negative frame successfully increased in dividuals’ alert toward phishing emails, particularly those not highly contextualized around work, and decreased their likelihood to click the link. The mechanisms of positively and negatively framed incentives produce different outcomes, which opens the door for further investigation.
