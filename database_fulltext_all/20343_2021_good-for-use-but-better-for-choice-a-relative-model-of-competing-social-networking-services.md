# Good for use, but better for choice: A relative model of competing social networking services

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Relative model Media richness Social identity We-intention Social networking Media choice Attention economy

## A B S T R A C T

Users of social networking service (SNS) are presented with a large number of competing products. As a result, their decisions to use one specific SNS may be affected by their relative usage experiences with other SNSs. However, previous studies have largely focused on the adoption and usage of one focal SNS, while ignoring the influence from similar alternatives. To address this gap and understand the importance of users’ relative per ceptions toward different SNSs, this study builds on media richness theory, social identity theory, and weintention literature to develop a relative model. Two similar but highly competing blogging services under the SNS category are chosen as the research context, and users who have used both services are recruited to test the proposed relative model. The empirical results indicate that relative immediate feedback and relative personal focus exert positive effects on relative social identity, which in turn, has a positive impact on relative weintention to use SNSs. Implications for both research and practice are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Nowadays, the rapid development of social networking has spawned a plethora of platforms that share a highly common user base and compete for the limited time and attention of users, thus leading to a new era termed “attention economy” [1]. Under the information-rich environment, the attention of users, as a scarce resource [2], has become the focus of competition among different social networking platforms [3]. Although this new phenomenon inevitably exerts a huge impact on users’ behaviors in social networking services (SNSs), current studies have given scarce consideration to this issue. Most of the recent studies on social networking adoption and usage behaviors have placed great emphasis on one focal technology without taking into account the potential impact from its competing technologies (e.g. [4–6],). In the era of attention economy, the success of social networking depends not only on the quality of the platform itself but also largely on its uniqueness and superiority over its rivals. Therefore, it will be more advantageous if one SNS platform can attract the limited attention of the users. This stimu lates us to investigate further how users make their choice decisions when different SNSs are available in the market.

Given the limited attentional resources of users and their limited time spent on SNSs [7], the use of one particular SNS platform most likely results in the decline in users’ attention and time spent on other platforms. In this case, usage decision may be more of a result of their relative experiences formed by comparisons with other competing SNS platforms, instead of merely the result of their absolute experiences toward a particular platform [8–10]. In fact, the absolute and relative perceptions of users matter in influencing the usage decision, depending on different contexts. When not facing competing SNS platforms, usage decision is more affected by the subjective and absolute perceptions toward a particular platform (e.g., good or bad experience). However, once faced with multiple and competing choices, usage decision may be more affected by a comparison, consciously or unconsciously, of the competing SNS platforms (e.g., better or worse experience). Therefore, the traditional absolute-value constructs and models, which measure the perceptions of users toward a specific object, without reference to its alternatives, may not effectively explain the usage decision of users when people face multiple competing products. Relative-value models, which refer to a comparative frame of reference model in which focal constructs are conceptualized as relative constructs [8,10,11], can address this issue by drawing a comparison among multiple competing objects (e.g., software, platforms, products, and vendors). Previous studies have also demonstrated that it is likely to draw inaccurate con clusions about the adoption of a specific product if scholars and prac titioners fail to consider the competing objects in a comparative environment [12–15]. From this point of view, the notion of relativeness should be more relevant to the phenomenon of competing technologies, and the relative model also represents a new way of thinking to develop the variables used in the research model.

Another important way to contextualize the research model is to consider the uniqueness of the research context (i.e., SNSs in this study). Existing studies have examined SNS usage from different theoretical perspectives (e.g., [16–19]). However, most of these studies are based on the analysis of individual intention (i.e., the subject of SNS usage is an individual). Given SNSs are primarily used for social interactions among a cohort of individuals, the decision to use SNSs is not only related to one’s own behavior, but also to others’ behaviors [20]. In the absence of explicit or implicit consensus to use SNSs, one’s individual intention to use SNSs is unsustainable or even unrealistic. Only when people believe others will perform an action (e.g., using SNSs) jointly at least with some nonzero probability, can they engage in the collective behavior. Therefore, it is necessary to take collective usage phenomenon into ac count when examining the use of SNSs [21–23]. In this respect, prior studies also suggested that actions in relation to a group of people should be conceptualized differently from actions that are more individual-focused, further leading to a discussion on group-referent actions and we-intention [24–26]. Following Bagozzi’s work ([25,27, 28]), this study presents an attempt to examine we-intention, which reflects one’s perception of a group acting as a unit (i.e., we-perspective) to perform a joint action. Compared with the widely used individual intention (i.e., I-perspective), we-intention makes a difference by capturing the nature of group-referent actions and one’s perception of others’ participative behaviors, which is the case in the use of SNSs. As mentioned above, given that the use of SNS platforms involves a com parison with its alternatives, the concept of usage we-intention itself also cannot fully delineate the behaviors of users in the context of competing SNSs. As a result, this study further reframes the concept of we-intention with a relative term - relative we-intention - to address this concern.

Previous studies on we-intention have demonstrated a high explan atory power of social identity in facilitating the development of weintention [23,27,29–31]. Therefore, this study follows the existing literature by applying social identity theory as an important theoretical perspective to explain how relative we-intention is developed. However, previous we-intention studies have largely neglected the role of IT fea tures in enabling social identity. In this regard, media richness theory is often regarded as one of the most prominent theories of media choice, considering it captures media features and functionalities [32]. Media richness theory is used in the current study also because this theory is considered complementary with social influence theories in explaining uses’ choices of communication media [33]. Based on the above dis cussion, this study attempts to explain how technological features drive SNS usage behavior via the translating mechanism of social identity. This attempt also echoes the call of the IS community for examining IT artifacts, which have been suggested as the core elements of IS research [34]. Drawing upon the literature on the relative model [8], we-intention [26], media richness theory [32], and social identity the ory [35], this study develops a contextualized relative model to under stand usage we-intention of competing SNSs. Similar to relative we-intention, social identity and media richness are also reframed as relative terms to meet the unique concerns of competing SNSs.

The remainder of this study is structured as follows. The next section reviews the theoretical background and related work, which are fol lowed by the research model and the associated hypotheses in Section 3. Then, Section 4 introduces the research method, including data collec tion and measurement, and Section 5 further presents the results of the data analysis. This study concludes by discussing the key findings, the limitations, and the implications for both research and practice.

## 2. Theoretical background and related work

This section first provides a background discussion of comparisonlevel theory, which forms the theoretical basis of the relative model. Then, the concept of we-intention and its difference from individual intention are provided, followed by a discussion of two key theories (media richness theory and social identity theory) used in this study to construct the research model.

## 2.1. Comparison-level theory and relative model

According to comparison-level theory, the internal standards used to appraise behavioral decisions basically rely on two types of compari sons: comparison level and comparison level for alternative [9,36]. In particular, the comparison level reflects a subjective criterion about how satisfying or unsatisfying a specific object is, and the performance above the comparison level is regarded as satisfying or, conversely, as unsat isfying [9,36]. However, different from the comparison level, the com parison level for alternative emphasizes that the evaluation of a specific object is based on the performance of its alternatives. In this regard, the comparison level and the comparison level for alternative have different merits in different contexts. When no alternatives are available or when people are unaware of the alternatives, people mainly use comparison-level analysis to evaluate the performance of the focal ob ject. Once people are aware of other alternatives, they tend to compare competing objects and make decisions in light of these alternatives [8, 37]. Prior studies in the marketing field have also suggested that cus tomers’ decision-making process that incorporates the competing al ternatives can lead to more accurate and favorable decision outcomes than merely focusing on a specific product [13,14,38].

The relative model can be regarded as a useful tool to evaluate the comparison level for alternative. Basically, the relative model is a comparative frame of reference model, in which constructs are conceptualized as relative terms obtained from the comparisons among competing products [8,10]. Relative model was originally used in the marketing field to investigate customers’ relative attitudes and behav iors when facing competing products [13,14]. Recently, an increasing body of research in the IS domain has recognized the importance of comparing competing technologies or channels, and further addressed the comparison level for alternative by examining constructs such as relative advantage [39–42], relative usefulness [43], alternative attractiveness [44], and rewards of alternatives [45]. The competing technologies are often regarded as a reference point when people answer questions related to a focal technology. Another line of IS research addressing the comparison of competing technologies has conducted path coefficient comparison to identify which factors are more impor tant to the use of one specific technology [46]. Although some IS scholars have initially examined the relative concepts [8,10,47], most of the current studies addressing SNS adoption and usage are based on absolute-value models, as indicated in Appendix A. Generally speaking, relative-value models using the comparative frames will provide more accurate answers than single-product-based absolute-value models [10]. Relative usage behavior can also better reflect the complexity and contradictory nature of a competing SNS market, compared to just focusing on the absolute usage behavior of one focal SNS. Therefore, this study attempts to use a relative model by applying comparison-by-difference to assess users’ relative evaluation of competing SNSs, as well as the consequent relative SNSs usage intention.

## 2.2. We-intention

We-intention is defined as “a commitment of an individual to participate in joint action [that] involves an implicit or explicit agree ment between the participants to engage in that joint action” ([48], p. 2). This definition suggests that we-intention is considered with refer ence to a joint action instead of an individual action that is more relevant to individual intention (I-intention). Compared to we-intention, I-intention represents “individual decision or plan to achieve a goal or perform an action by him/herself alone” ([49], p. 248). According to the definitions, the key difference between I-intention and we-intention is whether people think they carry out a particular behavior by themselves (i.e., individual action) or act together with others (i.e., collective or group-referent action), respectively. In the context of SNSs, we believe that usage behavior should be considered a collective behavior because unlike personal software, SNS cannot be actually used without the participation of other users.

It is also important to note that although we-intention targets col lective action, a closer scrutiny of the definition of we-intention clearly demonstrates that this variable is basically operationalized at an indi vidual level $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ commitment of an individual). In this regard, weintention refers to an individual’s subjective perception regarding the collective action to be performed, that is, an individual believes the extent to which a group of people would act together in the future. Notably, such beliefs are purely subjective and need not be true. As stated by Tuomela [50], “collective intentionality in its central sense is based on ‘we-thinking’, thinking in terms of a ‘we-perspective’” (p. 3). Therefore, we-intention reflects one’s own perception and judgment about group acting. In this regard, an individual can actually even be the “only person” ([51], p. 13) who has a we-intention in a focal group ([26], p. 8).

Some studies have empirically explored the concept of we-intention in the IS field and demonstrated that we-intention can play an important role in understanding the use of social technologies, including virtual communities, instant messaging, and social networks [21–23,25,27,30]. Despite these findings, we-intention is still a concept largely ignored and unstudied in prior studies. According to Appendix A, most of the recent studies on SNS adoption and usage have used traditional individual intention as the dependent variable of interest. In addition, prior studies on we-intention have only considered a single platform, without taking into account the effect of competing technologies. In this regard, this study not only captures the competitive environment, but also develops the concept of we-intention by focusing on “relative we-intention”, which addresses both the collective nature of SNS usage (i.e., we-intention) and the usage decision under a competitive environment (i.e., relativeness).

Prior studies addressing the antecedents of we-intention have also placed great emphasis on social influence processes, such as subjective norm, social identity, and group norm (e.g., [28]). Among the different social influence factors, social identity often explains the largest vari ance in we-intention [20,23]. In addition, social identity was found to have a greater impact on participation we-intention than other cognitive and affective factors, such as attitude, perceived behavioral control, and anticipated emotions [27]. The significance of social identity in pre dicting we-intention is mainly due to its emphasis on a self-inclusive social category, which is also the referent of we-intention. Therefore, social identity is an appropriate perspective to incubate we-intention in SNSs. Notably, media choice research has further suggested that media richness theory and social influence theory can provide complementary explanations of communication media choices [33]. However, previous we-intention studies have largely ignored the role of media richness and media characteristics in general. To achieve a comprehensive and integrative understanding of SNSs usage, this study adopts a contextual socio-technical perspective to understand the mechanism underlying the development of relative we-intention by integrating media richness theory and social identity theory, which are further introduced in the following sections.

## 2.3. Media richness theory

Media richness theory, also known as information richness theory, was initially proposed by Daft and Lengel [32] in the field of organi zational communication. Media richness theory provides a theoretical framework for describing the capacity of a communication medium to transmit messages that contain rich information. According to the the ory, different communication media have different levels of capacities to transmit information. Therefore, media richness can be assessed on the basis of the capacity of the medium to handle equivocality or uncer tainty. Media richness theory has been recognized as one of the key theories in explaining rational media choice in different media choice contexts [52–54], and it has proven to be an important theoretical perspective in understanding technology adoption and usage behavior [55–57].

Prior studies have identified four components of media richness, including immediate feedback, multiplicity of cues, language variety, and personal focus [32,58]. In particular, immediate feedback refers to the ability of a medium to provide timely feedback. Multiplicity of cues refers to the ability of a medium to convey a variety of explicit and implicit information cues. Language variety is defined as the ability of a medium to allow the use of rich and varied languages (e.g., texts, photos, videos, audios, links, and other multimedia data) to convey information. Personal focus refers to the ability of a medium to support the trans mission of messages based on one’s own circumstances [32,56,59]. The richer a medium is, the more quickly people can communicate with others, and the better people can understand ambiguous or equivocal messages [53,56,60].

## 2.4. Social identity theory

Social identity is defined as “the individual’s knowledge that he belongs to certain social groups together with some emotional and value significance to him of this group membership” ([35], p. 292). In the context of online environments, previous studies have often operatized “certain social groups” as a group of people with whom an individual frequently interacts within a particular online context [26–28]. Social identity is believed to be one of the most salient antecedents of we-intention [21,27,28], and it captures the identification process of social influence when using a particular technology [28]. Different from subjective norm, which is a more frequently used variable to explain technology acceptance in the IS field and reflects the compliance process of social influence [61], social identity represents the identification processes of social influence [26]. The key difference between the pro cesses of identification (e.g., social identity) and compliance (e.g., sub jective norm) lies in whether the influence is based on “liking or respect of another person” or “reward or punishment aversion” ([28], p. 146), and it is obvious that the former should be more significant in the context of SNS. In addition, the identification effect is more likely to occur in a context where a high degree of interdependence with others exists in performing an action [62]. This is also the case of communi cation technology such as SNSs. Therefore, this study adopts social identity theory as the underlying social influence mechanism predicting SNS usage we-intention.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

This study aims to investigate how users make decisions when facing competing SNSs. Drawing upon media richness theory, social identity theory, and the conceptualization of we-intention, this study develops a relative model, as shown in Fig. 1, to explain the SNS choice behavior. In particular, the research model includes four components. First, the concept of we-intention, instead of individual intention, is regarded as the dependent variable to capture the social nature of SNSs. Second. consistent with prior studies [21,27,28], social identity is modeled as the determinant of we-intention. Third, this study draws upon media rich ness theory to explore the effect of IT features. Fourth, instead of focusing on one specific SNS, this study places great emphasis on competing SNSs. As a result, all variables are conceptualized as relative constructs.

## 3.1. Effect of relative social identity

Social identity has been successfully used to explain we-intention to participate in collective or group activities in online environments [21, 27,28]. The reason why social identity plays an important role in pre dicting we-intention lies in its embedded core assumption that in dividuals categorize themselves into a self-inclusive social group (in-group categorization) and attach value connotations to the group membership [35]. In the context of SNS, a “self-inclusive social group” can be considered as a group of friends with whom an individual frequently communicates using SNSs. Such in-group categorization makes people perceive themselves in terms of “thinking, feeling and acting on the basis of a group level of self instead of a personal self” ([23], p. 156). This perception is consistent with the central idea behind the concept of we-intention in terms of thinking and acting from a “we-perspective” ([50], p. 3).

Due to the common basis on group-referent perceptions, social identity has been previously identified as a major determinant of weintention in different contexts [21,27,28]. In the competing SNS context, users may be involved in different social groups simultaneously in different SNS platforms. Under such a situation, users tend to make comparisons among competing SNSs according to the comparison-level theory [9]. A high level of social identity in one specific SNS may not necessarily lead to we-intention to use this SNS because users’ percep tion of social identity in other competing SNS platforms may be higher. Based on this reasoning, if users have a higher level of social identity in SNS X than in its alternative Y, then they are more likely to categorize themselves into X, feel more value embedded in the X membership, and further perceive a higher degree of “group level of self” in X [21,23], and as a result, they will develop a higher level of we-intention to use SNS X than the alternative Y. Therefore, we have the following hypothesis:

H1. Relative social identity is positively associated with relative weintention.

## 3.2. Effects of relative media richness

Previous studies have demonstrated that identification can be considered as a mechanism translating system design into the behaviors of users in virtual environments [63,64]. This is because identification arises from frequent social interactions [21], which can be facilitated by IT features [63.65]. A socio-technical perspective developed through integrating social and technical factors is also believed to be advanta geous in providing a comprehensive and meaningful picture of users choices of communication media [33]. This section thus addresses how technology features promote the development of social identity in the context of SNSs by adopting the perspective of media richness.

Media richness highlights the capacities of a medium to convey different information cues and handle equivocality or uncertainty in a timely manner [32]. Media richness can be measured by either objective or subjective criteria [66]. The objective measures of media richness are solely based on the invariant physical properties of a given medium, while the subjective measures of media richness are based on users perceptions toward a medium. Prior studies have shown that users can make a medium richer than what the objective features of a medium can create [66,67]. In this regard, the subjective measures of media richness can better reflect how users actually perceive the richness of a medium, and therefore, the subjective measures also become the preferred approach to investigate media richness in IS studies [53,56,68,69]. The subjective measures point out that people may have different percep tions toward the richness of communication media that may look similar based on the objective characteristics. This is also the case in the SNS market today.

In particular, media richness theory is an appropriate perspective in examining SNS due to the fit between the characteristics of SNSs and the four components of media richness. First, SNSs provide many features, such as commenting, replying, real-time chatting, and leaving messages, to support users to interact with others. As a result, SNS users are able to receive synchronous or asynchronous feedback from other users on SNSs. Second, SNSs support users in conveying multiple cues to express their explicit and implicit thoughts and feelings. For example, SNSs can convey static cues (e.g., textual and picture cues through the wordings in text messages) and dynamic cues (e.g., verbal cues through different intonation in audio messages or visual cues through gestures and facial expressions in video messages). Third, SNSs also allow users to send and receive information in a variety of language forms, such as texts, pic tures, emoticons, audios, links, and videos. Finally, SNSs enable users to receive customized information, for example, through subscription services. In addition, SNSs can trace the activities of users on the plat form and further create personas that can be used to deliver personalized information.

Immediacy of feedback refers to the extent to which a medium supports timely feedback during collaboration and communication [32, 59]. Immediate feedback can provide rich information and reduce equivocality and uncertainty [58]. In this regard, real-time conversa tions in SNS groups can increase the reinforcing effects of social inter action by reducing the psychological distance among all the concerned parties and by creating a less-mediated feeling of being with other people [57.70]. In the context of competing SNSs, almost all social networking platforms provide basic features, such as real-time chatting and automated alerts. Nevertheless, some new features, such as typing status, are constantly being developed and adopted by SNSs. If users perceive one SNS as superior to its alternatives in supporting more im mediate feedback, the resulting reduction of psychological distance and the less-mediated feeling will stimulate a more frequent, fluid, and responsive social interaction, thus leading to a higher level of social identity to the membership in the focal SNS. Therefore, we have the following hypothesis:

H2a. Relative immediacy of feedback is positively associated with relative social identity.

Multiplicity of cues refers to the ability of a communication medium to transmit various cues during communication [32,59]. The more types of information cues a medium can convey, the better the users can un derstand the information flow and transmit explicit and implicit meanings during social interactions [71]. As a result, multiple cues can decrease misunderstandings and further promote in-group relationship development [72–74], thus facilitating the formation of social identity [75,76]. Some prior studies have also corroborated this argument by indicating that richer media with multiple cues stimulate more frequent socio-emotional interactions among users [70,77], which lead to a higher level of social identity [21,75]. In today’s SNS market, we witness that SNSs support the transfer of a variety of cues and allow users to convey static and dynamic cues as aforementioned. In this regard, if users perceive one SNS as superior to its alternatives in transmitting a variety of different cues, then they will perceive a greater level of social interaction in the focal SNS [70], thus leading to a higher level of social identity than its alternatives [21,75]. On the basis of the discussion above, we have the following hypothesis:

H2b. Relative multiplicity of cues is positively associated with relative social identity.

Language variety refers to the extent to which a communication medium allows the use of a variety of symbols, including texts, images, links, videos, and audios, to deliver information [32,59]. Rich and varied language can convey a diversity of thoughts and feelings, thus supporting effective communication among people [32,78]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that language variety plays an important role in improving communication practices, and the lack of language variety may make communication and cooperation more difficult and ineffective [53,71]. Therefore, the variety of language can improve so cial interactions with other users [71,78], thus motivating users to achieve a sense of social identity [21,75]. In the context of competing SNSs, the functions of transmitting texts, images, emoticons, links, videos, audios, or even bullet comments are available on different SNS platforms. Therefore, if users perceive one SNS as superior to its alter natives in transmitting varied symbols, the SNS platform will foster more frequent social interaction than its alternatives. As a result, users will be more likely to feel identified with the social group in the focal SNS. Therefore, we have the following hypothesis:

H2c. Relative language variety is positively associated with relative social identity.

Personal focus refers to the ability of a communication medium to encompass different kinds of personal feelings and emotions [32,59]. Media rich in personal focus can deliver customized and personalized information to its users [53]. In addition, media rich in personal focus provide users with an enjoyable and value-added experience. Accord ingly, users are likely to feel emotionally involved with the communi cation group and attach high value to the group membership, thus indicating an increase in social identity [56,76,79]. Recently, with the rise of AI, some SNSs, such as Facebook and TikTok, start to integrate AI into their essential products and services to provide a more personalized user experience. SNSs also create personas to deliver personalized in formation and improve the personal focus. In fact, new technology features are rapidly emerging and constantly integrated into SNSs. If users perceive one SNS as superior to its alternatives in delivering personalized messages, they will be more likely to have a more enjoy able. and value-added communication experience in the focal SNS than what they experience on other competing platforms. In this regard, users will have more frequent social interaction with other people on the focal SNS, facilitating a higher level of social identity. Based on the discussion above, we have the following hypothesis:

H2d. Relative personal focus is positively associated with relative so cial identity.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Data collection and samples

We conducted an online survey to collect data used for validating the research model and the associated hypotheses. The survey was con ducted in Mainland China. The target population included individuals who had used both micro-blogging (e.g., Twitter, Sina Weibo) and lightblogging (e.g., Tumblr, Diandian) services. We chose micro-blogging and light-blogging services because they are both under the SNS cate gory, and the blogging service category in particular. In addition, both services are popular among young millennials [80,81]. The social network structures, including in-degree and out-degree distribution, separation degree, and component size, of Twitter and Tumblr are also similar [82]. In this regard, a great overlap exists between the user base of the two types of SNSs, thus leading to a very intense competition for the limited time and attention of the users.

Before the data collection, we conducted a pilot test with ten PhD students who majored in information systems and were familiar with both micro-blogging and light-blogging services. Their comments on the content, format, and wordings of the questionnaire were incorporated in the revised version. An invitation message with a URL to the online questionnaire and a brief introduction to the survey was then posted on both micro-blogging and light-blogging platforms. Screening questions were added in front of the questionnaire to ensure that all the re spondents had used both micro-blogging and light-blogging services in the past month. The respondents were further asked to answer the questions based on their most often used micro-blogging and light blogging services. Although recall bias is often considered a potential flaw in research involving self-reported data, the short time frame design (i.e., in the past month) and reference to the most often used technology can help to reduce the potential recall bias. In addition, when answering the questions related to social identity and weintention, the respondents were asked to refer to the group of friends with whom they frequently communicate on their most often used micro-blogging or light-blogging platforms as the reference group. To attract sufficient respondents to participate in the survey, a lucky draw was provided to the successful respondents. Finally, a total of 309 valid responses were received. Their demographic characteristics are pre sented in Table 1. The demographics indicated that most respondents were aged between 18 and 27 (76.1 %) and had educational level of undergraduate or above (71.2 %).

## 4.2. Measures

All the constructs were measured with the items adapted from pre vious studies (see Appendix B) to ensure content validity. Specifically, items measuring media richness were adapted from Lee et al. [56]. So cial identity, including cognitive, evaluative, and affective social iden tity, and we-intention were measured with items adapted from Bagozzi and Dholakia [27]. All the items were measured on a seven-point scale, and minor changes in the wordings were made to fit the current inves tigation context. Given that this study was conducted in Mainland China, the Chinese version of the questionnaire was obtained by following a forward-backward translation method. As mentioned above, a pilot test was also conducted to ensure the understandability and clarity of the questionnaire.

Regarding the ways to measure the relative constructs, previous studies in the domains of economic and cognitive psychology have demonstrated three different comparison mechanisms, including com parison by difference, comparison by ratio, and dummy comparison [83, 84]. In particular, comparison by difference is believed to be superior to the other two comparison mechanisms in accounting for a larger pro portion of the variance [8]. Additionally, comparison by difference is regarded as a more appropriate method when only two comparable choice alternatives are available [84], which is the case of this study. In the field of IS, we also witnessed some prior studies that had developed new constructs by the ratio or difference methods (e.g. [47,85,86],), thus further demonstrating the feasibility and availability of this approach. Therefore, this study measures the relative constructs by using the difference values of the original constructs. For example, the respondents were asked to rate their light- and micro-blogging usage we-intention. Then, the difference between we-intention to use the two blogging services can be expressed as RWe (WeLB, WeMB) = WeLB - WeMB, where RWe represents relative we-intention, WeLB represents we-intention to use light-blogging, and WeMB represents we-intention to use micro-blogging.

## 4.3. Common method bias

Common method bias (CMB) has been frequently mentioned as a major concern in empirical research where constructs are measured using self-reported data [87]. To assess the potential CMB, this study used both Harman’s single-factor test [88] and CMB statistical technique proposed by Lindell and Whitney [87–89]. The results of Harman’s single-factor test revealed that no single factor could account for most of the variance, thus indicating that the CMB was not a serious problem in this study [88]. According to Lindell and Whitney [89] and Malhotra et al. [87], we further chose the second-smallest positive correlation in the correlation matrix among substantive factors as the estimated shared correlation resulting from CMB, that is, $R _ { m } .$ The value of $R _ { m }$ was 0.067 in this study. After partialing out the shared variance caused by CMB using the procedure described in Lindell and Whitney [89] (see Appendix C), the results indicated that most of the correlations among substantive factors remain significant. Overall, the CMB issue is unlikely to be a problem in this study.

## 5. Data analysis and results

We have used the well-established partial least squares-based structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) approach to test our research model. We have chosen PLS-SEM for the following reasons. First, PLS-SEM is more robust with fewer issues of parameter estimation and model identification and has less-restrictive assumptions about the sample size and the distribution of the data, thus enabling PLS-SEM to address a wider range of problems as compared with covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) [90]. Second. while both CB-SEM and PLS-SEM can handle models with hierarchical constructs (e.g., social identity in this study), CB-SEM has more formidable constraints to the structure of hierarchical models $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ , the number of higher/lower-order factors, the number of items, and the relationships among higher-order factors), which are not warranted in PLS-SEM [90,91]. Following the two-step analytical procedures [90], we have used SmartPLS 2.0 to assess the measurement model to ensure the appropriate reliability and validity of the constructs in the first stage and then to examine the structural model to test the hypothesized relationships.

## 5.1. Measurement model

Prior studies have generally used internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity to assess the measurement model [90, 92]. Specifically, the internal consistency can be evaluated using the composite reliability (CR) and Cronbach’s α (CA), and both of which are supposed to be higher than 0.7. Convergent validity reflects the extent to which the items on the given construct are theoretically related, and it can be evaluated using the average variance extracted (AVE) values, which should be greater than 0.5. Discriminant validity can be estab lished if the square roots of the AVE for each construct are higher than the corresponding correlations between the construct and all other constructs. As shown in Table 2, all the criteria meet the requirements, thus suggesting that all the constructs, either in the original form or in the relative form, have a satisfactory reliability and validity.

Convergent validity and discriminant validity can also be evaluated by checking the item loadings. When all the item loadings on their theory-related construct are higher than 0.7, convergent validity is achieved. When all the item loadings on their theory-related construct are sufficiently higher than the corresponding loadings on other con structs, discriminant validity is achieved. Given that the validity of constructs in the original form has already been confirmed in Table $^ { 2 , }$ and that the focus of this study is relative model, we have reported the loadings and cross-loadings of relative items to make the results clear and concise. As shown in Table 3, all the relative constructs exhibit good convergent validity and discriminant validity.

As shown in Fig. $^ { 2 , }$ relative social identity has a significant positive effect on relative we-intention, with a path coefficient at 0.647 (t = 18.523), thus sup porting H . The effect of media richness is also partially supported. Relative immediacy of feedback and relative personal focus exert a positive effect on relative social identity, with path coefficients of 0.236 (t = 3.502) and 0.197 $( t = 2 . 6 4 9 )$ , respectively. ## 6. Discussion and conclusion

## 6.1. Key findings and discussion

Previous studies on SNS adoption and usage were largely focused on absolute-value models by considering users’ perceptions of a single SNS platform or investigating SNS platforms as a whole without taking into account the effects from the competing alternatives. However, multiple competing SNS platforms are available on the market today, and compete for the limited time and attention of users. Motivated by this rarely investigated phenomenon and to deepen the current under standing about competing SNS usage behavior, this study builds on media richness theory, social identity theory, and we-intention litera ture to develop a relative model. In particular, the variables used in the research model are operationalized using the relative values.

On the basis of 309 online responses from people who have used both micro-blogging and light-blogging services, empirical results indicate that relative social identity exerts a significantly positive effect on relative we-intention, with a large proportion (41.8 %) of the explained variance in relative we-intention, thus supporting $\mathbf { H } _ { 1 } .$ . The result confirm the important role of social identity in facilitating we-intention in a comparative situation. It is shown that, when users facing multiple competing SNSs and if they have a higher level of social identity towards one specific SNS, they will be more likely to have a higher level of weintention to use this service. The results of this study also show that relative immediacy of feedback and relative personal focus facilitate the development of relative social identity in a comparative situation, thus supporting $\mathbf { H _ { 2 a } }$ and $\mathbf { H _ { 2 d } } .$ It suggests that if one SNS is superior to it rivals in terms of immediacy of feedback and personal focus, users will have a higher level of social identity toward this SNS. However, relative multiplicity of cues and relative language variety fail to influence the perceptions of users toward relative social identity. Therefore, $\mathbf { H _ { 2 b } }$ and $\mathbf { H _ { 2 c } }$ are not supported. This may be caused by nonsignificant differences in the multiplicity of cues and language variety between micro-blogging and light-blogging. Both services support only verbal cues, such as texts, images, videos, and audio cues, which can be used for information communication. However, nonverbal cues, such as physical gestures, eye contact, and facial expressions, are explicitly restricted in both types of services [53,70]. In addition, Daft and Lengel [32] indicated that language variety is similar in most computer-mediated communication, such as video, audio, and text. Both micro-blogging and light-blogging services can be categorized as typical online blogging platforms.

## 6.2. Implications for research

This study contributes to the literature in the following three aspects. First, this study adopts a relative model using relative values to inves tigate SNS usage behavior, which has often been examined with singleplatform models using absolute values in prior studies (e.g., [4–6]). Single-platform models may not effectively explain SNS usage behavior under a competitive environment. For example, a person may have a strong intention to use SNS X. If its alternative Y is not considered, we can reasonably infer that he/she will be most likely to use X in the future. However, the actual situation may be that the person has a stronger intention to use the alternative SNS Y. Considering information overload in today’s society and the ever-increasing competition for users' limited attention. the person is more likely to use Y relative to X. In this regard, we believe that the relative model, with reference to alternative SNSs, can provide a more reliable and complete picture of what users really think and how they act.

Second, this study extends the well-established intention-based models by exploring usage we-intention. Notably, the traditional indi vidual intention has been widely used in previous SNS adoption and usage studies (e.g., [4–6]), ignoring the fact that social networking, as a communication medium, is only valuable when it is used by multiple decentralized users [21,28]. To address this issue, this study introduces the concept of we-intention, with reference to other users, to SNS adoption and usage research. Furthermore, this study employs the comparison by difference approach to develop a new construct, that is, relative we-intention, which not only reflects the impact of alternative SNSs, but also embodies the collective participation of SNS users. We believe this construct can parsimoniously capture the uniqueness of SNS usage in a competitive market environment, and future research can also extend this line of research by considering relative we-intention as the focus of investigation.

Third, this study integrates both social and technological perspec tives to understand the development of relative we-intention. Prior studies on we-intention have placed great emphasis on social influence processes [21,23,27,28,93] but has overlooked the important role of technological features. In this regard, media choice research has high lighted the complementary and synergic role of media richness and so cial influence in developing a comprehensive overview of the choice of communication media [33]. Therefore, this study builds on social identity theory and media richness theory to explore the formation of relative we-intention. In particular, social identity is considered as a social mechanism to translate technology features to relative we-intention to use SNS. Media richness and social identity are contextualized in the context of competing SNSs, and a socio-technical perspective is also highly appreciated and demanded by IS research [34,94].

## 6.3. Implications for practice

The findings obtained from this study also provide some managerial implications for practitioners. First, the relative model suggests that SNSs can win the favor of users when they are superior to its rivals ir terms of the concern of users. In this regard, practitioners must invest the necessary resources in the pursuit of relative competitive advantages. The core idea behind relative competitive advantage strategy is that media choice is basically driven by a comparison of various alternatives. This strategy can apply to both competing and complementary products. For competing products, practitioners should pay attention to whether they perform better than the rivals in all aspects of user concerns. For complementary products, practitioners should highlight the unique and irreplaceable value of their products in features that are compatible with other SNSs. Second, different from previous studies focusing on indi vidual intention to use a focal SNS, the current study establishes a novel strategy to drive the success of SNS by promoting the relative weintention to use SNS together. This study helps practitioners under stand how relative usage we-intention can be facilitated. In particular, this study points out that relative immediacy of feedback and relative personal focus deserve special attention from the practitioners. Some actionable suggestions include the delivery of personalized information and service, reduced information overload, and using human-like robots to provide timely feedback.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

Although this study has some important implications, it also suffers several limitations. First, to ensure that the model is simple and controllable, some important variables, such as attitude, anticipated emotions, subjective norms, and group norms, are not considered in this study. Although a substantial amount of variance (41.8 %) in relative we-intention is explained by relative social identity alone, we still recommend future research to extend this line of research to explore other possible mechanisms that may contribute to relative we-intention in SNSs. Second, this study was conducted in Mainland China, and an online survey was used. In this regard, all data were self-reported by Chinese SNS users. Generalizing the findings of this study to other contexts should be done with caution. Future research can extend the current study by investigating other social media platforms, such as Facebook and Twitter. We recommend the collection of objective data, such as competing SNS usage statistics, to understand better how users allocate their time and attention among competing SNSs. Third, relative usage intention is the dependent variable of interest in this study. To retain the focus on our research questions, we have not examined whether absolute-value constructs or relative-value constructs contribute more to the absolute usage intention of a particular tech nology in this study. Therefore, future research can further compare the effects of absolute-value constructs (e.g., attitude and satisfaction) and relative-value constructs (e.g., relative attitude and relative satisfaction) on the absolute usage intention.
