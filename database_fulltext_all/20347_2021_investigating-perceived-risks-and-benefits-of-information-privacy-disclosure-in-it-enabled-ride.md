# Investigating perceived risks and benefits of information privacy disclosure in IT-enabled ride-sharing

Xusen Cheng <sup>a</sup>, Tingting Hou <sup>b</sup>, Jian Mou <sup>c,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> School of Information, Renmin University of China, Beijing, China

<sup>b</sup> School of Information Technology & Management, University of International Business and Economics, Beijing, China

<sup>c</sup> School of Business, Pusan National University, Busan, South Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Information privacy disclosure Privacy calculus Ride-sharing Immediate gratification

## A B S T R A C T

This study investigates what affects perceived risks and how the trade-off between perceived risks and benefits affects individuals’ information privacy choices in the context of IT-enabled ride-sharing. Using privacy calculus theory and immediate gratification, we adopt a mixed-methods approach. We use interview data to identify perceived information disclosure risks’ antecedents, and then implement our research model, conducting privacy calculus on data from 507 rideshare users. The results indicate that privacy awareness, previous online privacy invasion, mobile payment security, and negative media exposure influence information disclosure’s perceived risks and that perceived risks and benefits are significantly related to immediate gratification.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid development of IT-enabled ride-sharing has facilitated the effective use of empty car seats and increased the efficiency of urban transportation [1]. As a type of sharing economy, IT-enabled ridesharing is characterized by temporary access, expanded customer role, assignment of economic value to services, platform mediation, and crowdsourced supply [2]. Unlike many business-to-consumer services. users must provide personal information prior to obtaining access to any transactions in ride-sharing platforms [3]. To efficiently match the de mand and supply sides, passengers must provide personal information, including their current location, pickup location, destination, and time schedule. Because of the nature of crowdsourced supply, information, including consumers’ identities, personal characteristics, and locations, can be accessed by a large number of users through ride-sharing plat forms. This information disclosure can result in privacy concerns. Perceived risks are also raised by news reports related to untoward in cidents between passengers and drivers of IT-enabled ride-sharing ser vices, as well as privacy information leakage. Concerns about information leakage further affect consumers’ use and recommendation intention. However, information disclosure can also benefit customers through the availability and concomitant convenience of IT-enabled ride-sharing services. Partly because of these concerns, information privacy has become an important practical topic in IT-enabled

ride-sharing.

The existing studies have demonstrated the significant influence of information privacy-related perceptions on individual behavior and decision-making in e-commerce [4–7]. However, research into infor mation privacy disclosure in the sharing economy remains limited. Although several studies have preliminarily investigated privacy con cerns in the context of ride and accommodation sharing [3,8,9], further studies are needed to systematically research information privacy. The literature on the sharing economy suggests that privacy concerns inhibit the development of e-platforms [3]. To supplement the literature, this study aims to investigate information privacy in IT-enabled ride-sharing. Privacy calculus theory [10] and immediate gratification [11] indicate that individuals’ information disclosure intention and behavior are influenced by the trade-off between the perceived risks and perceived benefits of providing personal data. Individuals might consider different types of risks of information disclosure, including short- and long-term risks, although they are usually influenced by the benefits in the spur of moment [12]. Hence, we focus on the following two research ques tions: (1) What leads to perceived risks and benefits of information privacy disclosure in IT-enabled ride-sharing? and (2) How does the trade-off be tween perceived risks and benefits affect individuals’ information privacy disclosure intention and behavioral responses in IT-enabled ride-sharing?

The current investigation is designed to answer the above research questions by combining qualitative and quantitative methods [13,14].

To answer RQ1, we performed a qualitative study to identify the ante cedents of perceived risks of information privacy disclosure in IT-enabled ride-sharing. To answer RQ2, we proposed a research model to empirically investigate the information privacy disclosure-related relationships through incorporating the findings of the qualitative study. The proposed model is grounded in privacy calculus theory and the effects of immediate gratification bias. Privacy calculus theory in dicates that perceived risks and benefits can determine information disclosure [6]. Immediate gratification bias reveals that a private in dividual’s choice can be motivated by immediate benefits over long-term ones [15].

This work provides theoretical and practical contributions to the research on information privacy. First, our work offers an original theoretical contribution by determining the IT-enabled ride-sharing context-specific antecedents of perceived risks. Second, our study con tributes to the privacy calculus literature by adapting this theory to the IT-enabled ride-sharing context, verifying this context’s antecedents of perceived risks and emphasizing the role of immediate gratification. Third, the empirical study provides evidence that challenges the privacy paradox by verifying the positive relationship between information disclosure intentions and behavioral responses. The study’s findings indicate that intention to disclose information positively affects behav ioral responses.

The following presents the structure of the research. First, we discuss the extant literature on IT-enabled ride-sharing, information privacy, privacy calculus theory, and immediate gratification. Then, we present the research method and data analysis, followed by the results, including a qualitative study into the perceived risks of information privacy disclosure in IT-enabled ride-sharing and a quantitative study combined with the results of the qualitative study. Finally, we discussed the theoretical and practical implications, its limitations, and advice for future directions.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

The literature review focuses on two focuses. The first part (sections 2.1 and 2.2) defines IT-enabled ride-sharing and information privacy, and reviews related works. The second set of sections (2.3 and 2.4) provides the study’s theoretical background. We employ privacy cal culus theory as the foundation of the research framework to explain the trade-off between perceived risks and benefits in IT-enabled ridesharing. Then immediate gratification is introduced as the supplemental role to explain the human decision-making process motivated by im mediate benefits.

## 2.1. IT-enabled ride-sharing

The IT-enabled sharing economy refers to “the peer-to-peer-based activity of obtaining, giving, or sharing the access to goods and ser vices, coordinated through community-based online services” ([16], p. 2047). Ride-sharing has developed to offer automated matching, rout ing, and payment using web and mobile applications [17,18]. Consistent with prior research [19], we define IT-enabled ride-sharing as car ser vices provided by private car owners to share seats with others using a smartphone application developed by ride-sharing platforms: for example, Uber and Didi. Ride-sharing platforms operate as a two-sided market that provides services to better match the supply of drivers with the demand for rides [19].

Much of the current studies recognize the value of IT-enabled ride sharing [20,21], which Table 1 summarizes, revealing two interesting themes. First, much of the literature has centered on the design and optimization of ride-sharing systems [1,18,19,22,23]. Second, recent studies have highlighted user behavior on these systems [24,25,102,21]. Despite abundant research about the design of ride-sharing platforms and motivation of ride-sharing services, surprisingly little research has addressed the importance of passengers’ information privacy in much detail or the use of a systematic approach or a holistic model within the context of IT-enabled ride-sharing. Given that privacy is a practical concern, this study aims to investigate information privacy-related perceptions that have not previously been well-researched.

## 2.2. Information privacy

Definitions of information privacy are inconsistent and relate to the elements of privacy. The types of personal information can be classified into four dimensions [27]. The use of personal information in the context of information privacy can also be classified into four di mensions [28]. This study addresses the individual level and investigates the passengers’ information privacy-related concerns and behavioral responses. For the purpose of this study and for consistency with previous research [29], we rely on Clarke’s (1999) definition for informa tion privacy. Information privacy refers to “the claims of individuals that data about themselves should generally not be available to other in dividuals and organizations and that, where data is possessed by another party, the individual must be able to exercise a substantial degree of control over that data and its use” ([27], p. 60)

Technological developments drive many information privacy con cerns; thus, information privacy has attracted the attention of informa tion systems (IS) scholars in recent years [29–32]. Information privacy has become an important topic in e-commerce [31,33–35] and web-based healthcare services [36,37], and these studies have been conducted at an individual [38], organizational [39,40], and soci etal/cultural level [41]. We conducted this empirical research at the individual level.

We incorporated the approaches and findings from previous studies into this information privacy study. Scholars have sought to explore the antecedents of information privacy in e-business and the Internet age, such as cultural values [41] and the importance of information trans parency [34], personality [38], and cognitive and cultural bias [42]. Drawing on the wealth of information privacy literature, primarily in the e-commerce context, reveals that the antecedents of information privacy are diverse and associated with context and theory. As previous studies have not dealt with information privacy issues in IT-enabled ride-shar ing, the context-specific antecedents have yet to be identified and explored.

Additionally, several studies have emphasized the relationship between privacy attitudes and behavioral responses [34,35,43]. Miltgen and Smith [32] studied the effects of privacy risk concerns on protection behavior related to governmental regulations. Ozdemir et al. [44] investigated the effects of benefits, trust, and privacy concerns on self-reported disclosure behavior. Tsai et al. [35] concluded that the display of privacy policy information is positively related to purchasing behavior. In addition, recent studies (e.g. [12,43],) have shown that a paradox exists between information disclosure intentions and behaviors. This study aims to explore passengers’ decision-making about infor mation privacy disclosure in IT-enabled ride-sharing.

## 2.3. Privacy calculus theory

Privacy calculus theory [10] provides the underlying foundation for explaining why individuals disclose information, explaining that their disclosure intention of personal information is grounded on the trade-off between risk and anticipated benefits [10,12]. Individuals might be willing to disclose personal information when the perceived benefits take precedence over perceived risks.

Prior studies have used privacy calculus theory to investigate the influence of both costs and benefits on information disclosure in different areas, such as e-commerce, mobile application, Internet use, and social media [4,6,45]. Dinev and Hart [4] extended the privacy calculus model to examine the balance among perceived risks, confi dence, and enticement beliefs that affect personal information disclosure intention. Dienlin and Metzger [46] applied this model to operational ized privacy concerns as costs and integrated privacy self-efficacy to explain both self-disclosure and self-withdrawal behaviors. Wang et al. [47] adopted the privacy calculus theory to investigate the influencing factors of the trade-off between perceived benefits and risks and their effects on personal information intention via mobile application; it operationalized costs as perceived risks. However, the perceptions of risks and benefits in IT-enabled ride-sharing have not been fully inves tigated in these studies.

## 2.4. Immediate gratification

Limited empirical research on privacy concerns in IS has focused on immediate gratification. According to behavioral models on immediate gratification bias, individuals prefer almost instantaneous benefits to long-term ones and tend to underestimate future risks due to self-control problems $[ 1 2 , 1 5 , 4 8 ]$ . These problems play an important role for many economic and social phenomena and are determined by the timing of an activity’s costs and rewards [15]. Perceived costs or risks are complex, including a variety of sources of risks, as well as both immediate and future risks. However, individuals’ decision-making processes are influenced by small benefits at the spur of moment [12]. Thus, based on privacy calculus theory and immediate gratification, we argue that in dividuals’ decision-making processes are determined by both perceived risks and immediate benefits.

## 3. Methods

Mixed-methods research combines both qualitative and quantitative approaches and is employed to develop extensive insights and explore varieties of phenomena [13,14,49,50]. A mixed-methods design can provide richer and more reliable results than a single-method approach [51], which prompted our selection of the former rather than a single quantitative approach for this study. Additionally, there is limited research into the risks of personal information privacy disclosure in IT-enabled ride-sharing. As discussed earlier, factors of information privacy are diverse and associated with context and theory. Privacy calculus theory serves as the foundation of our research framework to explain the trade-off between perceived risks and benefits. However, the diverse features of privacy perceptions necessitate a mixed-methods design to investigate the IT-enabled ride-sharing context-specific ante cedents. Therefore, we designed a sequential mixed-methods approach [52] that combines a qualitative study followed by a quantitative study for the purpose of development [13]. We used the qualitative study to develop constructs about the context-specific antecedents of perceived risks and develop hypotheses. Then the quantitative study tested those hypotheses and the research model. Based on a previous mixed-methods design [53], we created our mixed-methods approach, which is pre sented in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Phase 1: qualitative study

The qualitative study aims to gain in-depth insights into IT-enabled ride-sharing passengers’ perceived risks regarding personal informa tion privacy disclosure.

## 3.1.1. Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data was collected by semi-structured interviews. Each interview was conducted by two researchers and taped. One researcher oversaw the conduct of the interview, and the other researcher made notes and supplemented questions [54]. The interview guide consisted of three parts. First, we asked the interviewees to describe their expe riences with IT-enabled ride-sharing platforms. Then, we asked what led to perceived risks of information disclosure when they used them. Last, the interviewees answered an open-ended question to discuss IT-enabled ride-sharing platforms.

We sought interview candidates on social platforms in order to re cruit interviewees for our study. Then we selected those who have ridesharing platform experiences as participants for our research purpose. Our data collection strategy was to enlarge the interview size until the saturation point was believed to have been reached [55]. In total, we conducted 40 interviews. To avoid potential informant bias, we adopted several measures. First, following Eisenhardt [56], we trained all re searchers to react to responses rather than shift what seemed significant in order to record all information, and we transcribed each interview within 24 h. ## 3.1.2. Qualitative data analysis and findings

An inductive method was adopted to analyze the qualitative inter view data [55,57]. We used open, axial, and selective coding in analyzing interview documents [58]. First, during open coding, we extracted all relevant quotations about perceived risks of personal in formation privacy disclosure. All interview documents were reviewed sentence by sentence and classified into manageable pieces, and all research question-related discrete concepts and phrases were marked for further analysis. Second, we used axial coding. We reassembled inter view documents and related concepts and categories with each other by integrating raw materials. Initial concepts were linked to higher-level categories [57]. Third, we tried to frame this narrative at a conceptual level and incorporate theories to understand initial results and for further analysis in a quantitative study. To increase the validity, all context-specific factors listed in this study were mentioned multiple times by different participants. ## 3.2. Phase 2: quantitative study

Combined with the findings of the qualitative research, we conduct a quantitative study to research the phenomena of interest.

## 3.2.1. Research model and hypotheses development

The current investigation is grounded on privacy calculus theory and immediate gratification. Five antecedents of perceived risks of infor mation privacy disclosure were identified in the qualitative research. As presented in Figs. 2 and 3, we combined the findings from the qualitative research and developed the research model.

As defined earlier, privacy awareness refers to the extent to which one has knowledge about organization’s privacy practices [30,33,59]. Awareness of privacy practices is one dimension of concern related to privacy in online marketing [33], yet marketing organizations have often overlooked users’ awareness of privacy issues (Hey [66]). Privacy awareness is an important antecedent of privacy issue-related perceived risks. In IT-enabled ride-sharing, users’ personal information is required prior to accessing services, including location-based information and personal attributes. People with greater privacy awareness are more conscious of the importance of their personal information. Therefore, they will perceive higher risks than those with lower privacy awareness, such as potential higher risks of information privacy disclosure. The following hypothesis is proposed:

H1a. Privacy awareness will relate positively to perceived risks of in formation privacy disclosure in the context of IT-enabled ride-sharing.

Previous online privacy invasion, which encompasses negative ex periences related to online information disclosure, has been established as a component of cost and could affect individuals’ concern for privacy [34,60]. In an online environment, individuals experience different degrees of privacy invasion, and previous privacy invasions negatively relates to the willingness of partaking in online profiling [34]. In IT-enabled ride-sharing, it is possible for passengers to expose vast quantities of information (e.g., home, work, frequent addresses, and telephone number) to drivers and the ride-sharing platforms. Past ex periences with information privacy invasion can increase users’ con cerns regarding present privacy risks and information leakage, especially given the vast amount of information available to many users on ride-sharing platforms. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1b. Previous online privacy invasion will relate positively to perceived risks of information privacy disclosure in the context of ITenabled ride-sharing.

Mobile payment security refers to the security that individuals feel when using mobile terminals for payment [67]. Compared with cash transactions, mobile payment is safer and more convenient. Individuals benefit from mobile payment with the help of mobile networks and terminals [67], as they can pay bills anytime and anyplace [68]. IT-enabled ride-sharing services are implemented on mobile applica tions and paid through mobile payment systems, through self-provided payment interfaces or cooperation with online payment service pro viders. Mobile payment is one of the most important characteristics of IT-enabled ride-sharing in comparison to traditional taxi services. Mo bile payment contains electronic or pre-paid card information. Mobile payment security guarantees information privacy, such as the protection of payment information. Thus, we argue that greater mobile payment security indicates lower risk of information privacy disclosure. We therefore state the following hypothesis:

H1c. Mobile payment security will relate negatively to perceived risks of information privacy disclosure in the context of IT-enabled ride sharing.

Media exposure indicates companies’ reputations, which affect people’s judgment of an entity, and could influence individuals’ risk perception [61]. Repeated media exposure could affect familiarity with some objects, and the volume of available information could decrease the perceived risks [69]. Negative media coverage, such as news reports about vicious incidents between IT-enabled ride-sharing passengers and drivers, attracts more public attention than positive media coverage and accordingly raises individuals’ heightened risk perception. In recent years, the news reports about the vicious incidents between IT-enabled ride-sharing passengers and drivers have attracted people’s attention and caused concerns about personal safety and information protection. We argue that negative media exposure of ride-sharing services’ drivers, platforms, and organizations increases passengers’ risk perception. We therefore state the following hypothesis:

H1d. Negative media exposure will relate positively to perceived risks of information privacy disclosure in the context of IT-enabled ridesharing.

Personal information disclosure requirements refer to the informa tion that users are required to provide services in order to use the ridesharing mobile application. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of information disclosure requirements for organizations [64,70], and it has become commonplace for users to provide infor mation (e.g., real-time location and payment account details) on Internet platforms to access services. In IT-enabled ride-sharing, per sonal information disclosure requirements are necessary for individuals to use mobile application ride-sharing services. As discussed earlier, people must provide sensitive information prior to their use of IT-enabled ride-sharing services. The more information that IT-enabled ride-sharing passengers are asked to provide, the more they worry about potential information leaks. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1e. Personal information disclosure requirements will relate posi tively to perceived risks of information privacy disclosure in the context of IT-enabled ride-sharing.

Behavioral theory about immediate gratification bias plays a sup plementary role in addressing the balance between perceived risks and benefits in our study. As discussed above, perceived risks are complex and could include different considerations, while perceived benefits are immediate because individuals prefer almost instantaneous results [12, 15,48]. Because of self-control problems, individuals may overestimate benefits because they are influenced by small, spur-of-the-moment benefits [12] that directly affect decision-making [12]. In IT-enabled ride-sharing, passengers could decide to use ride-sharing platforms for small and immediate benefits (e.g., discount coupons, immediate ser vice). The perceptions of immediate benefits are directly associated with the perceptions of benefits. Thus, higher immediate benefits will lead to higher perceived benefits. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2. Immediate gratification will relate positively to perceived benefits of information privacy disclosure in the context of IT-enabled ridesharing.

Perceived risks and benefits are in the center of understanding the privacy calculus in IT-enabled ride-sharing [10]. Perceived privacy risks refer to individuals’ risk calculation regarding negative consequences and their perceived severity [71]. Previous research has shown the negative effects of perceived risks on users’ intention. For example, Nicolaou and McKnight [72] investigated information quality in data exchanges and found that perceived risks negatively affect intention to use data exchanges. Kim et al. [73] investigated the effects of trust and risk in individuals’ e-commerce purchasing decisions and found that perceived risk is negatively associated with purchasing intention. Pre vious information disclosure studies have also investigated the impor tance of perceived risks and found that risk is negatively related to attitude, online personal information disclosure intention could be positively affected by attitude [74], and the perceived risk of disclosing information negatively relates to disclosing intention [6,43]. Compared with traditional taxi services, passengers using IT-enabled ride-sharing services can be exposed to more privacy risks caused by information leakage during their transactions (Zhu, So, & Hudson, 2017), which could lead them to avoid IT-enabled ride-sharing services. Based on the privacy calculus theory and previous research, we therefore state the following hypothesis:

H3. Perceived risks of information privacy disclosure will relate negatively to intention to disclose information in the context of ITenabled ride-sharing.

According to privacy calculus theory, perceived benefits positively affect information disclosure intention [4] when the trade-off between perceived risks and benefits is considered during the decision-making process. Previous research has investigated the positive connection be tween perceived benefits and intention of information disclosure [6,75]. For example, Wang et al. [47] argued that the direct effect of perceived benefits on disclosure intention via mobile application is both positive and significant. The empirical study conducted by Keith et al. [6] indi cated that perceived benefits positively relates to privacy settings. In formation disclosure provides IT-enabled ride-sharing passengers the convenience of accessing ride-sharing services. We would similarly expect higher perceived benefits to lead to higher intention to disclose information in IT-enabled ride-sharing. Therefore, the following hy pothesis is proposed:

H4. Perceived benefits of information privacy disclosure will relate positively to intention to disclose information.

The relationships between information disclosure intention and disclosure behavior have been inconsistent in the literature. Previous research has argued that a paradox exists between information privacy disclosure intention and behavior [43,76]. Norberg et al. [43] explained the paradox by suggesting that risk could influence individuals’ inten tion to disclose information. However, other studies have found that information disclosure intention or willingness is positively related to actual self-disclosure [77] and that individuals will take measures to protect their privacy and engage in some privacy-protective behaviors [78]. We argue that in IT-enabled ride-sharing, intention to disclose privacy information positively influences behavioral responses of self-disclosure, in which people with higher intention to disclose infor mation will tend to engage in privacy disclosure behavior. Therefore, we state the following hypothesis:

H5. Intention to disclose privacy information will relate positively to behavioral responses of personal information disclosure in the context of IT-enabled ride-sharing.

Last, we incorporate the control variables in our study. User de mographics such as gender, age, education, length of Internet use, and frequency of use of ride-sharing platforms may affect information privacy-related concerns. Therefore, we added these as control variables on disclosure of information privacy.

## 3.2.3. Data collection

An online questionnaire was adopted to collect sample data. The full survey was subject to two pilot tests to pretest and refine the survey questionnaire to increase the measurement reliability. A total of 73 re sponses (male = 34.2 %, female = 65.8 %) were collected in the first round of the pilot study. We found that the average variance extracted (AVE) of mobile payment security was below the recommended threshold of 0.5. In addition, the cross-loading of perceived risks of in formation privacy disclosure was not satisfactory. Accordingly, we reworded the items to fit the research context and avoid ambiguity. A total of 85 responses (male = 29.4 %, female = 70.6 %) were collected in the second round of the pilot study. The reliability and validity of all scales were found to be acceptable.

Based on the two pilot studies, we further refined the questionnaire. For our purposes, respondents needed to currently use or have previ ously used a ride-sharing platform. A total of 507 responses (male = 34.71 %, female = 65.29 %) were collected for the final survey. Table 3 presents the demographic information about the final survey re spondents. Most respondents were aged 20–40 years, and 72.19 % had received a 3- or 4-year college education. Most of the respondents had used the Internet for more than three years. To further investigate the direct influence of perceived risks, perceived benefits, and immediate gratification on behavioral responses of disclosure, we implemented post hoc analysis. The results are pre sented in Fig. 5. ## 5.2. Theoretical implications

Our findings provide several theoretical contributions to current IS research. First, this study adds to the sharing economy and information privacy literature by identifying context-specific influencing factors of perceived risks involving privacy concerns within the IT-enabled ridesharing context. Compared with previous studies which suggested that perceived risks are a crucial antecedent of individuals’ information disclosure-related intentions and behaviors $[ 6 , 7 2 , 7 4 ]$ , the present research uses a qualitative study to contribute to our understanding of context-specific antecedents of perceived risks of information disclosure and their relationship with disclosure intention in IT-enabled ridesharing. This approach helps to find the original antecedents of perceived risk and expands our understanding of how different per ceptions affect people’s assessment of information disclosure. The pre sent study provides the first comprehensive assessment of information disclosure in IT-enabled ride-sharing services using a mixed-methods design.

Second, our work has contributions to the privacy calculus literature. This study adapts privacy calculus theory to the IT-enabled ride-sharing context by verifying the antecedents of perceived risks, emphasizing the role of immediate gratification, and testing the effects of perceived risks and benefits in this new context. Previous research examined the privacy calculus theory in information privacy $[ 4 , 6 , 4 5 ]$ and was limited to the effects of privacy calculus of outcomes and risks on intention [6,99]. This work contributes to the privacy calculus literature by extending the model through the exploration of antecedents of perceived risks and benefits. The findings indicate that privacy awareness, previous online privacy invasion, mobile payment security, and negative media expo sure significantly influence information disclosure intention. Further more, different from previous studies that verified the effects of general benefits and monetary benefits [6,45,46], this research pinpointed the role of immediate gratification. The study has confirmed the viewpoint of O’Donoghue and Rabin [15], as our findings showed that immediate gratification is positively and significantly related to perceived benefits, particularly disclosure behavioral responses.

Third, our empirical investigation helped to provide evidence that challenge the privacy paradox in IT-enabled ride-sharing. Some scholars have argued that there is a paradox between intentions and actual behavior, while others have asserted that intention and behavioral responses are consistent. For instance, Son and Kim [5] developed a model about privacy-protective responses and concluded that informa tion privacy concerns positively influence a variety of behavioral re sponses. Beresford et al. [76] found that, although people are dissatisfied with providing sensitive information, their behaviors are not consistent. The findings of our research indicate that a positive connection exists between information disclosure intention and behavioral responses.

## 5.3. Practical implications

This work also has implications for practitioners and policy makers. Despite the widespread belief that privacy concern is imperative for the development of the sharing economy, limited practical implications have been shared with managers of ride-sharing platforms. First, this study verified several context-specific influencing factors of perceived risks that are associated with information disclosure. Managers who recognize that privacy awareness and previous online privacy invasions are linked to perceived risks should craft a clear and conspicuous privacy policy on ride-sharing platforms. Furthermore, privacy management tools [3] could help passengers feel safe about their information data and decrease their anxiety about possible information leaks. Managers should adopt privacy management tools to protect passengers’ privacy. To avoid payment method concerns, managers must guarantee the se curity of each transaction and protect payment information. In addition, managers should attempt to minimize negative news and set up crisis planning and emergency communications.

Second, our research highlights that immediate gratification i associated with perceived benefits and personal information privacy disclosure. It is suggested that the managers should provide more im mediate benefits in using the platforms. For example, a discount coupon might be useful in encouraging passengers to provide frequently visited addresses. Furthermore, managers should provide immediate conve nience such as alternative transportation and new rider discounts to attract potential passengers.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

Inevitably, our study includes some limitations. We believe that these limitations offer rich opportunities for future research. First, we are limited in the generalization of the research findings. This research was conducted in IT-enabled ride-sharing. Therefore, the antecedents of perceived risks identified in our study might not be able to be general ized to other contexts. We believe the findings of this study may be strengthened by examining the results in different contexts. Further research should be conducted to explore the antecedents of perceived risks. Second, although this study extended privacy calculus theory by supplementing it with immediate gratification, there may be con founding factors that also influence the findings. For example, the tradeoff between perceived risks and benefits might be influenced by the perceived risks of other users in considering information disclosure. According to the third-person effect theory [100], individuals tend to believe that others will undergo greater harm than themselves. The trade-off between perceived risks to themselves and the perceived risk for other users may affect users’ information disclosure decision-making process. Future research could therefore also investigate the phenome non of information disclosure by including the third-person effect. Third, we adopted privacy calculus theory as the basis of our research, which means that the information disclosure intention is grounded on the trade-off between perceived risks and perceived benefits. Other variables can also play an important role in influencing such intention and behaviors, such as trust (Zimeer et al., 2010) and perceived justice [5]. Rich perspectives are encouraged in future research in investigating information privacy.

## 6. Conclusion

Information privacy is a persistent problem in the digital age. This work employed the privacy calculus theory and concept of immediate gratification to investigate the trade-off between perceived risks and benefits in personal information disclosure. We conducted a mixedmethods investigation to study the information privacy issues in ITenabled ride-sharing. Our study offers an original theoretical contribu tion by specifying the antecedents of perceived risks in the IT-enabled ride-sharing context. In addition, we developed and empirically exam ined a theoretical model that explains the role of perceived risks and benefits in determining individuals’ information privacy disclosure in tentions and behavioral responses. The research results provide man agers with rich insights into encouraging measures to reduce passengers anxiety about the perceived risks of information privacy disclosure. We anticipate that future research may investigate information privacy from more diverse perspectives and the effect of immediate gratification on behavioral responses.
