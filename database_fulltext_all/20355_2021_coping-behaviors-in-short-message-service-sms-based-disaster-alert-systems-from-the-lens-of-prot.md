# Coping behaviors in short message service (SMS)-based disaster alert systems: From the lens of protection motivation theory as elaboration likelihood

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Disaster alert management systems Coping behavior Protection motivation Elaboration likelihood

## A B S T R A C T

Short message service (SMS)-based disaster alert systems are used as critical methods that inform people of natural disasters. This study investigates how two different decision-making routes influence adaptive copings based upon users’ protection motivations. The two important routes, argument quality and source credibility, are presented as focal antecedents of coping adaptiveness. And perceived threat and coping efficacy are tested as a moderator as elaboration likelihood. To achieve these goals, we propose a theoretical framework and analyze survey data to test the hypotheses. The results illustrate that two elaboration likelihood variables show signif icant moderating roles in the proposed research model.

## 1. Introduction

To alleviate the impact of natural disasters (e.g., hurricane, tsunami, and earthquake), many countries have started to deploy and operate short message service (SMS)-based disaster alert systems. Disaster management refers to the activities designed for maintaining control over disaster and emergency situations and for providing a framework for helping at-risk people to avoid or recover from the possible disaster. This activity includes not only pre-disaster activities like early warnings systems, but also post-disaster activities like evacuation and relief [1]. And SMS-based disaster alert systems have been commonly used in numerous countries as early warning systems. For example, in Japan, an earthquake alert is pushed directly to a user’s phone through the service named Area Mail Disaster Information Service, which uses cell broadcast type SMS systems [2]. Nowadays, because it is assumed that mobile phones are used by everyone in most developed societies, SMS-based systems have been effectively used as a basic platform that can pro vide relevant information to the most recipients possible. And given the fact that more natural disasters and extreme weather has become common, SMS-based disaster alert systems will receive more attention from now on.

SMS-based disaster alert systems directly relate to the end-user’s safety risk by providing relevant information and guidelines [3]. And during this process, end-user information processing and coping be haviors can be one of the key factors that determine the success of such systems. However, these phenomena have not been well examined in the information system (IS) literature. On the other hand, in other areas, most studies illustrate the technical aspects of the service. For example, possible broadcasting methods [1], coordination with existing systems (e.g., Global System for Mobile [4];), or the effectiveness of specific applications [5] have been examined. It is essential to better understand how these services prepare the message, along with the reactions they elicit from recipients. For example, when end-users try to find ways to disable the alert systems because the system broadcasts irrelevant messages or untimely alerts to recipients, the result is these messages are deemed useless or irrelevant, thus nullifying the purpose of a disaster alert system. Although such systems are equipped with advanced tech nologies and protocols, if end-users do not respond to them properly, the goal is not achieved, and they are considered ineffective. Therefore, we consider that based on appropriate theoretical understanding, we need to test how end-users cope with the messages of disaster alert systems.

We believe that the context of SMS-based disaster alert systems is an interesting context wherein multiple theories meet and integrate to provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenomena discussed above. Based on the importance of these systems, this paper brings the elaboration likelihood model (ELM), coping theory, and protection motivation theory (PMT) to the behavioral framework of SMS-based disaster alert systems. ELM explains behavioral changes based on possible elaboration likelihood factors. For instance, individuals can evaluate a system’s effectiveness in preventing or avoiding disasters through the judgment with cognitive endeavors (i.e., central route) or with cue-based impressions of message (i.e., peripheral route). In this study, we employ argument quality (i.e., the degree to which the disaster alert message’s arguments are cogent or persuasive) as a central route, and source credibility (i.e., the sending authority’s positive characteristics that influence the recipient’s acceptance of a disaster alert message) as a peripheral route. Further details will be discussed in the hypothesis development. And coping theory addresses how in dividuals react to possible threats adaptively and maladaptively, based on appraisals and copings. In addition, we apply the PMT elements as factors that potentially impact on the relationship between two core routes and individual’s coping responses. PMT has been applied to find motivation factors that will change individual’s behavior to better protect themselves in disastrous events [6]. PMT identifies perceived severity of threat, perceived probability of the occurrence, perceived coping response efficacy [7], and self-efficacy expectancy [8] as moti vation factors to be secured. In the context of this study, we focus on the role of perceived threat (perceived severity of threat and perceived probability of the occurrence) and perceived efficacy as elements that may impact on the relationship between two routes and coping responses.

Therefore, this study addresses the following research questions: (1) How do argument quality and source credibility of the disaster alert messages influence the recipients’ coping responses? (2) How do in dividuals’ threat perception and efficacy belief work as elaboration likelihood to the message recipients in the case of SMS-based disaster alert systems? To answer these questions, an individual-level approach with the survey data and structural equation modeling technique were used. For the empirical verification, data were collected from end-users of SMS-based disaster alert systems in South Korea. This study explores possible central and peripheral routes by testing core variables, argu ment quality, and source credibility, along with possible elaboration likelihood factors (i.e., perceived threat and efficacy derived). And as responses, adaptive and maladaptive behaviors are also examined.

This study offers two main contributions to IS literature. First, this study examines how ELM, PMT and coping theory combinedly explain safety coping responses. As Boss et al. [9] addressed, most studies did not pay attention to how the messages are prepared, which critically determined how recipients react to the messages. This study provides a unique perspective by exploring how the roles of PMT-based constructs (i.e., perceived threat and perceived efficacy) influence the information processing routes that lead to coping adaptiveness. The results indicate a significant interplay among threat perception, efficacy, argument qual ity, and source credibility. Second, the continuum between adaptive and maladaptive behaviors was explored in the context of SMS-based disaster alert systems. Regardless of the importance of the topic, only a handful of studies have surfaced in the IS research. By applying well-proven theories to the area, this study provides the theoretical foundation for future research as well.

The organization of the paper is as follows: prior studies on SMS based disaster alert systems will be discussed first in the next section. Then, a literature review on ELM, PMT, and coping theory will be pre sented, and we will propose focal hypotheses as well as the research model, and describe the data collection and research methodology used in this study, prior to discussing the results and analysis. The paper will conclude with the theoretical and practical implications of the study, as well as limitations and future directions.

## 2. SMS-based disaster alert systems

Providing an appropriate message to potential victims of disaster situations (e.g., natural disaster, terrorist attack, etc.) is a substantial task, for it entails informing others about the current status or alerting them about upcoming emergency incidents [10]. These messages enable people to better understand the current disaster situation. In addition, such messages can also help people prepare for the next steps of the incident [11]. For instance, alert messages regarding the current status of an earthquake as well as its potential impact, allow people to be aware that they may need to expect an extreme situation or even move to a safer place. However, when these messages are ignored people are often uninformed about the emergency situation, which can lead to a lack of preparedness and ultimately more severe consequences. This is why it is crucial that the channels to distribute emergency alert messages are effective.

In order to effectively distribute disaster alert messages, multiple technologies, such as outdoor sirens, electronic media, route alert, Tone Alert Radio (TAR), or Social Network Service (SNS) have been used; prior research focused on the effectiveness of these message channels [12]. According to a report by the Vice Chairman of Deloitte, 17 developed countries and 6 developing countries that participated in the survey have very high mobile phone penetration rates (91 % and 90 %, respectively; [13]). This indicates that if alert messages are distributed via mobile phone SMS using an already pervasively used network, it can likewise be used to reach a huge number of people and have a very high level of message diffusion effectiveness. In addition, when comparing mobile phone SMS with many of the technologies aforementioned in the previous paragraph, recipients of phone alert messages can use more developed mobile technologies (e.g., text reader, text magnifier, geolocation-based service). Thus, SMS-based disaster alert systems can send messages to more targeted recipients.

Prior literature illustrated that sources and contents of the message are also important factors to effectively distribute a message [14.15]. Effectiveness of message distribution refers to high message receiving rate out of the total messages sent to the population. In the case of natural disaster, informing on the current situation or giving guidelines to a maximum number of people should be considered as high effec tiveness of the message diffusion. In this sense, studying ELM and coping theory with the consideration of PMT elements has important implica tions which are relevant for this study. First, ELM can examine how recipients process the messages and form judgments in the disastrous events [16]. Second, PMT elements can provide clues that impact an individual’s perception of incident to increase / diminish emergency alert communication effectiveness [17]. Finally, the different coping results of individuals in disastrous events can be examined. These the ories can be interactively integrated into the comprehensive model. Further discussion and details of the theories will be provided in the following sections.

## 3. Elaboration likelihood model

ELM depicts how decision-making, attitude formation, or behavioral changes are made through recipients’ information processing [18]. Previous ELM studies have explained that such individual phenomena are caused by two distinct information processing routes which are heavily influenced by information recipients’ cognitive efforts or external cues. In other words, the temporal or constant states of in dividuals allow them to prefer certain information processing paths to make the given information meaningful when changing behaviors or attitudes. For instance, in the cases of information processing for comprehension, learning, or retention of message arguments, individuals can analyze the given information in detail through various cognitive endeavors to make their judgments [19]. However, in the cases of information processing for basic states such as identifying pain, attractiveness, or power, individuals can simply make cue-based judg ments by trusting or accepting the given information when the source of the information has enough credibility [19]. These core contextual variables that determine the paths of information processing are defined as elaboration likelihood in ELM and has been investigated as a critical factor in the success of persuasion [20].

In the ELM literature, central routes and peripheral routes represent two different types of information processing [21]. The central routes relate to objective analysis for accuracy and relevancy of the given in formation, require in-depth understanding of the subjects, and accom pany valid and reasoned arguments [18]. Therefore, this process requires information recipients to exert many cognitive endeavors. For example, Miles and Morse [22] argued that the nature of the risk of the event and the information source influenced how an individual pro cessed the modern mass media information in the event of Hurricane Katrina. In another example, when a recipient of SMS-based disaster alert systems reads the message, and if she reviews the details of it to make sure whether the alert and behavioral instruction are valid and safe, they are taking a central route. According to the literature, the behavioral changes or decision-making influenced by the central routes have a tendency to be persistent, consistent, and predictive, because the decisions are made through the assessment of objective information and logical reasoning [23].

On the other hand, the peripheral route involves assessing the cues that surround the core argument of the message. According to the literature, the peripheral route requires less cognitive effort because the cue-based judgments are often made based on the tone of the argument, source of the information, general impression, or other surface-level characteristics [19]. Individuals tend to take a peripheral route to make judgments when the detailed assessment of the message is costly and time-consuming, or unavailable to them [21]. For example, Liu et al. [24] argued that even rumor can be easily retransmitted to people if it disguises good source credibility in the case of disasters. In another example, when a recipient of SMS-based disaster alert systems reads the message, and simply perceives the authority of the sender and judges the usefulness of the message, the peripheral route is taking precedence in the situation. Interestingly, studies have illustrated that judgments formed by peripheral routes have a tendency to be less consistent and easier to sway [21].

As we discussed above, ELM depicts how information recipients respond to and even elaborate on the given information based on various conditions, including capability, belief, availability, and preoccupation. These differences play a critical role in understanding and anticipating the information processing routes that individuals take, particularly when the information is critical in handling the possible threats and risks in the disastrous events [16,25]. The prior literature illustrated that individuals rely more on central routes when their elaboration likeli hood is high, and peripheral routes become more influential when their elaboration likelihood decreases [19].

In this study, we believe that ELM works as an overarching theory that interconnects two other theories we will discuss: PMT and coping theory. First, as we described above, ELM has been used to explain in dividuals’ perception, attitude, or behaviors. In that sense, coping be haviors can be explained within this framework from an individual perspective. Furthermore, ELM provides a comprehensive explanation of how coping adaptiveness can vary based on the routes that in dividuals take in the context of possible disastrous events. Second, elaboration likelihood is one of the key parts that determine the routes, i. e., central and peripheral routes. As the definition of elaboration like lihood illustrates, it is a certain state or condition that facilitates a spe cific decision-making path. Although it was introduced in the context of information security, Wall and Warkentin [26] demonstrated that threat perception can be used as an important elaboration likelihood. In a similar vein, we believe that in the disastrous event context, threat perception works as an important factor that determines following phases. And perceived efficacy has been well-examined as a represen tative elaboration likelihood factor [27]. Therefore, we develop a theoretical model that links ELM, PMT, and coping theory with core constructs which will be further discussed in the following sections.

## 4. Protection motivation theory and coping theory

PMT has been used to study factors that can motivate people to change their security-related behavior to protect themselves and their institutions [9]. According to the [28], p.411), “The purpose of PMT research is usually to persuade people to follow [a] communicator’s recommendations.” This theory has been studied in a variety of the research domains to find the variables that cause decisions associated with the risk and assessment of the ability to predict protective action [29]. The major elements that were studied as factors of PMT are: (1) perceived threat, which comprises with perceived severity of threat and perceived probability of the occurrence [30] and (2) perceived efficacy, which in turn comprises with perceived response efficacy and perceived self-efficacy. Perceived threat refers to the individual’s evaluation of the threat in the situation, and perceived efficacy is defined as “cognitions about the effectiveness, feasibility and ease with a response that alle viates or helps in avoiding a threat” ([31], p.381). In order for more people to adapt to the use of SMS-based disaster alert systems, these individual factors need to be considered as important elements.

Individual’s coping responses help them make decisions regarding how they react to a particular situation [32–34]. Understanding coping responses assists with discovering the suitable management skills (or best practices) for certain situations [31]. There are two types of coping responses that exist, namely, adaptive and maladaptive responses [35]. In general, adaptive coping responses are explained as those responses that can contribute to reducing risk for people (or low adjustment problems), whereas maladaptive coping responses are defined as re sponses that contribute to an increase in risk for people (or high adjustment problems [36];). Although these two coping responses differ in terms of the potential impact, due to the different characteristics of alert message recipients, both types of coping responses need to be investigated in an incorporated manner to implement more effective SMS-based disaster alert systems.

Psychologists have examined different dimensions of coping re sponses. For instance, Pearlin and Schooler [37] explained copings in three categories: 1) active behavioral, 2) avoidance, and 3) active cognitive. Additionally, Billings et al. [38] presented: 1) appraisal-focused, 2) problem-focused, and 3) emotion-focused as main coping behaviors. Folkman and Lazarus [39] instead suggested these two categories:1) emotion-focused and 2) problem-focused. Then later, Endler and Parker [32] proposed: 1) task-focused, 2) emotion-focused, and 3) avoidance coping. As we noted earlier, depending on the re searchers or the situation, categories of coping strategy can be changed. In addition, prior research from IS literature also applied different cat egories of coping responses. For example, D’Arcy et al. [40] introduced two categories of coping (i.e., problem- and emotion-focused), but only restricted their attention to emotion-focused coping to investigate the role of security-related stress on security violation intention. In the case of Wang et al. [31], three strategies of coping responses were applied, based on Endler and Parker’s [32] categorization to measure coping adaptiveness in the context of phishing detection In addition, there were prior studies that focused on the coping and incident responses/disaster management. For instance, the concepts of individual’s coping capacity and adaptive capacity were applied to improve the assessment of natural disaster resilience [41]. AMoran and Colless [42] examined firefighters reactions based on their coping styles in the context of incident re sponses. In the context of SMS-based disaster alert systems, we also expect to see the continuum of maladaptive-adaptive copings as coping responses from the perspective of message respondents. In this study, a multi-faceted construct “coping adaptiveness” is used to capture the coping results of the alert message recipients. We propose task-focused coping as adaptive coping, and emotion-focused and avoidance coping as maladaptive coping by following the precedent of Wang et al. [31].

## 5. Hypotheses development

Based on the discussion of ELM, PMT, and coping theory, Fig. 1 presents a conceptual model of the drivers of coping adaptiveness with alert message recipients. This conceptual model is used to test crosssectional associations encompassing argument quality and source credibility of the message, and recipients’ threat and efficacy perception on the possible alert events. Besides that, moderating the effects of perceived threat and efficacy are also tested. Detailed theoretical sup port for these relationships is provided in the following subsections.

## 5.1. From argument quality to coping adaptiveness

Previous studies on ELM categorized the routes that affect the changes of individuals’ attitude and behavior into two categories: cen tral and peripheral [23,27]. In this study, as a dependent variable, coping adaptiveness is used. Coping adaptiveness refers to the degree to which the recipients involve confronting problems directly, making reasonable appraisals of possible incidents and trying to comply with the provided instructions.

Argument quality, one of the most well-examined central route fac tors, is defined as a subject’s perception that a message’s arguments are strong and convincing [18]. The argument quality of the given message can be high if the message is prepared with a well-justified and compelling argument. According to the prior literature, through the central route (i.e., argument quality) individuals’ rational and cognitive judgment is activated rather than an emotional reaction [43]. In a similar vein, Pee and Lee [16] argued that messages with high infor mation quality draw recipients’ attention and promote active cognitive processes, such as learning message content, generation of cognition, and dissonance-induced reasoning, which led to trust in the crisis in formation in the Fukushima nuclear disaster in Japan. If messages result in predominantly positive thinking, the persuasion can successfully elicit individuals' changes in their attitude or behavior [44].

This study uses argument quality as a main, central information processing route in handling SMS messages. Argument quality reflects accuracy, completeness, and timeliness of the information for the disruptive events. And as illustrated in Fig. 1, coping adaptiveness in volves confronting problems directly, making reasonably realistic ap praisals of problems, recognizing and changing unhealthy emotional reactions, and trying to prevent adverse effects on the body. Alert message recipients use SMS-based disaster alert systems to be alerted of possible disruptive events, including extreme weather, possible pollutant epidemics, earthquakes, etc. If the messages provided by the system turn out to be irrelevant, insufficient, inaccurate, or out-of-date to their possible responses, end-users may not perceive that the system supports evacuation or response and this may cause maladaptive be haviors (i.e., not following guidelines). Yoo et al. [45] indicated that argument quality increases the decision support. As users need to put more effort into evaluating and scrutinizing the message, the argument quality of the message helps recipients solve the problem and cope adaptively through the central route. Thus, based on these arguments, we posit:

H1. Argument quality has a positive influence on coping adaptiveness.

## 5.2. Source credibility to coping adaptiveness

Previous ELM studies have used source credibility as one of the critical components of peripheral routes [23]. Source credibility generally refers to the extent to which message recipients perceive the expertise and competence of the disaster communications [25]. Persuasion attempted by credible communicators or authorities often triggers individuals’ behavioral changes by shifting attention and real locating cognitive abilities [46]. For example, Lord et al. [47] illustrated that messages from low credible sources often cause individuals to negatively respond when compared to messages from high credible sources. That is, persuasion can be effective when the message is communicated with high source credibility because recipients make less effortful decisions by trusting the argument, as they perceive it was verified by experts; they are less inclined to assess the actual quality or validity of the message by themselves [18].

In this study, source credibility is defined as how trustworthy, reli able, experienced, and professional the alert message senders are perceived by users. Alert messages can be sent from various organiza tions based on the type of disruptive event, or can be sent even without sender information. Other times, the identity of the sender can be recognized easily by users simply by looking at the message itself; however, its credibility can vary based on the subjective awareness of the message recipients. Therefore, when senders have a good reputation and public confidence, their messages can be perceived credible by re cipients. For example, an extremely polluted air warning can be sent out by city departments, a meteorological administration institution, or without any source. Since these sources may reflect different levels of competence on this issue, their results also can vary. Particularly, their guidelines can be accepted differently. In a similar vein, Umeh and Stanley [48] illustrated that communicator credibility has significant influence on adaptive coping reaction to HIV threat. We assume that the message with more source credibility causes more adaptive copings.

That is, instead of ignoring the message or just psychologically justifying recipients’ noncompliance, the message will cause actual behaviors. Based on these arguments, we postulate that when alert messages have well-established credibility known to the potential recipients, it is likely that recipients will show adaptive copings. Therefore, we posit:

H2. Source credibility has a positive influence on coping adaptiveness.

## 5.3. Perceived threat to coping adaptiveness

We suggest an additional two antecedents of coping adaptiveness, namely, perceived threat of emergency situation and perceived efficacy to manage emergency situation. Rogers [49] demonstrates coping in response to a threat. In addition, PMT identifies the perceived severity of threat, perceived probability of the occurrence and the perceived coping response efficacy as motivation factors to be secured from a disastrous event [7,50]; self-efficacy expectancy was added as an additional component of PMT [8]. In this research, we argue that PMT elements can be considered as elaboration likelihood factors that may affect individ ual coping responses, such as coping adaptiveness.

First of all, following Wang et al. [31], we conceptualized two ele ments, perceived probability severity of threat and perceived probability of event occurrence, as perceived threat of emergency situation. Witte and Allen [7] suggested that these two perceptions are additive when people think they are vulnerable to the attack. In the context of an emergency, perceived severity can be related to an individual’s perceived level of consequences as they relate to the emergency situa tion, and perceived susceptibility can be referred to as the perceived likelihood of the potential emergency situation happening. According to Liang and Xue [51], when people have increased perceived threat, this perception also increases people’s consideration about the level of dangerousness or harmfulness toward malicious information technology (IT). Boss et al. [9] demonstrated that perceived threat relates to individuals’ protection motivation to manage the situation. Moreover, Wang et al. [31] argued that perceived threat can increase task-focused coping and can decrease emotion-focused coping and avoidance coping in the situation of phishing detection. In the context of this research. to accept or reject the use of alert messages can be categorized as task-focused coping and avoidance coping. In other words, accepting alert messages is an adaptive coping behavior. We assume when the perceived threat of an emergency is high, people will show adaptive coping behaviors, and vice versa. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3. Perceived threat has a positive influence on coping adaptiveness.

## 5.4. From perceived efficacy to coping adaptiveness

As we mentioned from the previous section, we also consider perceived efficacy in relationship to emergency situations as an ante cedent of coping adaptiveness. According to prior studies, conflicting empirical findings exist. For instance, Yang et al. [52] argued that earthquake survivors with high self-efficacy tend to show task-focused coping behaviors. Benight et al. [53] indicated that self-efficacy is strongly linked to coping behaviors in the context of natural disaster. On the other hand, although it was not examined in the disaster context, other studies have demonstrated a different relationship, such as when an individual’s self-efficacy about security threats is high, they do not perform protective behaviors [54].

In this research, we argue that individuals who think they already know what to do during the emergency situation may not care to heed the message from SMS-based disaster alert systems because they might believe that they already grasp the detailed situation and best solution. Thus, there might be a low likelihood of such individuals directly accepting the messages and following their instruction. On the other hand, if individuals are not confident to handle the event, they will not disregard following the instructions of the alert messages. Wang et al.

[31] demonstrated that individuals may have a different ability to handle information security incidents, depending on their personal ef ficacy to detect security incidents. Again, following the findings about the relationship between perceived threat and coping adaptiveness based on Witte and Allen [7], we postulate that a person who has high perceived efficacy to manage an emergency situation may have a lower level of coping adaptiveness (i.e., accepting the alert messages) and high level of coping maladaptiveness (i.e., rejecting the alert messages or exhibiting emotional avoidance). Therefore, we propose the following:

H4. Perceived efficacy has a negative influence on coping adaptiveness.

## 5.5. Perceived threat as elaboration likelihood

In ELM, elaboration likelihood is a key driver for the recipient to provoke changes based on central routes or peripheral routes. PMT il lustrates that perceived threat arouses the fearful feeling of the attack and activates the defensive motivation for emotional adjustment [55]. It has also been argued that perceived severity can result in both mal adaptive coping (i.e., giving up or withdrawing) and adaptive coping (i. $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } _ { \cdot \boldsymbol { s } }$ using risk reduction methods), based on the contexts [56]. This study deals with the context that asks recipients to respond to possible threats promptly. In general, central routes require recipients to apply many cognitive endeavors and spend significant time to process the given messages. Therefore, we can assume that when the perceived threat is low, recipients might take the routes that can be done with more time and less emergence. On the other hand, we consider that when the perceived threat is high, recipients might search the routes that provide quick evidence to the following behaviors. Recipients with high perceived threat to the possible coming disruptive event will take the peripheral route and it will result in more adaptive copings, including compliance rather than disabling the alert or emotional-based coping. We, therefore, posit:

H5a. Perceived threat negatively moderates the relationship between argument quality and coping adaptiveness.

H5b. Perceived threat positively moderates the relationship between source credibility and coping adaptiveness.

## 5.6. Perceived efficacy as elaboration likelihood

Self-efficacy, as one of the key elaboration likelihood factors, refers to an individual’s perceived ability to obtain the expected results. In this study, perceived efficacy is defined as recipients’ perceived ability and coping skills with the possible coming disruptive event. This study uses perceived efficacy as the elaboration likelihood that may moderate the influence of central routes (i.e., argument quality) and peripheral routes $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ source credibility) on coping adaptiveness. As efficacy belief re flects level of confidence when individuals form motivation and behavior, and work with social environment, recipients who perceive high efficacy tend to have positive projections toward results [57]. In a similar vein, perceived efficacy might influence individuals’ information processing paths with alert messages, leading to different levels of coping adaptiveness or maladaptiveness. Perceived efficacy is formed based on knowledge, competency, and skillfulness in regard to coping with disruptive events. We argue that high perceived efficacy helps re cipients commit their effort to cognitive information processing. For instance, prior studies have illustrated that when individuals have more prior knowledge in the context of natural disaster, they are more willing to trust disaster-related information [16]. Knowledge helps recipients be undistracted and, therefore, leads them to better involvement and crit ical thinking [58]. And, as a source of engagement [59], competency makes individuals’ cognitive resources more available so that it can be utilized to assess the message [21]. However, if recipients are not well equipped with such knowledge, competency, and skillfulness about disruptive event copings, they would or could not exert their full cognitive effort into evaluating the quality of message in the case of disaster [21]. Based on the discussion above, we consider that perceived efficacy might have a positive impact on the relationship between argument quality and coping adaptiveness, and negatively moderates the relationship between source credibility and coping adaptiveness. Thus, we posit:

H6a. Perceived efficacy positively moderates the relationship between argument quality and coping adaptiveness.

H6b. Perceived efficacy negatively moderates the relationship be tween source credibility and coping adaptiveness.

## 6. Methods

## 6.1. Instrument development

Measurement instrument for focal variables was adapted from the existing literature on ELM, coping theory and PMT. Since we use empirical field data regarding specific disruptive events and the re cipient’s relevant prior experiences, we measured the informants recalled perception. Moreover, to minimize the recall bias, this paper carefully screened informants based on their memory, and also designed the instruments by assuming that data will be collected by respondents who have experience of the relevant event within two weeks. More detailed information of data collection is provided in the next section.

First, as ELM variables, four items for argument quality were adapted from Zhou [60]. In Zhou’s [60] paper, being relevant, sufficient, accu rate, and up-do-date of the argument were measured to determine the argument quality. This study applies the framework of completeness, accurateness, relevancy, and timeliness of the message to our emergency message context. Although the expression is not the same as that used by Zhou, we believe that these four dimensions embrace the construct with validity. For source credibility, Bhattacherjee and Sanford’s [23] work was consulted. In their study, being knowledgeable, trustworthy, cred ible, and having an expertise of the source was used to measure the construct. We believe that trustworthy and credible measure similar aspects. So instead of four, we used three items: the reliability, profes sionalism, and skill of the message sender.

For threat perception and perceived efficacy, four and three items were adapted, respectively, from Johnston et al. [61] and Johnston and Warkentin [62]. Items measure the degree to which informants perceived the severity of a disastrous event and their efficacy belief to handle the threats from it. In their studies, six items were used to measure severity, seriousness, significance risk, likelihood, and possi bility of consequences. Since some items are measuring similar aspects of the construct, following Wang et al.’s [31] approach, we measured the degree to which the consequences can be severe, significant, likely, and have high likelihood.

We have consulted Wang et al. [31] to develop items for the dependent variable, coping adaptiveness. However, because the context of the study is different, we approached the items differently. Above all, for task-focused coping, we concentrated on compliance with the mes sage, because unlike detecting phishing email, complying with public instructions received by the SMS system is more complex, since it relates directly to handling an emergency. And avoidance coping well reflects Wang et al.’s [31] paper with three items. For emotion-focused coping, we concentrated on respondents’ disabling the alert system, because it turns out that disabling the alert system is one of the most common behaviors that recipients show as an emotional maladaptive coping [63]. Coping adaptiveness was measured as a formative second-order construct. As a first-order construct, task-focused coping, emotion-focused coping, and avoidance coping were used. The items of emotion-focused coping and avoidance coping were reverse coded because they are for maladaptive copings. Lastly, we used age, gender, and job as demographic control variables. According to the standard instrument development processes (Straub 1989), survey items for this study were designed and developed. The first version of the survey items was consulted by four experts including two IS faculty and graduate students, and one staff member from the survey company to check the content validity of the instrument. Various issues, including wording of the items, item order, and the layout of the questionnaire were addressed based on the suggestions from the experts. After we revised the items, the questionnaire was translated into Korean for the actual data collection in South Korea. To minimize possible language-oriented issues, we deployed the backward translation tech nique with the support of three bilingual speakers. They checked the items to ensure there were no serious semantic discrepancies between English and Korean items. After the translation, the Korean version of the survey was pilot tested to check the clarity of Korean wording in the items, along with measurement validity and reliability. The pilot test was performed with the help of 31 graduate students in South Korea. Cronbach's alpha average variance extracted (AVE) and the crossloading calculations were done for the reliability and validity issues. ## 6.2. Data collection

For an empirical analysis, the data were collected in South Korea. Recently, South Korea has experienced various extreme climate events, including earthquakes [64] and toxic smog [65]. And as a result, to support citizens’ coping decision, various Korean government organi zations began actively deploying SMS alert systems. Since the systems only carry 64 characters at one time, with this limit organizations push their alerts in various formats. For example, some organizations prefer statistical information and others prefer evacuation guides. Since there are not standardized protocols in preparing the format of the SMS, end-users’ reactions also vary. In the season of Spring and Summer, because of specific climate conditions, toxic smog alerts are pushed quite frequently, as often as once a week. Considering the fact that SMS alerts are heterogeneous and end-users have various experiences with them, this environment provides a proper context for the field study.

We tested individuals’ coping responses based on their SMS alert systems experience. Therefore, informants of the survey should have had recent SMS alert systems’ experiences: any type of extreme weather events (e.g., toxic smog, earthquake, ozone alert, etc.). Therefore, we consider recall bias-related issues a possibility and paid close attention to it. To minimize the influence of possible recall bias, we collected data from the informants who recently experienced such events. For this research, we defined recent as two weeks. Therefore, we decided to collect data from the survey company which has a panel of informants that represent the characteristics of general individual groups for social research. We believe that using this method, we minimized the inci dence of recall bias in our data.

We chose OpenSurvey in South Korea as an agent that filters the informants and collects the data. As we discussed above, important data collection criteria were consulted with a representative of the firm. The company contacted 2000 individuals who reported recent SMS alert systems experience, and asked them a series of the following screening questions: (1) Have you received SMS alerts because of disastrous weather in two weeks? (2) Can you pick one alert message that you clearly recall the contents of the SMS or still have in your device? (3) Do you clearly remember the coping processes you went through and the emotions you felt during and after the processes? In total, 358 in formants answered “yes” to all screening questions. Those 358 in formants were reached again to reconfirm whether they met our data requirements. For this process, we asked them to briefly explain their experience on one of their most recent SMS alert events. After the collected responses from the second screening were reviewed, the questionnaire was sent by the OpenSurvey platform to the informants. We asked informants to answer all questions based on one of their most recent SMS experiences, as described previously. A total of 326 usable responses were collected and utilized for the analysis because there was no missing value. Table 2 provides details of the respondents’ de mographic properties.

Partial least squares (PLS) with the SmartPLS package [66] was deployed for the empirical analysis of our data. We used PLS for this study for the following two reasons. 1) Coping adaptiveness was measured as a formative construct including three sub-dimensions. It is well-known that the PLS method can effectively and efficiently process multidimensional constructs. 2) According to Ringle et al. [66], PLS is suited for the analysis of exploratory research models and the devel opment of new theory. In a similar vein, our research integrates the constructs of ELM and coping theory, which involve exploratory relationships.

## 7.2. Common method Bias assessment

Because we deployed dependent and independent variables coming from the same data source, we checked the possible presence of common method bias. We followed Malhotra et al.’s [68] approach, also known as partial correlation technique, by deploying a marker variable and comparing the influence of it on other core constructs. We added one unrelated question to the survey as a marker variable. This question was used: “I know well how 3D printers work.” Using the 5-point Likert scale (Totally Disagree … Totally Agree), we checked the partial correlation; first the correlation coefficients between the marker and then the other variables were calculated. The significance of the relationships among our core constructs, after partialing out the impact of the marker, was calculated again. Next, whether every relationship among the constructs still remained significant was checked. As a result, we found that the correlation coefficient between the marker and other constructs was not significant and the relationships among our core constructs, after par tialing out the impact of the marker, still remain significant. This test demonstrates that common method bias is not a serious concern in these data.

## 8. Discussion and conclusion

The purpose of this research was to investigate antecedents of coping adaptiveness in the context of SMS-based alert systems. We apply ELM as our main theoretical lens with the consideration of coping theory as well as PMT. We propose that potential antecedents of coping adaptiveness are alert messages’ argument quality and source credibility, along with individuals’ perceived threat and efficacy toward disruptive events. We performed a survey-based empirical analysis using the structural equa tion modeling and the results have yielded a rich set of insights on the role of PMT variables as elaboration likelihood in the context of coping adaptiveness.

Our analysis provides several key theoretical contributions to the literature. First, this study integrates three well-known theories, namely, ELM, PMT, and coping theory, to examine safety behaviors. ELM vari ables included argument quality of alert messages and source credibility of alert message senders. And as PMT variables, perceived threat and perceived efficacy were used. Coping adaptiveness were used as a dependent variable. To our best knowledge, although there are several studies that used PMT and ELM at the same time in the healthcare domain, study has not been done on how perception on disaster and IT driven interaction work together to produce coping behaviors. In this study, we illustrate these comprehensive relationships of three theo retical frames by testing the moderating relationships in this unique context along with coping adaptiveness. Although it is a different context, Wall and Warkentin [26] pointed out that persuasion is pro foundly related to threat and coping appraisals. The significant influence of argument quality and source credibility illustrates that when alert messages on disasters have clear reasoning and correct information, and come from well-known authorities, message recipients tend to conform with the given instructions instead of ignoring the messages. Although this result is intuitive, it reaffirms the importance of message argument quality and source credibility. As PMT variables, perceived threat and perceived efficacy were tested in the model. Perceived threat was hy pothesized to have a positive relationship with coping adaptiveness. On the other hand, perceived efficacy was hypothesized to have a negative relationship. As we discussed in the hypothesis development section, there have been conflicting results in the role of perceived efficacy [9]. Some studies followed the general approach $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ a positive role of perceived efficacy in coping appraisal [72];), while other studies found opposite results [73]. They explained that perceived efficacy can render people less likely to comply with the given rules because they think they know how to cope with the threats. Our finding demonstrates that in the context of SMS-based disaster alert systems, perceived efficacy nega tively influences coping adaptiveness.

Second, the continuum between adaptive and maladaptive behaviors was explored in the context of SMS-based disaster alert systems. Regardless of the importance of the topic, only a handful of IS studies have explored it. Based on the previous studies, we developed the following constructs: coping adaptiveness, with subdimensions of taskfocused coping, emotion-focused coping, and avoidance coping. Using this multidimensional variable, our model provides both positive and maladaptive sides of coping behaviors at the same time. For example, numerous PMT studies commonly focused on compliance by adopting a single dimensional factor. As a result, they only demonstrate how an tecedents of compliance increase or decrease compliance. However, as coping theory illustrates, adaptive coping and maladaptive coping work differently. Using coping adaptiveness, this study shows how the integrated model of elaboration likelihood and protection motivation explains adaptive coping and maladaptive coping at the same time. As the results show, strong perceived efficacy provokes recipients to mal adaptive copings. And information messages that are too long and full of details cause maladaptive coping, even when the threat level is high. Simple messages from well-known authorities also trigger maladaptive coping.

This study provides practical implications that can help to improve the citizen’s adaptation to the usage of SMS-based disaster alert systems. First, argument quality, source credibility, perceived threat, and perceived efficacy have direct and significant impacts on coping adap tiveness. The results imply that it is important to make messages accu rate, relevant, and up-to-date; maintaining a high level of credibility and implementing clear disaster-level indicators in the message is also essential. In addition, including announcements for people with high perceived efficacy can help the system be more successful. We found both argument quality and source credibility have positive influence on adaptive coping from message recipients. These argument quality and source credibility of the alert message can be improved if the message is prepared with a well-justified and compelling argument and when the message is prepared with accuracy, and completeness of the information regarding the events, respectively. Thus, we suggest service providers prepare well-justified, accurate, very detailed, and professional message in a timely manner to increase argument quality and source credibility of the messages. For example, the title of the message needs to be well justified and be capitalized (ALERT-WILDFIRE ALERT), message con tents include very detailed information (location, time, potential impact, and suggestion), the format and the tone of the message needs to be professional in a timely manner. Especially, in light of the current COVID-19 pandemic, as government agencies attempt to provide COVID-19-related information, such as location of recently confirmed cases, effective SMS-based alert systems can be very helpful to prevent further infections. Towards this end, our results can be applied as an effective and efficient strategy for utilizing SMS-based disaster alert systems in emergent situation control.

Second, the results imply that service providers need to adopt differentiated responses to increase adaptive coping. To provoke more adaptive copings from message recipients, the systems can be custom ized or categorized not only based on region, gender, age, and income levels, but also based on other recipient characteristics such as perceived efficacy. For instance, for those who live in regions where extreme events happen frequently, alert messages with source credibility can work better because those who live in the area may already have a strong perception of the threat itself. On the other hand, the alert mes sages that target recipients with relatively high perceived efficacy, message senders should focus on providing well-elaborated, quality messages to them because they tend to use their cognitive endeavors via the central route to handle the situation. Well-diversified and strategic targeting of different recipient groups can help users utilize adaptive coping.

We find that not only message features, such as argument quality and source credibility, but also individuals’ perceptions, such as threat and efficacy, need to be taken into account to improve the adaptation of emergency messages. This indicates that the adaptiveness of message alert systems in the context of disruptive events needs to be managed from multiple perspectives. In the case of content perspective, system developers need to focus on how to improve content quality and clearly present the sources of the message. In the case of individual’s percep tion, we suggest that agencies which are related to the disruptive event educate individuals to accept and use the alert system.

Although this study was designed and developed based on a valid theoretical background and was empirically examined with a reliable survey instrument, there are limitations that can be addressed for future research. First, our data depend on informants’ memory of the SMS alert event and their reactions. Therefore, recall bias can be a concern [74]. Previous studies also noticed the possible limitations in utilizing recall memory. For instance, relying on short-term memory may lead to memory bias, such as selectivity of recall. Relying on long-term memory may introduce biases such as potential differences between the re spondents’ recalled and actual experiences [75,76]. However, previous studies also acknowledged that data from recalling are still useful and insightful because the recall is “assumed to be representative of the underlying [memory] structure with respect to both content and orga nization” [77]. However, as a field study that reflects users’ real be haviors, we have paid special attention to this issue. For example, we particularly restricted our informants to those who had received disaster alert messages within two weeks, with multiple contacts through the professional survey organization. Although we believe that a recent two-week memory well reflects informants’ perceptions and behaviors with minimized bias, in the future research, if the time between in formants’ experiences and data collection is shorter, it could benefit and provide complementary findings.

Second, because our data were collected in South Korea, the results may reflect unique characteristics of the sample group. Even though we tried to diversify the demographic backgrounds using the professional survey organization, results could be influenced by other macro-level factors including nation-level wealth, culture, etc. That is, generaliz ability of the findings can be an issue. Therefore, when the results are applied to different countries, proper precaution should be taken. Future studies could collect data from diverse samples of users in different parts of the world to address this issue.

Third, we employed a survey questionnaire to measure the coping adaptiveness construct. We consider that the use of objective measure is preferable, if the data reflect the construct with validity. However, in this study, we had interest in examining coping responses embracing respondents’ psychological aspect, like avoidance coping. In that case, we believe that the survey method we deployed can provide valuable data. And Wang et al. [31], one of our core references, also used the survey method for their study. However, we consider that future studies can utilize objective data if they focus on specific aspects of coping re sponses which can be recorded in the database or archived.

Last, as we discussed in the hypothesis development section, this study tested the negative influence of perceived efficacy on coping adaptiveness. While two conflicting views, i.e., a positive relationship [29] and a negative relationship [54], are supported in the literature, this study provides valid evidence of the negative role of perceived ef ficacy in the context of SMS-based disaster alert systems. However, the results can differ in diverse situations, so future studies need to be done in different contexts.

Notwithstanding the above limitations, this research makes two significant contributions. First, this study illustrates how two wellknown theories, ELM and PLM, can be integrated into the comprehen sive model to examine safety coping behaviors. With constructs from the elaboration likelihood as well as PLM literature, this study provides empirical evidence of their significant, positive role in enhancing adaptive coping behaviors. Second, the contextual details on adaptive and maladaptive behaviors in the context of SMS alert events were explored. These insights not only help researchers better understand how the message characteristics and user propensities have become an important part of SMS-based disaster alert systems effectiveness, but also provide guidelines for SMS alert management to better adjust their responses for more adaptive results. With these significant additions to the body of knowledge in this important area, it is hoped that future research will further investigate the attributes and impacts of SMS-based disaster alert systems.
