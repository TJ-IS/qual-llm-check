# Personalizing 3D virtual fashion stores: Exploring modularity with a typology of atmospherics based on user input

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Atmospherics Personalization 3D virtual stores

## A B S T R A C T

This research develops a typology of atmospherics that contains user-identified modules and modular options for personalizing 3D virtual fashion stores. A content analysis of 46 focus group discussions (n = 170) was conducted to understand the user’s perspective for personalizing 3D virtual fashion store atmospherics. Based on three atmospheric categories (pathfinding assistant, environment, and the manner of product presentation), 17 modules and 207 modular options were identified for personalizing 3D virtual stores. This research pioneers the development of an atmospherics typology for personalizing 3D virtual shopping environments as a persuasive selling tool in the emerging field of 3D virtual reality (VR) retailing.

## 1. Introduction

The advent of 3D virtual worlds has blurred the line between con ventional brick-and-mortar stores and online stores [1]. A variety of studies of 3D virtual spaces have focused on virtual worlds or games such as Second Life (SL) or The Sims [1–3]. However, these do not pertain to the atmospherics of 3D virtual stores in the context of e-commerce per se and thus their implications for virtual reality (VR) retailing are limited. Some studies have focused on comparing consumer responses between virtual and physical store environments [4–7]. This line of research provided empirical support for the development of VR retailing but offered no comprehensive guidelines as to setting up a co-design system to personalize VR stores. As 3D virtual retailing is still in an experimental stage and commercial offerings in shoppable 3D virtual stores is yet to come, exploratory work is critical to help to conceptualize what a realistic 3D virtual store might look like from a user’s perspective.

In this era of stiff competition, retailing executives must identify new sources of competitive advantage to serve increasingly web-savvy shoppers in a very crowded market [8]. Personalization could provide differentiation by engaging users in creative ways to customize their online shopping experience. Such a store can be tailored to the users shopping habits in navigation, product selection, and visual preferences and thus truly provide a unique and immersive shopping experience for the user. Shankar [9] considered personalization an important direction in how artificial intelligence (AI) can benefit retailing.

But along with its wide array of benefits, personalization brings its own challenges. For example, users may have a limited ability to discern and articulate their preferences and they can also easily feel over whelmed by a great number of design options [10,11]. Modularity re duces complexity while allowing flexible alignment with user preferences and agile responses to changing trends [12]. Modularity is the degree to which a product, process, system, or environment can be divided into smaller components that “can be designed independently yet function together as a whole” [13]. McCutcheon et al. [14] argued that modular product design provides the best balance between variety and speed. The right configuration of modularity is an economical and sensible way to organize the selling environment to provide flexibility, variety, structure, and clarity for personalizing the selling environment. In this study, we defined “module” as a category of personalizable 3D virtual store features (e.g., navigation, store layout, lighting, music, product assortment), for which parallel options can be provided for personalizing the store. “Modular options” are parallel choices provided to personalize a module, which can also serve as sub-modules if further choices are provided.

We examined the nature and type of modularity that is optimal for personalizing atmospherics in 3D virtual stores as a part of a multi-year project investigating personalization of VR stores. Atmospherics refers to the design of store environments that evokes shoppers’ emotional and behavioral responses, which includes nonvisual ambience, visual design, and social factors [15,16]. When applied to store atmospherics in 3D VR stores, there is scant research on user needs and preferences. The aim of this research is thus to develop a typology of atmospherics that contains user-generated modules and modular options for personalizing 3D vir tual stores.

This research brings two seemingly unrelated fields together that fill in a gap in the study of 3D virtual store atmospherics: modular co-design and 3D virtual stores. Bridging disciplines to apply personalization knowledge innovatively to logically connect with new fields is a research direction recommended by Hankammer et al. [17] after a computational review of over a thousand scholarly articles on custom ization and personalization. Our research contributes to the develop ment of a niche e-retailing virtual market and also seeks to inspire new creations that combine the benefits of online retailing, brick-and-mortar stores, and 3D virtual environments.

Our article first reviews literature on 3D virtual stores and person alization, modularity in mass customization (MC) research, and the evolving developments of atmospheric typologies for physical, online, and VR retail environments. We then discuss the content analysis method used in the post-design focus group reports, followed by a results section that reports the ideas that users generated for modules and modular options, including a suggested prioritization of atmospheric features for personalization. Lastly, our article discusses the results, limitations, and future research directions, and summarizes our original contributions to theory building and retail practice.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Three-dimensional virtual stores and personalization

Virtual environments replicate real life in a three-dimensional (3D) digital space [18]. Compared to two-dimensional (2D) online platforms, the 3D virtual space provides a more immersive and interactive expe rience through a high degree of media richness and visual vividness that allows it to transmit a great amount of information and thus enhances engagement and enjoyment [19–21]. Holbrook ([22], 59) argued that the realistic rendering of the true-to-life spatial complexity of 3D visual presentations can provide “enhanced vividness, clarity, realism, and depth,” which can boost persuasiveness. Compared to 2D platforms, previous studies found that 3D virtual spaces more positively influenced the user’s perceptions of telepresence [2,23] and flow [24], which resulted in more time spent in the store and higher satisfaction and purchase intention. Klein’s [25] study also indicated that virtual product experiences enhance customers’ beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors toward the product. Though negative effects of 3D environments were also oc casionally reported [23,78], 3D virtual stores are generally believed to offer premium value to their users.

Personalization may further enhance users’ 3D virtual shopping ex periences by providing customized information to users based on their individual characteristics or preferences [26]. For example, personal ized product recommendations based on users’ previous browsing or purchasing history can best suit their interests and thus offer more efficient search options [27]. Increased personalization can also enhance brand attitude [28] and satisfaction and brand loyalty [29].

Füller et al. [30] asserted that the level of the consumer’s empow erment in the co-creation process highly depends on the design of the visual interaction tool. In line with this, the success of co-designing store atmospherics should depend heavily on whether these modular options are adequate and appropriate for stimulating the consumer’s creative articulation to create engaging store atmospherics. Extant research on personalization in virtual shopping environments has mainly focused on its convenience [31], personalized or “spokes-avatar” shopping assis tance [32,33], personalized product recommendations [34,35], virtual try-on [79], website design [36], or internet shopping agents that can search for the lowest price [37]. A typology of atmospherics of 3D vir tual stores can thus provide a holistic rubric for conceptualizing modularity in such retail environments to offer personalizable shopping experiences.

## 2.2. Modularity in mass customization (MC) research

To function as a profitable and sustainable business strategy, it is imperative to apply modularity in the co-design process. Pine [38] states that “the best method for achieving MC—minimizing costs while maximizing individual customization—is by creating modular compo nents that can be configured into a wide variety of end products and services.” Modularity has been extensively studied in MC research across a variety of disciplines such as product design and development, oper ations management, marketing, strategy and general management, and innovation and technology management [17]. Duray et al. [39] stressed that the essence of MC lies in two dimensions: 1) a means to include each customer’s specifications in the product design; 2) modular design to achieve manufacturing efficiency. Modularity provides a means for the repetitive production of components, thus differentiates MC from traditional tailoring, which is the critical aspect in achieving scale or “mass” in MC [38,39]. However, a clear distinction between variety and customization is stressed by Duray et al. [39] and Pine [38] as MC aligns with reasonable instead of infinite variety. Too much variety in the options may only increase the time involved in selecting the right ones and cause confusion in consumers’ decision-making process [40–42]. On the other hand, too few options could hinder consumers’ creativity and ability to achieve their co-design goals. Successful MC strategy relies on a proper balance between the potential of customers’ acceptance of both the customized products or services and their involvement in the co-design process (i.e., the money, time, and effort they are willing to invest).

Researchers have identified different dimensions of MC in product development, which could serve as a reference point for modularity development in personalizable 3D VR stores (see Table 1). MC can occur at various points along the value chain, ranging from “adaptation” to “total customization” [43]. As Da Silveira et al. [43] remarked, a careful determination of the range in which a product or service can be customized, and how individuals make options upon this range is considered a key to defining the MC dimensions [44].

Over the last three decades MC research has evolved into a multi disciplinary field providing key strategies for efficient, cost-effective, and differentiated offerings of products and services in both B2B and B2C markets [17]. Powered by information technology and AI, design platforms, configurator systems, agile service delivery, and smart supply chains have been upgraded to enable various customization processes [48,49].

## 2.2.1. Modularity in a physical retail environment

Previous studies on MC dimensions and definitions provide us with important frameworks when borrowing the idea of MC to apply to VR shopping environments. Though aspects of a physical store vis-a-vis \` a 3D VR store are a lot less personalizable, shoppers can acquire personalized shopping experiences through navigating the store via different routes and thus are exposed to different products, social interactions, and store environments. In that sense the layout of a physical store features a modular structure for personalization (See Fig. 1).

## 2.2.2. Modularity in an online retail environment

Similarly, but with a much-enhanced ability for personalization, online retailers use modules for organization. Take Amazon.com as an example: its level-one modules are located both horizontally on the menu bar and vertically on the left side of the website (see also [50]). A search for women’s wear results in a list of modules: Amazon prime, prime wardrobe, department, average customer review, etc. (see Fig. 2). Compared to the more linear, modular structure of physical stores, online modules are often cross referenced and multilayered. However, as websites are two-dimensional, their space orientation, navigation, and display philosophy and methods are thus entirely different from a 3D VR environment.

Exploring and experimenting with modularity for personalizable 3D VR stores expands the realm of MC to the field of VR retailing to provide unique and differentiated value to customers. However, to maximize the unique values of personalization in 3D virtual stores, it is critical to have a well-rounded understanding of the atmospherics of 3D virtual stores.

## 2.3. 3D virtual store atmospherics typologies

Various store atmospherics typologies were suggested following the evolution of store formats. Nearly half a century ago, Kotler [15] started to use the term “atmospherics” to refer to a conscious design of buying environments that evoke buyers’ positive emotional and behavioral responses. An atmospheric variable is “any component with an in dividual’s perceptual field which stimulates one’s sense and thus affects the total experiences of being in a given place at a given time” ([51], 68). Previous literature showed that a variety of store atmospheric variables, such as layout, displays, colors, lighting, fixtures, textures, scents, and sounds, can influence customers’ perceptions, attitudes, and behavioral intentions in both physical and online stores [52]. A similar mechanism exists regarding how atmospherics of 3D VR stores influence customers cognition, emotion, and behavior. For instance, Liu and Uang [53] re ported that both the control mode and layout design of virtual stores influenced older users’ emotional and behavioral responses, i.e., plea sure, arousal, desire to purchase, and desire to stay in the store. Simi larly, Krasonikolakis et al. [54] found store layout of 3D online environments influenced shopping enjoyment, entertainment, and ease of navigation. Krasonikolakis et al. [55] further asserted that “core store features,” such as product price and store atmosphere, and “security and privacy” constitute the most important store selection criteria in visual worlds.

## 2.3.1. Atmospherics typologies for physical stores

Kotler [15] described the visual, aural, olfactory, and tactile dimensions of an atmospherics typology. Baker [16] then suggested a general typology of atmospherics for physical stores featuring ambient (e.g., nonvisual cues such as smell and sound), design (e.g., visual cues such as layout, color, and cleanliness), and social factors (e.g., con sumers and employees in the store). Following Baker’s typology, Turley and Milliman [56] proposed five general internal variables (e.g., scent, music, and lighting), layout and design (e.g., fixtures and allocation of floor space), point-of-purchase and decoration (e.g., product display and posters), human (e.g., presence in the store of both consumers and ser vice personnel and their interactions), and external variables (e.g., store-front, surrounding area, and building architecture). However, with the advent of the online store, a new taxonomy is needed to capture the dynamics found in online stores.

## 2.3.2. Atmospherics typologies for online stores

Eroglu et al. [57,58] suggested an alternative taxonomy for online stores highlighting a task-relevance component in the typology. They categorized atmospherics into high-task relevant environmental cues, which are directly related to shopping tasks (e.g., descriptions of merchandise, price, terms of sales and return policies), and low-task relevant environmental cues, which are not directly related to shop ping tasks (e.g., colors, background patterns, and fonts). Placing a single focus on task relevance and the dichotomy between high and low-task relevance in this typology might not fit the context of 3D VR retailing as many VR features are both utilitarian and hedonic, such as animated product presentations. Later, after an investigation of the analogy of atmospherics between offline stores and online stores, Ha et al. [50] developed a typology for online apparel websites, which consisted of three categories: pathfinding features (e.g., visualized services to help users locate a product), environment (e.g., atmospheric/environmental and sensory features), and the manner of product presentation (e.g., product display and detailed presentation). Manganari et al. [59] sug gested an updated typology by adding a social presence construct, echoing Baker’s Baker [16] social factor and Turley and Milliman’s [56] human variable. This typology of online retailing thus includes layout and design (e.g., navigation options to a desired section and view pages), atmospherics (e.g., background colors, color scheme, music, fonts, and scent), theatrics (e.g., animation technique, image vividness, and interactivity), and social presence (e.g., virtual communities, avatars, recommendations, and communications). However, this conceptualiza tion of online retail atmosphere treats “atmospherics” as a parallel variable to “layout and design,” “theatrics,” and “social presence,” while we treat “atmospherics” as an overarching concept that encompasses sensory stimuli including layout and design, and theatrics, etc. However, there are similarities between Ha et al.’s [50] and Manganari et al.’s [59] typologies: “pathfinding features” correspond with “layout and design,” “environment” corresponds with “atmospherics,” and product presentation somewhat overlaps with “theatrics,” while “social pres ence” in the later typology can be infused into the various pathfinding, environmental, and product presentation features.

## 2.3.3. Atmospherics typologies for virtual reality stores

More recently, Hassouneh and Brengman [3] developed a typology of virtual store atmospherics through a content analysis of virtual stores in SL. They identified nine categories of atmospheric variables, including “the macro environment,” “the store exterior,” “interior functional design factors and store layout,” “interior aesthetic design factors,” “store ambient factors,” “point-of-purchase factors,” “customer services,” “store social factors,” and “entertainment factors.” Though these categories could inspire new ideas in conceptualizing virtual store atmospherics, some of them are specific to the context of SL or social virtual worlds. “Entertainment factors,” e.g., refer to games, free drinks, and other types of social entertainment that exist in SL. Also, this ty pology is not ideally parsimonious since aesthetic and functionality are two integral parts of a design.

The aforementioned store atmospherics typologies were developed according to various physical, online, or virtual retailing channels. Roggeveen et al. [60]’s design-ambient-social-trialability (DAST) framework for retail atmospherics claims to be applicable to diverse inand out-of-store customer experiences. This typology is largely based on Baker’s [16] ambient-design-social trio but added a dimension of trial ability that emphasizes an intentional configuration of interactivity between the retail offering and the customer. However, VR is tucked under the dimension of trialability in this typology.

Though previous studies are useful for understanding the typologies of atmospherics of various retail environments, a specific typology of 3D virtual store atmospherics that is suited for personalization from a user’s perspective does not exist, the development of which is the aim of this study. Our study adopts Ha et al.’s [50] typology as a theoretical framework for data analysis mainly because: 1) Ha et al.’s typology was developed based on empirical analyses of apparel websites and thus is more relevant to our study compared to Hassouneh and Brengman’s [3] typology that is based on a specific social virtual world. 2) Ha et al.’s typology takes into account the analogous features between online and offline stores, which better suits a 3D VR context as it reflects both online and brick-and-mortar store characteristics.

## 3. Method

Our study aims to develop a typology of atmospherics that contains user-generated modules and modular options for personalizing a 3D virtual store. Thus, we involved users to discuss the appropriate type and number of modules and modular options to personalize a 3D virtual store after users individually personalized a virtual store using Mockshop, a 3D virtual retailing software package. Semi-structured focus group dis cussions were content analyzed by two independent researchers.

For a new and exploratory topic, such as modularity for co-designing a 3D virtual store, the spontaneity, candor, in-depth engagement, and group dynamics associated with the social setting of the focus group can stimulate thinking and simultaneously weed out unrealistic ideas as the group process is instrumental in assisting participants to explore and clarify their points of view [61,62]. Thus, in this sense, the method is more ecologically valid than data collected from individual participants [63]. The social setting of a focus group is particularly suited to provide collective power to less articulate participants [61]. Though risks of participant censoring and conforming in focus group studies exist [62], they are less of a concern in our study due to the nature of our design issue at hand, as compared to the sensitive nature of personal or social issues. Our semi-structured setting provides participants the full op portunity to interact with each other while the instructor and TA serve to facilitate instead of direct the discussions, which is a common approach in social science research with the aim of understanding participants perspectives, views, and ways of reasoning [61].

## 3.1. Participants and procedure

A total of 170 undergraduate students from several visual merchandising classes at a midwestern American university randomly formed 46 focus groups with 3 or 4 students in each group over a twoyear time period from 2015 to 2017. The college student sample is appropriate in this research because this group is likely to adopt new technology [64]. A survey of over 300 Gen-Zers (born between 1995 and 2010) revealed that 41 % had tried VR and 12 % had used VR on a daily or weekly basis [65], suggesting that this “digital native” generation is poised to further embrace VR in retail settings [66]. Also, the innovative aspect of the VR setting fits the profile of college students as innovators [54]. Though this sample is generally familiar with VR technology, prior to their participation in this study participants had no experience with Mockshop, a VR software package used in the fashion industry. The relatively homogenous sample offered some level of control on age, gender, education, and computer familiarity [67].

The researchers designed a consistent procedure for participants to follow to maintain a controlled lab environment to ensure internal validity [68]. Participants were given the same instructions and the same amount of time and consultation opportunities from the instructor. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that they have similar knowledge and competency to design 3D virtual stores though individual differences in visual literacy and technological fluency always exist.

The participants first individually personalized a 3D store during a six-week time period. Then, the participants shared their individually personalized 3D virtual stores within their focus groups using lab com puters. The instructor explained the idea of modularity in personalizing the 3D virtual store. The participants were then asked to take an equal share to discuss in focus groups the two questions listed below from a consumer’s perspective and to submit written group answers. We left it to individual focus groups to work out any conflicts with the intention of fostering a natural and comfortable setting for the participants to freely contribute their ideas [69]. All focus groups were asked to write down their discussions of these questions in a conversational format (i.e., to keep the context of their discussions).

(1) What aspects/features (modules) of the virtual store are most appropriate for personalization and co-designing (to create a personalized 3D virtual store)?

(2) What (modular) options for each of the aforementioned aspects/ features (modules) should be provided for a modularized codesign process (to create a personalized 3D virtual store)?

Three stages of coding were used to content analyze the focus group written reports: developing the coding structure, analyzing written text, and interpretation [70]. In the initial stage, a coding scheme was defined adopting Ha et al.’s [50] typology (See Table 2). Two researchers independently coded the written data and achieved an inter-rater reli ability of 87.9 % at the first stage. Then, all disagreements were dis cussed until 100 % agreement was reached for the main categories following the guidelines suggested by Macias and Lewis [71]. As the purpose of this study is to identify what store features consumers deem appropriate for modularizing, all of the store features mentioned in the focus group discussions are included in our report. Disagreements mainly related to classifying the store features as level one or level two modules or as modular options.

## 4. Results

Based on the three atmospheric categories (pathfinding assistance, environment, and the manner of product presentation), 17 modules and 207 modular options were identified as appropriate for personalizing 3D virtual stores. As a “module” is a category of personalizable 3D virtual store features, level-one modules represent large categories, such as navigation, layout, overall theme, lighting, in-store ads, and music, etc. Level-two modules represent smaller sub-categories of personalizable 3D virtual store features, which are usually modular options of level-one modules and can in themselves contain various options. For example, an “overall theme” as a level-one module may include thematic options based on mood, location, size, or personal aesthetic, which are treated as level-two modules. Then, further options for the “mood” sub-module may include “feminine, edgy, modern, classic, traditional, contempo rary, homey, urban, European, and relaxed.”

In the “virtual pathfinding assistance” category (i.e., visual services provided to help users locate a product or move about), four features classified as level-one modules were identified as suited for modulari zation: navigation, layout, product organization, and clerk assistance (see Table 3). Product organization (87 %) is the most frequently mentioned feature, followed by layout (60.9 %), navigation (23.9 %), and clerk assistance (8.7 %).

In the “environment” category (i.e., atmosphere, environmental, and sensory features), ten main features classified as level-one modules were identified as suited for modularization, listed as follows from the most to the least frequently mentioned (see Table 4): color (71.7 %), use of fixtures (65.2 %), lighting (56.5 %), interior material (54.3 %), in-store ad (54.3 %), music (47.8 %), product assortment (39. %1), overall theme (37 %), task areas (19.6 %), and scent (8.7 %). Focus Group-04 argued that “the overall feel of the store was the most important part to personalize. Stores and brands tell stories. If there is no clear vision for the store, the customer will not feel a connection to the store.”

In the “manner of product presentation” category (i.e., visual merchandising elements that are related to methods of showing indi vidual products and product information), three features, classified as level-one modules, were identified as suited for modularization that are listed as follows from the most to the least frequently mentioned (see Table 5): Ways of displaying (52.2 %), changing views (17.4 %), and virtual try-on (6.5 %).

Next, a variety of ideas regarding a second level of modularity (classified as module level 2) as well as modular options were suggested by the participants. For example, for the “product organization” module, participants suggested that products could be sorted, grouped, or orga nized based on color, outfitting, popularity, size, price, style, mood, trend, lifestyle, target market, designer, material, country of origin, etc. (for more detailed information, see Table 3). More specifically, for instance, regarding the level-two module “style,” options to sort or group styles of products could be provided as follows: casual vs. formal, basic vs. trend, athletic vs. work apparel.

## 5. Discussions and conclusions

Our study uniquely contributes to the 3D virtual retailing literature by pioneering a typology of atmospherics that contains modularity developed based on what consumers deem appropriate and need for personalizing a 3D VR store. These user-generated modules and modular options also contribute original design ideas that can be readily used by 3D VR retailers. In the main, our original contribution can be summa rized as: 1) Our study extended Ha et al.’s [50] typology of retail at mospherics from a 2D online retail environment to a 3D VR one and identified additional atmospheric variables, such as layout, fixturing, lighting, interiors, etc. We further indicated a priority order of atmo spheric features as well as an appropriate level of modularity for personalization. 2) We also crossed disciplinary boundaries and applied MC research to the design of VR store environments. Original user-generated ideas provide a new vision for VR retail space configu ration that no longer simply replicates a physical store and can also inspire e-tailers to elicit information on user characteristics and prefer ences to enhance personalization.

## 5.1. Comparing with Ha et al.’s [50] typology of retail atmospherics

To fulfill our goal of developing a useful typology of atmospherics characterized by a user-generated modular structure for personalizing 3D VR stores, our findings reveal that in all three main categories (pathfinding assistance, environment, and manner of product presen tation, see [50]), the most frequently mentioned feature appropriate fo modularization is product organization, i.e., the sorting, grouping, or recommendation of products, followed by color and use of fixtures. Group-30 states: “feature that could easily be personalized would be sorting of the products based on personal preferences. The shopper could choose how they want to shop the merchandise when they first arrive at the website.”

As in brick-and-mortar stores, product grouping and organization in virtual stores is a critical function of visual merchandising and plays an important role in enticing and assisting shoppers. Ha et al. [50] reported that fashion websites organized merchandise in a two-step manner, which have a similar structure to the two-tiered modules identified in our study. But in our study, sub-modules include a much fuller list of ideas. Additionally, we provided third-level modular options. Also, unique features of 3D VR spaces, such as layout, use of fixtures, lighting, interiors, models, task area, virtual try-on, etc. (see Tables 3–5) were identified in our study that do not exist in the 2D online retail envi ronment that Ha et al.’s [50] study is focused on.

## 5.2. A new vision for configuring 3D VR retail space for personalization

Another important contribution of our study is to have enriched MC research and branched out to a new area of personalizing 3D VR retail environments. We unleashed our participants’ creativity to envision a VR shopping environment that is no longer physically confined and restricted but one that is instead enhanced by surreal, imaginative shopping experiences. Even the location of a virtual store can be played with imaginatively as described by Focus Group-35: “Location can vary greatly in virtual stores, they can either be placed in a realistic setting or a fantasy setting. The options are endless which makes it such an appealing platform for creativity.” This finding that location can be personalized enhances our current understanding of location and macro environment as merely a static atmospheric feature of 3D VR stores as classified by Hassouneh and Brengman [3].

Most previous studies of 3D virtual stores were oriented toward simulating the real physical world and stressed the usefulness of 3D visual technologies only in the sense that they could make 3D VR en vironments realistic and closer to the real world and real experiences [54,72,73,80]. Previous studies envisioned and visualized layout op tions in 3D virtual stores as avant-garde, warehouse, pragmatic, boutique, or department [54] and freeform, grid, and racetrack [73] – all resembling layouts in physical store environments. But the virtual environment offers a great advantage in innovative methods of way finding and layout as it can easily bypass the laws of the physical world, such as gravity, speed, and human anatomic capacity (see [74]). The placement of a product should no longer be restricted by its weight or confined by the reachable height of shelves. Also, in addition to being able to personalize how products are organized or displayed, users can easily switch back and forth between the various ways of displaying and organizing. For example, products organized based on their casual or formal style could be switched to be based on their country of origin with a quick click. Thus, users do not need to choose and stick with one method of wayfinding or displaying; they can freely change where and how products are displayed. Focus Group-04 envisioned interesting navigation and layout options with some only possible in virtual envi ronments, such as: “walking around store instead of hovering – realistic perspective. If there was a feature that allowed customers to ‘walk through the store they might see the virtual store as more of a traditional shopping experience and might be more prone to spend more; virtual tour of store—could possibly include store’s history, or the background on the production process for certain products when a customer clicks on a product (like the Burberry flagship store); include a feature like Google Earth—where you can see the exterior of the virtual store and travel within the store; click on item to enlarge (zoom in/out)—an easier function to see store in greater detail.”

## 5.3. Eliciting information on user characteristics and preferences to enhance personalization

More advanced user-centered information design can enhance personalization, such as providing personalized product recommenda tions and layout based on user characteristics as suggested by our par ticipants, which is in accordance with Gai and Klesse [75]’s study on user- vs. item-based framing to increase recommendation click-through rates. Currently, data mining and recommendation algorithms have enabled online retailers to provide personalized promotion and product recommendations for users mostly based on browsing, search, and purchase histories [75,76]. Our study suggests the possibility of using short preference quizzes to provide exactly what and how consumers want personalized. For example, Focus Group-38 suggested: “if cus tomers took a quiz before entering their personalized virtual store, they could choose casual vs. formal, expensive vs. affordable, color prefer ences, size preferences, or any other specific items they want to see. For example, if customers wanted casualwear, they could shop a selection of basic t-shirts, cardigans, sweaters, and jeans. During this quiz, customers could also choose the kind of layout they want, such as racetrack, grid, or freeform. With this, whatever merchandise they chose would appear in their preferred layout.”

## 5.4. An appropriate level of modularity

As Füller et al. [30] pointed out, to promote consumers’ active participation in the co-design process, it is critical to give consumers a proper amount of modular options to fulfill their needs and preferences, to provide confidence and enjoyment, and also to avoid information overload. Our results shed light on the number of modular options deemed appropriate by the participants. For example, in the “path finding assistance” category, four major modules were suggested, including navigation, layout, product organization, and clerk assistance. Typically, the participants recommended three to nine options for any specific modules depending on the complexity of the modules. The pathfinding assistant feature is related to shopping tasks [57] such as helping users find products using a proper product grouping technique or via a recommendation system based on the preferences of consumers. In virtual worlds, though users are not confined by having to physically walk on an engineered pathway, they could still easily get lost amidst a vast variety of products. Thus, logical, sensible, and easily defined pathfinding assistance features are still crucial for an engaging and enjoyable experience in a virtual store.

Our findings indicate that the category of “environment” contains the greatest number of suggestions of the ten large modules, including overall theme, lighting, use of fixtures, interior material, color, product assortment, scent, in-store ads, music, and task area. Color is the second most frequently mentioned feature for its appropriateness for modula rization. As pointed out by Group-30: a “feature that could be easily personalized in the virtual store is the color scheme. The virtual store designer could choose a color scheme based on the mood they are trying to portray, or the customer could have the option of choosing a color scheme upon entering the website.” Before anything else, “people buy color! Color is what sells” ([77], 144). Nearly any colors for the walls, ceiling, flooring, carpet, fixtures, signage, and lighting can be made into modular options. Participants also suggested that choices for colors could be expanded to allow users themselves to bring in colors they found online elsewhere, which is distinctively different from what is currently available on websites as shown in Ha et al.’s [50] study or in Second Life as shown in Hassouneh and Brengman’s [3] study.

Although providing an appropriate number of modules and modular options are the key to developing an effective personalization system, there is no study that empirically holistically defines these key measures. A comprehensive typology of atmospherics developed based on user input is useful for any e-tailers who are moving toward offering personalized 3D virtual shopping experiences.

## 5.5. Limitations and direction for future research

Additional insights can be obtained by interviewing field experts or consumers who have used 3D virtual stores. Future studies could also conduct lab experiments to test out the effects of these identified modules and modular options on users. Our participants could be influenced by their knowledge and perceptions of virtual stores because they are students in visual merchandising courses, which might limit the gener alizability of this study to the average consumer. Similarly, their un derstanding of modularity might also be influenced by their performance in personalizing the 3D VR stores. However, our partici pants’ visual merchandising knowledge also enables them to act in roles both of the visual merchandiser and the consumer. Our study should inspire future research of 3D virtual retailing to take an entirely new direction that is not a simulation of existing physical stores. The idea of space and even what constitutes a shopping environment could all change. In the future, more radical innovation might replace what we conceive of as a 3D virtual store today. For example, apparel products could be shown on potential users in various 3D VR environments that simulate their usage contexts when virtually interacting with familiar avatars of friends or colleagues. Image search might also be enabled in 3D VR environments to enhance shoppers’ abilities to identify ideal looks and mix and match. All of these ideas can be empirically tested in future studies.
