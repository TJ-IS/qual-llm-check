## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Knowledge sharing Communication visibility theory Work motivation theory Message transparency Network translucence Social media Work efficiency

## A B S T R A C T

While employees gradually use social media for work purposes, it remains unclear how such a use influences their knowledge sharing and work efficiency. Thus, this study explores how work motivations and communi cation visibility afforded by social media affect employees’ knowledge sharing and work efficiency. We find that message transparency of communication visibility enhances knowledge sharing and work efficiency but weakens the impact of reputation on knowledge sharing, whereas network translucence of communication visibility in creases work efficiency and strengthens the influence of social networking on knowledge sharing. Our findings add to the literature and provide guidance on how to use social media for work.

## 1. Introduction

In today’s increasingly digitized workplaces, employees often take advantage of social media’s rich functionalities, such as affording real time messaging and file transmission, providing/receiving peer feed back (e.g., commenting and liking), and enabling video meetings, to communicate and collaborate with co-workers [1]. Research shows that 82 % of employees believe that social media is conducive to colleague relationships, 69 % uses social media to expand work networks, and 60 % agree that it facilitates decision-making processes [1]. Hence, orga nizations (especially those knowledge-intensive organizations) encourage their employees to communicate work-related issues on so cial media, including enterprise social media [2–4] and popular social media (e.g., Facebook and WeChat Groups) [1,5].

Given the touted benefits of social media, one fundamental question arises as to how employees’ use of social media affects their work effi ciency. Past literature has reported mixed findings regarding the rela tionship between social media use and work efficiency [6]. Some studies have identified a positive effect in the way that social media use supports information sharing, work coordination, socialization, and project management [3,7,8]. In contrast, emerging studies and business reports uncover a negative effect of social media use, such as leading to dis tractions at work and degraded task execution [9,10]. Meanwhile, other studies have argued that it is a dynamically complicated procedure to use social media within the workplace [11,12]. For example, Chen and Wei [13] found that social media use has an inverted U-shaped rela tionship with employees’ perception of information and social overload, which would impact their capability in task execution [14]. Those inconsistent findings could be due to the fact that previous studies (1) treat social media use as a unitary construct rather than a multi-dimensional one [6,9], (2) do not explicate the impacts of different social media features [9,12], and (3) do not consider the dif ferential impacts of social media use together with employees’ motiva tions [4,12]. Thus, there is a lack of research in conceptualizing the impacts of the multi-dimensions of social media use and examining their interactions with employees’ motivations on work efficiency.

The work motivation literature provides insights into how the desire for social rewards drives employees to share knowledge within organi zations [15,16], thereby improving work efficiency [17]. Hermans and Hermans-Jansen (p.28) [18] argued that “motivation can be conceived as a duality” where individuals’ desire oscillates between self-improvement and relationships with others. Similarly. this study substantiated the dual motives as reputation and social networking, respectively, whose importance in the workplace has been noted in substantial research [9,16,19]. Reputation refers to the expectation of improving personal image and status in one’s profession among co-workers [20]. Social networking is the desire to maintain and broaden social relationships in the professional life [21]. Although extant research has offered useful insights into work motivations, little research has explored how employees’ work motivations interact with social media use (particularly communication visibility) to affect knowledge sharing and work efficiency [6]. This is important as social media might increase or decrease the importance of specific motivations in determining work efficiency [6,10,22]. Thus, there is a need for a nuanced understanding of the interactions between work motivations and communication visibility.

To address the literature gaps, we first borrow from a new theoretical lens, i.e., the theory of communication visibility [23,24], to theorize the impact of social media use on work efficiency. This theory posits that social media increases the visibility of online communications in terms of organizational metaknowledge about “who knows what” (message transparency) and “who knows whom” (network translucence). The enhanced metaknowledge could facilitate knowledge sharing [25] and improve work performance. Based on this theory, we theorize the dif ferential impacts of message transparency and network translucence of communication visibility on employees’ knowledge sharing and work efficiency. Second, we draw on the work motivation theory to identify reputation and social networking as the two essential work motivations [17] and explore their interactions with communication visibility in influencing knowledge sharing and work efficiency.

To test our theorization, we surveyed 238 employees from various industries who are using social media platforms for work purposes (e.g., QQ, WeChat, and DingTalk). The results show that both message transparency and network translucence of communication visibility are positively related to employees’ work efficiency, but only message transparency increases knowledge sharing. Notably, message trans parency weakens the impact of reputation on knowledge sharing, whereas network translucence enhances the impact of social networking.

These findings contribute to the literature in several ways. First, our research adds to social media literature by providing a new theoretical lens to study the relationship between social media use and work per formance. This new lens theorizes the two aspects of communication visibility and provides insights into the impacts of social media use on work-related outcomes. Second. this study contributes broadly to the nascent theory, i.e., the communication visibility theory, that has yet to receive empirical examinations [23]. Our work empirically tests the constructs and relationships relevant to this theory, thereby validating this theory. Third, we extend the theory of communication visibility by identifying its moderating effects on the impact of work motivations. Our findings also provide insights into how to regulate social media use within organizations.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The theory of communication visibility

The theory of communication visibility posits that social media af fords message transparency and network translucence to increase in dividuals’ awareness of “who knows what” and “who knows whom” [23]. Specifically, message transparency refers to the degree to which one can be aware of and monitor the content of co-workers’ information exchange on social media [25]. In a typical workplace, communications among employees are largely invisible to others except for the message sender and recipient. Social media makes communications among co-workers even more visible to third parties than previous enterprise technologies, e.g., email, ERP system, or intranets [24]. Network translucence refers to the degree that third-party observers have an awareness of the existence of and monitor other people’s interpersonal relationships through observing their interactions [25]. Social media offers various functions such as exchanging knowledge, making group calls, sharing hyperlinks, and commenting on and liking co-workers postings [8]. These functions can lead to a high level of network trans lucence by enabling observers to discern co-workers’ social relationships within the organization. However, observers may not exactly know the scope and nature of the observable relationships [23]. Although people can see others’ interactions and discern who knows whom, it is hard to tell the nature and strength of their relationships merely based on what is observed.

This theory posits that communication visibility can facilitate knowledge exchange and promote work efficiency [25,26]. Work-related information and knowledge such as work locations, workflows, activity status, problems, and solutions posted on social media can be accessible to other group members simultaneously and sometimes automatically. The benefits of social media use in saving time and effort to disseminate work-related messages to a broad group of recipients make it an attractive platform for employees to share knowledge with co-workers [3,12]. Visibility in the information and knowledge shared on social media enables employees to leverage the existing resources and avoid task duplication and hence leads to improved work efficiency [23].

However, the influence of communication visibility is more complicated than previously thought. Research has noted that commu nication visibility is not invariably beneficial for employees and orga nizations [27,28]. For example, Kane [29] documented a case that message transparency afforded by social media hinders collaboration within a company because employees are reluctant to present novel ideas that may not align with the company norms when communication messages are entirely monitored. This implies that employees’ work motivations play a role in determining their knowledge sharing on social media and work efficiency. Furthermore, researchers have found that higher communication visibility does not always lead to better collab oration, boundary spanning, or knowledge sharing due to employees varying work motivations [4,12]. This suggests that communication visibility might need to be considered together with employees’ moti vations [4]. However, detailed empirical investigations that examine the interaction between employees’ work motivations and communication visibility remain relatively rare (ibid.). Thus, this study seeks to advance our understanding of how different aspects of communication visibility alter how employees whose motivations differ engage in knowledge sharing and work efficiency.

## 2.2. Work motivations and knowledge sharing

It is essential to understand the fundamental mechanisms motivating employees to share knowledge on social media [30,31]. Knowledge is sticky such that it is internalized in individuals and regarded as em ployees’ private assets [32]. Individuals would share their knowledge only when there are incentives that motivate them to do so. The moti vation is the reason why an individual takes action [33]. It is a psy chological driver that heightens one’s willingness to expend efforts and persist to achieve a goal [34]. Work motivation theory focuses on un derstanding employees’ behaviors from a motivational perspective [17]. Social rewards have been identified as an important type of motivation that drives employees to engage in pro-organizational behavior, such as knowledge sharing [17,35]. In this research, we focus on two particular social rewards as important work motivations: reputation [16,36] and the desire to broaden and maintain social networking [21,31,37].

Reputation refers to the expectation of improving personal image and status in profession among co-workers [20]. Reputation emerges in interpersonal interactions [38]. In the workplace, a good reputation is a critical intangible asset that employees want to possess [16,39]. Em ployees build up their reputation through winning co-workers’ and leaders’ recognition, in the way of approval, trust, dignity, and respect [16]. Stuebs and Sun [40] surveyed a sample of highly reputable firms and demonstrated that the desire for a good reputation could motivate employees to achieve high job efficiency and job productivity. Re searchers also suggest that reputational benefits motivate employees to share knowledge in enterprise social media [4]. Thus, the perception of achieving a good reputation could drive employees to share what they regard as beneficial for the organization on social media.

Another important work motivation is social networking. Social networking refers to the expectation of maintaining and broadening professional connections with co-workers in an organization [21]. An individual’s social networking benefits could include interpersonal trust, reciprocal obligation, and harmony [41]. Individuals have the inner needs for social networking [33]. Employees who desire a more extensive social network will share more on social media [22]. Thus, per previous literature, both reputation and social networking can motivate employees to share knowledge.

Furthermore, recent studies have suggested that relationships be tween motivations and social media use might change in the emergence of new technological features afforded by social media (e.g., [42,43]), e. g., communication visibility. For example, when technical affordance conflicts with employees’ motivations, they will adapt their use of the technology to align with their motivations/goals [42]. However, little research has empirically investigated the joint impacts of communica tion visibility and work motivations [6]. Therefore, this study aims to explore how communication visibility will interact with work motiva tions. Overall, there is a need for empirical research on both the direct effects of communication visibility on employees’ knowledge sharing and work efficiency and the interaction effects of communication visi bility and work motivations, which is addressed by this research.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Integrating the communication visibility theory and work motiva tion theory, we propose the research model (Fig. 1) and develop hy potheses in the following sections.

## 3.1. Effects of communication visibility on knowledge sharing and work efficiency

Communication visibility affects work efficiency through meta knowledge enhancement and redundancy avoidance [23]. Visible work-related knowledge empowers employees to interact conveniently and avoid unnecessary waiting time and duplicated work, which sup ports business alignment and agility, and hence improves employees work efficiency [44]. In particular, network translucence enables ob servers to understand co-workers’ social networks within an organiza tion. Leonardi [23] suggests that network translucence allows individuals to acquire an introduction or endorsement to someone they did not know previously through a mutual friend on social media. The awareness of others’ social networks will ease knowledge transfer [45, 46] as people can develop shared context, vision, and languages more easily [19,45]. Knowing others’ social networks also enables knowledge seekers to obtain more favors outside their current social circles by forming a swift trust with a mutual friend [19]. As a result, individuals can improve work efficiency. Thus, we expect:

H1a. Network translucence is positively related to an employee’s work efficiency.

Employees gain metaknowledge about who is competent and knowledgeable in a specific realm by observing others’ activities and work history on social media [47]. The organizational metaknowledge about “who knows what” being enhanced by message transparency plays a critical role in employees’ knowledge management and task execution [48]. It helps employees take advantage of and recombine available knowledge to solve problems without undertaking duplicated work [23]. In particular, message transparency allows employees to learn from others’ messages vicariously and create a shared memory of the knowledge pool [31]. As a result, they promptly identify the right information or solutions when relevant problems occur [25]. Thus, we expect:

H1b. Message transparency is positively related to an employee’s work efficiency.

Communication visibility could affect knowledge sharing in three major ways. First, social media is a visible communication channel broadcasting collective and pro-organizational behaviors (e.g., knowl edge sharing) to all members [23]. Being aware of “who knows what” and “who knows whom”, the knowledge seeker is cognizant of which members on social media master the knowledge he/she needs and have the social ties he/she wants to access. Besides, knowledge providers benefit from the two types of metaknowledge through showing their enthusiasm, professionalism, and capabilities to others they want to connect.

Second, network translucence and message transparency make social media a transactive memory system [23]. A transactive memory system consists of two components: a structural component and a processual component [49]. The first component focuses on every group member’s knowledge (metaknowledge of “who knows what”), while the second one requires interpersonal communications and interactions to enhance the metaknowledge of “who knows whom”. The latter component up dates the former one [50], and the two components together create a dynamic system facilitating knowledge sharing and producing group cognition. Within this system, employees can observe the whole work course and understand what has happened. They can also encode, store, edit, and retrieve knowledge from the transactive memory system for job-related tasks. Such a shared transactive memory enables employees to know when and what they can share on social media [31].

Third, network translucence and message transparency are effective social lubricants to facilitate knowledge exchange [46]. The major problems limiting knowledge sharing are a lack of ties between the knowledge seeker and the knowledge source and the complexity of the knowledge held by the source [45,51]. Network translucence and mes sage transparency could effectively help organizations address these problems and pave the way for knowledge sharing. In particular, network translucence allows group members to develop the cognition of others’ social structures [25]. It thus creates the opportunity for knowledge seekers to expand current social ties or leverage an indirect relationship to gain knowledge. Message transparency escalates group members’ awareness of others’ expertise and availabilities. This awareness reduces the ambiguity about the knowledge source and the knowledge itself. Besides, the enhanced metaknowledge enables a knowledge seeker to identify the source of needed knowledge and initiate the knowledge sharing activity [46]. Therefore, the condition of high network translucence and message transparency may conduce to more active knowledge sharing. Thus, we expect:

## 3.2. The effect of knowledge sharing on work efficiency

Knowledge sharing refers to the activity of exchanging personal knowledge with other members within an organization [52]. According to knowledge management literature, the effective use of enterprise knowledge can boost both organizations’ and individuals’ competi tiveness [53]. Prior research suggests that collective work can help overcome individual limitations, especially for subjective or innovative tasks that require employees to brainstorm [54]. An individual might not be equipped with all task-required knowledge and skills, while a group of employees is more likely to have the complete skill set for accomplishing tasks well [55]. Social media can help gather together every single person’s knowledge, expertise, and viewpoints [31]. As a result, employees can learn from their co-workers’ experiences, thereby enhancing their work efficiency [19,31].

Past literature also indicates that knowledge sharing benefits not only knowledge seekers and free-riders but also contributors [56,57]. Helping others solve problems can stimulate the knowledge contributor to learn from past experience and contribution, thereby improving their work productivity and efficiency [46,56]. Following this logic, we expect that sharing knowledge on social media could improve an em ployee’s work efficiency. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3. Knowledge sharing is positively related to an employee’s work efficiency.

3.3. Interaction effects of communication visibility and social rewards on knowledge sharing

Social rewards (reputation and social networking) have been consistently found to promote knowledge sharing on social media [19, 31,36]. Enhancing prior theorizing, we posit that communication visi bility afforded by social media can moderate the effects of social rewards on knowledge sharing. Communication visibility enables individuals to present their competency of helping others to a broad audience, which satisfies emplovees' needs for social rewards. However, when the benefits from social rewards cannot cover the costs, e.g., a distraction from others’ communication, the enhanced communication visibility may discourage employees from using social media for work. In this section, we will develop two hypotheses to advance our understanding of each specific mechanism of social media affordance, particularly the separate moderating effect of the network translucence and message trans parency mechanisms of communication visibility. Employees gain reputation by demonstrating their expertise and skills through the message transparency mechanism that shows “who knows what” rather than through the mechanism that reveals “who knows whom”. In contrast, the network translucence mechanism discloses employees social structures and actions within an organization, which conceptually relates to social networking benefits. Thus, we propose that network translucence moderates the effect of social networking on employees knowledge sharing activity, whereas message transparency moderates the impact of reputation.

Network translucence enables employees to be aware of and monitor co-workers’ interpersonal relationships by observing their interactions [25]. On the one hand, being aware of others’ social networks, knowl edge providers will know their contributions can be accessed by not only the knowledge seekers but also the seekers’ social networks, which would increase the chance of forming new ties through those networks [31]. Therefore, knowledge providers can gain heightened networking rewards from the expanded social networks [21]. Also, network trans lucence afforded by social media can propagate the knowledge pro viders’ competences and willingness to help knowledge seekers [19] to a broad audience by showing what they have contributed and who they have helped. The resultant gratification and endorsement from that process would encourage them to share knowledge to maintain and broaden social relationships.

On the other hand, increasing the translucence of co-workers interactive activities on social media [25] can facilitate observers to judge the nature and quality of the interpersonal relationships among co-workers. They can therefore leverage this judgment to establish new social ties through the introduction by the right co-workers [22]. And, observing co-workers’ interactions would also highlight the attractive ness of having close and wide social relationships, making social networking a more salient social reward [31]. Therefore, employees who desire the benefit of social networking are more likely to share knowledge when they perceive the social network on the social media to be translucent. Thus, we expect:

H4. Network translucence positively moderates the effect of social networking on an employee’s knowledge sharing.

In contrast, we argue that message transparency may weaken the effect of reputation on knowledge sharing. People’s behaviors vary with their self-regulatory focuses [58]. Promotion-focused self-regulation is characterized by the pursuit of gains, whereas prevention-focused self-regulation by loss aversion [59]. Acharya et al. [60] found that managerial employees who care more about their reputations are usu ally self-insured and risk-aversive. In general, employees who are reputable and at a high status tend to pursue personal achievements and protect what they already have, rather than invest much time and effort to earn social rewards that have diminishing marginal returns [22].

Moreover, an overly transparent communication environment might pose risks to individuals’ reputations [61] and lead individuals who protect and cherish reputation to be more cautious in knowledge contribution [22]. On the one hand, message transparency allows em ployees to discern others’ job responsibilities and expertise. Although the clarity in job responsibilities could facilitate social interactions, it might also circuitously reduce employees’ enthusiasm for sharing as they may perceive it to be irrelevant to their responsibility [4]. When a co-worker asks for help and seeks advice on social media, employees may believe other more knowledgeable people will respond with a better idea and solution, resulting in a bystander effect [62,63]. On the other hand, employees might be cautious in knowledge sharing out of the concerns that their incorrect or unprofessional ideas might damage their reputation within the organization in the face of nonanonymity (i. e., high message transparency) [62,63]. Combining these arguments, we expect that message transparency might weaken the positive effect of reputation on knowledge sharing. Specifically, we hypothesize:

H5. Message transparency negatively moderates the effect of reputation on an employee’s knowledge sharing.

## 4. Research methodology

We employed a survey methodology to examine our research hy potheses for its advantage in providing more generalizable results [64].

## 4.1. Operationalization of constructs

Table 1 shows the construct definitions, and Table 2 shows the construct measurement. Where available, construct definitions and measurement items were adapted from prior research. Given that items for measuring the constructs were adapted from various research or developed for our research, all items underwent a two-stage conceptual validation [65]. Four graduate students engaged in the first stage (un structured sorting), and another four participated in the second stage (structured sorting) as sorters. Results from the unstructured sorting stage showed an 89 % agreement rate. We reworded five items and removed two items as suggested. In the second stage, all sorters classi fied all items into targeted categories correctly. Finally, 25 items were consolidated into the instruments used in the survey.

## 4.2. Survey administration

We first translated our questionnaire into Chinese. We invited three PhD students whose mother tongue is Chinese to check the coherence and consistency of the translation. Two Chinese students helped test the time required for completing the survey.

We used the snowball sampling method. We sent out an anonymous survey invitation link via WeChat. The survey URL was first sent to the authors’ WeChat friends who worked in various companies, and those friends then forwarded the URL to their friends and colleagues. Each WeChat account was permitted to access the survey only once. In order to attract more people to engage in our study, we rewarded each valid respondent one dollar for their engagement. Two criteria helped us screen out invalid responses. First, only respondents who spent over 2 mins in answering all questions were deemed valid. Second, we used several binary questions to screen respondents during the period of answering the survey, e.g., “Do you use this social media for work pur poses?” Only respondents who answered “Yes” to such questions were instructed to proceed to the survey questions.

Discriminant validity reflects the extent to which factors are signif icant and unrelated to other constructs. It can be verified by the square root of each factor’s AVE when it is higher than the value of its corre lations with others, and structure loadings are higher on the corre sponding structures than on others. We assessed the discriminant validity by evaluating the indicator-factor loadings and AVEs with interconstruct correlations [70]. Results in Table 6 show that all correlation coefficients are less than 0.7, and the square roots of AVE for each construct (diagonal terms) are larger than inter-construct correlations (off-diagonal terms). Also, variance inflation factors (VIFs) are less than the threshold value of 10, suggesting that multi-collinearity is not a problem. Hence, we have established the construct discriminant validity.

## 5.2. Common method bias test

Since all data are self-reported, there might be common method biases resulting from a single source [71]. We evaluated the severity of common method bias from two aspects. First, we conducted Harman’s single factor test. The results show that neither a single factor nor one general factor accounts for a majority of the variance (<50 %). Second, following Liang et al. [72], we created a common method factor in PLS measured by all principal constructs’ indicators. Table 7 shows that the average substantively explained variance of indicators by the principal constructs is 0.879, while the average method-based variance was 0.0006. The ratio of substantive variance to method variance was about 1465:1. Only 2 of the 25 method factor loadings were significant, sug gesting that common method bias was not an issue.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

There are several important findings from our study. A salient result is that knowledge sharing and communication visibility in terms of message transparency and network translucence are key determinants of employees’ work efficiency. Thus, knowledge sharing and enhanced metaknowledge about “who knows what” and “who knows whom” can improve employees’ work efficiency. Another notable finding is the differential roles of the two mechanisms of communication visibility in fostering knowledge sharing. Specifically, we found message trans parency has both a direct effect and interactive effect with reputation on knowledge sharing. Additionally, network translucence only influences employees’ knowledge sharing activities indirectly through interacting with social networking. In accord with H4 and H5, network translucence strengthens the relationship between social networking and knowledge sharing, while message transparency weakens the relationship between reputation and knowledge sharing.

However, this study did not find that network translucence could encourage employees to share knowledge. There could be three reasons for this result. First, even without social media, members within a company are colleagues or co-workers who usually know each other, especially in a small organization. Thus, an increased perception of network translucence may not add an additional incentive for em ployees to share knowledge. Second, the inner nature of co-workers networks may be less transparent than their messages. Thus, despite an increment in the metaknowledge gained from network translucence, it may not significantly enhance employees’ understanding of others precise connections and connection strength. Third, network trans lucence and message transparency mechanisms are distinct but also interrelated [23]. Ultimately, the network translucence is developed on message transparency. That is, the metaknowledge of “who knows whom” is generated through observing people’s messages and interactions.

## 6.3. Implications for research

Our research contributes to the information systems literature in several ways. First, this study contributes to the communication visi bility theory by empirically validating the hypothesized relationships between communication visibility and knowledge sharing and work efficiency, and extends the theory by exploring new relationships. Leo nardi et al. [76] and Leonardi [23] developed the communication visi bility theory, positing that communication visibility affordance is manifested as two distinct but interrelated features: message trans parency and network translucence. Leonardi [25] suggested that the two communication visibility affordances of social media can increase observers’ metaknowledge accuracy, which would improve their work innovation and performance. This study takes a further step by theo rizing the direct and interactive impacts of message transparency and network translucence on knowledge sharing and work efficiency. Furthermore, with a dominance of conceptual research on this theory, our study contributes by empirically validating such effects. Our study further extends this theory by finding that message transparency can enhance both knowledge sharing and work efficiency, but network translucence only benefits work efficiency.

Second, this study advances the theory of communication visibility by identifying contingent moderation effects of message transparency and network translucence on knowledge sharing. In particular, by integrating with work motivation theory, we find that network trans lucence enhances the positive relationship between social networking and knowledge sharing, but message transparency attenuates the posi tive relationship between reputation and knowledge sharing. Such dif ferential moderation effects improve our understanding of this theory and pave the way for further research to validate this theory in various other contexts.

Third, our study contributes to the literature on social media use for work purposes [6,9,31]. We employ a new theoretical lens to study social media use for work purposes. Such a new theoretical lens provides additional insights in terms of differential moderating impacts of mes sage transparency and network translucence on knowledge sharing. Hence, it enriches our understanding of this research area [19,31]. Such a new theoretical lens might also help address the mixed findings in past literature by theorizing two aspects of social media afforded commu nication visibility rather than a unitary aspect of social media use.

## 6.4. Implications for practice

From a pragmatic perspective, our study provides insights to de velopers that popular social media should not be limited to perfecting the function of recreation or sociability but extended to work-related functions. The results demonstrated that it is meaningful to design more services to strengthen message transparency and network trans lucence, which will improve work efficiency directly and indirectly. Even though network translucence cannot motivate employees to share knowledge with co-workers directly, it performs well in enhancing the effect of social networking on knowledge sharing.

Second, our research indicates that employees use social media differently. The results illustrated that reputation and social networking are powerful motivations for employees to share knowledge. Moreover, the effective use of social media is also beneficial for work efficiency due to its communication visibility features, including message transparency and network translucence. Social media empowers employees to discern “who knows what” and “who knows whom”, which helps create meta knowledge and avoid redundancy in work. Assessing two sets of moderation effects, we find that employees with a strong desire for reputation are less motivated to share knowledge via social media under a high level of message transparency. Thus, organizations and the platform should consider more about their requirements to motivate them to share. Besides, employees can make use of the network trans lucence affordance of social media to help them share knowledge for broadening social networking.

## 7. Conclusion

This study was motivated by the fact that a considerable number of employees are using social media for work-related communication, but limited studies have explored the influence of social media use on knowledge sharing and work efficiency with a detailed investigation. Nascent social media–related theories and affordances are interesting but lack empirical examination. To fill this gap, we built a theoretical model based on the communication visibility theory and work motiva tion theory. The proposed model assessed how work motivations and communication visibility affordance of social media would encourage employees to share knowledge and impact their work efficiency, as well as how communication visibility affordances interact with work moti vations to affect knowledge sharing. Our research findings indicated that message transparency could motivate employees to share knowledge via social media and improve work efficiency. The findings also illustrated that message transparency and the expectation of obtaining reputation interact to attenuate knowledge sharing, and network translucence and social networking interact to increase knowledge sharing. These find. ings not only enlighten future research on social media use for work but also inform practitioners on how to design and implement social media for work communications to promote knowledge sharing and work efficiency.
