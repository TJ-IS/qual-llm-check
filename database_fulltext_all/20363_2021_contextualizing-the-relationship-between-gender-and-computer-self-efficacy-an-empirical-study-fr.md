## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital divide Gender Computer self efficacy Indian context Caste Marriage

## A B S T R A C T

In this study, by considering household, societal, and institutional contextual factors, we attempt to theorize the relationship between gender and digital divide with the help of social learning theory (SLT) and the literature on computer self-efficacy (CSE). Then, using a large-scale national representative sample data from India, we empirically evaluate these relationships. Our empirical analysis corroborates anecdotal evidence that gender: based digital divide exists and is severe. Caste, household size, marriage, and presence of digitally literate women in the family are found to be influencers of women's CSE. which. in turn. impact gender-level digital divide.

## 1. Introduction

“Gender inequality is a far-reaching societal impairment, not merely a special deprivation of women. That social understanding is urgent as well as momentous.” ([1], p250) – Dr. Amartya Sen

Gender disparity is a widespread concern mainly for a developing nation like India. Over the last few years, the governments have strived to reduce gender-based inequalities by providing equal opportunity to women through affirmative actions. However, in spite of different pos itive actions from the governments. there are gender disparities. One of the disparities is evident in the context of access and use of information and communication technology (ICT), which is commonly termed the digital divide. In the recent years, internet penetration has exponentially increased worldwide, but several studies indicate that internet access and use is unequal among men and women [2]. The Economist Intelli gence Unit (EIU) reports that the proportion of men accessing internet is approximately 80.2 per cent higher than that of women in the low-income countries [3]. Studies conducted at the country-level also report similar disparity. For example. Indian reports show that only 29 per cent of internet users are women [4]. This is in contrast with South American countries such as Brazil, in which more than 70 percent of the women are internet users and South-east Asian countries such as Malaysia and Singapore, where 79 percent and 88 percent of the women respectively are internet users as depicted in Fig. 1 [5]. However, it can be observed that the difference in the percentage of men and women who are internet users is almost negligible, except in the South-Asian countries like India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan (Figs. 1 and 2).

While over the last decade, India among other countries in South Asia has observed significant economic development and increase in per capita income, gender-based digital divide seems to be increasing. Also, despite having better digital technologies and corresponding infra structure, cheaper mobile and internet services, gender-based digital divide seems to be prevalent more in India when compared with some African countries. India ranks at 98 out of 100 countries in terms of ‘gender-gap in internet access’ index of the Inclusive Internet Index – 2020, only ahead of Malawi and Pakistan [6].

From a gendered perspective, the unequal access of internet is chal lenging for the overall socio-economic development, as ICT is considered as a new channel for delivering public services and other developmental initiatives [5]. International Telecommunication Unit (ITU), the ICT Wing of the United Nations, advocates ICT tools as one of the ways to reduce gender inequality and achieve women empowerment [7]. The promotion of universal access of ICT will not only reduce inequality and increase interaction, learning, and productivity but will also enhance social welfare through access to education, healthcare, and markets for commerce [8]. Considering the importance of the internet in the digital age, in order to reduce the barriers for women to access and use the ICTs, there is need for further understanding of the gender-based digital divide, particularly in the developing countries, like India where the disparity is significant [6].

Though digital divide was earlier conceptualized as the disparity be tween those who have access to the internet and those who do not [9], in the last decade, it has evolved into a multifaceted notion from ‘inequality to access ICT’ (access divide) to ‘inequality of capability’ (capability divide) and a resultant ‘inequality in appropriating benefits’ (outcome divide) (e.g., [10–12]). Extant literature indicates that CSE is one of the strong predictors of digital divide. CSE refers to the judgement of one’s own capability to use ICT [10,12] and is influenced by socio-demographic factors such as age, gender, education, income, and location [12,13]. However, in studies that explain CSE and digital divide, we find contrasting empirical evidence on the role of socio-demographics, particularly gender [11,14]. The gender-based digital divide is found to be significantly higher in the developing countries as compared with the developed countries [2].

A compelling reason for such mixed results could be that most studie have examined these relationships on a piece-meal basis without explicitly accounting for socio-cultural context [14,15], thus leading to digital divides. Here, the context refers to circumstances, situations, conditions, or environment that are external to the phenomenon, and can enable or constrain it [16]. Hence, an effort to study the phenom enon of digital divide within the ‘socio-cultural and decision-making context' [14] would facilitate its understanding. However, there is scarcity of studies on the contextual understanding of differences in the levels of CSE<sup>1</sup> across genders and gender-based digital divide. Hence, this study aims to fill this knowledge gap by seeking answers for the research question: Does disparity exist in CSE levels across the genders? If yes, why?

We explore the contextual factors shaping CSE across the genders with large-scale secondary data from India by considering the ‘multi faceted’ contextual lens comprising social, institutional, and spatial di mensions [17]. We employ the social learning theory (SLT) [18–20] to explain this phenomenon. SLT provides the necessary lens to contextu alize the gender-based digital divide in the form of sources and di mensions that influence CSE (e.g., Mastery Experience, Social Persuasion etc.) [12,13]. Furthermore, through this theoretical lens, we demonstrated the role of contextual factors such as social class, marital status, household size, and vicarious experience in shaping CSE.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: In the next section, we begin with a review of digital divide literature, particularly the gender based digital divide. We then develop hypotheses based on our research questions. Furthermore, we provide an account of the methodology employed and the description of the dataset. Finally, we present the findings of this study and theoretical and policy implications of the findings.

## 2. Literature review

With increasing prominence of ICT, the focus on digital divide has increased over the last decade [12]. Dewan and Riggins [10] develop a two-level framework to study digital divide in which the first lev el—digital access divide—refers to the disparity among individuals hav ing access to information technology and those who do not. The second level—digital capability divide—refers to the inequality in capabilities of individuals to use the digital technologies. Wei et al. [12] extend this digital divide framework to include the differences in the actualized benefits of digital technologies and refer to it as digital outcome divide. Thus, digital divide can be defined broadly as the disparity between those who have access to technology as well as possess the capability to use it, i.e., ‘the technology haves’ and those with no access, i.e., ‘the technology have-nots’ [21]. Furthermore, studies have attempted to explain the antecedents of the digital divide such as socio-demographic factors [22], personality characteristics [21], and psychosocial factors [23]. Among other things, CSE has been identified as a central factor influencing the digital divide among individuals [10,12].

CSE plays a pivotal role in understanding the digital divide, as access to technology provides individuals with the opportunity of developing their CSE, which, in turn, affects the use of this digital technology [10, 12,13]. Some of the important factors that affect CSE of an individual are age [11,24,25], gender [12,26], academic ability, income [27–29], availability of computer at home and school, computer usage patterns, etc. [12].

As the focus of our study is gender-based digital divide, especially the differences in CSE between men and women, we did a thorough review of the extant literature on gender-based digital divide. Table 1 lists a set of articles illustrating the influence of gender on digital divide and CSE with regard to the context. Few studies on digital divide indicate that CSE levels across genders are comparable [30.311. Studies based on data from Asian countries such as China [32], Singapore [12], and India [21] find evidence related to the differences in CSE of men and women. However, no such evidence is present in the studies from the European countries [30,33] and USA [34,35]. A recent meta-analysis [36] also indicates that while men on average have more favorable attitude than women toward technology, there are significant variations in the effect sizes among the studies.

The pursuit of explanation of these differences led the extant litera ture to primarily focus on personal factors such as age, income and education and positional and contextual factors related to household and society have largely been under researched [55]. We are thus motivated to ‘contextualize the gender-based digital divides’ and theo retically explain the plausible causes for the inconsistent findings (e.g.

[54,56],).

To examine the role of contextual factors in determining the differ ences in CSE across genders, theoretical frameworks related to CSE that can provide a contextual lens were needed. In this study, we have thus adopted two theories: ‘Omnibus Context’ lens and ‘Social Learning Theory (SLT)’. While, Omnibus Context lens helps us in organizing thoughts and providing coherent explanations for the different contex tual variables, SLT enables us to provide explanations for diagnosing how these contextual variables influence the disparities that manifested. Davison et al. [57] refer to these as ‘focal theory’ and ‘instrumental theory’. That is, we do not explicitly operationalize predictors of self-efficacy from SLT; instead, we use it to make theoretical arguments for contextual factors that are expected to influence the target phe nomenon strongly [58]. We use the instrumental theory (SLT) to provide the theoretical base to logically connect the omnibus contextual factors to the CSE.

## 2.1. Omnibus contextual Lens

Context is defined as circumstances, situations, conditions, or envi ronment that are external to the phenomena, and which enable or constrain it [17]. Welter [17] argues that the behavior of individuals can be better understood within its historical, social, institutional, temporal, and spatial contexts, as these settings provide opportunities and set boundaries for their actions.

Johns [16] classifies context into omnibus and discrete contexts. Omnibus context refers to the broad perspectives focusing on who, what, when, where, and why, while discrete context is about the contextual variables like the physical or social factors. Earlier studies on CSE consider discrete contextual factors such as age, education, and income (e.g., [12,30]). Therefore, in this study, we emphasize on the much-neglected omnibus context for understanding the differences in CSE across the genders.

According to the omnibus perspective, we need to know ‘what’, ‘who’, ‘where’, ‘when’, and ‘why’ of the phenomenon for understanding the behavior of an individual [17]. In this study, we attempt to under stand the differences in CSE levels (what) across the genders with a special focus on women (who). Owing to absence of longitudinal data at the individual level on digital literacy, we use cross-sectional data (when)<sup>2</sup> from India (where).

The answer to ‘where’ plays an important role in contextualizing the phenomenon, as it depicts the manifold location and has impact on ‘who’. The manifold location for this study is India and contextually the country provides a rich and unique perspective because of her political, social, cultural, and family norms. Welter [17] further distinguishes ‘where’ under four broad categories: business, social, spatial, and institutional contexts for including the political system, social environ ment, and local neighborhoods. ## 2.2. Social learning theory

The origins of CSE can be traced back to the notion of self-efficacy (SE) derived from SLT proposed by Bandura [12,13]. SE is the belief of individuals in their ability to perform a specific task and is a strong predictor of subsequent task-specific performance [13]. SE is an outcome of the self-belief system, which controls individual’s cognitive processes, motivations, and behavior [18–20]. The self-belief system operates in a causal model of triadic reciprocity, where personal factors (age, education), behavioral patterns (computer usage pattern), and environmental factors (availability of a computer at home) influence one another [12,19]. The four main sources of influence that shape an individual’s SE are performance accomplishments/mastery experience, vicarious experience, social/verbal persuasion, and emotional arousal [12,13,18]. Table 3 provides a short description of these four factors. In the next section, we examine the manifestation of country-specific contexts (where) into different sources (why) for shaping the differ ences in CSE across genders. Adopting Welter’s framework of contextual factors, extant literature about overlapping inequalities [59], and triple burden [60] faced by the women in India we identified certain variables. For instance, Dr\`eze and Sen [60] show that caste directly influences the literacy rates among individuals in India. The Economist [61] report showcases that India is the only country where women’s employment rates are going down and they attribute it to Marriage among other things. This led us to explore the unique contextual factors in India which might affect women’s literacy and, in our context, CSE.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. Gender and CSE

While examining CSE, a recurrent theme has been the study of disparity in CSE on the basis of gender. Literature suggests that women tend to have lower aptitude [63] and usage capability but higher anxiety in learning computer [64]. Some studies find that the diffusion of technology is quicker among men than women, so women have lower CSE than men $[ 1 0 , 1 3 , 6 5 , 1 2 , 6 6 ]$ . The family values, social structure, and cultural biases have also been reported to negatively affect CSE of women $[ 6 7 , 6 6 , 2 1 ]$ ].

In India, the girl child of a family is deprived of quality education or professional training [1]. Many studies have shown evidence related to gender-differential educational expenditure within families [68,69]. For instance, Azam and Kingdon [68] find that households spend less on the girl child, as they send their daughters to the government-funded schools but spend more on their sons by sending them to high fee-charging private schools. Most of the private schools are not only equipped with good computer infrastructure but also provide compul sory computer training along with other courses [70]. Such a training at a public school is a luxury which is a function of the availability of infrastructure and dedicated teaching faculty. Such differential educa tion directly affects the CSE of the individual. Sen [1] notes that “even when there is relatively little difference in the basic facilities including schooling, the opportunities of special facilities, such as higher education training, may be far fewer for young women than for young men” (pp 232-233), which is also essential for shaping of CSE. Based on the above arguments, we hypothesize:

H1. CSE levels in women are likely to be lower than in men.

## 3.2. Caste and Women’s CSE

Social Groups in India are based on Caste. A caste is “an endogamous and hereditary social group limited to persons of the same rank, occupation, economic position” (Dictionary.com). Caste is the principal identity for many Indians [71] and plays a deterministic role in the community structure and political power [72]. The caste system is an ancient and complex social structure, which has evolved over thousands of years. Though the caste system can be traced back to Vedic era’s varna system, the current caste, i.e., the Jati system is much more complex and embedded deeply in social life; hence, it plays a crucial role in deter mining the rituals, social ceremonies, marriage relations, and political formulations. Modernization has reduced the effect of caste system in urban India, but it still dominates life of rural people ([73], for exam ples, see [72]). De’ [72] argues that, “caste also shapes the political and social contours of village life, [74] determining fault lines of the contest and emerging affiliations” (p3).

The Government of India broadly classifies the caste system under four broad categories [75]. The General Category (GC)—constituting the upper castes—and Other Backward Classes (OBC)—constituting the middle-ranked castes—are generally referred to as the socially dominant groups. Schedule Caste (SC) and Schedule Tribe (ST) are historically considered as the disadvantaged groups and are referred as the socially weaker groups.

The caste system in Indian villages signifies domination and legiti mation; and it reinforces position and power, which determines the access to village resources through the creation of a structure on its own [72]. Dominant groups control public resources, social positions, and political power, which, in turn, directly influence the beneficiaries of government welfare programs. This structure further determines the people gaining benefits of the social welfare programs and access to digital resources. While the dominant groups occupy most of the prominent positions, the socially weaker groups are trapped in casual labor [60,76]. The role expectancy of such socially weaker group in dividuals may not motivate them to become digitally literate. and this lack of motivation inversely affects the shaping of CSE [13,77,78].

Also the lack of CSE success stories in the socially affiliated groups of the weaker community acts as a negative vicarious learning experience, thus pushing them further away from shaping their CSE. Furthermore, the socially weaker group also suffers owing to negative persuasion from the dominant group and lack of situational support [71]. All these fac tors negatively influence the shaping of CSE among individuals of the socially weaker group. Hence, we hypothesize:

## Hypothesis 2a. CSE levels of an individual from a socially weaker group are likely to be lower than that of a dominant group individual.

We argue that with regard to the role played by CSE in shaping in dividuals, the effect of being in a socially weaker group is more profound for women because they are considered as a distinct category [79]. In dividuals occupy multiple categories, and the defining category (in this case gender and social group) gets activated at different time, places, and circumstances [59]. A woman of the socially weaker group has to bear the double burden of social group and gender [80]. ## 3.3. Household size and CSE of women

Indian household structure provides a unique context to this study. With increase in the household size, the availability of resources per person decreases, which is reflected in the per capita income or expen diture in a family. However, in a patriarchal society like India, familie maintain a social hierarchy, and all the members are not considered equal. Compared to nuclear families in developed nations, where the average family size has two or three individuals, Indians mostly live in joint families (according to our data, mean family size is 5.5 and maximum number recorded is 32) [82], where male siblings live together with their parents even after marriage. However, a woman goes to her in-law’s place to live with her husband and his family. A man in the family usually exercises authority over his wife and children, and a woman on her unmarried daughters and daughter-in-law. Elder brothers have authority over younger siblings and are expected to respect the wishes of their mother. Thus, each person’s authority and power is governed by his/her position in the family and yields to the presence of higher family authority [83]. The same can be represented in a family hierarchy tree as shown below (Fig. 3). This is also evident from the intra-house food sharing arrangement [59,84].

The inequality of sharing is not only limited to consumable goods such as food but is also evident in the sharing of property [85] and re sources for education [1]. The traditional idea of ‘provinces’ [1] – the social, administrative, and ideological factors [86] underlie such persistent inequality. Resources like computer, mobile phones, etc., are neither perishable like food nor transferable from one generation to other like land. Still, they are not easily accessible to women, as they have low bargaining power within the household [87], in case there are more men than women. For instance, Kafai et al. [67] report that in a scenario where both brother and sister want to use the home computer, the parents are likely to give priority to the boy.

With regard to the Indian families, one of the major factors that impede the well-being of the female child is the gender bias, lack of social attention, and negligence toward them [1]. It is also evident from the highest incidence of the female child mortality rate in India [60]. With increase in the household size, there is increase in negligence to ward the girl child, which manifests into lack of persuasion, guidance or situational support that are considered as the major factors shaping any form of SE [18]. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. CSE levels of a woman from a family with larger household size are likely to be lower than that of a woman from smaller household size.

## 3.4. Influence of digitally literate women in the household

A famous political discourse states that “If you educate a girl, you educate a family”<sup>4</sup> . Studies show that women are more ‘expressive’ than men [88]. Women are motivated from the need of strong affiliation and inclined toward interpersonal goals and success in interpersonal re lationships [89,66]. Hence, they have greater awareness about others feelings than men [90], which makes them better mentors. Studies show that women in the urban area learn the way of using ICT tools and pass on the same knowledge to other family members [32]. The availability of mentorship in the family enhances the mastery experience of an in dividual because they receive training and situational support within their family, which positively influences the CSE of the individual. Along with the mastery experience and situational support, vicarious experi ences are also considered as vital for the development of SE [18]. The presence of digitally literate women in the household enhances the chances of vicarious learning for the individuals [12]. Many individuals also look within their family for role models [91]. Moreover, among the family members, children faithfully adapt themselves to the behaviour of women compared to that of men [92].

## 3.5. Marriage and Woman’s CSE

Marriage plays an important role in the life of an individual in India because societal values and beliefs are attached to it. The roles and re sponsibilities of both men and women change after the marriage, especially in terms of sharing the burden of household work and child care [1]. A married man is bestowed with the responsibility of fulfilling all the needs of his family [21], and hence, he is expected to work and earn for his family. However, married women are expected to concen trate on household chores including rearing and caring of children [93, 21]; and therefore, their profession, ambitions, and hobbies take a backseat. Considering the cooperative conflicts within the family [94], married women decide to invest more in fulfilling husband's wishes and children’s upbringing and education over professional achievement and career development, which ultimately results in skewed work-family equation [95]. Employment for women is deemed acceptable only if she chooses to work in addition to her household duties [1]. It is also evident from the fact that women’s employment in India is the lowest in the world, and it is even lower than most countries at the same income level [96].

The Technology Acceptance Model [77] and Diffusion of the Inno vation Theory [78] argue that upon perceiving technology as useful and advantageous in accomplishing a work, an individual will readily adopt the technology. ## 4. Methodology

The previous section conceptualized five hypotheses contextualizing the gender-based digital divide. Fig. 4 provides a research model depicting these five hypotheses. In this section we describe the empirical analysis undertaken to examine the hypothesized relationships.

## 4.1. Data

For this study, we use secondary data collected by the National Sample Survey Organization (NSSO) on social consumption related to education [97]. The survey was conducted countrywide as part of the 71 st round during 2014. The survey captures rich information at the individual and household levels, and its main objective is to measure the extent of use of educational infrastructure and facilities provided by the government. The survey also collected data related to digital literacy.

The survey gathered information on the educational facilities avail able at the household level for individuals [97]. The data include in formation on the detailed demographics, including age, gender, household size, maximum education attained, household expenditure, social group, household type, and individual’s place of residence. The survey data were collected from 65,926 households that constituted of 310,827 individuals, and it captured information related to computer literacy like whether an individual can operate a computer, knows how to use a word processor, can search on the web for desired information, and knows how to send and receive an email. The computer literacy related information was collected only from adults of age more than 14 years. Hence our analysis will be restricted to individuals of age greater than 14 years (n = 235,496). Among these records, there were 138 records with NAs (less than 0.001 % of the sample), and data loss due to removing the NAs was negligible. Thus, once we removed these records, we were left with 235,358 valid records. Table 4 below provides a snapshot of data available and the descriptive statistics of the data.

## 4.2. Constructs and measures

The general computer self-efficacy (GCSE) is an aggregation of taskspecific computer self-efficacy (TCSE) [13,18]. A way to measure the strength of GCSE of an individual is by aggregating different TCSE [13]. The dataset consists of four fundamental TCSE measures: ‘Operating computer’, ‘using word-processors’, ‘searching internet’, and ‘sending an email’. All the TCSEs are binary parameters with answers as numeric value one (representing yes) or zero (representing no). In this study, we use arithmetic summation of these four TCSE measures to calculate the strength of GCSE, thus representing a number of skills possessed by an individual. Thus, the calculated GCSE strength ranges from zero to four, where zero indicates that the individual does not showcase any TCSE and four indicates that the respondent has answered ‘yes’ for all the measures. The calculated strength of GCSE is used as the dependent variable for this research.

Gender is the primary independent variable. To represent the context, the other demographic independent variables used are age, marital status, social group for caste, and household size to measure the number of individuals in a household. The proportion of divorced and widowed individuals in the data was less and hence we grouped them with the unmarried. Along with these variables, we derive the propor tion of other women CSEs in the house, and consider it as another in dependent variable. The formula below is used for deriving the proportion of women CSEs in the household.

## 4.5. Post hoc analysis – caste and digital divide

We find no support for our claim that women of socially weaker group have lower CSE than others. This was contrary to our expectation and hence requires further in-depth analysis. Therefore, based on the social groups, we divide the enter dataset into two separate units: one containing the data of socially weaker group and the other containing the data of socially dominant group members. It was interesting to note that the results of this further analysis (Table 10) demonstrate that the gender-based digital divide is negative (lower) and significant among the socially dominant group, while it is positive (higher) and significant among the socially weaker group. We postulate that the result can be an outcome of reducing women employment, which demonstrates a pre cipitous decline in the past decade. The women employment rate in India has plunged from 35 percent in 2005 to 26 percent in the present scenario [61], and this drop is partly due to the country’s economic development. We argue earlier that in a patriarchal society like India, the default role of women is within the house as a homemaker, and she is not permitted to work unless the family finds no way to meet the ends. A part of the family’s social standing is based on women staying at home and performing the role of housewives. Thus, as the families get richer, they (primarily the male head of the family) prefer women to stop working and take care of the family and handle the household chores. This social scenario has become even more prominent among the so cially dominant groups [62]. Since most of the digitization in India has taken place in the last decade and technology has been adopted mainly by those for whom it is important in their occupation, we believe there might be a link between the drop in the women employment and gender-based digital divide among the socially dominant groups. How ever, the government-supported digital literacy programs are mostly directed toward the socially weaker groups, particularly women. This might have resulted in an advantageous position for these women over men from the same social group. However, when we account for the other contextual factors such as marital status and household size for socially weaker group, the overall effect becomes negative. Though, these results point toward an interesting dimension of gender-based digital divide, an in-depth analysis on this phenomenon is beyond the scope of this study.

## 5. Implications

## 5.1. Theoretical contribution and implications

Upon fullest exploitation, ICT provides a necessary platform to build a harmonious and egalitarian society' [98]. It enables social connectedness as well as enhances social capital, and it has the potential to transform societies [2,99]. However, the digital divide can pose a significant chal lenge for uniform distribution and appropriation of the benefits of ICT and the resulting societal transformation. Our study uncovers and empirically establishes the factors influencing the gender-based digital divide and to provide a theoretical explanation for the same. In this pursuit of finding factors influencing the gender-based digital divide, our study makes a few relevant contributions to theory development.

Earlier studies find contrasting results regarding the existence of the gender-based digital divide and effect of socio-demographic factors on the digital divide [11]. While these studies have been conceptualized and studied in different contextual settings. they mostly draw inspiration from appropriation theory of digital divide [55] and focus primarily on the personal factors as the primary influences of digital divide [100]. The theoretical significance of context on gender-based digital divide has rarely been given importance. As technology is conceptualized, designed, and developed in the social and cultural contexts, our study discusses about digital divide with a special emphasis on context. Our first contribution to the extant literature is a theoretical framework to contextualize the digital divide by adopting the concepts from Johns [16] and Welter [17]. That is, our results depict that contextual factors account for considerable amount of the variations in the digital divide. Though, our study does not directly account for cross country differences, it provides a viable contextual theorization that can be used as a lens for examining contextual factors influencing the gender-based digital divide in other similar emerging economies. Future studies on digital divide in any other context may account for contextual factors which help in understanding the nuances of this complex social phenomenon.

Second, we theoretically and empirically establish that the gender based digital divide is dependent on many socio-cultural contextual factors. By contextualizing gender-based digital divide, we provide theoretical explanation to the existence of contradictory results on digital divide in the extant literature. Our results illustrate the mani festation of social, institutional, and household contexts into influential factors that shape the CSE of individuals, especially women. Specifically, we show that the adverse impact of these contextual factors is more pronounced for women, thus leading to a glaring gender-based digital divide.

Third, we employ a large-scale representative sample in the Indian context for our study. Several noteworthy contributions to academia and practice have come from the analysis of past data from NSSO. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in CSE and Digital Divide liter ature, which has employed such a large representative data to contex tually study the concept of digital divide.

## 5.2. Implications for practice and policy making

Our empirical analysis using a large-scale nationwide survey data has led us to some interesting results unique to the Indian context (see Table 11 for some important implications). While agencies around the world and particularly the Government of India is employing significant efforts in bridging the digital divide between men and women, our findings can play an important role in formulating effective strategies and policies.

Marriage, being an important event in the life of an individual, brings many changes in the roles and responsibilities of both men and women. In India, socio-cultural influences and limited utility of technology, force a married woman to stay away from technology. We argue that married women are a unique category and their CSE levels would be significantly lower than other groups; and we find support for the same. Here, it is important to note that our analysis is not aimed at criticizing marriage as an institution but rather at making policymakers recognize married women as a special category. Therefore, in our study, we find that the enhancement of CSE levels in this category would require targeted efforts, which in turn would positively influence the whole family through vicarious learning. For example, testimonials from the ‘Internet Saathi’ campaign, which was initiated by Google India and Tata Trusts in May 2016 for addressing the gender-based digital disparity, especially among rural married women, indicate that it has achieved a far-reaching and holistic impact on the entire community than just the targeted women [101].

We also investigate the influence of the presence of a digitally literate individual in a household on the CSE of others. Our results indicate that the impact is significant and more pronounced for women than men, which also resonates with the popular belief that educating a woman is equivalent to educating the whole family. Hence, we strongly believe that imparting digital literacy to a married woman would have multiplicative effect and would enable bridging of the digital divide at a faster pace and at a lower cost to the system. Future studies can conduct experimental studies on intervention of imparting digital literacy to a married woman in a household and study its temporal effects on the household.

ICTs have the power to transform societies by helping governments meet developmental objectives such as better education, effective healthcare and financial inclusion. ICTs can also empower the margin alized sections of society (including women) to overcome traditional barriers, which have historically restricted them from accessing many opportunities [102,103]. However, without the right knowledge (CSE), an individual cannot appropriate the benefits that ICT can afford. Thus, to realize the goals of inclusive development and to leapfrog into a smart future with technologies like AI, IoT, and FinTech, policymakers need to invest not only in providing the infrastructure, but also need to empower its citizens, particularly women in learning and developing CSE. In order to bridge the gender-based digital divide, India can draw inspiration from other developing economies such as China and Brazil which have significantly low levels of gender gap in technology use. For instance, the Brazilian government has instituted the Meninas Digitais pro gramme to promote girl students to attend ICT based short courses to develop their CSE [104]. Similarly, rapid growth in digital payment adoption (primarily WeChat and Alipay) by users in China has provided impetus to bridging the gender-based digital divide. To achieve inclusive growth, particularly to make women a part of it, governments need to adopt such innovative ways to overcome the contextual barriers.

India can also learn draw inspiration from the developed world. While things are changing for the better, developing countries such as India have traditionally been late adopters of ICT innovations. Given the potentially inhibiting social structures in India, a robust digital infrastructure access would allow a woman to access and use ICTs from within the household without needing to access the internet from the public spaces, which are mostly restricted for her [56]. The other thing India can learn from the developed world is the emphasis given to technology training at an early stage of education. Since computers and internet play a significant role in the learning and career, most of the schools in the developed countries include the computing and use of internet as part of their early education to children. Apart from these, programs such as DigitalSmarts program in Canada [105] and Digital Education Action Plan in European Union [106] promote and encourage women participation in STEM programs. How ever, schools in India, particularly the public schools, which facilitate learning for the kids from the lower economic status, often ignore such training. In order to bridge the digital divide, particularly gender-based digital divide, these public schools need to emphasize on the computer and internet related trainings and it should be part of mandatory curric ulum in the early education.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Our study also has certain limitations. The dataset used has its own strengths and limitations. We use a large-scale secondary survey data obtained from a national survey office, and it is considered as one of the most reliable sources and a representative sample of the population. While source reliability and representativeness are the prominent strengths of the dataset, there were only limited number of variables at our disposal to measure digital divide. In this survey, the data on the ability of individuals to use computer, computer software, and the internet were collected in the binary format from the respondents. Despite this limitation, the dataset has rich data on computer usage abilities of a large number of individuals from across the country and is the best data that were available for us to evaluate the research ques tions we had posited in this study. As there is scope for better mea surement of individuals’ TCSE, a focused study on digital divide considering different spatial, social and institutional contextual vari ables is warranted and may provide a much richer explanation. We would have also liked to use richer data on social and institutional factors such as occupational status, and bank account ownership. However, the same was not available in the dataset we used.

Furthermore. we did not hypothesize about the role of spatial contextual variables such as ‘region’ or ‘place of residence’ in this study. As noted earlier, past studies have discussed the impact of ‘place of residence’ on CSE and found that urban individuals are better off than rural individuals. Our study controlled for ‘place of residence’ and results indicate the similar effect<sup>8</sup> . Given the fertile nature of the idea of gender-based digital divide in rural/urban context, future studies can actually explore it in detail with a focus on policy interventions such as digital and financial inclusion. Moreover, future studies can also look into regional differences within the country and their influence on the gender-based digital divide given the vast and diverse population located in different regions of India. We believe that both these studies could have important policy implications.

Moreover, the generalizability of the study is another limitation. We understand that the results from statistical random samples are gener alizable only to the larger population from which the samples are picked [107], which in this case is India. These variables may or may not have an influence in the other context such as African countries or North American countries. However, there are two reasons why we believe that these results can advance theory in this germane field through our study: (1) Given the similarities among some developing countries, in terms of family structure, societal norms and cultures, our study’s results can be extended to understand the differences in these countries as well. The gender-based digital divide is also similar and extremely high among these countries [6]. These results can help better policy devel opment in these countries to bridge the gap effectively. (2) This study emphasizes the importance of social and institutional contextual vari ables specific to the context of a country which will influence gender-based digital divide. Generalizability exists at this level of abstraction and we believe that our study contributes in guiding future studies to identify country specific factors to study this phenomenon. However, there will be specific special, social, and institutional contextual variables, exclusive to the context of that country which will influence the gender-based digital divide. The generalizability of this study lies at this level and future studies need to identify country-specific

factors.

The other limitation of the study is the cross-sectional nature of the data, which has become publicly available in late 2015. As the dataset is cross-sectional in nature, the causality arguments can only be inferred from the theoretical expositions. Also, the data do not capture digital developments in the recent past. Considering the pace at which tech nology is evolving, the implications of the emerging technologies (such as 5 G, artificial intelligence, Internet of Things, big data tools, and cloud computing services) and their influence on digital divide are germane topics for research. Such an advancement in ICT has led to an issue of a ‘series of digital divides’ or ‘multi-dimensional digital divide’ ([108], p34). Future research extending these issues to gender-based digital divide could be an interesting exposition.

As these technologies become ubiquitous and affordable, the mani festation of the digital-divide into different facets could also be an interesting topic of deliberation. For instance, in China, a very high percentage of villages (96 % by 2018) have affordable and high-quality, fiber optic network infrastructure thus addressing the last mile problem [109]. While countries like China, the United States, and other OECD countries are developing infrastructure in smart cities for all with fast internet connections, autonomous vehicles, smart security, and resilient urban environments, low developed countries and even developing countries such as India are still scaling up infrastructure to get everyone connected to the digital grid. Technically, though these economies are digitally included (with large mobile penetration) and working hard to keep up with the rest of the world, the affordances of their population limit them to appropriate larger benefits from the infrastructure pro vided. Thus, the disparity between them and developed countries has been increasing. Such differences make the notion of the digital divide as a nonstatic concept and a moving target to be bridged [110].

As it is the first edition of NSSO which gathered data on this crucial issue, hopefully further editions of the survey will help to analyze this phenomenon better using longitudinal data and information on these emerging technologies

## 6. Conclusion

This paper discusses a contemporary and persistent form of inequality—the gender-based digital inequality. The objective of the paper is to present a fresh perspective on the issue of disparity in the levels of CSE across the genders; hence, this study theorizes the problem considering the contextual factors. The study employs Indian micro level data for this purpose, and the results obtained show that the relationship between gender and CSE is not direct but contingent on different contextual factors such as social class, household size, and marital status.
