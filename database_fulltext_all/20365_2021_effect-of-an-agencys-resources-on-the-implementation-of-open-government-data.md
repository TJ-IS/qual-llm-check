## A B S T R A C T

Open government data (OGD) exhibits substantial political, economic, cultural, and social values that have gained considerable attention globally. Based on the investigation and analysis of the OGD practice, this study raises the research question, “Why do considerable differences exist in the degree of OGD implementation among different agencies under the same local government?” Our study takes resource-based theory as theoretical foundation to explore the factors that affect OGD implementation of constituent agencies within the same local government. A questionnaire survey is conducted to analyze the effect of factors including technical capacity. organizational awareness, organizational arrangement, and rules and regulations on OGD implementation. Re sults show that the technical capacity, organizational arrangement, and rules and regulations of government agencies have a direct positive effect on OGD implementation. Notably, rules and regulations moderate the relationship between technical capacity and OGD implementation. Besides, the matching degree of technical capacity and other organizational factors in a government agency exerts a positive influence on OGD imple mentation. Finally, our study proposes policy suggestions that emphasize the direction and focus for OGD implementation.

## 1. Introduction

Open government data (OGD) are of considerable importance to the political, economic, social, and cultural development of a country. OGD could stimulate the public to participate in government events further to improve government transparency [1]. Moreover, various social forces can be mobilized to reuse and create economic and social values from government data [2]. Previous studies show that the benefits of OGD are mainly clustered in political and social, economic, and operational and technical perspectives [3]. In recent decades, numerous countries worldwide have started to attach considerable importance to OGD, considering the potential value and benefits of OGD. Furthermore, massive resources have been invested in OGD practices of releasing the potential value of government data. In China, OGD is primarily launched in municipal government levels. Specifically, every agency is required to publish its data resources on the OGD platform. However, municipal governments merely own the right to audit the data, whereas data-holding agencies hold the specific OGD workload. This approach differs from the implementation model led by the central government or the state government in other countries. The practice of the OGD implementation in various agencies may differ in influencing factors, such as organizational structure, resources and awareness [4,5]. The rapid development of OGD has fostered an increasing number of scholars to focus on the promise of OGD. OGD capacity varies among different countries [6]. Similarly, OGD implementation in government agencies under the same local governments might differ in the influence of organizational structure, resources, and other factors. Therefore, the current study needs to explore further the factors affecting OGD implementation under the context of China.

For instance, until December 1, 2017, the Shanghai Municipal Gov ernment Data Service Network has provided 869 datasets from 40 government departments. However, as shown in Fig. 1, vast differences exist in the number of opened data among the agencies. Furthermore, our previous study measured the OGD capacity of different agencies in Shanghai, which indicated the significant differences in data quantity, data format, data maintenance, and Application Programming Interface (API) port provision [5].

OGD implementation reflects the status of the openness of govern ment data, such as the amount of opened data, data availability, data format, metadata, and data updates. Shanghai municipal government proposed the uniform requirements for the open data of every agency. Moreover, these agencies have similar obligations and motives to open data under the same external political and institutional contexts. How ever, the reasons that different agencies show considerable huge dif ferences in OGD implementation indicators, such as amount, data format, data update, data management, and API, should be explained. Therefore, we examine the two following research questions: what fac tors lead to considerable differences in OGD implementation among different government agencies under the same local government? How does the specific mechanism work?

In this study, we use the concept of open data index proposed by the Open Knowledge Foundation to define OGD implementation in different government agencies. The open data index was initially intended to control and evaluate the level of OGD implementation in specific regions or cities at the macro level, thereby reflecting the extent to which the government has implemented the government data commitments. Ac cording to the definitions and measurement indicators of open data index, our study corrected relevant indicators to reflect OGD imple mentation in a single government agency at the micro level.

The sections of this paper are arranged as follows. We introduce a brief review of the relevant literature on the evaluation and influencing factors of OGD implementation in Section 2. Section 3 presents the theoretical foundation and research framework of our study. In this section, we take the resource-based theory as a basis to propose the research framework. Based on theoretical foundation and existing research of OGD implementation, hypotheses are developed in Section 4. Research methodology is described in Section 5. We obtain the analysis results in Section 6 based on direct effects analysis, moderate effects analysis, configuration analysis, and a robustness test. Finally, the conclusion is provided in Section 7.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. OGD evaluation

The definition of OGD varies among scholars and different research fields [3,7,8]. According to the existing studies, we define OGD as fol lows: to realize their potential value, government data resources held by the government should be open to the public free of charge and in nonexclusive and machine-readable format such that anyone can obtain, reuse, and disseminate them without restrictions, except for data involving national security, business secrets, and personal privacy [5,9].

Previous studies have developed various OGD implementation evaluation frameworks from different perspectives. Lee and Kwak [10] proposed an open government maturity model to analyze the effect of transparency, interactivity, participation, and collaboration of the gov ernment on OGD implementation. Kassen [11] determined that factors such as legal basis, political context, and economic environment, affect OGD implementation. An evaluation framework from three main as pects was developed to measure OGD implementation [11]. Veljkovi´c et al. [12] proposed a benchmark model of open government from the perspective of open data, including indicators of basic dataset, openness, transparency, participation, and collaboration. Zuiderwijk and Janssen [13] created an evaluation framework to compare open data policies from the perspective of environmental background factors, policy con tent, performance indicators, and public value, showing the maturity level of OGD implementation. Donker and Loenen [14] focused on the OGD ecosystem with different stakeholders to analyze the maturity of OGD. Open data supply, open data governance, and open data user characteristics were included in the evaluation framework to elaborate on the impact of OGD implementation. Previous studies proposed the assessment frameworks from different aspects, which could improve our understanding of the OGD evaluation and the influencing factors of OGD implementation or maturity.

## 2.2. Influencing factors of OGD implementation

Numerous researchers have conducted studies on the influencing factors of OGD implementation from different perspectives. In general, these influencing factors can be roughly classified into technical, orga nizational, and policy and regulation dimensions.

First, in terms of the technical dimension, OGD is not merely to publish the data resources held by government departments on the Internet. Relevant requirements on data content, data form, and data management involved, which is a challenge for government de partments to equip with a specific level of technical capacity. Janssen et al. [3] determined that technology was a significant barrier to OGD implementation. However, Yang et al. [4] proposed that technology positively affects OGD implementation. On the other hand, data quality was a significant challenge of technical capacity. Martin et al. [15] suggested that data characteristics and metadata were critical risks of OGD implementation. Hardy and Maurushat [16] indicated that the standards of metadata could be the principal technical obstacles in the implementation of Australian OGD. Meanwhile, Conradie and Choenni [17] suggested that the manner of data storage, data acquisition, and data usage can significantly influence the government to open the data they are holding. Finally, the governments would not release their data resources to the public, given the potential risks of data security and privacy issues. These issues need to be avoided as much as possible by technical ways [18–20].

Second, the organizational dimension also exerts a significant impact on OGD implementation. Organizational awareness and culture might restrict the willingness and degree of OGD implementation. OGD require government agencies to open various types of valuable data to the public, thereby transforming government agencies from data holders to data providers [21]. Most government departments perceived data as a symbol of power and the primary source of income. Many agencies might concern that OGD could results in the loss of power and income, the increase of work burden, and the raise of the potential legal liability and personal privacy risks from the misuse of the opened data [4,11].

Thus, if government agencies fail to provide a clear picture of the value and significance of OGD with the concept and specific content, they might be reluctant to open the data they held to the public. Meanwhile, organizational culture exerts a positive influence on OGD implementa tion. Since OGD is a relatively new government activity, government departments with an open culture are more likely to accept OGD prac tices than their counterparts with a closed culture. Numerous studies indicated that cultural factors are an essential challenge in the process of OGD implementation [19,22,23]. In addition, leadership participation plays a vital role in the success of OGD implementation. To be specific, the attitude of the leader toward OGD will affect the OGD initiatives [4, 24]. If the head of an agency could pay considerable attention, the subordinates would actively participate in OGD implementation. Moreover, OGD means to break the existing organizational restrictions and sectoral interests, thereby encountering various obstacles and challenges. Therefore, a specific agency responsible for coordinating open data should be established. Furthermore, the increase of OGD workload could be a critical challenge for existing staff members; hence, governments need to assign specific staff members to be responsible for specific OGD tasks [25].

Third, OGD policies and regulations are also vital to OGD imple mentation. The long-term success and sustainability of an OGD project heavily depend on the policy and legal framework. OGD implementation needs to solve various policy and legal issues, such as data licensing and reuse, privacy protection, and data protection. In the initial stage, establishing policies, laws, and regulations of open data, identifying existing issues, and enabling policies as early as possible is crucial for OGD implementation [25]. Yang et al. [4] indicated that legislation and policies constitute the most critical perspective that needs to be first considered. The authors found that various regulations have been enacted to protect government data. Moreover, government agencies need to undergo a series of sophisticated checks to ensure that related legislations are not violated before opening datasets. Several scholars suggested that legal and regulatory issues are the central challenges encountered in OGD implementation [3,19,23,26]. Hence, policies and regulations could be regarded as the premise and basis for OGD implementation.

## 2.3. Research gap and direction

Based on the review of previous studies, we summarize the research space of our study from various aspects. The first aspect focuses on the research perspective [10,27–29], particularly the lack of a micro research perspective. Existing studies on OGD mainly concentrate on the macro level, like OGD systems and projects, whereas attention to the implementation of individual government agencies or individual cate gories is limited. Second, the literature on the influencing factors of OGD implementation lacks empirical quantitative research. Most of these factors from different frameworks and dimensions are used to theoret ically analyze the challenges and obstacles faced by OGD implementa tion without quantitative empirical tests. Third, prior investigations analyze the relationship between variables and OGD implementation; however, comprehensive studies on the interactive relationships among variables and the complex relationships of factors for OGD imple mentation is insufficient.

Therefore, with the highlight of OGD implementation at the micro level, our study constructs a research model based on the factors that influence the OGD index (OGDI) of government agencies. The effect of the internal factors of government agencies on OGD implementation and the complex relationships among various factors are analyzed by quantitative empirical research. Furthermore, we intend to enrich and extend the research perspective and scope of existing OGD studies and respond to practical needs by pointing out directions and targets for improvement for further OGD implementation.

## 3. Theoretical foundation and research framework

## 3.1. Theoretical foundation

Resource-based theory (RBT) states that the diverse resources owned by an organization differentiate its performance level and give it a competitive advantage [30]. This theory provides a theoretical under standing of how resources can be utilized to enhance outcomes, which has been widely emploved in different fields [311. RBT includes two core assumptions that are used for testing performance differences from resource heterogeneity. First, organizations in an industry may own different resources. Second, these resources are irreplaceable among industries [32]. This theory regards organizations as a collection of re sources, which provides a robust framework for uniting several dis similar resources that can be combined to generate competitive advantage [33]. The theory focuses on performance differences among firms that are perceived as the result of resource differences with different efficiency levels [30]. Although numerous resource categories are available, we adopt the typology suggested by the Grant classifica tion standard [34–36]. Grant classified resources into three groups, namely, tangible, human, and intangible resources. Tangible resources include financial resources that determine a firm’s resilience and ca pacity for investment and physical resources, which reflect the firm’s production potential. Human resources are productive services that organizational members offer to a firm in terms of skills, knowledge, and decision-making ability. Intangible resources, including technology-related components and reputation, is widely used in IS research [37–39]. We maintain the use of this typology in subsequent studies to divide organizational resources into tangible, human, and intangible resources. RBT is also widely applied in public administration studies to explore the relationship between organizational resources, attributes, and performance [40,41]. RBT could likewise provide meaningful implications for the study of public agency performance [41]. Our study aims to explore the relationship between the internal factors of agencies and OGD implementation performance, identify organizational resources that can promote the degree of OGD imple mentation, and provide guidance for improving the OGDI. The core relationship in this study is internal organizational resources and OGD implementation performance, which fits with the connotation of the RBT. Therefore, employing RBT as the theoretical framework of our study is highly appropriate to effectively respond to the raised questions.

However, this model has encountered some criticisms [42], including the static nature of these theoretical views. While in line with RBT, some researches attempt to overcome these critiques, especially resource allocation in the form of the bundling, splitting, and repacking of resources, which have become important aspects of RBT [43]. In this research, the resource allocation perspective is employed. This perspective intends to recognize the complicate relationship structure between various resources rather than the simple pairwise relationship, which provides a unique perspective to explore the interaction of mul tiple interdependent and complementary elements of an organization to generate improved performance.

## 3.2. Research framework

According to previous studies, the influencing factors of OGD implementation could be divided into four categories, namely, tech nology, organization, laws and regulations, and external environments [4,23,44]. In this study, we explore the OGD implementation of different government agencies under the same municipal government. Hence, different agencies encounter the same external environment. We like wise focus on technology, organization, and laws and regulations. Based on previous studies on RBT in IS and enterprise management, we divide the resources that influence the OGDI of different agencies into tangible, human, and intangible resources. Tangible resources include technical capacity and rules and regulations. Rules and regulations are defined a operational guidelines and implementation rules for OGD implementa tion formulated by each government agency. These guidelines and rules indicate the institutional capacity of OGD construction for a government to affect the OGD implementation performance of different government agencies. Thus, rules and regulations should be considered a type of government agency resource. Human resources mainly refer to organi zational arrangements. Finally, organizational awareness is grouped into intangible resources.

## 4. Hypothesis development

## 4.1. Technical capacity (TC)

TC is defined as the level of capability and expertise held by the government in the implementation of OGD. The relationship between TC and organization performance has been discussed in the field of in formation management research for a long time. Byrd and Turner [45] found that information technology hardware facilities and software support could enhance core competence and organizational perfor mance. Moreover, Rai et al. [46] revealed that TC affects supply chain integration and consequently improves organizational performance. Government agencies, as data providers, need to obtain sufficient TC to publish data on an OGD platform in accordance with relevant standards [3]. The originality, integrity, and format of data resources are equally important factors that affect OGD implementation [47,48]. Data format, as a major challenge for OGD, could restrict OGD implementation and the release of data [4,49]. Grimmelikhuijsen and Feeney [50] proposed that TC are the main factors affecting the in-depth advancement of OGD. Meanwhile, Klievink et al. [51] believed that the technical preparation of government agencies is a prerequisite for taking advantage of gov ernment big data. Furthermore, the privacy and information security issues of OGD must be avoided as much as possible by technical means [15]. OGD implementation entails specific requirements regarding the technical ability of government agencies, meaning that OGD could not simply involve publishing the data resources held by the government directly on a portal website. The TC of agencies directly restricts the degree of OGD implementation. Consequently, we propose the following assumption.

H1. The TC of government agency positively affects the OGDI

## 4.2. Organizational awareness (AWA)

AWA refers to the perception of government agencies of open data and the value of OGD. Although OGD is not entirely new concepts, it remains difficult tasks for the public and organizations who are unfa miliar with the idea and lack understanding of specific contents [52]. Numerous agencies are confused about the requirements and intrinsic value of OGD, which could seriously affect OGD implementation [16, 53]. OGD require government agencies to publish valuable data to the public, thereby transforming from data holders to data providers [21]. However, data is perceived as a symbol of power and the primary source of income for government agencies. Many agencies are concerned that OGD may cause power and income losses, additional work burden, as well as potential legal liability and personal privacy risks from the misuse of the opened data [4,11]. McDermott [54] proposed that open data awareness is crucial to the construction of an open government and realization of goals, such as transparency, participation, and collabora tion. Hardy and Maurushat [16] indicated that government departments fail to understand open data and provide a supportive organizational culture that could seriously affect OGD implementation. If government departments fail to observe the positive effect of open data. OGD implementation would stagnate or cease. Therefore, awareness plays a crucial role in OGD implementation [17]. Consequently, we develop the following hypothesis:

H2. AWA is positively associated with the OGDI.

## 4.3. Organizational arrangement (OA)

OA refers to leadership participation and staffing arrangements in OGD as well as support from organizations represented by such orga nization arrangements, especially from senior leaders. OGD imple mentation inevitably generates changes in organizational systems, culture, and technology and affects stakeholders inside and outside the government. Under this context, focused, reliable, and sustainable leadership arrangements are significant to help government de partments overcome various forms of resistance. These arrangements also encourage actors to take timely and effective measures to achieve the goals and potential value of open data [50]. Leadership participation plays a vital role in successful OGD implementation [55,56]. OGD break existing organization status and encounter numerous obstacles and challenges. Therefore, a specific agency responsible for the coordination of open data is needed. Furthermore, the enormous OGD workload is a vital challenge for existing staff members. Hence, certain staff members should be assigned to specific OGD tasks. Lee and Kwak [10] indicated that the provision of professional staff and sufficient staffing could ensure the smooth implementation of OGD. Therefore, we present the following hypothesis:

## H3. OA has a significant positive effect on the OGDI.

## 4.4. Rules and regulations (RR)

The performance of bureaucratic organizations is primarily bound by the institution. Furthermore, institutional ability determines the di mensions of organizational performance and the interests of bureau cratic organizations [57–59]. Kassel [60] believed that organizational behavior in strict accordance with institutional rules could effectively improve organizational performance. Zhang and Chen [56] indicated that institutional capacity exerts a significant effect on government agencies’ information disclosure performance, meaning that govern ment agencies should focus on constructing relevant institutions and regulations. A substantial number of empirical studies show that the institutional capacity of government departments is closely related to the performance of e-government [61,62]. Legal basis is key to the realization of government projects. Moreover, the analysis of laws and regulations related to the overall implementation of e-government projects can help evaluate the strategic direction and overall layout of this aspect [63]. Yang et al. [4] proposed that laws and policies are the primary considerations of government agencies to open data. Safarov et al. [64] argued that legal policies and institutional regulations are essential in promoting OGD implementation and reducing barriers. Moreover, Janssen et al. [3] pointed out that institutional barriers would encourage government departments to refuse changes and become un willing to risk opening government data resources and bear potential consequences. We believe that RR fundamentally provides the basis for OGD implementation. Therefore, we present the following hypothesis:

H4. RR exerts a positive effect on the OGDI of government agencies

## 4.5. Resource allocation perspective

The resource allocation perspective assumes that organizational el ements are interrelated. This perspective aims to find an ideal “gestalt” or resource allocation and their relationship structure rather than merely determining the pairwise relationship of variables with the contingency method [65,66]. The resource allocation method provides a unique analysis model for the interaction of multiple interdependent and complementary elements of a company to demonstrate improved performance [67]. Previous studies indicated that TC is an essential condition for the successful implementation of a government informa tion project and the improvement of the efficiency and effectiveness of government activities through information systems [68–70]. Several researchers proposed that IT investments do not necessarily improve organizational performance and will significantly take effect only when combined with organizational processes and characteristics [71]. Simi larly, many scholars determined that organizational and business factors could moderate the relationship between IT and organizational perfor mance [72]. Martin and Begany [23] found that technology could pro mote organizational changes, while OA, organizational institution, and technology play interactions role in improving the performance of OGD implementation. Wilson and Linders [73] emphasized the role of pol icies, organizational culture, and organizational readiness in OGD. RBT states that the competitive advantage and performance of an organiza tion are determined by its resources and the combination and configu ration of various resources [65]. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H5a. AWA moderates the relationship between TC and the OGDI.

H5b. OA moderates the relationship between TC and the OGDI.

## H5c. RR moderate the relationship between TC and the OGDI.

The resource allocation viewpoint combines various factors and this notion provides an effective means for explaining complex multidi mensional phenomena, which have been widely applied in operation management and performance management research [74]. Liu et al. [75] asserted that the resource allocation perspective is practical for understanding the effect of allocation of resources and capabilities, like supply chain integration capability and TC, on improving organizational performance. The key impact of organizational performance depends on supply chain integration or technical capability and the matching degree or calibration consistency between these two independent variables. Finally, the matching degree of supply chain integration ability, tech nical capability, and organizational performance is analyzed. This perspective provides a unique model for analyzing interdependence, interaction, and matching relationships among different organizational performance factors [67]. We believe that the TC of government agencies need to match ideal resource-related equipment to improve the performance of open data in the short term when the TC of government agencies is limited. That is, the interaction between the two factors might affect OGD implementation. Moreover, the matching degree be tween the two variables might have an essential relationship with OGD implementation. Hence, the following hypothesis is developed:

H6. The closer the organizational factors (AWA, OA, and RR) to the ideal level matching existing TC, the higher the agencies’ OGDI.

The selection of the sample population and the data collection method considerably affected the accuracy of the sample data. The ideal respondents are the personnel engaged in informatization related work within the department, such as staff from the information technology departments and comprehensive departments, which could ensure the accurate and comprehensive understanding of the current status of OGD implementation by respondents. However, restricted by the closed characteristics of government departments and limited resources and channels of this study, not all the respondents in this research are ideal. Our study sets conditions for the respondents that are not ideal re spondents to ensure the quality of the questionnaire data. First, the re spondents should be a public servant of a Shanghai government agency. Second, the respondents should have been working in the agency for at least three years to guarantee a comprehensive understanding of the technical capacity and organization conditions of the agency. With the help of a Shanghai municipal government consulting project, we ob tained an opportunity to contact staff members in charge of the infor matization of different agencies. Therefore, we combined purposive sampling and snowball sampling to collect data. From June 1 to August 12, 2018, a total of 346 questionnaires were distributed, and 264 questionnaires were returned. After checking the returned question naires, we eliminated the samples that failed to satisfy the second criteria. Furthermore, questionnaires that were obviously inconsistent were also removed. Hence, after removing 27 unqualified question naires, 237 valid questionnaires were finally obtained. The effective recovery rate was 68.50 %.

Table 2 summarizes the descriptive characteristics of the re spondents. The respondents were mostly between the ages of 20 and 40 years with the necessary understanding of OGD. Most of the respondents have been working for 3–10 years, thereby ensuring that they under stood the overall status of their agency. Moreover, grassroots employees accounted for 54 % of the sample, middle-level officers accounted for 37.1 %, and a cadre of division-level officers accounted for only 8.9 %, which is roughly consistent with actual situations. The respondents came from 29 government agencies from the Shanghai municipal gov ernment. According to the descriptive result, 31 government agencies have released data resources on the Shanghai OGD platform, 29 of which were included in this study, thereby accounting for 94 % of the total number of OGD agencies in the Shanghai municipal government. Therefore, we believe that the sample in this study is complete and representative, and data quality is guaranteed.

Our study used the method proposed by Armstrong and Overton [79] to test the non-response bias of our research data. Moreover, we per formed a variance analysis on the first 49 and last 50 questionnaires during the questionnaire recovery process to test whether significant differences existed in certain variables from different groups. We con ducted variance analysis on the gender, age, working years, and employment level of the two respondent groups. Table 3 shows the results. The findings revealed that the significant levels were higher than 0.05, which means that no significant differences were found between the two groups. ## 6.5. Analysis

OGD implementation, as a complex issue, is affected by various factors. The OGDI of a government agency is the result of the combi nation of multiple resources held by the agency. First, as the prerequisite and necessary condition for OGD implementation, the TC of a govern ment agency plays a vital role in OGD and OGDI performance. This study confirms this conclusion by verifying H1. Research has consistently focused on technical factors. Technical challenges are the primary hin drances encountered by government agencies in the process of OGD implementation. OGD provides requirements on data quality, data format, data security, and privacy, which differ from previous govern ment information disclosure activities. OGD sets requirements on the technical indicators of the data, such as the originality and integrity of the data are essential indicators of open data maturity. Moreover, open data must be machine-readable, which means that data could be pre sented in a nonexclusive format [47,85]. Yang et al. [4] indicated that data format conversion is a huge challenge for most government de partments. Furthermore, OGD privacy issues and data security need to be avoided as much as possible by technical means [15]. After enforcing the Cyber Security Law, increasing attention has been paid to data se curity issues. Simultaneously, the lack of data security prevention ca pabilities of TC results in the hesitation of numerous government departments, which could evade OGD implementation. Government agencies could conduct OGD implementation and address potential is sues they encountered only when they have equipped with sufficient technical capacity. The TC of a government agency restricts the depth and breadth of OGD practices and the development of OGDI of the agency.

H2 is not supported in this study. Hence, a government agency’s AWA of OGD fails to influence its OGDI, which is inconsistent with previous research results. If government agencies fail to understand the connotation and specific content of OGD and the value of OGD, they might be unwilling to open data. According to the survey statistics, the average value of the four measurement items of AWA is 3.14 (ranging from 1 to 5). This result indicates that government departments gener ally have a low awareness of OGD, showing an incomplete under standing of concept and nature. The practice of OGD in China is relatively new for many government departments. Numerous govern ment agencies fail to distinguish the difference between OGD, govern ment information disclosure, and government informatization. Most agencies lack the cognition of the reason for opening government data to the public. Moreover, based on a simple interview, we find that pressure from the Shanghai municipal government forces government agencies to open data, whereas they have no knowledge on the significance and value of OGD. Such agencies merely open their data resources me chanically following the requirements of the Shanghai municipal gov ernment. Therefore, the passive effect of AWA on OGD implementation is not verified in this study. However, the critical role of AWA in OGD implementation can not be ignored. Government departments should continuously focus on the improvement of the understanding of OGD and initiative for OGD.

OA has a significant effect on the OGD implementation and OGDI of a government agency, which is consistent with existing findings on OGD. OA refers to the participation of an agency leader and staff allocation in the practice of OGD, which reflects the degree of organizational support, particularly from senior leaders, represented by such an arrangement. Many scholars believe that OA, especially leadership involvement, is a vital factor that affects organizational performance [55,56]. In the Chinese administrative system, department leaders, or “first leaders,” have absolute authority in organizations; thus, leaders’ attention and promotion could send a signal of obedience to departments, which in turn will pay attention and invest resources to carry out OGD. OGD entails breaking the current organization status, which involves the vital interests of a government agency and various challenges. Therefore, a specific agency or working group should be responsible for coordinating different agencies. The 2016 Annual Work Plan of Shanghai Government Data Resources Sharing and Opening clearly states that the following elements are necessary to ensure the standardization and normalization of the sharing and opening of government data resources, including optimizing working mechanisms, strengthening organizational guaran tees, optimizing the promotion mechanisms of comprehensive de partments responsible for coordination and technology departments responsible for implementation, and clarifying responsible agencies and personnel. These organizational and mechanism arrangements could significantly promote OGD implementation. Lee and Kwak [10] emphasized that professional staff and sufficient workforce allocation should be assigned to ensure successful OGD implementation. Our investigation reveals that OGD is a heavy workload for most government departments restricted by staffing issues. Thus, we believe that the attention and participation of organization leaders, the establishment of specific agencies or working groups, and staff allocation could signifi cantly contribute to OGD implementation.

RR plays a vital role in OGD implementation. Yang et al. [4] confirmed that laws and policies are the primary consideration of the OGD implementation of government agencies. Moreover, a government agency should ensure that OGD conforms to existing RR. The long-term success and sustainability of OGD projects heavily depend on the policy and legal framework. Thus, policies and regulations related to core OGD issues should be formulated and established, and potential challenges should be identified [4]. Regulations relevant to OGD ensure that gov ernment agencies follow legal procedures. These regulations can help solve the problem of government agencies’ unwillingness to open necessary data because of the concerns about potential accountability regarding data misuse, personal privacy, and other issues [3]. According to actual conditions and clarification of regulations in terms of OGD standards and scope, the formulation of a government agency’s imple mentation rules on OGD is conducive to promoting further OGD implementation and development.

Significant standardized regression coefficients for the interaction terms between RR and TC $( \beta = - 0 . 0 8 3 , \rho < 0 . 0 5 )$ indicate that RR could moderate the relationship between TC and the OGDI. Therefore, under existing TC, highly standardized and detailed RR related to OGD that are formulated by government agencies can reduce the effect of TC on the OGDI, which is inconsistent with previous research findings. Wilson and Linders [73] proposed that governments should emphasize the impor tant role of policies, organizational culture, and organizational readiness in OGD rather than technological capabilities. The World Bank [25] also indicated that policies and regulations related to core OGD issues should be formulated to identify potential issues. Evidently, the agencies in our study have certain TC to conduct OGD and OGD practices, which have been established without specific RR. When a government agency for mulates OGD RR and specifies the scope, data format, data management, data port, and other issues, the current technical ability of the agency might be challenged. Furthermore, the quantity of opened data and enthusiasm of the government agency could be affected for a certain period of time. However, the continuous improvement of TC, in the long run, promotes the formulation of detailed and complete OGD regula tions that can guide OGD implementation. Such RR can also enable a government agency to provide qualified datasets with specific regula tions, thereby improving the OGDI of a government agency.

The configuration analysis results show that the focus of adjustment and improvement varies with the TC level of government agencies in the OGD process. Regarding an agency with a high TC level, the closer the OA to the ideal degree, the higher the OGD implementation and OGDI performance. Therefore, agencies should concentrate on the adjustment of OA to further promote OGD implementation and their OGDI. By contrast, as for an agency with a low TC level, the closer the RR to the ideal degree, the higher the OGD implementation and OGDI perfor mance. That is, optimization adjustments to regulations and detailed OGD specifications and implementation rules that are suitable for an agency should be formulated. Such endeavors can promote OGD implementation effectively and the OGDI of a government agency. Government departments with medium TC can improve OGD imple mentation by optimizing and adjusting OA and RR close to the ideal levels.

## 7. Conclusions

OGD is valued globally, and substantial resources have been invested in OGD practices to release the potential value of government data re sources. China is one of the countries that actively promotes the opening of data resources held by the government to the public. Thus, our study explores the influential factors that affect OGD implementation from the micro perspective of a government agency. This study is significant theoretically and in practice for OGD implementation in China and other countries.

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

First, this study explores OGD implementation in single government agencies from the micro perspective. This work expands the breadth of existing research and enriches content on OGD, which are significant theoretical implications. Previous studies mainly focus on the macro level OGD platforms and OGD projects but rarely explore the OGD sit uation from the perspective of a government agency at the micro level. Based on the view of a single government agency, our study explores the differences in OGD implementation in various government agencies by analyzing the different factors that affect the OGDI of diverse agencies within the same administrative unit. Our study extends the OGD liter ature from the macro level to the micro level, which could effectively supplement the current research scope.

Second, we take RBT as a basis to study OGD implementation, which could broaden the scope of RBT application to a certain extent. RBT is widely applied in supply chain management, performance management, and strategy management [41,86,87]. In the study of OGD, we believe that the resources held by a government agency affect its implementa tion of OGD. Therefore, we use RBT in OGD research from the perspective of TC, RR, AWA, and OA to explore the impact of these factors on the degree of OGD implementation. Furthermore, this work organically combines RBT with OGD research.

Third, we integrate the development of RBT with the practice of OGD and tentatively explore the theory. In this theory, the resources held by an organization are static and independent of one another, which is a point criticized by other scholars [73]. Following previous studies on RBT, we combine RBT with dynamic resource theory and resource allocation theory to explore the interaction and matching degree be tween the different resources and OGD implementation of a government agency. The relationships between TC, AWA, RR, OA, and OGDI are explored along with the interaction and matching degree between TC and the other factors. Furthermore, we explore RBT in OGD research, which demonstrates theoretical significance.

## 7.2. Practical implications

Our findings provide valuable references for OGD implementation to a certain extent. Government agencies should exert effort to promote OGD implementation based on the following aspects. First, agencies need to improve their TC, specifically the data security and risk pre vention ability. Second, they should enhance the awareness and enthusiasm of government leaders and related staff on OGD by combining passive infusion and active attraction. Third, agencies should promote the implementation of a top-down chief data officer system to establish an OGD promotion and management system. Fourth, high level government officers should conduct overall planning on OGD and relevant laws and regulations, particularly on data security and personal privacy issues. Each government agency should focus on formulating OGD implementation rules. Fifth, the government should highlight different priorities for agencies with varying TC levels con cerning OGD implementation. Agencies with low TC should improve OGD RR, organize internal resources, specify the scope of OGD, and standardize internal OGD process and management systems. Mean while, agencies with high TC should emphasize OA. On the one hand, this arrangement could strengthen leaders’ awareness of OGD. On the other hand, such an arrangement could provide sufficient high-quality personnel. However, in the long run, technical ability is vital to OGD implementation. A government agency needs to focus on improving it technical ability by employing personnel with professional knowledge and relevant work experience and strengthening the training of relevant personnel.

## 7.3. Limitations and future studies

This study poses several limitations. First, given the restriction on external resources and the research object, we obtain only 237 samples, which is relatively small. We intend to obtain a large sample from various channels in future studies. Second, the sample fails to consist purely of professional staff engaged in informatization. Therefore, we aim to obtain information from professional personnel in succeeding studies to increase the accuracy of the data.Third, the research model mainly focuses on supply-side factors and does not consider demandside factors. We intent to construct a comprehensive model to better understand the implementation of OGD in future. Finally, the data are obtained by questionnaires. In future research, interview methods can be employed to explore the potential relationships of variables.
