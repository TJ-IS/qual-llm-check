## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Knowledge management strategy Portfolio perspective Diversity Complementarity and substitutability Supermodularity test

## A B S T R A C T

To investigate the mutual effects of knowledge management (KM) strategies on firm performance, we categorize the portfolios of KM strategies into four patterns: unrelated (scope- and type-based), related, extended, and high diversity portfolios. An inverted U-shaped relationship of KM diversity and firm performance is proposed across the portfolios based on the underlying cost–benefit tradeoffs. Supermodularity test results indicate that strategies of unrelated diversity portfolios show substitutable effects, whereas strategies of related diversity portfolios have complementary effects on firm performance. While strategies of extended diversity portfolios exhibit mixed re sults with complementary and substitutable effects, strategies of high diversity portfolio exhibit a substitutable effect. Our findings indicate that multiple KM strategies, as a firm’s portfolio, mutually generate complemen tarities or cause substitutabilities in improving firm performance. We also discuss practical implications fo managerial decisions on successful KM.

## 1. Introduction

Integrating distributed organizational knowledge into actionable, competitive, and innovative strategies is an essential aspect of man agement. According to a survey of executives, most believe that suc cessful knowledge management (KM) improves organizational productivity by 30 percent or more [1]. Many organizations make a tremendous effort to realize the benefits of KM by effectively utilizing various knowledge types [2,3] and sources [4,5]. An appropriate KM strategy enables an organization to efficiently create, acquire, and access knowledge by reducing search and transfer costs, resulting in higher organizational performance [6]. Thus, KM literature has devoted sub stantial attention to analyzing the impact of KM strategies [7].

However, the real challenge for the successful implementation of KM strategies lies in the diversity of knowledge scopes and types. Knowledge sources are located both inside and outside firms [8], and there are different types of sourced knowledge, namely, codified or personalized knowledge [9]. Prior studies have examined the effects of specific KM initiatives and found equivocal results. Several studies suggest that internally codified knowledge initiatives generate a competitive advantage [2,10], while other studies show a negative effect of similar initiatives on firm performance [11]. Such conflicting results may be due to the firms’ adoption of multiple KM strategies that interact with one another and affect firm performance. Therefore, these interactive effects must be investigated rather than the isolated effects of individual strategies.

Recent research has adopted a portfolio perspective by drawing attention to a firm’s varying strategies according to KM scope (internal vs. external) [4] or type (codified vs. personalized) [3]. KM scope de scribes whether organizational knowledge originates from within or outside the firm [4,5]: leveraging either internal knowledge sources, such as intra-firm training and development programs, discussion fo rums and task-oriented communities [12], and internal R&D projects, or external knowledge sources like supplier–buyer partnerships, consulting and outsourcing services, and interactions with and feedback from customers [13]. KM type refers to the degree to which organizational knowledge is disseminated through codification or personalization [2]: either codifying knowledge via computer-aided documentation processes, intranet-based portal systems, and user-generated content management [14], or personalizing knowledge via mentoring programs, communities of practice, and dialogic communication of interpersonal relationships with co-workers. Given the limited organizational re sources and the different challenges of implementing specific strategies, firms must weigh the consequences of individual KM strategies in a portfolio with multiple strategies [15].

Although prior research has examined the performance effects of different KM strategies, a consensus on which portfolios enhance or impair firm performance has not been reached. Several studies have shown that diversified KM strategies hinder firm performance [16], whereas others have suggested that the same strategies improve firm performance [17]. Therefore, this study aims to develop theoretical and empirical insights about the portfolio effects of multiple KM strategies on firm performance. By separating the KM strategic portfolios into four categories (i.e., unrelated, related (scope- and type-based), extended, and high diversity portfolios), we draw upon the theory of complementarities to propose the effects of different portfolios on firm performance. We propose an inverted U-shaped relationship between the portfolios of KM strategies and firm performance based on the underlying cost–benefit tradeoffs. Based on the empirical analyses of data obtained from surveys and financial statements of 152 firms, we discuss the key findings, theoretical contributions, and practical implications of this study.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Knowledge and knowledge management

Since knowledge has been considered as a multifaceted concept with multilayered meanings [18], many studies have defined knowledge and KM differently. A widely accepted definition of knowledge is the set of justified beliefs to enhance an entity’s capacity for effective action [19], while KM is defined as activities and processes designed to manage knowledge in an organization [20]. Although some researchers focused on object characteristics of knowledge, the definition of KM in this study focuses on the process-oriented perspective of knowledge because knowledge is not primarily about facts but more about context-specific characteristics. As the heart of successful KM, many studies have emphasized the role of KM influencing factors and processes. KM influencing factors can be defined as organizational mechanisms to facilitate knowledge consistently, consisting of technical, structural, and cultural components [21]. KM processes refer to the interconnected and intertwined set of activities aimed at managing knowledge effectively [19]. The processes have been divided into several sub-processes, such as creating, storing, sharing, and using.

Although KM influencing factors and processes are essential for a firm’s capability to manage knowledge effectively, it is still unclear how to strategically employ them. Adopting appropriate factors and imple menting processes for managing knowledge are very costly and fragile. KM strategies are necessary for facilitating these factors and processes; they determine how to adopt and utilize KM influencing factors and processes. Therefore, it is crucial to find how a firm adopts appropriate KM strategies to use KM influencing factors and processes effectively. In this study, a KM strategy is defined as a logical pattern regarding firms decisions on KM scope and type [22].

## 2.2. Approaches towards knowledge management strategies

Firms facilitate the flow of internal knowledge by developing and sharing it within their organizational boundaries [11]. However, orga nizational knowledge is also externally circulated and captured beyond these boundaries [5,23]. With information technologies, codifying knowledge enables corporate members to reuse captured and docu mented knowledge and improve organizational efficiencies [24]. For example, KM systems consisting of KM tools and KM practices are widely employed to connect people with reusable codified knowledge. KM tools and KM practices facilitate conversation among organizational mem bers, leading to improved performance [14]. According to the person alization approach, the socialization of organizational knowledge can be achieved through person-to-person contacts [3]. These two dimensions of KM strategies mutually interact with each other [22]. For instance, codifying externally circulated knowledge (external person-to-system) requires strategic activities and generates competitive advantages that differ from the activities and advantages of codifying internally regu lated knowledge (internal person-to-system), personalizing internally regulated knowledge (internal person-to-person), and personalizing externally circulated knowledge (external person-to-person).

The distinct characteristics and advantages of each KM strategy depend on the composition of KM scope and type that identify four different KM strategies, as follows: internal knowledge codification strategy (IKCS), internal knowledge personalization strategy (IKPS), external knowledge codification strategy (EKCS), and external knowledge personali zation strategy (EKPS). Fig. 1 depicts the four KM strategies based on the dimensions of KM scope and type.

## 2.3. Diversification of knowledge management portfolios

Portfolio theory provides a lens to view how firms balance different assets, tactics, or strategies and recognize their specific benefits, costs, and risks [25]. We define a KM portfolio as an intentional combination of individual KM strategies to capture the benefits of multiple knowledge scopes and types. A central question in portfolio design is the level of diversi fication across the strategies [26]. Portfolio diversification allows firms to harness the cumulative benefits of multiple KM strategies.

However, the implementation of diversification causes additional costs [27]. Drawing on the work of Hashai [28], we identify two specific costs in assessing the effectiveness of various portfolios: adjustment and coordination costs. Adjustment costs refer to the resources and time required to integrate disparate strategies within a portfolio. For example, a focus on integrating internal and external KM scopes requires investments in large-scale and new routines and processes for blending the two KM scopes [29]. Integrating codified and personalized KM types can cause necessary adjustments in specificity, frequency, and con textuality [30]. Greater diversification demands more adjustments across multiple KM strategies. Hashai [28] defines coordination costs as those arising from the need to create and maintain effective communi cation, information processing, and decision-making mechanisms across different KM strategies. Coordination costs are likely to decrease when firms rely on a specific KM strategy but are likely to increase as they seek to integrate different KM types into their portfolios of KM strategies [4]. ## 2.4. Complementarities and substitutabilities in knowledge management portfolios

According to Milgrom and Roberts [31], complementarity between activities can exist if and only if increasing the level of any one activity leads to a higher marginal return from increasing the level of the other activity. The concept denotes a joint implementation of several activities which results in enhanced outcomes [32]. In contrast, substitutability between activities exists if and only if increasing the level of any one activity decreases the marginal return from increasing the level of the other activity.

We apply the concepts of complementarity and substitutability to evaluating the effectiveness of the different KM portfolios. Comple mentary KM strategies create “super-additive” value synergies that are not captured by individual KM strategies [33]. Alternatively, KM stra tegies substitute each other if adopting a KM strategy lowers the mar ginal payoff of other strategies. Complementarity and substitutability are conceptualized in terms of a payoff function. If an interaction occurs, its outcomes must be observed in super-additive value or sub-additive costs. Several studies have employed complementarity or substitutability tests to assess the impact of KM strategy on firm performance [10,35]. For example, Kyriakopoulos and de Ruyter [36] investigated the complementarity of person-oriented strategy with internal-oriented strategy using their interaction effects. Cassiman and Veugelers [37] examined complementarity between internal-oriented strategy and external-oriented strategy in knowledge acquisition using super modularity functions. However, most prior studies have assessed complementarity or substitutability with less than three KM strategies. They have simply estimated pair-wise interaction effects between two strategies, ignoring the interaction among more than three strategies. Therefore, testing complementarity or substitutability with all possible KM strategy portfolios using supermodularity functions can fully reflect a true complementary relationship [38].

## 3. Development of hypotheses

We link the concept of KM diversity with complementarities and substitutabilities to propose the portfolio effects of KM strategies on firm performance [17]. Diversity has attracted a lot of attention from various management disciplines, including strategic management and finance. Many studies have examined diversity benefits and have empirically tested whether increases in diversity are related to gains in firm per formance [39]. A widely shared view of the relationship between di versity and firm performance is the curvilinear relationship (particularly the inverted U-shaped relationship) [40,41]. This view insists diversity up to a certain level increases firm performance due to benefits of di versity, including cross-utilization and exploitation of resources, oper ational capabilities, and reputation. However, as a firm’s diversity goes further, the effects of diversity on performance become negative because the drawbacks of diversity such as coordination and adjustment cost outweigh its benefits [42].

Similarly, we assume the curvilinear relationship between KM di versity and firm performance and adopt the inverted U-shaped rela tionship. KM diversity introduced into a firm as a result of using different KM strategies enables the firm to possess new knowledge with its novel insight, avoid the familiarity trap, and generate novel combinations between knowledge, providing a basis for promoting firm performance [43]. However, the benefits of KM diversity hold only up to a certain point. The very KM diversity that makes it attractive for enhancing firm performance creates problems as the number of KM strategies integrated and assimilated increases. Excessive KM diversity creates cognitive barriers that make it more difficult for organizational members to un derstand each other [44]. Thus, it increasingly requires adjustment and coordination costs among different KM strategies, generating harmful consequences.

Fig. 2 synthesizes the KM diversity with the benefits and costs, thus yielding an inverted U-shaped relationship between KM diversity and firm performance. The inverted U-shaped trajectory of firm performance across KM portfolio patterns can be arranged along with KM diversity in multiple strategies combined and interacting in a firm’s portfolio. Our portfolio perspective proposes that the inverted U-shaped relationship might be explained by inter-strategy complementarities and sub stitutabilities, as hypothesized based on the underlying cost–benefit tradeoffs for firm performance.

## 3.1. Substitutabilities in unrelated diversity portfolios

An unrelated diversity portfolio incorporates the high levels of two KM strategies that share any commonality in neither KM scope nor type. This portfolio includes either the high levels of IKCS and EKPS or the high levels of IKPS and EKCS, as explained in Table 1. These pairs (high IKCS and high EKPS; high IKPS and high EKCS) are unrelated in KM scope and type dimensions. The unrelated diversity portfolio generates and integrates the knowledge of different KM scopes or types. Adopting KM strategies that share neither KM scope nor type increases adjustment and coordination costs [28,45]. This portfolio reduces a firm’s search costs to remain updated about broad multiple knowledge sources, both internally and externally, by constraining its search scope within limited knowledge sources [46].

Adopting two KM strategies that focus on different dimensions across KM scope and type hinders a firm from achieving economies of scope. For example, IKCS provides decontextualized knowledge and encoded routines that cause ambiguity [47] and outgoing knowledge spillovers, thus eroding a firm’s performance advantage [48]. This problem may be exacerbated with EKPS, which sources tacit knowledge from external personnel (e.g., experts or consultants) because such personalized knowledge is tightly embedded in external individuals [2,23]. Under standing and interpreting knowledge from external sources may lead to frequent misapplications. This problem is also exacerbated when a firm lacks expertise in a particular domain that requires decontextualized and encoded knowledge [29]. Because external codified knowledge sourced through EKCS is usually not firm-specific, internal experts are reluctant to consider such knowledge as a core knowledge base. Although firms spend time and effort to source external codified knowledge, such knowledge is too general to be useful in generating a firm-specific competitive advantage [49]. Thus, IKPS contributes less to improving firm performance when it interplays with EKCS (e.g., Internet-based learning about best practices and organizational capa bilities from external sources) [50]. In all, knowledge benefits are low due to the limited potential of scope economies, whereas adjustment costs are relatively high in unrelated diversity portfolios. Therefore, the high levels of KM strategies across KM scope and type cause sub stitutabilities in enhancing firm performance within unrelated diversity portfolios.

H1. KM strategies in unrelated diversity portfolios exhibit substitut able effects on firm performance.

## 3.2. Complementarities in related diversity portfolios

Related diversity portfolios contain the high levels of two KM stra tegies that share a commonality in either KM type or scope. As described in Table 1, a scope-based-related diversity portfolio includes a pair of two strategies—i.e., the high levels of IKCS and IKPS (internal leverages) or the high levels of EKCS and EKPS (external leverages)—which are related to each other regarding the KM scope only. On the other hand, a type-based-related diversity portfolio shares a commonality in the KM type by adopting the high levels of IKCS and EKCS (both are systemoriented) or the high levels of IKPS and EKPS (both are humanoriented). The two strategies in each pair are related to each other in terms of the KM type only.

The value of combining system-oriented strategies with personoriented strategies is higher than that of using the strategies in isola tion, regardless of whether these strategies internally or externally source knowledge in the scope-based-related diversity portfolios. Spe cifically, IKPS provides new meanings, interpretations, or linkages among existing internal codified knowledge (IKCS) based on firmspecific skills and experiences of internal personnel [51]. Furthermore, the erosion of internal personalized knowledge (IKPS) can be prevented by capturing and sharing the internal knowledge from information systems and applying it within a firm (IKCS). Besides, EKPS enables a firm to remain open to multiple external viewpoints [36] so that the firm can interpret, understand, and utilize the codified external knowledge (EKCS) in a diverse and creative way. Such interplays between the KM strategies of scope-based-related diversity portfolios provide the means to develop new knowledge and capabilities [52] at a lower cost of knowledge adjustment and coordination [42]. Therefore, high levels of KM strategies within the same scope dimension generate complemen tary effects on firm performance.

H2A. KM strategies in scope-based related diversity portfolios exhibit complementary effects on firm performance.

In type-based related diversity portfolios, internal and external knowledge sources complement each other regardless of whether they interact with the system- or human-oriented ways [53]. For example, a complementarity is generated by implementing IKCS and EKCS together. A firm assimilates and applies external and internal knowledge agilely with both IKCS and EKCS because the two strategies provide knowledge in the same way as system-oriented accumulation [10]. Such a dimensional commonality in the KM type also reduces knowledge adjustment and coordination costs. Likewise, a firm can create new, unique, and personalized knowledge with internal sources (IKPS) when the knowledge is combined with external personalized knowledge (EKPS) [54]. This can happen because EKPS encourages internal mem bers to perceive their internal knowledge in a different and creative way and widens a firm’s breadth of KM scope to external sources [55]. Therefore, high levels of KM strategies in type-based related diversity portfolios generate complementarities in improving firm performance.

H2B. KM strategies in type-based related diversity portfolios exhibit complementary effects on firm performance.

## 3.3. Complementarities in extended diversity portfolios

Extended diversity portfolios include the high levels of three KM strategies (high IKCS, high IKPS, and high EKCS; high IKCS, high IKPS, and high EKPS; high IKCS, high EKCS, and high EKPS; high IKPS, high EKCS, and high EKPS), as defined in Table 1. The three KM strategies in each extended diversity portfolio share commonalities in both KM scope and type, thus extending the scope-based commonality into the typebased commonality or vice versa. The extended commonality of an extended diversity portfolio maintains knowledge benefits with flexi bility and adaptability, which are enough to address business un certainties and reduce knowledge adjustment and coordination costs owing to the following reasons.

Extended diversity portfolios provide a mixed competitive advan tage, a hybrid of advantages from related and unrelated diversity port folios. While strategies in related diversity portfolios share a common dimension of either KM type or KM scope, strategies in unrelated di versity portfolios do not share commonalities. As a result, a firm’s effi cient learning processes are leveraged through its combined exploitation and exploration, even though its knowledge adjustment and coordina tion costs increase slightly. Compared with the high diversity portfolio (adopting the high levels of all the four strategies across KM scope and type), extended diversity portfolios accumulate firm-specific knowledge from more cohesive knowledge sources, similar to each other regarding the KM scope and type. Thus, knowledge adjustment and coordination costs are lower for an extended diversity portfolio than for a high di versity portfolio. The increased adjustment and coordination costs for extended diversity portfolios may not be too acute to cancel out the increased knowledge benefits [28]. Therefore, high levels of the three KM strategies across the KM dimensions generate complementarity in improving firm performance.

H3. KM strategies in extended diversity portfolios exhibit comple mentary effects on firm performance.

## 3.4. Substitutability in high diversity portfolio

The high diversity portfolio adopts the high levels of all the four strategies which share commonalities in both KM scope and type, as illustrated in Table 1. This portfolio facilitates full access to cumulative knowledge by diversifying all the range of knowledge across KM di mensions. Knowledge adjustment costs continue to increase with a high level of KM diversity [28]. Adopting all the four KM strategies increases the costs for processing and communicating knowledge owing to heightened efforts to access and acquire dispersed knowledge [38]. For example, coordination costs are relatively high when EKPS is integrated with IKCS [49], as aforementioned in developing H1. Furthermore, knowledge benefits from a broad set of knowledge sources may be difficult to achieve [56]. As the importance and value of knowledge as a critical strategic resource increase, sourcing knowledge from other or ganizations entails more costs in the industry sector [57]. Therefore, we propose that high levels of the four KM strategies across the KM di mensions will eventually generate substitutability in improving firm performance.

H4. KM strategies in the high diversity portfolio exhibit a substitutable effect on firm performance.

## 3.5. Research model

Our hypotheses address the portfolio effects of combined KM stra tegies on firm performance by using the KM portfolio patterns catego rized by the concept of KM diversity. As depicted in Fig. 3, the research models indicate all the hypotheses, which address whether the multiple KM strategies—as a firm’s portfolio—mutually generate complemen tarities or cause substitutabilities in improving firm performance, across the four different portfolio patterns: i.e., unrelated diversity portfolios (H1), scope- and type-based related diversity portfolios (H2), extended diversity portfolios (H3), and high diversity portfolio (H4). Our inves tigation with the hypotheses in the models enables us to empirically show the complementary and substitutable effects of multiple KM strate gies on firm performance from a holistic perspective.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Sample characteristics

To enhance the representativeness of our study sample and the generalizability of the results, we selected Korean organizations whose industrial composition is proportional to that in South Korea, according to the ninth edition of the Korean Standard Industrial Classification. We surveyed three to five organizational managers who were found to play key roles in KM of each firm and were reached through their CEOs and CIOs and used a total of 646 questionnaires from 152 firms for hy pothesis testing. We filtered out the responses that included missing values or failed to meet the within-agreement criteria, including $r _ { \mathrm { w g } } , \eta ^ { 2 } ,$ and intra-class correlation coefficients, i.e., ICC(1) and ICC(2). Following the recommendation of Klein and Kozlowski [58], we calcu lated all these indices because each index exhibits its own strengths and weaknesses and no measure alone is best used for the within-agreement criteria. The availability of the firm-level aggregated data was confirmed from the interrater reliability tests with $r _ { \mathrm { w g } } , \eta ^ { 2 }$ , ICC(1), and ICC(2). We found no significant differences in the ratings of the study variables across the respondents’ rank, age, gender, years spent in the industry, and organizational tenure. Hence, all the final data were pooled for the firm-level analyses. Table 2 presents the details on firm size, age, and industrial knowledge intensity of the sample firms and the respondents demographic information.

## 4.2.1. Knowledge management strategies

Single-item measures of the four individual KM strategies were developed by combining a pair of separate approaches to KM scope and type (see Appendix A). Using single-item measures of the four KM strategies seems acceptable given the procedures and survey design [60]. To help the respondents clearly understand each KM strategy before rating the measures, we provided in-depth explanations and ex amples of how organizational knowledge is internally regulated or externally circulated (KM scope) and accumulated through systems or human networks (KM type). This explanation was followed by a Q&A session to ensure accurate understanding of all the KM strategies. Four single-item measures of the individual KM strategies were also presented in parallel to help the respondents easily differentiate the KM strategie and compare them with one another. This presentation of the KM strategy measures in the survey allows respondents to determine the relative dominance of certain KM strategies from others in a firm [61]. Table 4 shows that our firm-level measures of the four individual KM strategies are supported by acceptable levels of the multiple indices for within-firm agreement and between-firm variance, as well as by the F-test results.

## 4.2.2. Firm performance

We measured firm performance using (1) four-year average Tobin’s q ratios, (2) aggregated self-reported ratings from each firm on multiple items, and (3) single self-reported ratings by respondents with top po sitions. Our hypothesis tests used the three measurements of firm per formance to check the sensitivity of our empirical findings. In particular, we expected consistent results across the different measures to support the robustness of our findings. The three measurements of firm perfor mance have different advantages. Tobin’s q ratios incorporate a market measure of firm value because the q ratio is forward-looking, risk adjusted, and less susceptible to changes in accounting practices [62]. The market-based measure is the capital market value of a firm divided by the replacement value of its assets [63]. Our main analyses with the q ratios test the causal effects of KM diversifications on a firm’s future performance. Our additional analyses, with the self-reported firm per formance ratings free from potential bias (firms’ strategic intentions to project their successes with their financial outcomes), may complement the main findings by providing consistent results, indicating robustness and decreased sensitivity of our empirical findings. The four-yea average Tobin’s q ratios for the 152 firms over time are reliable mea sures (Cronbach’s α among Tobin’s q ratios of each firm for the four years = 0.930). ## 4.2.3. Control variables

The analysis controls for firm size, firm age, and industry because of their potential effects on firm performance. Firm age is controlled [10] because it manifests the external legitimacy of firm existence in the relationships with other firms, the firm’s staying power, and the pervasiveness of internal rou tines that influence firm performance [66]. To control for industry, we adopted the OECD industrial classification [67] and categorized sample firms into either high or low industrial environments based on knowl edge intensity, which reflects a set of industrial sector-level characteristics.

## 5. Analyses and results

## 5.1. Supermodularity tests

We tested all the possible complementarities and substitutabilities among the four individual KM strategies to thoroughly investigate their multiple interactions in organizations.<sup>1</sup> Prior empirical studies limited their analyses to the estimation of pair-wise interaction effects—i.e., two-way interactions [e.g., [69] [70],] or only some pair-wise in teractions of interest [e.g. [71],]—due to the absence of robust methods for empirically testing complementarities and substitutabilities with more than two strategies. Furthermore, the empirical evidence of these studies was exposed to information loss due to their dichotomous measurement of continuous variables [e.g. [37],].

The adoption approach<sup>2</sup> is potentially problematic because it does not consider the impact of additional cross terms, such as three- and four-way interaction effects in the case of four strategies. Although most prior empirical studies relied on the adoption approach because of its simplicity, the approach only examines a partial expression for the cross derivative, thus exposing itself to an omitted exogenous variable bias that affects all coefficients [72]. In this sense, Athey and Stern [73] emphasized considering complete sets of organizational strategies in testing their complementarities and substitutabilities. They also sug gested using the production function approach, which combines multi ple strategies to explain the organizational outcome. This approach enables an empirical analysis of all the potential complementarities and substitutabilities among the four individual KM strategies combined by a single organization.

Organizational practices can be measured “dichotomously” or “continuously" in testing their complementarities and substitutabilities. In this study, individual KM strategies were measured as continuous variables because the organization-level decision on KM strategies re sults from the consensus shared by collective members of an organiza tion. In the case of KM strategies, we can assume that an organization depends on all four strategies but to different extents. Following the relative importance of each strategy in an organization, dominant and inferior types of the four KM strategies may be different across organi zations. Organization-level decisions on KM strategies result from the consensus shared by collective members of an organization, in which a considerable amount of knowledge for multiple tasks and different businesses are managed by people with various functions [74]. Thus, individual KM strategies were understood with continuous measure ments in this study.

## 5.2. Results of Complementarity/Substitutability tests

## 5.2.1. Findings of testing H1 on unrelated diversity portfolios

H1 indicates the substitutabilities between KM strategies in unre lated diversity portfolios with three firm performance measures. As summarized in Table 5, H1 is supported by the two supermodularity tests for the two-way interactions of KM strategies. For example, the supermodularity tests with four-year average Tobin’s q ratios reveal substitutabilities between the KM strategies in unrelated diversity portfolios, that is, the substitutabilities between high IKCS (x ) and EKPS (x ) and between high IKPS (x ) and EKCS (x ).

As predicted in H1, adopting two distinct strategies from different tactics of the KM dimensions indicates a negative influence on firm performance. The benefits of a KM strategy are diminished by adopting another KM strategy from a different KM dimension. For example, IKPS that focuses on creating firm-specific knowledge can lose its strength by combining decontextualized external knowledge from EKCS [2]. Furthermore, problems of a KM strategy are exacerbated when a firm uses another KM strategy from a different dimension. Misapplication problems of external personalized knowledge can intensify when such knowledge is used with decontextualized and encoded internal knowl edge [29,49]. Identifying such substitutabilities between KM strategies in unrelated diversity portfolios indicates that organizational knowledge does not necessarily have intended and positive effects on firm perfor mance. The portfolio effects of knowledge from one source combined with knowledge from a different source can be harmful because of their substitutable relationship. Interactions between organizational knowl edge from different sources exhibit unintended effects, which could not be captured by prior studies.

## 5.2.2. Findings of testing H2 on related diversity portfolios

A set of four supermodularity tests for the two-way interactions of four pairs of strategies supports H2A and H2B (see Table 5). This result suggests complementarities between each pair of KM strategies in the scope- and type-based related diversity portfolios, with firm perfor mance measured by the four-year average Tobin’s q ratios. The com plementarities are as follows: between high IKCS (x ) and IKPS (x ), between high EKCS (x ) and EKPS (x ), between high IKCS (x ) and EKCS (x ), and between high IKPS (x ) and EKPS (x ).

As proposed in H2A and H2B, firm performance is improved by adopting related diversity portfolios within the same KM dimension. Through KM strategies in scope- and type-based related diversity port folios, firms can increase their operational efficiency by codifying their knowledge and acquiring external knowledge. Likewise, firms can in crease their effectiveness by expanding their knowledge base with externally generated new and unique ideas [36]. Furthermore, they can reduce their risks of knowledge loss from the knowledge holder’s turn over or retirement. These complementarities between KM strategies in related diversity portfolios demonstrate positive and synergic effects on firm performance, and their interaction effects are unintended by each individual KM strategy.

## 5.2.3. Findings of testing H3 on extended diversity portfolios

Table 5 shows that the results of the four supermodularity tests for the three-way interactions of KM strategies partially support H3. Only one extended diversity portfolio shows complementarity among high IKCS (x ), IKPS (x ), and EKCS (x ) whereas the other extended diversity portfolio exhibits substitutability among high IKCS (x ), IKPS (x ), and EKPS (x ) with firm performance measured by Tobin’s q ratios. The other two extended diversity portfolios indicate neither complemen tarity nor substitutability among high IKCS (x<sub>1</sub>), EKCS $( x _ { 3 } ) ,$ , and EKPS (x ) and among high IKPS (x ), EKCS (x ), and EKPS (x ). Thus, H3 is not fully supported by the supermodularity tests, indicating that the extended diversity portfolios among high IKCS (x ), EKCS (x ), and EKPS (x ) and among high IKPS (x ), EKCS (x ), and EKPS (x ) have tradeoff effects in improving firm performance. Specifically, their complemen tarities and substitutabilities among the KM strategies cancel each other out rather than outweigh one another.

## 5.2.4. Findings of testing H4 on high diversity portfolio

Finally, a supermodularity test for the four-way interaction of all the four KM strategies supports H4 (see Table 5). This result indicates that the effect of the high diversity portfolio on firm performance, as measured by Tobin’s q ratios, is substitutable.

Simultaneously using all four KM strategies increases the adjustment and coordination costs needed to process diversified knowledge because efforts to access and acquire dispersed knowledge from multiple knowledge sources are sophisticated and complex [38]. Knowledge benefits from complementarity in the high diversity portfolio can also be canceled out because of challenges, such as natural comparisons, sus picions, and shirking among heterogeneous sources [77]. This substi tutability in the high diversity portfolio is consistent with Bowker’s [78] view on selective and strategic forgetting. A firm should selectively focus on organizational knowledge on specific disciplines by excluding exist ing knowledge on irrelevant disciplines, according to the dynamic pri orities required by contemporary business environments that change over time.

We also conducted the same supermodularity tests with other alternative measures of firm performance, including aggregated selfreported ratings from each firm and single self-reported ratings by re spondents with top-ranking positions. Results of such robustness checks support our findings further by showing that the results of the super modularity tests with Tobin’s q ratios as firm performance are quite consistent with the empirical results with the alternative measures of firm performance, i.e., the self-reported ratings. ## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Implications for research

Our portfolio perspective generates several theoretical contributions by defining KM portfolio patterns along with KM diversity and measuring intended and unintended effects with the mixed conse quences of organizational knowledge sources.

First, we propose the inverted U-shaped relationship between KM diversity and firm performance across different portfolio patterns. Prior studies in the strategic management literature have insisted on the inverted U-shaped [39,40] or S-shaped [28] relationships between di versity and firm performance [41]. This study tests the relationships with more than two strategies, the interactions of which go beyond the simple linear relationships. According to KM diversity, our results reveal that little diversity via unrelated diversity portfolios and too much di versity via the high diversity portfolio shows substitutabilities with firm performance whereas very little diversity via related diversity portfolios exhibits complementarities with firm performance. In addition, extended diversity portfolios show substitutabilities and complemen tarities. Our results manifest that the relationship between KM diversity and firm performance seems to be close to the inverted U shape rather than the S shape. The diversity effects are investigated from a product or market perspective, even though knowledge is the root of sustainable competitive advantage [79]. This study sheds new light on how a firm effectively manages its knowledge sources to improve firm performance by investigating the diversity effects from a knowledge perspective.

Second, this study extends our extant knowledge by simultaneously examining the KM scope and type dimensions to identify multiple KM strategies and classify their portfolio patterns. Previous studies on KM portfolio effects focused primarily on the complementarities and sub stitutabilities in either the KM scope or type instead of across the di mensions, even though the organizational ability to achieve superior performance is a function of the flow of selected valuable knowledge stock across firm boundaries [22]. Few studies have investigated po tential complementary and substitutable interactions among KM stra tegies, based on KM scope and type, with a firm’s performance improvement [e.g., 37]. We investigate the portfolio effects of multiple KM strategies based on KM scope (organizational knowledge flow) and KM type (organizational learning via appropriated knowledge stock) to understand this function in the strategic KM context.

Third, this study contributes to organizational knowledge research by investigating the effects of KM portfolios with an advanced test method. In the current KM literature, no previous research has assessed complementarities and substitutabilities with all possible combinations of individual strategies, measured as continuous variables, from a portfolio viewpoint. Prior studies measured complementarities among strategies by adding interaction terms, thus providing limited findings on only the effects of pair-wise strategies on firm performance. However, firms can simultaneously consider three or four KM strategies. Furthermore, previous studies have used discrete variables to measure individual strategies such that the precision of estimates may be low ered. A few studies [e.g., 10, 36], in which multiple strategies were considered, simply estimated their pair-wise (only two-way) interaction effects and ignored all possible interactions among multiple strategies. In the IT productivity literature, prior work [e.g., [80] [81],] examined a complementarity model with three factors based on the supermodularity concept. However, it dichotomized their continuous measures and tested all the possible complementarities with a mean-difference approach (F-tests). We employed the supermodularity test method, developed specifically for complementarity and substitutability testing by Carree et al. [72], to extend such complementarity tests into multiple continuous factors.

Lastly, our findings suggest that organizational knowledge would be a double-edged sword with the combined effects of its intended and positive consequences and unintended and negative ones. This paper identifies the inverted U-shaped relationship between KM diversity and firm performance, which implies that too little and too much KM di versity can hurt firm performance. We addressed the complementarities and substitutabilities among individual KM strategies, implemented together yet with different intensities by an organization, by exhaus tively testing all possible multi-way interactions among the strategies.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

First, KM strategies in related diversity portfolios are complemen tary. The synergistic interactions between IKCS and IKPS in a scopebased portfolio, between EKCS and EKPS in a scope-based KM portfo lio, between IKCS and EKCS in a type-based portfolio, and between IKPS and EKPS in a type-based portfolio may prove particularly valuable for improving firm performance. Increasing the level of one strategy leads to higher marginal returns from increasing the level of the other strategy [36]. Managers who intend to improve firm performance through implementing effective and efficient KM portfolios must adopt strategies that share common tactics with the KM dimensions.

Second, the strategies in extended diversity portfolios show mixed relationships. An interaction among the combined strategies in the extended diversity portfolios is complementary, another is substitutable, and others are neither complementary nor substitutable. The unpre dictable results for the extended diversity portfolios imply that exces sively diversifying KM activities does not help firms improve firm performance [56]. Managers should carefully evaluate the results of extended diversity portfolios before diversifying their KM strategies.

Third, unrelated and high diversity portfolios damage firm perfor mance. Increasing the level of a KM strategy results in lower marginal returns from other KM strategies consisting of different tactics of KM dimensions. Certain studies suggest that unrelated and high diversity portfolios enable managers to source unique knowledge beyond indus trial boundaries and reduce industry-specific risks [39]. However, our findings show that unrelated and high diversity portfolios negatively influence firm performance. Managers need to combine KM strategies that share a commonality within the same KM dimension to increase firm performance. These inter-strategy commonalities rapidly acquire new knowledge and assimilate it with learning processes through related and extended diversity portfolios [82]. This study provides managers with deep insights into gaining efficiencies from existing KM strategies and maximizing firm performance through KM strategy diversification.

Fourth, our practical implications correspond to guidelines from a recent article [56]. In increasingly complex business environments, an organization needs to maintain its heterogeneity of knowledge sources and sustain a modular structure to be adequately prepared for uncer tainty. In our view, such an adaptive mechanism for feedback and se lection was clearly described by our findings from a portfolio perspective based on the concepts of complementarity and substitut ability and unpacked cost–benefit tradeoffs among the multiple KM strategies. Contemporary firms must achieve this mechanism as the primary driver of competitive advantage in the digital age with emerging new technologies for big data innovations from diversified knowledge sources.

Finally, this paper provides managerially valuable results and sug gestions on specific patterns of combined KM strategies that improve or lower firm performance by classifying the eleven KM strategy combi nations into four distinct KM portfolio patterns. That is, our portfolio view explains interactions among combined strategies, adopted in different degrees by a firm, and identifies their complementarities, which may be underleveraged by a firm, and their substitutabilities, which a firm may need to avoid for its better performance.

## 6.3. Limitations and future directions

Given that firm-level effects of combined KM strategies are a complex phenomenon and a thorny issue, this study will not be the last to explore the topic. Our study has several limitations, some of which offer addi tional possibilities for further research. First, we considered the fouryear average Tobin’s q ratios and self-reported performance measures (aggregated self-reported ratings and single self-reported ratings by high-ranking respondents) as the performance outcome of a firm. The results of the KM portfolio effects are almost consistent regardless of performance measures. We believe that consistent results from our supermodularity tests robustly support our findings on the comple mentarities and substitutabilities among KM strategies. However, the performance measures do not fully capture the intangible benefits from KM portfolios. A study to investigate KM portfolio effects with different firm performance measures [83], such as innovation [84] and learning outcomes [85], will further provide robust results.

Second, cross-sectional data were used in this study. Researcher should use longitudinal data to understand the effects of KM portfolios on corporate performance over time. Such a longitudinal data approach may address the different timing of performance effects of multiple organizational factors [86]. Another avenue to expand this study is to develop and test similar models with longitudinal data and attempt to understand how the KM portfolios affecting firm performance evolve over time, despite the relative stability of strategies [41].

Finally, firm performance with the same KM portfolios differs depending on conditions [87], such as firm size, knowledge gaps, and social constraints [13,48]. Thus, exploring conditions under which specific KM strategies work well together is of significant interest. Examining factors that can help certain firms maximize their use of the same KM portfolios is an interesting objective for future research [23, 85].

## 7. Conclusion

For a holistic view of the portfolio effects of KM strategies on firm performance, we investigated the performance implications of four distinct KM portfolio patterns based on KM dimensions and KM di versity. Our findings suggest that successful KM portfolios require a judicious and selective combination of IKCS, IKPS, EKCS, and EKPS within the same tactics of the KM scope or type. Such portfolio patterns reflect the reality of the current business intelligence and environment. Thus, a successful KM is not a means of doing everything possible through organizational memory but a never-ending cycle of selfreflection and self-discipline through selective and strategic forgetting [78]. In the contemporary data-centric, information-intensive environ ment, firms selectively erase and clarify organizational knowledge ac cording to their strategic priorities. The importance of such selective and strategic forgetting indicates that diversifying multiple knowledge sources improves firm performance when their complementarities outweigh substitutabilities.

## Concept of Knowledge Management Strategy

In an organization, the objectives of KM strategies are (1) to access external or internal knowledge sources (KM scope dimension) and (2) accu mulate organizational knowledge in a system- or human-oriented way (KM type dimension) for acquiring and maintaining competitive advantages for sustainable corporate growth.

The two dimensions (KM scope and type) classify the KM strategy into four types, namely, internal knowledge codification strategy (IKCS), internal knowledge personalization strategy (IKPS), external knowledge codification strategy (EKCS), and external knowledge personalization strategy (EKPS) As shown in Fig. 1, a singular KM strategy consists of paired composite approaches based on the two dimensions. Specifically, (1) based on the KM scope dimension, one component of a KM strategy can be either internal knowledge leverage (Approach 1− 1) or external knowledge leverage (Approach 1–2) to access knowledge sources: (2) based on the KM type dimension, another component of the KM strategy can be either system oriented codification (Approach 2− 1) or human-oriented personalization (Approach 2− 2) to accumulate organizational knowledge. An individua KM strategy consists of the paired approaches, and each approach is based on either the KM scope or type. ## Appendix C. Robustness Checks

As shown in Table A2, the results of H1-H4 tests with the two other measures are almost consistent with the results from the supermodularity tests with the four-year average Tobin’s q ratios (see Table 5), except for a difference in the complementarity among high IKCS, IKPS, and EKCS. This difference is identified between the supermodularity tests with Tobin’s q ratios (Table 5) and those with the aggregated self-reported ratings (Table A2). The limitation of the latter may cause the difference. Although the multiple raters statistically showed consensus on firm performance within each firm (Table 4), their within-firm aggregated ratings may be less accurate than the other measures (four-year average Tobin’s q ratios and single self-reported ratings). This inaccuracy may be explained by the following: Compared with their superiors and other financial measures, some subordinate members are less likely to recognize their organization-wide performance accurately [89]. Except for this difference, our consistent result across the three firm performance measures support the robustness of the supermodularity tests on the complementarities and substitutabilities among combined KM strategies in the portfolios.
