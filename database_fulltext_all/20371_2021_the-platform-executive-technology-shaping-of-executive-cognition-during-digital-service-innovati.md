# The platform executive: Technology shaping of executive cognition during digital service innovation

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Digital Platform Executive Cognition Service Innovation

## A B S T R A C T

The upsurge of digital innovations around the world has led to the establishment of many digital organizations, mostly small-medium, that focus on digital service innovation. For such organizations, the dominant use of digital technologies significantly shapes the cognition of their executives. But technology shaping of executive cognition is underresearched and, hence, underexplained. This paper seeks to address this limitation through an empirical study informed by critical realism and media ecology theory. The study explains how and why digital technology shapes executive cognition through combined influence of digital platforms and applications, service innovation, and executive functions. This explanation leads to the conceptualization of the executive of the digital organization as a platform. The Platform Executive is characterized by cognitive restructuring in a rapid and innovative manner during engagement with digital technologies. Based on these explanations, the paper discusses the theoretical, research, and practical implications.

## 1. Introduction

The digital logic of information and communication technologies (ICTs) provides digital organizations with a wide range of service innovation possibilities [1,2]. Service innovation is the process of the development of a new or an improved service concept that enables the customer to get his or her job done [3]. A digital organization is one that depends on digital platforms such as Google’s Android Studio and Ap ple’s iOS. For such organizations, the executive (an individual or a group) is critical for service innovation.

The executive is responsible for various functions that include management, motivation, and mentorship [4–6]. Thus, even the exec utive’s personal values affect the formation of organizational values and culture [7,8]. The executive needs dynamic cognition capabilities for sensing, monitoring, and configuring digital resources to sustain the organization’s competitive position in an industry [9]. Cognition is the mental process of acquiring, maintaining, and applying knowledge [10]. Executive cognition involves the attention toward, and interpretation of, internal and external factors of the organization. It also involves learning by doing, knowing, and becoming [11]. Therefore, an under standing of how and why technology shapes executive cognition is theoretically and practically critical for digital innovation leadership in

their organizations.

Our review of existing Information Systems (IS) and Organization research shows that digital technology shaping of executive cognition in digital organizations during service innovation is underresearched and hence underexplained. Such an explanation is important because exec utive cognition is becoming more critical in the mediation between technology (platforms and applications) and the organization’s service innovation. Strategic mediation based on sound executive cognition is required to optimally leverage capabilities in digital technologies. Explaining executive cognition based on technology shaping provides theoretical and practical knowledge about how and why an executive who may not be digitally savvy can gain digital capabilities for service innovation. As more executives engage with digital platforms, they need such knowledge to make those engagements more meaningful and profitable. This research seeks to provide this knowledge based on the question, how and why does digital technology shape executive cognition during digital service innovation?

Swanson and Ramiller (2003, 1997) have used the concept of “organizing visions” to attempt this question. An organizing vision is “a focal community idea for the application of information technology in organizations” (1997; p.460). Typical contemporary examples are ubiquitous computing, data analytics, digital innovation, and smart computing. Swanson and Ramiller studied the importance of organizing visions in shaping thoughts about technology-related threats and op portunities. Thus, their research, like this one, focuses on cognition and functions of the IS executive, but differs from this one in three main ways. First, organizing visions are social or “discursive constructions” ([12]; p.16) because they are based on “community attention invested in the organizing vision” (p.15). Discursive construction is an “ongoing community discussion, rather than a material entity” (p.16). However, in our research, technology is viewed as a material artifact – an enduring enabler and constraint across space and time. Second, the IS executive in their research only responds to organizing visions and chief executive demands. But in our research, the executive both uses and responds to digital platforms. Third, while innovation is common to both studies, digital organization is absent from theirs. Hence, their theorization of the IS executive thinking is limited in scope.

Kaplan [13] has also attempted the question to explain how CEO cognition, organizational capabilities, and organizational incentives interact to shape strategy. Her work duly focuses on “CEO’s attention” to fiber-optic technology and aims to provide “cognitive explanation of firm response to technical change” (p.673). Thus, she explains strategic response instead of executive cognition. Similarly, Vandenbosch and Higgins [14] research on the relationships among executive support systems (ESSs), executive learning, and competitive performance also pays attention to executive cognition. They define learning as a cogni tion process and explain how ESSs contribute to executive learning. However, they assume that ESSs (digital applications) are instrumental, while we view them as essential. Hence, technology shaping is missing in their explanation of executive learning. Furthermore, their attention is mostly on how ESS or technological information contributes to ex ecutive learning, while our attention is primarily on how digital tech nology itself shapes executive cognition.

This research analyzes this shaping in a small-medium size digital organization. It explains that executive cognition is shaped by the combined influence of digital platforms and applications, service inno vation, and executive function imperatives. Based on this explanation and following Ciborra [9], this research paints the portrait of the Plat form Executive which undergoes rapid and innovative cognition struc turing during engagements with digital technologies.

In the next section, we review literature on executive cognition, technology shaping, digital service innovation, and media ecology. This is followed by a discussion of the theoretical framework. In the third section, the methodology used for the empirical study is presented, followed by the results in section 4. Data analysis is presented in section 5, followed by discussion of findings and theoretical contributions in section 6. Concluding remarks are presented in section 7.

## 2. Literature and theory

## 2.1. Executive cognition

In psychology, cognition is defined as a mental exercise of acquiring, maintaining, and applying knowledge [10]. Its dominant dimensions are process and structure. Cognitive process manifests in thinking or reasoning, problem-solving, learning, attention, recognition of patterns, and perception [11,15]. This includes mental processing in a dynamic and recursive manner ([16]; p.121). Cognition structure refers to the “resultant product” such as mental models that generate further cogni tion processes [10,17]. A mental model is a structural representation of reality.

The strategic and competitive foundations of an organization are traceable to executive cognition process of environmental scanning [18, 19]. Executive cognition informs an organization’s operational effi ciency and purposeful effectiveness (Hambrick & Mason, 1984). More over, engaging and interpreting an organization’s environment enables its executive to partake in both cognition “differentiation and integra tion” ([20]; p.597). Cognition differentiation and integration refer to structure. They underscore, expand, and enable cognition processes of weighing options for environmental exploitation and exploration [21].

## 2.2. Technology shaping

There are two main perspectives on technology shaping: extreme and moderate. The extreme perspective is called technology determinism. It assumes that technology imposes certain ways of behaving, thinking, and feeling on human beings. The premise is that technology has enduring properties that outlive and outlast their developers; it also acts autonomously to compel certain behaviors, thoughts, and feelings [22]. This is because technology invites specific forms of action [23]. For example, staircases determine the movement of those who use them, and code compilers define code-writers’ input syntax.

The moderate perspective on technology shaping is technology ma teriality. It refers to “particular forms that endure across differences in place and time” ([24]; p.31). The arrangement of material phenomena produce effects that are not due to human agency but to its own agency [25,26]. However, materiality does not determine human behavior; rather it enables and constrains without imposition [26]. For example, the openness of Android digital platform enables digital innovations by software engineers. These innovations go beyond the imagination of platform developers, yet it does not permit application developers to do whatever they want.

## 2.3. Digital service innovation

Service innovation is “the process of devising a new or improved service concept that satisfies the customer’s unmet needs” [3]. In the new service economy, service innovation relies heavily on digital innovation [27]. According to Mathiassen and Sørensen [28], this reli ance reflects the need for organizations to strategize around everyday use of mobile devices and applications. It includes specific customer requirements that may be emergent and thus require heterogeneous portfolios of capabilities. The increasing predominance of the service-dominant logic, coupled with digital innovation, has made ser vice innovation indispensable for organizational competitiveness [29].

Digital service innovation occurs when an organization exchanges new digital services with customers. It is understood in terms of the organization’s service actions (e.g., design, develop, and provide), the customer’s physical jobs (e.g., evaluate, buy, and apply), digital job (e. g., take a decision, pay a bill, and board a bus), service verb (e.g., learn, experience, and see), and service outcome (e.g., satisfied, reduced, and enhanced) [3]. For example, digital service occurs when a customer uses the organization’s mobile app to pay a bill, leading to a satisfying transaction experience. In this example, the organization’s service ac tion would be its “development” of the “mobile app” as a digital tech nology. “Uses” is the customer’s physical job, “pay a bill” is the digital job, “experience” is the service, and “satisfied transaction” is the service outcome.

## 2.4. Media ecology theory

Potential ecological theories such as Ecological Systems [30], Media Ecology [31], and General Systems Theory (GST) [32] were considered for analysis in this research. Ecological Systems theory ascribes human development to everything in the surrounding environment. While it is suitable for ecological analysis in general, it is not focused on technol ogy. Hence, it is less suitable for this particular study of technology shaping. GST is ecological in scope and thus assumes that understanding an entity in its environment must be in terms of its relationship with others. Each of the entities has a similar structure. Thus, together, they constitute a general system. But both ecological and general systems theories do not pay particular attention to technology, and so compared to media ecology, their technological explanations are weak.

Media ecology theory pays particular attention to technology while its scope goes beyond technology. The theory assumes that “the medium is the message” ([31]; p.7). In this context, medium is a perceptible manifestation of a dynamic process of mediating [33]. The medium and message contents are not the same but of different logical types. The medium is the primary shaper of human behavior ahead of its contents through the process of internalization. To internalize is to incorporate external media properties into one’s cognition. It occurs during the person’s engagement with the medium. Through internalization, the person develops a cognition structure characterized by technological knowledge. But media internalization is not given or automatic since media are not totally deterministic. Other environmental and cognition factors such as task difficulty and customer needs may combine with media to actualize or undermine internalization (see Fig. 1).

Because internalization is based on media and other factors, media are not deterministic [34] or restrictive in scope [35]. The message of any medium is just “the change of scale or pace or pattern that it in troduces into human affairs” ([31]; p.8). For example, a typewritten letter from a son to his mother would have a different effect on her compared to the same letter in handwritten form. According to [33]; p.116–117), the range of phenomena that qualify as media includes technology, means, communication mode, channel, link, surface, lan guage, grammar, code, relationship, container, extension, and method. But in this research, we are concerned with ICTs as a narrower set of media and their shaping of executive cognition.

Media are environments [36], and they are studied in their interplay with competition, adaptation, and evolution [37,38]. The environment of ICTs includes “technology and techniques, modes of information and codes of communication” [33]; p.45; [39]. Together, they form part of the global ecosystem of devices, software, data, networks, platforms, infrastructure, and carriers [40,41].

## 3. Empirical research setting and methodology

The empirical research was conducted in a Ghanaian small-medium size digital organization (mCom: all names used are pseudonyms). It was founded in September 2011 by two computer science students of a Ghanaian university and provides mobile communication services to other organizations and individuals. Its first product was an information dissemination application for higher educational institutions. The application was piloted with over 1300 students in the university. Since then, mCom has grown steadily in terms of staff, products, revenue, and space.

It had nine staff, including the two cofounders who constitute the executive. During the study, it was also operating from the two largest cities in Ghana – Accra and Kumasi (it had begun in a small room in Kumasi). At the Kumasi office, it shared two rooms plus the front desk with another organization and housed three of the staff there (two software engineers and one administrative secretary). At the Accra of fice, it had one dedicated room, which was not shared with another organization. One of the cofounders (Kofi) plus four staff all operated from this room that was not partitioned with any solid dividers. The other cofounder (Kwame) worked from an external location in Accra that was not owned by mCom (Table 1).

Its communication service innovation was delivered through native and web SMS (short messaging service) and mobile applications to help organizations improve relations with customers and suppliers. It was selected for the empirical study because of two primary reasons. First, it was an enterprise that relied on digital platforms (mobile and web) for communication service innovation. Secondly, its executive was heavily dependent on, and dominated by, ICTs for overcoming some of the constraints presented by limited resources. mCom used ICTs for every aspect of the business, including accounting, human resource manage ment, customer service, marketing, task scheduling, research, learning, planning, and development. For example, physical files in both offices were negligible, and the resident cofounder was pleased with that. This heavy dependence on digital technology had been its character from the very beginning.

The study pursued understanding of their experiences, events, and underlying technologies that shape the executive cognition and func tions – accounts from the “native’s point of view” [42]. ## 3.1. Research philosophy

We approached this research from a critical realist perspective. Critical realism is a philosophical basis for technology materiality as well as media ecology theory. Ontologically, critical realism assumes the “existence of an intransitive domain of generative mechanisms” ([44]; p.97). For example, digital platforms generate digital organizations and service innovation. It is an intransitive domain because it has a real structure that is more enduring than the things generated. Henfridsson & Bygstad [45], for example, have used critical realism to explain gener ative mechanisms of digital platforms, viewing them as enduring re alities that generate actual events and observable experiences [46–48]. Hence, our analysis, interpretation, and explanation of how and why digital technology shapes executive cognition are primarily generative.

Epistemologically, critical realism assumes that actual events and people’s experiences must be perceived by the researcher in their nat ural, social, and cultural settings [49]. Furthermore, the events and experiences reflect different perceptions and interpretations of social actors (people), emphasizing epistemic but not judgmental relativity [50]. Data collected on these events, experiences, and perceptions informed us initially about how and why ICTs shape executive cognition processes.

## 3.2. Case study and data collection

With the aim of developing a process theory [51], a case study was undertaken at the organization spanning 18 months. We sought to gain a greater understanding of technology shaping processes in the context of digital service innovation than we would through a survey or an experiment. This context is unique to the explanation of executive cognition and functions in this research because it has not been adequately explored in relation to technology shaping and executive cognition. We took the advice of experts of case study strategy (e.g. [52–54],) that this strategy is most appropriate when there are no clear relationships between the phenomenon of interest and context.

Empirical data collection included semi-structured interviews (the primary method), meetings, observations, and document studies (Table 2). These were used for data and between-method triangulations [55] to support validation of our explanation. In data triangulation, diverse viewpoints of the relationship between ICTs and executive cognition were sought. Between-method triangulation combines methods for data collection and within-method triangulation combines different subissues of a semi-structured interview instrument to capture complementary viewpoints on the central issues. All the nine staff members of mCom were interviewed to achieve full sample coverage, and also to ensure within-method triangulation. The interviews were approached and framed with Myers and Newman’s [56] dramaturgical model that suggests the treatment of each interview as a drama. Each of the interviews was conducted at the office where the staff was working (Table 1).

Informed by Qu and Dumay [43] and critical realism, the interviews sought to obtain data on technology-enabled processes and structures of cognition in the digital service innovation context. People demonstrate technology-enabled cognition processes and structures through learning [11,15]. In the organizational context, learning is understood in terms of the generic constructs of professional identity and practice, namely knowing, doing, and becoming [57,58]. Thus, we used these constructs to understand technology-enabled cognition (Appendix B). The extent of questioning differed according to the staff category. Questions posed to the cofounders (the executive) included items that sought for particular data on ideas generation, concept formation, and their translation into digital service innovation through business and technology de velopments. Questions posed to the software engineers sought for particular data on technology development. Finally, questions posed to the marketing officers, administrative secretary, and administrative as sistant did not particularly seek for data on technology development. All the interviews were digitally recorded after permission was sought from the cofounders, and the recordings were transcribed to result in close to 200 pages of text.

In accordance with the principles of data and between-method tri angulations, the interviews were complemented with five major meet ings for data collection. The first meeting was between one of the coauthors and Kofi at a conference in Accra where mCom was intro duced. Although the organization, as the empirical study site, had not been selected yet, the data obtained provided first-hand insights on the organization’s digital service innovation. The second meeting was with Kofi at his office following the researcher’s request. At this meeting, further data were sought about the organization’s size, structure, physical space, and service innovation. The third meeting was with all the Accra office staff during one of their weekly Monday morning crossbriefings about work done in the previous week and work to be done in the current one. During this meeting, data on these briefings were gathered through hearing and note-taking. Attention was paid to service marketing efforts, new product developments, types of customers, and executive function. Two other meetings were held with Kofi, outside mCom office but in Accra, after the interviews had been transcribed and shared with him. He had not read all the transcripts, but was able to confirm and corroborate the statements in what he had read. Data on mCom’s prospecting of new customers to sell digital services as well as on its structure were gathered during these meetings.

Documents on the organization’s profile, products, mission, vision, and history were copied from its website and studied. These were mainly data on the company’s service innovation – its range of digital solutions, services, customers, and success stories. They provided a different viewpoint of service innovation that complemented the data obtained from the interviews and meetings.

Phenomena that were directly observed included the organization’s physical space, arrangement of furniture, interfaces of different business applications being used, management practices, and customer support. These data corroborated the fact that mCom was indeed a digital orga nization; that it was almost a paperless organization; and that executive cognition and functions were shaped considerably by ICTs. Observations were done during meetings and before and after interviews. The ob servations also contributed to the triangulation of data on executive functions, heavy dependence on ICTs, and the digital service innovation context.

## 3.3. Data analysis

The main unit of analysis is the executive of the digital organization – its engagement with digital platforms and other ICT applications, its functions within the organization, and its mindset. Besides, the guiding principle of our data analysis is that when interpretation is possible in the pursuit of causal relationships, as in technology shaping of executive cognition, it must be undertaken [59]. Hence, analysis must not end at the establishment of relationships between variables. Thus, the data were interpreted inductively alongside the data collection. The accounts of mCom staff were believed to be interpretations of their situations because the questions posed sought for their explanations of how and why. Those accounts were validated with corroborative accounts by other staff, documents studied, meetings, and observations. Following the data collection and their transcription, we identified themes in them that pertain to our predefined codes of technology shaping due to its materiality. Examples of these codes are enabling or constraining verbs such as change, redefine, monitor, frame, integrate, enhance, and fragment.

The themes were identified by each author’s careful readings of the nearly 200 pages of text from the nine interviews. Drawing upon Vais moradi et al. [60] suggestions on coding and theming qualitative data, each of the coauthors searched manually for statements made by the co-founders on decisions and actions they had taken because of, by, with, and/or through ICTs. We were particularly interested in the transforming power of ICT-enabled decisions and actions because of our critical realist approach. Furthermore, in line with Hunter et al. [61] creative meaning-making in qualitative research, we also looked for statements made by the seven staff members that corroborated the co: founders’ own. For example, what one cofounder said (“we’re still monitoring [technologies], waiting for it to be fully developed then we can take advantage of it”) was corroborated by what one software en gineer said (“this new thing is coming, what are you going to do about it? Then I go in, find my analysis, and then start”). After that we compared them to aggregate the different ones and join the similar ones.

## 4. Results

The results are presented in terms of the organization’s digital ser vice innovation and of the three ICT-enabled learning processes that are the main facets of cognition development: knowing, doing, and becoming. mCom’s applications and systems were produced from digital platforms, web development frameworks, and mobile telecommunica tions platforms owned by other organizations. For example, the SMS application was built with a Laravel PHP framework; and the short and long codes messaging systems were derived from one of the mobile telecommunications operators in the country. mCom’s heavy depen dence on external technology frameworks and platforms for production underscored its status as a digital organization. Kwame confirmed this with these words:

“for us, we’re a technology company. So without technology we wouldn’t even exist as an organization.”

Aside from software development, one of the organization’s domi nant activities was customer service. Thus, the administrative officer in Accra and administrative secretary in Kumasi were dedicated to receiving and responding to customer complaints throughout the day. The organization’s website also had an instant messaging application that always stayed on top of every page visited, serving as another means for offering customer services. The customer service calls that were received and addressed were entered into PipeDrive, the sales and customer relationship management system.

The smallness of mCom and its digital service innovation required continuous learning by all staff, but particularly by the executive. Ex ecutive learning by Kofi and Kwame was for developing their personal capabilities, for generating ideas, and for innovating services. All these ends were being pursued with dependence on ICTs. Therefore, the pursuing processes were technology-based learning by knowing, doing, and becoming.

## 4.1. Learning by knowing

Knowing what ICTs are available for mCom to leverage was one of the foundations for ideas generation and service innovation. This exec utive function included monitoring and identifying newly added prop erties of individual technology media.

“initially when we started out … erm … we were looking more at SMS based solutions. But then as time went on we realised that … ok, no, we could leverage on let’s say … some technologies that are available. So for example may be … that’s we could use mobile apps to still achieve the communication goal. So that is one thing that we say … ok, probably, erm some advancements in mobile app development has benefited us as a company because some of the services we now want to provide, previously we couldn’t have done that but with the introduction of these advance ments we could do that, because even though let’s say smartphones have existed for a while now, the smartphones then and the smart phones now are not the same. There have been some improvements, there’s been im provements in let’s say how apps are developed” – Kwame

“I think for us as a company, we have identified that within the voice space, there’s a lot that we can take advantage of. … we’ve identified that USSD will become quite big so that’s an opportunity for us to step in … erm … voice will also become big, we’ve identified that so that’s also an opportunity for us to step in.” – Kofi

One of the software engineers based in Kumasi, and one who often sought ideas from Kwame on technology development issues, said the following to corroborate these executive statements on learning-byknowing:

“And [Kwame] … he is a senior software engineer so with most of my difficulties he has been helping me day in and day out so that’s the sort of experience that I’m getting from him. And he is more good and competent with the new technologies that are coming up, so day in and day out I’ve been learning from him.” – Yaw

## 4.2. Learning by becoming

Kofi and Kwame were not only monitoring and identifying the op portunities presented by ICT media to develop ideas for service inno vation, they were also using ICT media to develop their personal capacities according to their specific roles in mCom. Kofi, as the Board Chairman, CMO, and COO was more interested in organization and management issues. Thus, he was using various ICTs to practice learning-by-becoming a better manager and marketer. He said:

“So in terms of learning, me I think from day one, I was always reading books on marketing though I don’t have any formal background on it, I … even right now on LinkedIn I’m doing a course … that one is more on management but it combines a few of business stuff and all that. So I do a lot of online courses, I used to do some on Coursera and then Lynda.com as well, and I read a lot online, so I’m also good in social media marketing as well.”

At the same time, Kwame, being the MD, CTO, and CFO, was more interested in digital platforms, frameworks and, development issues. Thus, he was using various ICTs to practice learning-by-becoming a better CTO. He also said:

“ok as an individual it helps … based on some of the new things that are coming up, it opens up your mind to other things that you can do. So ok probably let’s take … let’s take Android as a platform for example. As an individual I don’t have control over … that much control over what feature will be included in Android or what it needs to do, but then when it gets to the point where ok there is a news update or a release that ok this new feature has been included, then ok, I have to ask myself ok, what can I do as an individual to take advantage of this thing that has been introduced? So that is one part. For self-improvement, I think there’s been a lot of platforms for taking online courses some free, some paid. Previ ously I think that would have been a challenge. So you don’t really have to find yourself in a typical classroom to acquire new knowledge. There are forums and stuff out there. So that’s another … erm … I think I would say ok, without the help of technology when it comes to self-improvement it has made it much for me.”

During the empirical study, both of them, through their dependence on ICTs, were becoming more competent in their technology and man agement functions. All three software engineers and Kofi lauded Kwame for his high technology competence. Likewise, all the three software engineers, the administrative secretary, and the administrative officer also praised Kofi for his management competence. For instance, Ama, the telesales officer, made the following comment on how she had been trained on the job:

“It was mainly with Kofi. He walked me through when I came around. So he made me understand that these are the services they offer.

In addition, Abena, the administrative secretary, also made the following comment about how she had come to know about and learn from Harper.com:

“So, I think last two weeks Kofi sent me … I mean their fliers that if I’m interested I can go and then learn about social media marketing, yes; and so which I did”

## 4.3. Learning by doing

Kofi and Kwame were also using ICTs to learn-by-doing; that is, each of them was developing personal technology and management capac ities through ICTs. By managing the operations, marketing, and customer service activities through the software tools on his laptop computer (Google Drive, PipeDrive, Asana, etc.), Kofi was increasing his management competence through experience and feedback.

## 5. Technology and executive cognition

As a “technology company,” mCom’s range of digital technologies (devices, applications, infrastructures, protocols, platforms, and frame works) are understood as media. The entire organization was a typical technology condition that shaped its executive. The case data show quite clearly that mCom was characterized by fusion of learning, technology, information, decision-making, and production due to technology media. But how and why did technology shape executive cognition?

The technology media in the mCom environment exhibited materi ality by generating several material and human agencies (Table 3). The human agencies are analyzed in terms of inward (technology-enabled assimilation) and outward (technology-enabled management) dimensions.

## 5.1. ICT-enabled assimilation

The executive of mCom was assimilating digital service innovation ideas from various ICTs. The specific technology-enabled processes in assimilation that were shaping the executive’s cognition are: assembling ideas, exploiting opportunities, and totalizing awareness. Table 4 shows a summary of these processes.

## 5.1.1. Assembling

Assembling was an act of searching the internet to gather pieces of executive function ideas for personal development. The ICT media that enabled assembling of ideas by the executive were online audio and text, and development frameworks and platforms. These media individually and collectively generated various material and human agencies (exec utive functions) that explain technology shaping of the ICT executive (Table 3).

The material agency of audiobook technology is that it converts text into digital audio (spoken-word output). Voice technologies are ICT media that talk to people (Table 4). This material agency enabled the executive to do self-listening and self-learning which are part of what constitutes its human agency. Based on the Media Ecology assumption that the medium is the primary shaper of executive learning ahead of the contents, the voice format and electronic format of audiobook technol ogy are foregrounded in this explanation. At mCom, the first step taken by Kofi on getting all nine staff to read-by-hearing was that he “created an online account,” which gave preeminence to the technology ahead of its contents for assembling ideas. The voice format activates the audial human sense and the digital property integrates the technology’s voice with the human voice, leading to simultaneity of learning, production, marketing, and customer serving. Thus, voice technology media had generated an oral executive cognition, which was evidenced by Kofi's oral dissemination of technology, management, and innovation ideas within mCom. The oral executive cognition is further understood in two ways.

First, the voice technology media were amplifying and extending the audial senses for assembling ideas for executive function. The visual sense is amplified by mechanical media such as the printed book, ahead of the audial and tactile senses [62]. But the audial sense is amplified by digital media such as audiobook. Secondly, the voice technology media were causing the executive to align learning with the oral and verbal culture of a typical small-medium enterprise (SME) such as mCom. ## 5.1.2. Exploiting

Exploiting was evidenced by continuous monitoring of ICT in novations and the identification of opportunities by the executive for mCom’s communication service innovation. It is a process that led to enhanced executive cognition because the ICT medium (SMS technology in this instance) was a condition that evoked the executive’s learned reaction. The messaging technology. having been developed by man previously, has become a technology medium between man and the environment. Thus, before the exploitation of this medium by the ex ecutive, it was an external condition. To convert this external condition into a communication service innovation, the executive must first internalize it as its internal condition. The material agency of the SMS technologies is that it routes information between a sender and receiver. Therefore, they are ICT media that convey information (Table 4).

This material agency enabled the executive to gain instant compre hension of the largeness of “the voice space” in SMS technology and opportunities therein. The material agency also enabled the executive’s use of envisioned service innovation prospects to filter the messaging technology’s properties. These enablements reflect the digital technique of integration inherent to SMS technology. It instantly integrated the messaging systems of the human mind and SMS technology (the human mind’s messaging system also routes information). Thus, Kwame said that “some of the services we now want to provide, previously we couldn’t have done that but with the introduction of these advance ments we could do that.” He was referring to SMS technology ad vancements being exploited for service innovation, and that reference was to his knowledge that “the smartphones then and the smartphones now are not the same.” By relating digital properties with service innovation continually, the executive was routing ideas between them. In these processes of comprehending, identifying, relating, and conveying by the executive, its mind was also a conveying system. Given the conveying similarities between the media and the executive mind, it can be concluded that the media had generated a conveyor executive cognition. Further evidence of how the media were implicated in the conveyor cognition is in Kofi’s operational function as a human SMS. He was regularly providing quick answers to spontaneous questions posed by staff members and also posing spontaneous questions to demand quick answers at the Accra Office.

## 5.1.3. Totalizing

Totalizing refers to the act of mCom executive’s use of ICT media to obtain organizational information to achieve complete awareness of production, prospecting, marketing, and customer serving by staff of the organization – what McLuhan [31] calls “total field awareness” enabled by digital media. The host of business applications constituted the ICT media with several material agencies that in turn generated several human agencies among mCom staff (Table 3). For these reasons, we first characterize these business applications as ICT media that converge with companies and work together (Table 4). Among the executive of the organization, the material agencies generated the predominant human agency of supervision. Kofi, for instance, mentioned Google Drive and Asana as specific applications that enable and enhance his supervision: “you’re able to tell what task has been worked on, which hasn’t been worked on, which task has been completed.” On the whole, the execu tive either logged into these applications or configured them to send notifications as means of achieving complete awareness of staff mem bers’ learning, production, prospecting, marketing, and customer serving. This awareness included continuous checks or updates that led to executive interventions such as support, follow-ups, cautions, and instructions.

These business technology media constituted an external technology condition that was shaping the executive cognition ahead of the outward function of supervision. Such media combine with service innovation demands to avail business activity information to the executive at digital or electronic speed. But since “the medium is the message” rather than the contents, the business technologies instead of the information are foregrounded in this explanation. The business technologies and service innovation demands enable the building of the executive cognition ca pacity by the human agency of technological perception: “instead of asking questions always I go online and find all the information that I need,” says Kofi. The specific aspect of business technologies that en ables technological perception by the executive is their convergence in one organization’s repertoire of technologies according to service innovation demands. The convergence of these otherwise scattered technologies on the internet is necessitated by the nonlinear service innovation activities, which the executive has to perceive in their to tality almost at once. The convergence of business technologies and the digital or electronic speed characterizing their functionalities lead to an integrated executive cognition, which resonates with McLuhan’s idea of “cosmic consciousness.”

## 5.2. ICT-enabled management

The outward agencies are witnessed in mCom’s executive’s technology-enabled management by decisions and actions toward staff and organizational development. The empirical data show that in taking decisions and actions, the executive was technologizing the organiza tion, liberating the staff, and creating service solutions. But before the executive ensures these processes, it must have a certain cognition that has been shaped by ICT media. The following subsections explain how the shaping occurs.

## 5.2.1. Technologizing

The executive function of technologizing every domain of the orga nization is demonstrated by how it was getting every staff member to know some basic ICT, even if the staff member was a marketer but not a software engineer. Kofi’s reason in respect of a marketer was “because else you don’t know what you’re selling, because you’re selling a technology, you’re not selling a chair.” This is in addition to how it was making every staff member use business technologies for activities that traditional companies would use paper for. Thus, technologizing is an executive function that is traceable to ICT media within and without the organization. The material agency of ICT media is that they receive, process, store, secure, and disseminate information. When they converged at mCom. they constituted an external technological condition that enabled staff development, innovation management, and strategy execution. To the executive, this external condition had to be translated into an internal organizational condition to drive the orga nization’s communication service innovation. But there had to be a translation into an internal cognition before further translation into an external function, according to the Media Ecology assumption that ICT media transform the human sensorium.

In the context of service innovation by a digital SME such as mCom, the executive cognition had been transformed from nondigitized to digitized by ICT media (Table 4). This transformation may be ascribed to the two leaders' academic training as computer scientists. Still. that background is not necessary since there exist executives of digital SMEs who have no formal training in any computing discipline. The techno logical transformation of executive cognition is appropriately ascribed to the ICT condition or environment – digital organization, digital products, digital tools, and digital infrastructure: “for us, we’re a tech nology company. Therefore, without technology we wouldn’t even exist as an organization,” said Kwame. This was both a belief and a reality at mCom, but it is generally true of ICT executives because “to a man with a computer, everything looks like data” ([63]; p.14). A digitally trans formed executive cognition that is focused on nonlinear and integrated service innovation pretends to be a computer. When there is a problem, the solution is technology: “what technologies are out there that can be used to solve this problem?” said Kwame. “When there is a technology, the solution is technology” “I have seen this technology. What can I do with it?” repeated Kwame.

## 5.2.2. Liberating

When the executive of mCom both encouraged and coerced staff to continuously undertake online learning in a self-directed mode, they were liberating the staff because they were providing them resources for freedom to think. In its service innovation context, freedom to think was necessary for staff to learn more and for the organization to provide its customers with excellent services. All the staff, including the executive, received a lot of customer information and feedback, which needed to be processed and converted into service innovation efforts and solutions. This was done through considerable thinking individually and collec tively (at their weekly meetings). Online tutoring websites such as Lynda.com and Coursera plus online discussion forums were therefore very valuable sources toward this process of liberating the staff. Therefore, these websites are media that inform (Table 4). Their material agency is that they teach courses, and this agency generates the human agency of self-directed learning. These media constituted an external technological condition that had to be internalized by staff members and the organization to enable staff development and service innovation.

For this external technological condition to be internalized in mCom at large, it had to be internalized first by the executive (as we have indicated in the process of assembling ideas through audiobook). Thus, these media had generated a pedagogic executive cognition through its self-directed learning. Both Kwame and Kofi had used online resources not only to learn more about the latest technologies and management but also to learn about how to teach these ideas in a self-directed mode. They had not just become technology and management gurus and ex ecutives; they had also become teaching executives, teaching the staff to learn about technology and management in a self-directed mode (just as the online media was teaching them). Therefore, Kwame said, “I believe in the process of doing that you learn much more than if I just tell you, ok, this is what you need to do to get this done. Therefore, the oppor tunity mainly lies in trying to get them to solve problems with little directions from us so you figure out on your own.” In sum, the pedagogic executive cognition is more about instruction than information for generating the process of liberating.

## 5.2.3. Creating

The process of creating innovative digital service solutions was the organizational development goal of mCom. To achieve this goal, the organization depended on existing ICT media by appropriating their properties and combining them to develop customized solutions. These processes for creating innovative digital service solutions at mCom were only possible because the relevant ICT resources (infrastructure, plat forms, and applications) are media that beget (Table 4). Technology be gets technology through a process of combination [64], and that is a basic material agency of ICT media. At mCom, this material agency generated the manifold processes of assembling, exploiting, totalizing, technologizing, and liberating, which underpinned the organization’s digital service innovation. The executive traced mCom’s production and market position to how the organization can appropriate and combine existing ICT media into new solutions: “so what we want to achieve now is to get to the point where we are more like a virtual mobile network operator … via web which some traditional mobile network operators don’t do because their main focus is on the traditional communication medium,” said Kwame.

We ascribe the technology-enabled creating process at mCom not only to the begetting material agency of ICT media but also to inter nalization by the executive to make up its combinatory cognition. Com bination, as the material agency of ICT media, shapes executive cognition by enabling thought processes that combined the organiza tional goal or challenge with ICT media properties. Through these thought processes, the executive had not just become informed about new technology ideas; they had also become begetters of new digital service solutions based on appropriating and combing existing ICT media.

## 5.3. The executive as platform

The analysis in the previous section suggests that executive cognition is strongly explained by ICT media, service innovation, and executive functions. The interpretation of the data and technological explanations point to the considerable changes of the scale, pace, and pattern of ex ecutive cognition and functions due to ICT media. But technology shaping of cognition is neither generated by the materiality of ICT media alone nor do the media by themselves point to any particular executive cognition. The analysis shows clearly the significant roles played by the service innovation context and executive functions in the shaping of executive cognition. Service innovation and executive functions have emerged as moderating variables that have enabled our specification of cognition characteristics such as oral, open, conveyor, integrated, digi tized, pedagogic, and combinatory.

Executive functioning with ICT media and service innovation con stitutes the necessary condition for shaping cognition to possess these characteristics, but it is not sufficient. The sufficiency of this condition is in the degree of service innovation-driven internalizations of ICT media functions by the executive. Internalizations have been explained in terms of human agency processes such as assembling, exploiting, total izing, technologizing, liberating, and creating. These human agency processes have in turn been explained in terms of respective ICT material agencies such as talk, open, route, converge, transform, inform, and converge. Ultimately, these agency specifications are derived from the variety of ICT media, the service innovation drive, and executive engagement with the media. The greater the variety, drive, and engagement, the greater the sufficiency of the condition for in ternalizations and technology shaping, and vice versa.

In the process of technology shaping, the cognition of the ICT exec utive undergoes rapid structuring because of rapid ICT innovations globally. The high rates of innovation of ICT media and the high competition among ICT enterprises that induce executives to learn also prevent them from maintaining a settled cognition. ICT media as a technological condition and environment plus its shaping effects require rapid generation of new technology-enabled human agencies (assimi lation and management) to remain at the forefront of digital service innovation. This portrait of the executive is significantly different from the previous ones known in the literature. Because of significant tech nology shaping of executive cognition and functions, the executive being portrayed by this research is a rapidly shifting entity similar to the “chameleonic organization, the platform, conceived as a laboratory for rapid structuring” ([9]; p.104: see also [65,66]). Therefore, we also characterize the ICT executive as a platform. The platform executive is one that possesses capabilities due to and for rapid restructuring ac cording to ICT media innovations.

In sum, we explain how and why technology shapes executive cognition as follows: by executive internalizations of ICT media due to service innovation and executive functions; and because the combina tion of ICT media, service innovation, and executive function impera tives generate internalizations. These explanations are summarized in Fig. 2.

The figure shows a causal link between ICT media and executive cognition. But the cause is not necessary, and so the link is not direct, because the two variables are moderated by ICT service innovation and executive functions. These two variables moderate the link because the effects of ICT media on executive cognition depend on them individually and collectively. In terms of the theory of explanation, although the two variables moderate the link, they are significant final causes of executive cognition. Thus, in practice, cognition precedes functions; yet the fi nality of something to be practiced can significantly shape cognition ahead of the practice. This moderation underscores the materiality of ICT media, which denies technology determinism. Therefore, the effects of ICT media on executive cognition are stronger or weaker, which de pends on the varieties of ICT services innovation and ICT media applied to executive functions. The greater the variety, the stronger the media effects on executive cognition, and vice versa.

## 6. Discussion

This research sought to explain technology shaping of executive cognition in the context of service innovation with a technology approach. We have operationalized this approach through the materi ality and ecology of ICT media. The outcome of this approach is a technological explanation of executive cognition understood in terms of how and why the shaping occurs. This explanation, dubbed the platform executive, symbolizes a set of theoretical contributions that are different from previous theories of the executive yet complementary to them. Based on both the findings from the explanations in the previous section and the approaches taken to make them, the platform executive departs significantly from previous characterizations of executive cognition. Therefore, in this section, we discuss the theoretical contributions, research, and practical implications.

## 6.1. Theoretical contribution

The explanation of the relationship between ICT and cognition fills an epistemological gap in the theory of the executive in IS research. For, existing publications on studies of the executive do not include this explanation. In the first place, we have searched the mainstream IS journals and scholarly books and found that there have not been any explicit studies on ICT and executive cognition. The nearest studies that may stand as exceptions have been undertaken by Swanson and Ramiller [12,67], Kaplan [13] and Vandenbosch and Higgins [14]. The theories developed from these studies are comparable to the findings of this paper because all of them provide some explanation of the relationship between ICT and executive thinking. Moreover, because this paper’s conceptualization of ICT media includes cognition, change, perception, and organizing visions pertaining to them, its technological explana tions complement theirs. However, this paper’s explanation departs significantly from theirs because we explain executive cognition as technology and ecology (platform). They explain executive cognition about technology.

Swanson and Ramiller’s main argument is that the IS executive senses and responds to technological change and organizing visions. In their argument of executive response, ICT internalization is not included as technology remains external to the cognition of the IS executive before, during, and after its sensemaking and response. The absence of ICT internalization in their theory leaves us with a dyadic view of the relationship between ICT and executive cognition. Hence, they explain executive cognition about technology. But, we argue that executive cognition integrates ICT media in addition to sensemaking and response. Similarly, ICT internalization is also absent in Kaplan’s theory. Laying emphasis on CEO attention and fiber-optic patenting, the latter being an executive function, her findings duly refer to CEO cognition and in terpretations made of technical change. But, they are not technologyintegrated. Hence, she also explains executive cognition about technol ogy, while this research explains it as technology. Vandenbosch and Higgins also explain that in executive learning, the sole aspect of ESS that is internalized is information (not the technology). All the independent variables that cause executive learning are information based, leaving the ESS medium itself as a passive entity in their expla nation. Thus, their explanation is similar to those of Ramiller, Swanson and Kaplan.

Therefore, executive cognition as technology and ecology discloses technological enrichment of its ontology as well as a technological extension of its epistemology. Furthermore, our explanation is broader, more focused, and more integrated than previous ones. The breadth and focus are due to the ecology and materiality of ICT media (respectively) with which we approached this research. The integration is due to ex ecutive internalizations of ICT media functions moderated by service innovation and executive functions. The contribution of an extended explanation is in how and why it highlights the executive as a mediating platform for internalizing and externalizing ICT innovation and problem-solving. Previous studies on ICT media and the executive have focused on the two concepts (internalization and externalization) in a direct (one way) adoption or use-dependent relationship. Findings from this research position the executive as a mediator between internalizing ICT media and externalizing executive functions in respect of ICT service innovation. The findings extend the existing literature by explaining the causal relations between the two concepts.

In terms of the process dimension of our extended explanation, this research contributes theoretically to the current IS literature by juxta posing media ecology and materiality of ICT with contemporary lead ership executives who are being driven by digital service innovation endeavors. By ascribing executive cognition (moderated by service innovation and executive functions) to insightful processes such as assembling, exploiting, totalizing, technologizing, liberating, and creating, we add to the conceptual vocabulary that demonstrates tech nology shaping of executive cognition in service innovation contexts. These additions are substantive because they underpin the processual rendition of our explanation; they are also distinctive because they are overlooked in previous explanations of the relationship between ICT media and executive cognition. Hence, on the whole, this research is different from previous ones not only in terms of approach (material and ecological) but also in terms of the process and outcome dimensions of our explanation.

## 6.2. Research implications

A limitation of this idiographic research is that its empirical study was limited to one SME with nine staff members. Therefore, we cannot use its findings to make statistical generalizations. This means that our theory of executive cognition is strong in explanation, but weak in prediction. The underpinning critical realist philosophy that informed our analysis enables us to make analytical generalizations from our theoretical contribution [68]. As Tsoukas [48] explains, idiographic explanations are valid if they have been based on real structures epito mized by the materiality and ecology of ICT media. As real structures, ICT media have causal tendencies or generativity even if we cannot reliably predict recurring patterns of executive cognition from them. Hence, our explanation of executive cognition, based on these real structures. has external validity. To address its predictive weakness of the theory, we suggest that future researchers should apply it as an explanatory framework of executive cognition in other research con texts. Recurring patterns of executive cognition, which will be found in those research contexts, and have also occurred in this research, will begin to increase the predictive strength of this theory.

Furthermore, this research implies the need for IS research on ICTs in SMEs and the executive to extend focus and design from the existing limited, dyadic explanations toward triadic ones. Existing dyadic re lationships are between the executive and the organization, between ICT media and the executive, and between ICT media and the organization. The triadic research should be done to provide fresh explanations on the more complex or multiple linkages between ICT media, the executive, and the organization. As the findings and the discussion show, the relationship between ICT and the organization concerning digital innovation and problem-solving is not solely based on a direct rela tionship where the ICT impacts the organization without the mediating role of the executive. Thus, using the existing direct, dyadic relation ships limits the complex nature of the SME executive phenomenon in contemporary digital innovation research.

This paper’s integrated perspective also provides fresh insights into future research in digital service innovation (see, for example, [27,69, 70]). Having explained executive cognition as technology here (as an epistemological link in the translation of ICT media into service inno vation capabilities and products), we encourage future researchers to delve deeper into how aspects of executive cognition relate to diverse digital service innovation types. If the executive is viewed as a platform because of service innovation-driven internalization of ICT media functions, then both forward and reverse causal relationships are plau sible explanations yet to be made by researchers. These implied future research endeavors will contribute refined explanations of the rela tionship between ICT media, executive cognition, executive functions, and digital service innovations.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Practically, the study records its presence in the management liter ature by accounting for the intense application of technology for providing innovative IS service solutions in the interest of both long term and short-term growth of a digital SME. For example, it suggests that continuous executive engagement with ICTs through anticipating new ones, learning about them, and applying them in an SME is primary to other activities that ensure service innovation. Such engagement is therefore the dominant means for gaining and maintaining executive control of ICT media capabilities most of which are outside the organi zation and in the wider ecosystem. Controlling ICT media resources may be impossible for an SME, but this research shows that its executive can gain ICT capabilities for service innovation. Furthermore, this research suggests that gaining and maintaining executive control of ICT media capabilities is a critical means of developing competitive advantage. Among several digital SMEs that draw heavily upon ICT media, the one that implements these suggestions will develop competitiveness in ser vice innovation that cannot be copied easily by others.

The study also suggests that ICT becomes extremely essential to shaping the executive cognition toward diverse service offerings tailored to unique customer needs. Executives of digital SMEs can draw from this paper to enrich their cognitions by diversifying the ICT media learned and used for service innovation. We are talking about service innovation diversification based on diversified engagement with ICT media ahead of other traditional strategies such as marketing and operations. This research suggests that such diversification is not entirely open to the inclusion of every ICT medium. Rather, it suggests that it must be guided by the service innovation and related executive functions. Thus, engagement with diverse ICT media may be open but only to customer demands that determine the range of service innovations provided.

There are executives of digital SMEs who do not have formal tech nology knowledge and skills. They are being encouraged by this research that, as far as their cognition and functions are concerned, they do not need to be inferior to those with formal technology knowledge and skills. This research shows that innovative thinking about ICT depends on continuously engaging with ICT media (intensively and extensively) according to customer demands for digital services. It does not neces sarily depend on formal ICT background because relevant cognition is generated from the engagement ahead of formal background knowledge and experience. Thus, the formation of ICT enterprises must not be impeded by potential executives’ trepidations about inadequate formal technology educational backgrounds. There is substantial integration of ICT media with executive cognition during engagement with ICT media. This cognition is sufficient for executive functions that lead to service innovation.

Technology shaping, as this research has explained through the processes of assembling, exploiting, totalizing, technologizing, liber ating, and creating implies that executive cognition can experience technology growth or decay. Growth occurs through more learning by doing, knowing, and becoming; and decay occurs through less learning. For the composite executive (composed of two or more individuals) where individuals have specialized functions such as CEO, CTO, and CIO, cognition growth may not be possible for each individual at the same time. In such an instance, those who engage with the core pro duction technologies such as platforms and frameworks must intensify and extend their engagement with ICT media to compensate for the decay, which the others may experience. Otherwise, the cognition of each individual must be consciously enhanced to different degrees through continuous engagement to remain responsive to customer de mands for digital service innovation.

## 7. Conclusion

Our research confirms that the medium is the message, but it goe beyond the confirmation to explain how and why this proposition occurs among ICT executives. It has focused on how and why ICT media shape executive cognition to develop a technological explanation. This focu has required technological and ecological approaches that are different from the ones which have been used in previous studies of executive cognition. Based on the philosophical assumption that ICT media are material and that the materiality generates specific human agencies, the study has identified those agencies in service innovation and executive functions. The meeting of ICT media, service innovation, and executive functions have been interpreted as sufficient conditions for shaping executive cognition. Both the shaping process of internalization and the cognition are characterized as a platform. By these explanations, the paper has addressed the problem of inadequate technological explana tions of executive cognition and provided future research and practical implications.

He holds a PhD in infor mation systems from the University of Salford, UK and MBA in MIS and BSc in Business Administration (Accounting Option) with First Class Honours both from University of Ghana. He is a Senior Editor for the Electronic Journal of Information Systems in Devel oping Countries and a Deputy Editor-in-Chief for the African Journal of Information Systems. John was the immediate past head of MIS Department at the University of Ghana Business School and Ag. Director of Institutional Research and Planning for the University of Ghana. Before joining academia. he worked as a Senior ICT consultant with Deloitte & Touche, John currently serves as an ICT consultant for the Government of Ghana on ICT Policy and Planning, advising the National Development Planning Commission on ICT related issues. He is the lead consulting for Ghana’s ongoing long-term ICT Planning and Policy Development. John also serves as ICT consultant for the National Accreditation Board for Higher Education in Ghana and the Institute of Chartered Accountant of Ghana. He can be reached at jeffah@ug.edu.gh.
