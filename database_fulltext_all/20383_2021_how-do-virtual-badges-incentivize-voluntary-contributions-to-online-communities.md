## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online communities Badges Self-determination theory Goal-setting theory Econometrics Panel data Gamification Simultaneous equations model

## A B S T R A C T

Online communities rely heavily on badges to incentivize users to participate in voluntary activities. We examine the relationship between earned badges/pursued badges and contributions in the Stack Overflow question and answer community. Our main results reveal that different levels of earned and pursued badges increase the amount of subsequent answering activity. In addition, earned gold badges are the most influential in the motivation of users while earned bronze badges are the least impactful. Furthermore, the effect of badges in volves positive spillovers from higher levels of earned badges to lower ones. Our findings offer important theoretical and managerial implications for online communities

## 1. Introduction

Despite the popularity of online communities among Internet users, many communities cannot constantly motivate their members toward voluntary knowledge contribution and therefore, fail to sustain partici pation after an initial burst of involvement [1]. The lack of contribution activities from a sizable group of individuals on an ongoing basis is the main reason for the failure of online communities or, at least, for not realizing their true potential [2]. Therefore, motivating users toward ongoing contributions is the key to success across a wide range of com munities [3–5]. Given the importance of contributions for the livelihood of online communities, many communities have tried to better motivate their users through both tangible (e.g., monetary incentives) and intan gible rewards. Recently, online communities have also attempted to take advantage of various design elements such as virtual points and digital badges.

Digital badges ‒ virtual trophies awarded for helpful participation – are the hallmarks of the incentive design in online communities. They represent achievements of particular objectives in the community based on contribution activities. Many times, badges are often structured within a hierarchy (e.g., bronze, silver, and gold) in an ascending order of diffi culty in earning them. These qualitative levels provide users with a sense of progress that is similar to levels in games. Online communities use badges and leverage their levels to acknowledge contributors for their accomplishments, and therefore, bring them back to the community for further contribution [6]. Nevertheless, it remains a challenge for many online communities to design and implement these digital badges without profound knowledge of how badges actually motivate their users to contribute.

Prior research has developed and tested motivational models to understand the underlying reasons for active participation in terms of initial contribution as well as continued contribution [7–9]. However, the question of how identified motivational factors for voluntary user contributions can be energized by using design elements such as digital badges has rarely been addressed. Moreover, little is known about the significance of hierarchical levels among badges to engage users in community activities.

We aim to fill this gap in the literature by providing a nuanced un derstanding of the effect of badges on user contributions in online com munities. Specifically, we address four important questions with regard to the value of badges and the differential effects of the hierarchical badge levels for continued engagement. First, we ask the question of whether earned badges<sup>1</sup> influence the contribution behavior of users and therefore, trigger more participation from them in the periods following the badge awards. Second, do earned badges at different levels (i.e., bronze, silver, and gold) have a quantitatively different influence on the contribution behavior of users? If so, are these effects consistent with hierarchical levels? Specifically, are gold badges more influential than silver badges and are silver badges more effective than bronze badges in driving engagement? Third, because at any given instance, contributors can differ based on the levels of badges they have accumulated from their prior contributions, do incentives provided by earned badges change depending on the prior badge history of contributors? Fourth, because the pursuit of new badges as “goals” can shape users’ participation behavior, do pursued badges that belong to different levels have a differential impact on the contribution of users? If so, are the motivating effects of pursued badges similar to those of earned badges in reference to hierarchical levels? Taking all these questions together, we aim to assess the effectiveness of badges and levels among them to promote ongoing engagement in the community.

To address the questions raised above, we first theoretically discuss how earned and pursued badges induce user participation and develop hypotheses around our research questions. After that, we analyze a large panel dataset from Stack Overflow, a well-known question and answer (Q&A) community, to empirically investigate the relationship between badges and actual knowledge contributions to test our hypotheses. If we follow goal-setting theory [10], users can perceive badges as mere goals and engage in knowledge exchange to fulfill their desire to acquire more badges. Thus, a badge-seeking behavior, as represented by pursued badges, gives users the motivation to contribute to the community. However, if badges act as a controlled form of extrinsic motivation only, earned badges should not provide much incentive for further contribu tion. On the contrary, through the lens of self-determination theory (SDT) [11], we argue that the externally motivated contribution behavior induced by earned badges becomes more self-determined in periods following the badge awards. This is because earned badges facilitate the process of internalization through which users recognize the value of their contribution behavior and gradually accept it as their own. That is, external motivation provided by badges is experienced as more autonomous after users are rewarded with these accomplishment symbols. As a result, users subsequently contribute to the community in a more self-determined way. To summarize, although pursued badges provide a controlled form of extrinsic motivation, driving contributions from users in their quest for reaching goals, this motivation is progres sively internalized with the help of earned badges, which results in a more autonomous form of extrinsic motivation and further contribu tions. Hence, pursued badges and earned badges work together to facilitate the continued participation behavior among users.

With regard to earned badges, our results reveal that all three levels of badges increase the amount of subsequent answering activity from users who earn them. Even after controlling for prior badge history, users still respond to recently earned badges by choosing to participate further. In addition, our results indicate that earned gold badges are the most im pactful to motivate users while earned bronze badges are the least im pactful. Hence, the marginal influence of an earned badge increases with its hierarchical level. We also show that the contribution activity induced by an earned badge gets larger once the user obtains a badge from a higher level. Hence, there exists positive spillovers from higher levels of earned badges to lower levels of them. With regard to pursued badges, we show that the marginal influence of a badge decreases with its hierarchical level. That is, pursued gold badges are least inspiring while pursued bronze badges are most stimulating in driving contributions. Thus, earned badges and pursued badges have a reverse rank order in terms of contribution effect with respect to the hierarchical levels. Finally, when we compare earned badges and pursued badges, we observe that internalized moti vation from earned badges as predicted by SDT surpasses purely external motivation to acquire new badges as predicted by goal-setting theory, for higher levels of badges. All these findings imply that badges –both earned and pursued– and their hierarchical levels give rise to continued user participation at online communities.

Our study makes valuable contributions to the literature on online communities. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to assess and quantify the unique, and seemingly not obvious, motivational influence facilitated by badges in community participation. Our findings provide strong empirical evidence about the value of badges in promoting pro social behavior, both in pursuit to acquire them and beyond the period of acquiring them. In addition to confirming the stimulating effect of badges on contributions, our results reveal that badges belonging to different hierarchical levels have varying degrees of influence. Yet, this differential influence is also moderated by the type of badge (i.e., earned vs. pursued): The higher the level of earned badges, the higher the influence; the higher the level of pursued badges, the lower the influence. Furthermore, earning a badge from a higher level not only boosts subsequent contributions directly, but also causes contributors to be more responsive to earned badges from lower levels, thereby it influences the contribution behavior indirectly as well. Overall, our study provides valuable implications for online communities as well as for other knowledge exchange platforms that seek to promote user-generated content among members with the help of badges.

## 2. Literature Review

There is a substantial body of literature in online communities. Re searchers have made inquiries not only to understand the voluntary behavior of users but also to assess the role of various mechanisms to facilitate knowledge exchange. In this section, we confine our review to most relevant studies to articulate how this work relates to prior research on online communities, in general, and online Q&A commu nities, in particular. By comparing and contrasting with existing studies, we highlight the unique contributions of the current study.

Table 1 summarizes the major drivers of knowledge contribution to online communities. Reputation or social image is an important driver of contribution and participation in online communities [7.5]. Rooted in social exchange theory [12], reciprocity has been identified as another factor that plays a prominent role in shaping contributions [5]. Related to our study, users with attachment to a community, a strong sense of community, are more likely to engage in their com munity to help other members with their questions [13,14]. For example, [15] reported that people’s attachment to the community as a whole and their attachment to individual community members result in different levels and forms of community participation. The feeling of attachment also builds commitment, or a feeling of obligation, to the community. [3] showed that different types of community commit ment result in unique participation behaviors. Similarly, one can argue that a sense of community can lead to affect-based commitment in online Q&A communities, and this might influence users’ intentions to answer questions.

Some users may share their knowledge in online communities simply because they enjoy helping others [5]. They consider solving problems of community members challenging and fun. Demonstrating their altruistic or prosocial behavior brings internal satisfaction to knowledge contributors [16,7]. In online Q&A communities, answering questions leads to satisfaction by confirmation of the value of voluntary act [17].

Self-efficacy is another motive for contributions, which reflects an individual’s belief in his/her capacity to attain the desired performance [18,19]. An individual who has confidence in his/her ability to solve the problems of others is likely to contribute his/her knowledge. When people share their expertise, they gain confidence in their capability to be valuable to others, leading to increased self-efficacy [16,7]. In the context of Q&A communities, knowledge self-efficacy is an important determinant of users’ continuance intention to answer questions [17].

Rewards are often identified as an important motivation factor [7]. The effect of tangible rewards on knowledge contribution varied from negative [20] to nonsignificant [21] to positive [7]. [22] found that monetary incentives can increase community members’ participation only in the short term. If we view the knowledge repository of an online Q&A community as a public good, the economics literature suggests that tangible rewards or monetary incentives should not be used; monetary incentives have a negative effect on prosocial behavior [23–25]. Therefore, if virtual badges are to serve as explicit rewards only, thei effect on contribution would be doubtful.

While many prior studies have focused on the motivational factors for voluntary contribution to online communities, there exist two important research gaps. First, while prior research examined whether rewarding participants for their voluntary behavior incentivizes contributions, how design elements such as digital badges can motivate voluntary contribu tion to online communities have been rarely examined. Because tangible rewards may not be effective, many online communities today try to encourage their crowd by offering digital (intangible) rewards in the form of virtual points and badges [26,27]. Offering virtual points increases the intention to contribute among users [28], but to this day, very little research has been conducted to empirically assess the role of virtual badges on community contributions.

Second, most studies have investigated the quantity aspect of knowl edge contribution [7,8,5], but fewer studies have explored the quality aspect of knowledge sharing to understand the factors that can explain the helpfulness of contributions [28,29,5]. In fact, quality is as crucial as quantity for the viability of an online community because low-quality contributions significantly reduce the value of shared knowledge and can even cause users to withdraw from the community [28].

We aim to fill in these research gaps by answering how the use of design elements such as digital badges that are mainly awarded for quality contributions energizes voluntary user contributions in an online

Q&A community. In comparison to a reputation system based on points and ranks, which offers a vertical incentive hierarchy, badges have the feature of both a vertical and horizontal incentive hierarchy. Offering different badges with diverse rules for earning them targets and exploit users’ differences in horizontal preferences. Theoretically, apart from the prior studies, building on the arguments of goal-setting theory, that consider virtual points only as a reward contingency to drive partici pation, we study, under the rubrics of SDT, badges as a means that has an impact on contribution activities even after they are earned through the process of internalization for continued participation and transition to a more autonomous state of motivation. Furthermore, different from all these studies that examine the relationship between particular motives and voluntary behavior, we quantify the effect of earned and pursued badges to enhance the overall motive of contributors through the impact on the level of their voluntary behavior.

## 3. Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

People have basic tendencies to seek out opportunities to explore and learn [11]. When activities are inherently enjoyable, satisfying, or challenging, individuals feel an innate desire to perform the activities because they have intrinsic motivation. With intrinsic motivation, there is a natural inclination toward the activity and therefore, the behavior emanates from the self. Unfortunately, many activities are not always interesting, and therefore, behavior cannot be motivated by an intrinsic desire only. For those activities, people may require extrinsic motivation, in which the activity is performed as a means to obtain a separable outcome [30]. To better understand the role of different types of moti vation, we provide a brief overview of SDT that is closely related to our main hypotheses on the effects of earned badges, and then propose our hypotheses based on this theory.

## 3.1. Self-Determination Theory

SDT is a meta-theory of human motivation that aims to explain what forces move human beings to act in certain ways, why different conse quences arise from their behavior, and how social context plays a role in the whole process [11]. Unlike other motivation theories that treat motivation as a unitary concept that varies in amount but not kind [19, 10], SDT identifies distinct types of motivation with unique conse quences. At the core of the theory lies a distinction between autonomous motivation and controlled motivation. When people exercise a will or a feeling of choice in performing an activity, they exhibit autonomous motivation, where the behavior is experienced as emanating from one’s self. In contrast, controlled motivation expresses a feeling of compliance or being pressured to act and, consequently, the behavior is governed by forces situated outside [31]. Thus, these two types of motivation have distinctive regulatory processes and unique behavioral consequences.

Intrinsic motivation is truly regarded as autonomous. However, extrinsic motivation varies in the degree to which the regulation is felt to be autonomous versus controlled [32]. The least autonomous form of extrinsic motivation is referred to as external regulation. When a behavior is externally regulated, the person acts because of the perception of a contingency between the behavior and a consequence of the behavior. For instance, if a desire to earn a reward or avoid punishment moves the person toward an activity, it is considered as externally regulated behavior. External regulation is a typical form of extrinsic motivation contrasted with intrinsic motivation in early studies on human behavior. Because of its perceived external locus of causality, it is considered the hallmark of controlled motivation [11].

SDT posits that an externally motivated behavior becomes selfdetermined over time, under some facilitating conditions, through the process of internalization [33]. Internalization is a natural process through which individuals accept the value and regulation of the requested behavior and actively transform those into their own values and self-regulations [34]. As a result, people endorse the behavior and maintain it over time because the behavior reflects inner values and therefore becomes part of the self.

SDT describes a continuum of self-determination in motivation, as depicted in Fig. 1. On one extreme of the spectrum is extrinsic motiva tion, which varies in self-determination; on the other extreme is intrinsic motivation, which is invariantly self-determined. Among the four types of extrinsic motivation, external regulation is the most controlled form of motivation, which drives the least self-determined behavior. In contrast, integrated regulation is the least controlled (i.e., most autonomous) form of motivation driving the most self-determined behavior. Introjected regulation and identified regulation fall between these two forms of extrinsic motivation. SDT explains how external regulation is progres sively internalized through the processes of introjection, identification, and integration, which result in more autonomous motivation and more self-determined behavior.

Ryan and Connell [35] confirmed that different styles of regulations lie along the continuum proposed by SDT. He also showed that as the regulation becomes more internalized, people expend more effort, thereby to link more positive behavioral outcomes to more autonomous motivations. Subsequent studies further confirmed the association be tween greater internalization and greater effectiveness and persistence in the behavior in various settings, like performance in education [36], adherence to medications [37], long-term maintenance of weight loss [38], and environmental activism [39].

SDT argues that both intrinsic motivation and internalization of extrinsic motivation require nutriments to function effectively. The theory postulates that these nutriments as important facilitators are a satisfaction of the three basic psychological needs, namely autonomy, competence, and relatedness [31]. Autonomy refers to volition – the desire to self-determine the behavior and have freedom in its experience [33]. Competence is effectance or having the ability ‒ the desire to achieve success through personal behavior and to take responsibility and credit for the resultant outcomes [40]. Relatedness is the desire to be con nected to others in social work, to care for, and to be cared for ([41]. The social context presents supporting or inhibiting elements and therefore either facilitates or impedes motivation [11]. Social and environmental factors enhance intrinsic motivation and facilitate the internalization process to the extent that these factors satisfy the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Stated differently, the degree to which people’s intrinsic propensity toward an activity and assimilation of external regulations is an increasing function of the degree to which people feel connected to others, effective in their social world, and have a choice [33]. A facilitating social context providing support for the satisfaction of psychological needs promotes not only better internali zation of existing regulations, but also the transition to a higher stage of internalization over time [33].

Researchers studying extrinsic motivation and its internalization through the lens of SDT have demonstrated not only the importance of the basic psychological needs for the internalization process, but also the positive effect of internalized extrinsic motivation on behavioral out comes. For example, [42] showed that when parents provided support for competence, relatedness, and autonomy with regard to their children’s homework, their children internalized school-related activities, which predicted the children’s school performance. Satisfaction of the needs of employees for autonomy, competence, and relatedness at work was positively related to work engagement [43]. [44] found that when med ical students internalized the values presented in the course and became more autonomously motivated for learning the course material, their positive behavior persisted even after the end of the course. Overall, empirical studies in various domains adopting the regulatory styles of extrinsic motivation in SDT have confirmed that (i) people transform values and regulations into their own and therefore, internalize extrinsic motivation when they feel that their needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are supported by the social environment and (ii) a higher level of internalization is associated with greater involvement, greater behavioral persistence, and better performance. Therefore, SDT is ex pected to provide a useful framework to understand how badges that were initially considered a means of controlled form of extrinsic motivation may enhance users’ motivation to contribute more even after acquisition through the process of internalization.

## 3.2. Hypotheses Development

Although intrinsic motivation can be a driving force for some, many users of Q&A communities are likely to have extrinsic reasons for contribution. Answering technical questions in Stack Overflow is not a simple task. It requires time and effort to comprehend the questions and relate them to personal knowledge. People are not intrinsically moti vated for difficult tasks [32]. Thus, it is important to examine the kinds of extrinsic motivation that users of Q&A communities may have to understand how (i) the four types of extrinsic motivation, under the rubric of SDT, drive voluntary behavior and (ii) virtual badges enhance extrinsic motivation for each type.

External regulation can be a motivation for participation in com munity activities when users receive some explicit rewards for their participation. Virtual badges can serve as a type of rewards that are contingent on performance. External regulation, or goal pursuits, is particularly motivational for Q&A users because only quality (i.e., performance-contingent) contributions can lead to badges. Thus, users exert time and effort to answer questions basically to satisfy the reward contingency [11].

Some users can be moved by introjected regulation for community participation. They contribute because they want to experience a sense of worthiness by demonstrating their knowledge and as a result, improve their status within the community. Earned badges serve as a vehicle to signal a user’s reputation. Badges, as individual achievements, communicate reputation information of a focal user, which helps others digest the quality of his or her contributions. Reputation plays a crucial role to induce good prosocial behavior [45] and is a strong motivator for active participation in community activities [46]. Thus, users contribute to the Q&A community because they perceive that badges will boost their image and status.

Some users have identified regulation for contribution. With this type of extrinsic motivation, users consciously value the goal of the Q&A community to be a repository for sharing knowledge related to pro gramming. They identify with this purpose and become involved in achieving this goal by answering questions posted by other users. These people have strong emotional attachment to the community because being associated with the community is quite fulfilling for them [3,7, 47]. That is, users contribute to the community because they recognize the value of the community and want to identify with it. With virtual badges, users can better understand the goal of an online Q&A com munity, which helps them develop emotional attachment.

Some Q&A users may rely on integrated regulation to take part in community activities. These people not only identify with the impor tance of active participation for maintaining a strong community, but also see contributing to the community as central to their true identity [48]. As a result, they devote time to helping others with their questions because they care about the well-being of others. This type of motivation can be characterized as having an affective commitment to the com munity [49]. People with strong affective commitment exert their effort to respond to questions posted by other community users [3]. Many virtual badges are designed to recognize user’s altruistic behavior and commitment, which helps their affective commitment to an online Q&A community. In summary, many factors that have been identified in prior research as motives for voluntary behavior can be mapped to different categories of motivation under the rubric of SDT, and these categories of motivation are all cultivated by virtual badges.

More importantly, badges once earned, create facilitating conditions for contributors to nurture their psychological needs. Earned badges help users signal their unique identity, efficiency, and community belonging through the satisfaction of psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, respectively. First, earned badges reflect contributors’ skills and interests that result from their own choices, and therefore, convey their self-identity to other community users. When contributing users perceive that other users are cognizant of their self identity, their feeling of autonomy is improved because their self selected contribution activities lead to badges. Second, earned badges convey positive feedback to contributors, and this feedback implies effectance. When users gain confidence that their contribution can solve others’ problems, their self-efficacy improves [50], which results in increased perceptions of competence. Third, earned badges communi cate the message of caring for the community, thereby increasing the feeling of connectedness to the community and its goals and objectives. When users feel related to the community, their community identifica tion improves. Thus, consistent with conceptualization based on SDT, we consider earning a badge as a contextual event that allows contrib utors to feel more autonomous, competent, and related; thereby, satis fying their innate needs [11]. These three dimensions of needs that are propelled by earned badges also reflect the antecedents of satisfaction identified in prior research in online communities [8,47].

Through needs satisfaction, earned badges foster greater integration of extrinsic regulations for contribution activities. That is, to the extent that Q&A users satisfy their innate psychological needs while they engage in contribution activities, they will be energized to contribute more to the community. According to SDT, when the social context supports autonomy, competence, and relatedness, the level of intro jected, identified, and integrated motivation increases [32]. With respect to online contribution behavior, consistent with SDT, earned badges promote the fulfillment of the three basic psychological needs and consequently, enhance the internalization of extrinsic motivation, which thereby results in more autonomous motivation and contribution. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1. (H1): Earned badges are positively associated with contribution.

The hierarchical levels that exist among badge types are likely to lead to a qualitatively different influence on the needs of contributors, and thus their impact on contribution activities can also be quantitatively different. A bronze badge, the lowest badge level, is likely to motivate the user less than a silver badge, which in turn motivates less than a gold badge, the highest badge level. This is because the higher the level of the badge earned, the greater the extent of satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness that enhance intrinsic motivation and facilitate the internalization process. First, an earned badge is a means to acknowledge the value of a user’s freely chosen contribution and a higher level badge enhances the sense of self-initiation more – of being the origin of the behavior [51]. Once the user perceives herself as the source of success, her feeling of autonomy improves. Second, an earned badge essentially delivers positive feedback to the user, signifying her effectance in carrying out the activity. Positive feedback increases perceived competence [52]. A higher level of earned badge increasingly boosts the user’s feeling of competence. People adopt socially valuable activities more when they feel efficacious with respect to these activities [11]. Third, a badge prompts a feeling of attachment or belonging to the community, and support for this feeling is likely to be provoked more with a higher level of earned badge.

As the degree to which internalization of extrinsic motivation and enhancement of intrinsic motivation are positively correlated with the desired target behavior, and as this is a function of the degree of fulfillment of the three psychological needs [33], we posit that a higher level of earned badge will induce more contribution. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2. (H2): A higher level of earned badge is positively associated with more contribution than a lower level of earned badge.

Earned badges provide necessary nutriments for engagement in community activities. In addition to having different degrees of in fluence on behavior as argued in H2, badges of different levels influ ence each other’s effect. That is, the magnitudes of badge effects on contributions vary depending on the badge history of users. Given the nested nature of hierarchical badge levels, a user can initially earn only a bronze badge as a result of his/her contributions. As the internalization of regulation for the behavior is likely to be in an early stage ‒ like introjection ‒ for this user, the earned bronze badge may not highly motivate the user to participate in community activities because the user has not yet synthesized the value of his/her contri butions into own value to engage in those activities on a large scale. However, once the user is rewarded with a silver badge for the con tributions to the community and moves up in the hierarchy of ac complishments, this achievement causes the user to experience greater satisfaction of his/her needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness because it would reflect a clear sense of progress [31]. As a result, the user grasps the meaning of activity for his or her goals and values and subsequently internalizes extrinsic motivation more, resulting in a higher level of internalization – like identified regulation – and more autonomous contribution behavior. Self-endorsed regu lations are associated with higher commitment and performance [33]. After this point, even a bronze badge serves as a stronger motivator because the user has identified the importance of contributing to the community and has accepted the contribution behavior as his or her own, which enhances her attachment to the community [4]. Once the user earns a gold badge, psychological needs are supported to the fullest extent because it is the highest level of accomplishment. This satisfaction results in more internalization of the external motivation for contribution activities – like integrated regulation. Thus, the user not only internalizes the extrinsic regulation more but also experi ences that internalization is fully integrated with other aspects of the self. Following this achievement, an earned bronze badge gives a strong boost to the user’s motivation because the contribution behavior is felt not only to be more valuable, but also to be in greater harmony with other user values and identities.

Given the arguments in SDT, the extent of satisfaction and the extent of internalization will be greater with higher levels of prior milestones like earned badges. Therefore, the positive influence of a bronze badge on contribution behavior will be greater if the user has received a silver badge before, and this influence will be even greater if the user has already earned a gold badge. Similarly, the effect of a silver badge will be greater if the user has a gold badge. A higher level of earned badge will not only induce more contribution directly, but also stimulate more involvement indirectly through its positive effect on the badges of a lower level to be earned subsequently. Thus, there is a positive spillover from badges of higher levels to badges of lower levels. In a sense, a badge level from which a new badge is earned serves as a facilitating factor for more contribution. The greater the number of facilitating factors, the greater the extent of internalization and correlation with the target behavior [31]. Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 3. (H3): A higher level of earned badge creates a positive

spillover for a lower level of earned badge for contribution.

As noted above, rewards that are contingent on performance in the form of external regulation can serve as an important factor to motivate users for participation in online community activities. The function and role of external regulation on behavior can be well explained by goal setting theory, which posits that goal pursuits are the efficient causes of behavior [10]. In addition to enhanced incentives that result from earned badges, the lure of a next badge as a “goal” can influence community users and motivate them to contribute further. Because of its distinct role, we define a pursued badge as the badge that is aimed to be acquired by a user. Building on the arguments of goal-setting theory [10], psychology liter ature has extensively studied goal-pursuit behavior in various settings to understand how goals influence behavior [53–55]. These studies have collectively documented empirical evidence that performance of in dividuals improves in the presence of goals. In addition, prior studies have shown that specific and achievable goals better move individuals toward the desired behavior. As badges serve as specific reference points for contributors to target, they can act as effective “goals” and result in goal-directed contribution behavior among contributors. Thus, we expect that the presence of pursued badges as an achievable goal are positively associated with a user’s contribution just like earned badges.

However, because there are different levels of badges, users may target different levels of badges as a goal. Thus, it is natural that each level of pursued badge has a differential influence on contribution behavior. We note that the distance between two consecutive badge levels in a badge hierarchy gets progressively larger. This implies that the amount of contribution needed to earn a gold badge after earning a silver badge is greater than the amount of contribution needed to earn a silver badge after earning a bronze badge, and this amount is, in turn, greater than the amount of contribution needed to earn a bronze badge. The goal-gradient hypothesis under goal-setting theory suggests that users exert more effort to achieve goals when the distance to goals is shorter [27]. Thus, it is much easier for an individual to pursue and acquire a badge in a lower level by participation in contribution activ ities than that of a badge in a higher level. That is, a bronze badge as a next “goal” is more within reach than a silver or a gold badge, and consequently pursued bronze badges are likely to be more motivating in inducing more contributions from goal-oriented users relative to pur sued silver badges. Similarly, pursued silver badges are expected to be more influential than pursued gold badges to facilitate contributions. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4. (H4): A lower level of pursued badge is positively associated with more contribution than a higher level of pursued badge.

## 4. Data Description

## 4.1. Sample Construction

Our data come from Stack Overflow, which is the most popular Q&A site for programmers. Stack Overflow was launched in August 2008. Since its inception, it has attracted more than 1.1 million users and these users have generated more than 3 million questions and 6.1 million answers, with an answered rate greater than 80%. We employed the Stack Exchange APIs to crawl data from August 1, 2008 to January 1, 2012. We excluded the observations in 2008 because user activities were relatively unstable because of the nascence of Stack Overflow. Our final sample period included three years of data from January 1, 2009 to January 1, 2012.

The initial assessment of the crawled panel dataset revealed that a very large fraction of the registered users was inactive during the entire three years. For instance, about 40% of users had not engaged in any activity after registration. To assess the impact of badges on users with some level of contribution, we excluded users whose cumulative repu tation score was never greater than 200 throughout the three-year sample period. According to Stack Overflow, users with a cumulative reputation score less than 200 are not tracked in the ranking system. Note that this cutoff does not imply that we only considered users with ongoing contribution patterns over time. Our sample also included users who might have a burst of activity in one week and be rather inactive in the remainder of the three years. In fact, a user can earn 200 reputation points from contribution activities in one day. With this cutoff, our final dataset consisted of 46,571 users.

The scraped data include cross-sectional user profiles and a userlevel panel dataset. A user profile contains a user ID, display name, self-reported age, and self-reported location in an unstructured format. The user-level panel dataset consists of a detailed history of reputation, types of badges received, and types of activities engaged with a time stamp, which is at the seconds level. Our unit of observation is at the “user-week” level. That is, for each user, we have a history of weekly contributions in our panel dataset.

## 4.2. Dependent Variable: answering questions

At Stack Overflow, there are two basic contribution activities. People can either ask questions about their problems or answer questions posted by others. In addition to these two activities, people can participate in subordinate activities: community users can edit or comment on questions that exist and answers to clarify/improve them. In this study, we focus on the answering activity only because it is the most vital ac tivity for the success of any Q&A community. In addition to being the most valuable activity, getting users to answer questions is relatively more challenging than getting users to ask questions. Users are inher ently motivated to submit their questions because they are likely to consider answers to their questions as a direct benefit. Hence, they may not need any other incentives to post their questions. Plus, to encourage users to ask more questions is not the objective. Q&A communities, first and foremost, aim to incentivize community users to answer questions that exist such that people derive value from the community. However, users who answer questions about problems faced by others do not earn any direct benefit in return for their time and effort. Therefore, it is more important to offer them some psychological stimulus, like badges, to engage them to provide answers. To that end, we assess the impact of badges on the level of participation in the answering activity. Hence, our dependent variable is the number of answers per user and per week.

## 4.3. Independent Variables: badges

In Stack Overflow, a user is awarded a badge when his or her participation reaches a threshold, which can have both quality and quantity dimensions. The earned badges appear on the user’s profile page along with his or her posts. Therefore, badges can induce not only direct psychological effects, but also indirect effects through a social image. As of January 2012, Stack Overflow had 73 badges, some of which have all three levels while others do not. Badges are awarded for contributions using criteria that incorporate some quality aspect. Peers can vote up and down on each answer to assess its quality. An aggregate score indicates how many users found the answer useful. In addition to peers, the user who asked the question can confirm the answer’s quality by accepting an answer as correct. Hence, both dimensions of quality are included in badge definitions.

Badges are classified by Stack Overflow into three levels (gold, silver, and bronze) in terms of the hierarchical difficulty to obtain them. For instance, “Nice Answer” is a bronze badge (answered a question found useful by 10 people), “Good Answer” (answered a question found useful by 25 people) is a silver badge, and “Great Answer” is a gold badge (answered a question found useful by 100 people). As highlighted by this example, a user must have earned a bronze badge before they qualify for a silver or gold badge. A lower level badge is not replaced by badges in higher levels; instead, the user keeps all the badges earned. Badges and their levels provide us with an opportunity to examine the effectiveness of a hierarchical badge system to induce user participation.

## 4.4. Control Variables

Control variables consist of (i) tenure, (ii) reputation earned in the previous time period, and (iii) cumulative reputation earned till the pre vious time period. Among the control variables, tenure captures users experience. By including tenure, we eliminate the alternative explanation that users participate more with experience or over time. Furthermore, reputation earned in the previous time period captures users’ short-term engagement patterns in terms of contributions. Reputation itself is an aggregate measure that reflects the amount and quality of engagement on Stack Overflow. A user with more free time is likely to participate more and make more contributions. By the inclusion of this control variable, we exclude the alternative explanation based on free time and other shortterm unobservable factors. Similarly, cumulative reputation captures users’ long-term engagement patterns and control for factors related to continued participation behavior, such as the learning effect.<sup>2</sup>

## 5.1. The Spillovers among Earned Badges: a Sub-Sample Analysis by Badge History

In the previous subsection, our analysis did not address the question of whether the stimulus provided by recently earned badges is influ enced by the types of badges already accumulated by contributors. To see whether this type of spillovers exist from higher levels of earned badges to lower levels of earned badges and to formally test H3, we estimated our main specification in (1) for each stage of badge history separately using observations from subsamples. First, for each user, we partitioned observations based on the history of badges into four stages: (i) no badges earned, (ii) only bronze badges earned, (iii) only silver and bronze badges earned, and (iv) all levels of badges earned. Next, we reran the fixed-effects model using the subsamples from the last three stages.

Table 5 presents the results with regard to the spillovers among earned badges. In model 1, we included all observations from all users after they received their first bronze badge but before they earned their first silver badge. Similarly, in model 2, we included all observations from all users after they got their first silver badge but before they received their first gold badge. Finally, in model 3, we included all observations from all users after they received their first gold badge. As shown in Table 5, the esti mated coefficients of badges are always positive and significant across different stages of badge history. Thus, badges drive user participation regardless of the kinds of badges accumulated before. More importantly, our results reveal that the amount of contribution induced by an earned badge gets larger once users obtain a badge from a higher difficulty level, which implies spillovers among levels of badges. Specifically, the co efficients of a bronze badge in models 1, 2, and 3 are 0.406, 0.654, and 0.748, respectively. Hence, although earning a bronze badge always boosts users’ incentive to subsequently answer more questions, users with at least a silver badge answer more questions than users with only bronze badges. Similarly, users with at least a gold badge answer more questions than users with only bronze and silver badges. This relationship also holds for silver badges. The coefficients of a silver badge in models 2 and 3 are 0.463 and 0.769, respectively. Thus, although earning a silver badge al ways incentivizes users to subsequently answer more questions, users with at least a gold badge answer more questions than users with only bronze and silver badges. Thus, our results fully support H3.<sup>7</sup>

## 5.3. Robustness Checks

We perform a number of tests to confirm our findings and to rule out alternative explanations. First, although no direct monetary reward exists to reimburse users for their contributions, users can receive in direct benefits that accrue from their contributions to the community. In particular, some contributors might use Stack Overflow as a platform to signal their ability and competence to potential employers. Being recognized as a knowledgeable programmer with many badges on pro file can help the user get a new job and/or advance his/her career. Hence, some users may spend their time and effort to contribute high quality answers not only because of their motivations fostered by earned badges, but also due to indirect monetary rewards that result from spillover effects to the labor markets. Specifically, users who would like to signal their competence to prospective employers may be more active in the community when they seek a job. During these active periods, users are likely to answer more questions and earn more badges. Hence, the job-seeking status of community participants may confound our results. Although we cannot directly observe these users, we know that a group of participants on Stack Overflow is also users of “Stack Overflow Career,” an online labor market for programmers.<sup>9</sup> These users may have active seasons that can bias our estimations. We identified that 2,071 users (out of 46,571 users) in our dataset participated in the Stack Overflow Career. To ensure that our results with regard to the impact of earned badges are not driven by the group of career-focused contribu tors, we exclude users who participated in Stack Overflow Career from our sample and rerun our econometric analyses. We present the results in Table A1. From the results of model 1, we conclude that all three levels of earned badges are positive and significant (supporting H1), and there is a hierarchical order in impact in terms of the difficulty of levels (supporting H2)<sup>10</sup>. The results of models 2, 3, and 4 indicate that there are significant spillovers among badges (supporting H3). Hence, our findings pertaining to all three hypotheses testing regarding earned badges remain qualitatively the same after excluding the Stack Overflow Career users.

Second, contributions to the community can be driven by some other unobserved factors, not necessarily by earned badges. This can be particularly problematic if these factors are also correlated with earned badges. As many of these potential factors are likely to affect users contributions in the current period as well as the previous period, using a lagged dependent variable approach provides a way to account for other factors not controlled in the estimation model. To address the concern about omitted variables driving our results, we estimate our main specification after the incorporation of the contribution level of each user in period t-1 as an explanatory variable in period t, namely lagged answer. The estimation results are given in Table A2. From the results, H1 and H2 are fully supported and H3 is partially supported, even after controlling for unobserved factors using lagged dependent variables.<sup>1112</sup> All these findings increase our confidence in the validity of our results regarding the value of earned badges on subsequent contributions.

Third, each contributor can go through different phases of learning and contributing over time. One may argue that as users gain experience by contributing to the community, they learn how to make effective contributions and consequently become more capable of answering questions over time. The user fixed effects control for unobserved timeinvariant factors but cannot control for learning that can vary over time. Hence, not controlling for this learning effect may lead to a bias in estimations. As a user’s capability improves with contributions, and because the user with more contributions has a higher cumulative repu tation, cumulative reputation can effectively serve as a proxy for learning, mitigating its bias. Therefore, we use the proxy variable of cumulative reputation in our estimations to control for different levels of learning of users. We confirmed that our results are robust and that badges are not picking up the residual effect of learning. Hence, capability through learning cannot be an alternative explanation for the observed results.

Fourth, the observational data we use may make it a challenge to establish causal effects in a strict sense. Nevertheless, econometric tests and methods provide us a way to evaluate the existence of causal in fluence. One common robustness check for causality in this context is the Granger causality test. The Granger causality test is a statistical tool to determine whether one time series causes another time series or vice versa [57]. We employed the panel-level Granger causality test [58] to untangle the causal effect between badges and contributions. The time variable is defined as a user’s tenure (in weeks) in Stack Overflow. For each user, we run an autoregressive model that incorporates the indi vidual fixed effect, using the number of badges a user earned in the previous week as a predictor to predict the user’ answering activity. We then compute the average Wald statistics using individual Granger causality tests and test its statistical significance. Our results suggest that earned badges Granger-cause the change in subsequent contributions to the community (χ<sup>2</sup>= 646 and $p = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . This finding confirms and reinforces our conjecture that receiving badges indeed causes users to answer more questions.

Fifth, in our main specification, we control for the effect of user experience by the inclusion tenure and cumulative reputation. However, if community experience has a nonlinear impact on contributions, our badge variables may pick up the residual effect of experience, which results in a bias in the estimates of badge effects. To capture a possible nonlinear relationship between user experience and contributions, we add the squared terms of tenure and cumulative reputation to our main specifi cation given in Eq. Overall, our results remain qualitatively the same, which confirm that the effects of earned badges are not caused by any nonlinear effect of experience.

Sixth, to test the effects of earned badges on contributions, we esti mate the impact of badges received in the previous period t-1 on the user contributions in the current period t. It is possible that the badges earned in periods before t-1, also have some impact on contributions in the current period. ## 6. Discussion

In this paper, we examined the impacts of digital badges on user contributions to online Q&A communities in the context of Stack

Overflow. Specifically, we assessed the extent to which users are incen tivized by badges in answering questions of other community members, both in pursuit of these accomplishment symbols and after receiving them.

Consistent with the theoretical development through the lens of SDT and its process of internalization, our results reveal that all three levels of earned badges (i.e., gold, silver, and bronze) increase the amount of successive contributions. In addition, the impetus provided by newly received badges is so strong that even after controlling for previously earned badges, the marginal impact is still significantly positive. In addition to documenting the contribution-boosting role of badges, our results imply that earned gold badges are the most impactful in moti vating users, while earned bronze badges are the least impactful. Thus, the magnitudes of earned badge effects increase with the difficulty in hierarchical levels. Furthermore, our results indicate that the positive influences of all three levels of badges are not limited to initial phases of users’ community involvement. Hence, badges induce the desired behavior of community participation in different phases. Finally, we show that the answering activity induced by a badge in a given level increases once users obtain a badge from a higher level. Hence, there are positive spillovers from higher levels of earned badges to lower levels of earned badges. As for the pursued badges, consistent with the argument of goal-setting theory, our results reveal that the magnitudes of badge effects decrease with the difficulty in hierarchical levels. That is, pur sued gold badges are the least inspiring while pursued bronze badges are the most stimulating in driving contributions. Hence, earned badges and pursued badges display opposite rank orderings in terms of contribution effects with respect of hierarchical levels. Based on all these results, we conclude that badges –both earned and pursued– and the hierarchical levels among them are the true forces behind ongoing user participation and continued engagement at Stack Overflow.

Our study makes the following important contributions to the growing literature on online communities. First, to the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to distinguish and empirically verify the significance of both earned badges and pursued badges in motivating knowledge con tributors for participation as well as the pivotal role played by the hierar chical badge system in inducing ongoing participation among community members. Notably, prior studies examined other rewards mechanism such as virtual points [28] or considered badges as reward contingencies [59]. We not only show that earned badges are strong motivators for contribu tions, they are also instrumental to motivate users in different phases of community involvement. Our findings confirm that a virtual badge is more than just an explicit reward, a controlled form of extrinsic motivation, but is a way to facilitate the internalization of various forms of extrinsic motivation. Thus, unlike the prior studies related to badges in which badges are treated as a motivating factor ex ante, we consider them as motivating factor ex post. Our study also encompasses both quantity and quality aspects of contributions because badges are mainly awarded for high-quality contributions. We also highlight the spillovers that arise from higher levels of earned badges to lower levels of them, which sheds light on how different levels of earned and pursued badges vary in terms of influ ence on contribution behavior.

Second, we bring together seemingly competing theories of motiva tion, namely SDT and goal-setting theory, in an effort to better understand the effect of badges in explaining voluntary contributions. Building on SDT, we present a theoretical framework to argue how contribution behavior becomes self-determined with the help of earned badges through the process of internalization. Our framework based on goal-setting theory also accounts for the goal-directed behavior of users who engage in community activities in a quest to receive more badges. Because contri butions in the pursuit of badges is a means to earn more badges, and contributions after earning badges make it easier to pursue new badges, continued engagement is driven by participation enabled by both earned badges and pursued badges.

Third, because badges are heavily used in gamified systems to moti vate people to engage in a desired behavior in nongame contexts [60], our findings implicitly lend support to the importance of hierarchical badge system for gamification.<sup>16</sup> We show that badges boost users’ motivations, creating a reinforcing loop between pursuing and earning them and making contributions, thereby leading to sustained engagement in the desired behavior over time. Therefore, our findings can also contribute to the literature on gamification that calls for a better understanding of effective design of gamification elements as well as the quantification of the value of a gamified approach [61].

Our study provides valuable managerial insights into the question of how to design a hierarchical badge system for greater engagement over time. Because higher levels of earned badges result in continued participation to a greater extent, online communities should strive for more users to achieve superior accomplishment symbols. Getting more users to acquire silver and gold badges is important because higher levels of earned badges also boost the influence of lower levels of earned badges due to positive spillovers. Hence, online communities should take advantage of direct and indirect positive effects of higher badge levels to facilitate ongoing contributions. A badge system can be resigned both horizontally and vertically to achieve greater involvement among community users. As for the horizontal design, more badges of all levels (namely bronze, silver, and gold) with new rules can be intro duced to better target the users with various interests and skills. Simila to product differentiation, badge differentiation can make some badges more attractive to pursue to different user segments. The fact that Stack Overflow has continuously introduced new badges over time confirms the value of horizontal differentiation for community engagement. However, a potential issue of cannibalization has to be carefully assessed while designing the rules for the new badges to make sure that new badges do not reduce the appeal of existing badges. As for the vertical design, Stack Overflow can enrich the current hierarchical badge system with badges from higher levels, like platinum and diamond, to keep contributors increasingly engaged in the community, similar to having various levels in computer games. Users who have already earned gold badges can be incentivized to stay focused to contribute to the com munity. Even if a new level of badges may not increase contributions from users who are just pursuing to earn badges, for users who contribute for more internalized reasons, a new level of badges would boost subsequent contributions upon earning them. Furthermore, a new badge level can also be useful for veteran community members because users with a large tenure tend to be motivated less by earned badges as compared to new users. In addition, users with a large cumulative number of badges are incentivized less by earned badges as compared to users with a small cumulative number of badges. Hence, with higher badge levels, the Q&A community can mitigate the undesirable effect of tenure and badge history on contributions, or at least alleviate their effects.

Our study also provides insights into the recent phenomenon of gamification [61]. The popularity of gamification as a new engagement model has taken off with Foursquare as its success was largely attributed to its use of badges [62]. Today, organizations of all sizes and types are embracing game-based thinking and design to engage their audiences to achieve their goals, including innovation, customer support, education, and community participation. Badges can be utilized within a gamified incentive mechanism to promote large-scale participation in a desired behavior in various online communities. These accomplishment symbols can motivate people by presenting practical challenges, encourage them to stay active as they progress through hierarchical levels, and get people emotionally attached to the community. However, using badges to ach ieve engagement has to be aligned with people’s motivations to signify the achievement of meaningful goals. In July 2011, Google introduced Google News Badges to encourage people to read news articles about their fa vorite topics. Offering more than 500 badges and different levels among badges, Google aimed at increasing the readership of the news articles. However, the project has failed miserably and eventually discontinued because it has not addressed the question of why people would value these badges. Hence, gamified solutions that leverage on badges should first understand the motivations of people to design badges to symbolize progression to mastery. These awards should be considered as signs that represent meaningful achievements, not as achievements themselves. The success of Stack Overflow in using badges to encourage people to partic ipate in voluntary activities lies in understanding the motivations and goals of its target audience and designing effective badges to tap into these aspects. Earning badges gives users an opportunity to feel accomplished, bringing them back to the community while hierarchical levels among badges provide users with a sense of progress in their accomplishments.

## 7. Concluding Remarks

Our study is not without limitations. First, we acknowledge that it is very difficult to isolate the impact of a specific badge in our setting. As users can earn multiple badges in a given time period, the effects of earned badges are likely to be confounded with each other. Therefore, we assessed the average impact of a badge based on its level using various econometric models. Similarly, it is possible that the badges recognizing the answering activity may have a stronger effect on our dependent variable (i.e., the number of answers) than other types of badges. Nevertheless, we have argued that the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness can be nurtured by any type of earned badges, which lead to the better internalization of extrinsic motivation and the positive effect of earned badges. Thus, although the magnitude of the effect may be stronger for answer-related badges than for nonanswer-related badges, any type of earned badge is to have a signifi cant effect on contributions.

Second, although our study empirically confirms the value of offering virtual accomplishment symbols in providing stimuli for ongoing partic ipation and engagement, due to the nature of observational data, we cannot show the detailed link between intrinsic motivational factors/ internalization of extrinsic factors and badges. To propose an optimal hierarchical badge system, one has to understand the underlying psy chological constructs that are triggered by earned badges in motivating users to contribute to the online Q&A community. More research based on survey and experimental methodologies is needed to assess how earned badges support the relationship between the satisfaction of three kinds of psychological needs and different regulatory styles in various phases of internalization.

Third, because Stack Overflow is not a discussion forum but a com munity for questions about actual problems, we focused on the answering activity, which is the most vital contribution activity for online Q&A communities. As users can participate in the community by asking ques tions and editing or commenting on existing questions and answers, it would be interesting to study whether badges can also incentivize these other community activities.

Fourth, because our data are crawled from the registered users on Stack Overflow, we do not have any information on lurkers, who benefit from the site by reading posts but never contribute by answering ques tions. Analyzing how badges can turn lurkers into contributors offers another interesting research direction.

Lastly, despite many robustness checks that we performed, we cannot account for all the sources of individual heterogeneity, particularly the effects of time-varying unobservables. For instance, users may be different goals in terms of pursued badges, and their motivation may change over time. While some may pursue a higher level of badge after earning a bronze badge, others may want to collect more bronze badges. In other cases, such behaviors may also change at different times even for the same user.

Overall, our study aims to enhance our understanding of the role of virtual badges in motivating users to maintain their contribution to online communities. Today, we witness a growing number of online communities and social media covering diverse topics and activities while users’ mental and physical resources are limited by their available time and energy. In the end, these services have to compete with each other to secure attention and contribution from users. While these ser vices experiment with many different tools and gamified techniques to achieve their goal, our understanding of their mechanism based on sound theory has been hampered by few empirical studies using the objectively measured secondary dataset. We hope that our study paves the way for more studies using a large-scale dataset to better understand online user behavior.
