# Preventing relapse to information technology addiction through weakening reinforcement: A self-regulation perspective

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Information technology addiction Relapse Reinforcement mechanism Self-regulation, mindfulness Self-control

## A B S T R A C T

Information technology addicts often exhibit a high rate of relapse, which reflects the failure of previous recovery approaches. However, research on relapse to information technology addiction has been largely neglected, and few means have been developed to address this issue. To fill this research gap, this study identifies essential factors for preventing relapse to information technology addiction with a self-regulation perspective. A longi tudinal online survey is conducted. The results show that mindfulness and self-control contribute to reduced urges, which, in turn, prevent relapse. This study provides useful insights by expanding our understandings of relapse to information technology addiction.

## 1. Introduction

While advancements in information technology (IT) provide real benefits to various areas of life such as recreation, e-commerce, and telecommuting [e.g., 1–3], addictive behaviors may also emerge. In line with previous research [e.g., 4], IT addiction describes a psychological state of maladaptive dependency on using a technology. When people become addicted, they may suffer from many unwanted consequences. For instance, addiction to social networking sites is shown to foster task distraction, which further impairs work performance [5]. WeChat (a popular social networking tool in China) addiction, as another form of IT addiction, is found to be harmful to users’ mental, physical, and social health [6]. Realizing the misery of such consequences is likely to impel addicts to quit addiction [7].

Meanwhile, previous research has indicated that addicts who have reduced their usage to a normal level may easily fall into an addictive state again, and IT addicts show a similar high rate of relapse [8]. For instance, much evidence has shown that the relapse rate among alco holics can be as high as 95% [9]. Anecdotal evidence<sup>1</sup> also shows that over two-thirds of heavy IT addicts cannot completely eliminate their addiction. It indicates that addictive behaviors emerge repeatedly, even many interventions are provided. The serious outcomes of relapse to IT addiction should not be ignored. A recent social news<sup>2</sup> showed that students who have ever performed satisfactorily are dismissed from universities due to a relapse to IT addiction after a three-month quitting effort to quit. Generally, the term “relapse” describes the tendency to revert to previous patterns of excessive use after periods of control or abstinence [10]. Relapse signifies that previous efforts to overcome addiction have been ineffective, which indicates the failure of recovery approaches. Only addicts who completely desist from their addiction without relapse can be considered as having a successful recovery approach [11]. Given that relapse poses a great challenge for successful recovery approaches of IT addiction, it is important to discern how to effectively prevent it.

In recent years, scholars have shown increasing concerns over IT addiction. Unlike earlier studies which have traditionally assumed IT usage is positive and beneficial to users [e.g., 12], new research efforts have started to examine the dark side of using ITs. Studies have emphasized the negative consequences of IT use [e.g., 13], explored ways to measure IT addiction [14], and revealed predictors of these issues [e.g., 15]. However, despite considerable merits of these studies, more efforts are needed to deepen our knowledge about IT addiction, as what has been achieved with research on substance addiction.

Many studies have attempted to reveal factors for preventing relapse to substance addiction [e.g., 16]. In contrast, little research has been undertaken on relapse to IT addiction. The need for enriching the un derstanding of this area has become progressively urgent, as the relapse problem grows larger and implies more failure or ineffectiveness of previous interventions. Noting these limitations in information systems (IS) research has convinced us that a conceptualization of relapse to IT addiction and an interpretation of the prevention process can help to fill the gaps in the literature and contribute to providing effective interventions.

Accordingly, this study aims to first conceptualize relapse to IT addiction based on a review of previous research. As relapse prevention has been regarded as a process of self-regulation [16, 18], we use a self-regulation perspective to identify methods for preventing relapse through weakening reinforcement, which is a key mechanism in the formation of addiction and relapse. Other factors that may predict addiction are controlled in our research model. A longitudinal survey is conducted. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. IT addiction

Compared with a lot of research that addresses the positive aspects of IT use [e.g., 19–22], scientific understandings on IT addiction are still evolving. Our review shows that the main literature can be classified into three categories (see Table 1).

Several studies have attempted to propose a number of symptoms to identify IT addiction. Pathological symptoms including behavioral salience, withdrawal symptoms, conflict, relapse and reinstatement can be used to differentiate addictive behavior from high engagement [25]. Symptoms like cognitive salience, euphoria, and tolerance are periph eral criteria which cannot capture the pathological essence [25]. Moreover, the behavioral addiction perspective indicates that symptoms of IT addiction should include salience, withdrawal, conflict, tolerance, and relapse and reinstatement [4]. Research also makes efforts to develop scales to measure IT addiction. For instance, the scale of Generalized Problematic Internet Use Scale (GPIUS) measures Internet addiction [24], and a scale with 5 items has been used to measure social networking website addiction [31].

More recently, IS research has shed light on the predictors and subsequent negative consequences of IT addiction. To understand the formation of IT addiction, research has focused on the roles of psycho logical characteristics and demographics of IT users with traditional descriptive approaches [39–42]. The literature also uses theory-guided approach to explain the underlying mechanism of IT addiction. For instance, IS scholars find that reinforcement theory is essential in explaining IT addiction [e.g., 33,34]. Addictive behavior derives from a reinforcement mechanism in which the reward process gives rise to addictive levels of engagement [43]. The reward process includes gratification and mood enhancement (i.e., positive reinforcement), as well as relief from negative emotions (i.e., negative reinforcement) [44]. To maintain these rewards, people may repeat and increase their ac tivities, which can further induce addictive behavior at the cost of other behaviors [34]. Reinforcement theory has been used as a theoretical lens to explain that pleasure (positive reinforcement) and relief from nega tive emotions (negative reinforcement) can elucidate the formation of excessive playing of multiplayer online games [34].

Regardless of the different approaches used to investigate IT addic tion, most research indicates that addictive IT use leads to various adverse consequences for users in their environments (i.e., work, school, and family). For instance, addiction to mobile email will increase perceptions of technology-family conflicts and work overload. Work overload further undermines organizational commitment. Elevated technology-family conflict together with augmented work overload are likely to foster work-family conflict [36].

In spite of the merits of prior research, IS research in this area stil needs further development, especially compared with the extensive research on addiction conducted in the substance field [45,46]. Given the serious consequences brought by IT addiction, it becomes more necessary to help addicts recover. One of the core problems in treating addiction is the high rate of relapse [47–49]. Relapse shows an obstacle to a successful recovery of addiction. Thus, it is important for IS re searchers to investigate how to prevent relapse. However, there is limited knowledge regarding relapse to IT addiction.

Note that there are differences between IT addiction and substance addiction. Thus, the understanding of substance abuse may not be directly applied to IT addiction. For instance, IT addiction does not involve physical signs or chemical intoxication as is the case with sub stance (e.g., drug, nicotine, heroin, and alcohol) addiction [50]. This means that IT users are not addicted to chemical substance, but the feelings or behaviors brought about by relevant actions [51]. From the neurobiological view, IT addiction only indirectly affects the neuro transmitter systems of the brain, while substance like heroin directly affect these systems, such as the dopaminergic system [51]. Clinical evidence also shows that subjects with substance addiction (e.g., alcohol dependence) have positive functional connectivity<sup>3</sup> between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, striatal areas, and temporal lobe, while IT (e.g., Internet game) addicts show negative functional connectivity. The correlations between functionally related brain regions are different [50]. Furthermore, the formation mechanisms are likely to be different as well. Research with the view of reinforcement theory indicates that negative reinforcement (i.e., relief of negative feelings) is shown to be more powerful than positive reinforcement (e.g., enjoyment and social enhancement) in developing IT addiction [52]. In contrast, positive reinforcement has been shown to be more important for substance addiction. For instance, positive reinforcements like social motive have been regarded as better predictors for alcohol and cigarette addiction [53,54].

Considering the differences between substance addiction and IT addiction, the key point in the process of preventing relapse may be different for IT addiction and substance addiction, which further sig nifies the necessity to conduct investigation of relapse to IT addiction. Future IS research should endeavor to investigate the process of pre venting relapse, which will provide noteworthy insights and offer sup port for an effective therapy for both clinicians and users.

## 2.2. Relapse

Relapse is one of the most important terms in the literature of addiction. The occurrence of relapse raises a fundamental barrier to effective recovery approaches of addiction [16]. The concept of relapse arises in the medical field [55], and it has become a major concern among researchers of substance abuse [16]. Relapse is an indicator that a recovery approach of addiction remains a perplexing challenge [9]. Previous research has provided a series of evolving definitions of this term [16]. For instance, relapse has been described as continuous smoking after an attempt to quit or a period of abstinence [11]. In the study of Marlatt and George [55], relapse is defined as a transitional process, resulting in a return to the pretreatment level of a target behavior. To date, however, a consensus on the definition of this term has not been reached.

Given that relapse is a major obstacle to curing addictive behaviors [129], the understanding and prevention of relapse become an impor tant goal in addiction research. In the field of substance abuse, many researchers have focused on identifying risk factors related to relapse. They attempt to design screening tools that can be used in relapse-prevention approaches. For instance, cravings/urges are regar ded as principal causes of relapse [56]. Negative emotions involved in persistent withdrawal symptoms (caused by abrupt cessation of sub stance use) play a major role in increasing the risk of relapse [57]. Based on these findings regarding the drivers of relapse, various interventions have been developed. For example, mindfulness-training programs have been proposed as clinical interventions [58]. One of the primary goals of mindfulness programs is to prevent relapse by targeting response to craving/urge [59]. This approach emphasizes the acceptance or toler ance of emotions and cognitions, as a means to diminish the motives for alleviating discomfort by using substances. It hence effectively reduces the prominent trigger of relapse, i.e., urge/craving for avoiding negative states [59]. In the mindfulness program, people learn how to tolerate discomfort in order to compete responses to craving/urge, which put them at high risks for relapse [1,2]. The study of Bowen et al. [58] demonstrated that people who receive mindfulness programs are less likely to experience craving/urge in response to depression. Prior liter ature also provides evidence that mindfulness-based recovery ap proaches influence urge from the neurobiology perspective [61]. Other recovery approaches like nicotine replacement therapy have been demonstrated to be valuable in promoting smoking cessation. Their effective remaining abstinent is due to the reduction of physiological and psychomotor withdrawal symptoms by replacing nicotine in the blood [62]. In the context of addiction, the goal of recovery approach is to either totally refrain from conducting a target behavior (e.g., drug or alcohol taking), or impose regulatory controls and limits over the occurrence of such behaviors (e.g., control of food intake) [55].

Despite the growth in research on relapse to substance addiction, the issue of relapse has seldom been studied in the context of IT addiction, and this pattern of neglect certainly applies in the IS field. The existing IS research merely mentions relapse as a symptom of addiction [e.g., 10]. For instance, in the study by Lee et al. [10], relapse is regarded as one of the seven addictive symptoms (i.e., salience, mood modification, toler ance, withdrawal, conflict, relapse, and loss of control). Their study treats relapse as the tendency for reversion to earlier patterns of exces sive multiplayer online game usage, following periods of controlled use or abstinence. Relapse symptoms have also been referred to as re sumptions of an activity with renewed vigor, after making attempts to abstain [25].

The findings in relapse to substance addiction may inspire relapse studies in the IT context. However, as discussed above, IT addiction is distinct from substance addiction. This implies that relapse prevention may be different between IT addiction and substance addiction, which further signifies the necessity to conduct investigation of relapse to IT addiction. An initial task of research on relapse to IT addiction is to provide a clear definition of the term. Appendix A lists some definitions of relapse in both substance and IT addiction contexts. Although the definitions in the substance context have not achieved consistency, much research on smoking, alcohol, and drug addiction, which are prevalent forms of substance addiction, has diagnosed the reuse of these substances as relapses [63–66]. Note that this method may not be applicable for the IT context. Simple reuse of ITs does not necessarily mean a relapse. In Appendix $\mathrm { A } ,$ a few studies of relapse to IT addiction indicate that relapse emerges together with uncontrolled behavior, despite subsequent negative consequences. Evidently, ITs provide many benefits, such as enjoyment $[ \mathbf { e } . \mathbf { g } . , \mathbf { \ 6 7 } ]$ and game-based learning [e.g., 68]. IT usage after a period of abstinence does not necessarily result in unstoppable or uncontrolled behaviors. Therefore, conceptualizing reuse of ITs as a relapse is considered improper. Based on these concerns, this study defines relapse as a return to addictive use of an IT after a temporary recovery to a normal usage level. Moreover, some recovery approaches in the substance context may not be appropriate for solving relapse problems in the IT context. For instance, one study published in a top substance abuse journal called Addiction [69] has suggested that the recovery approach of heroin includes avoidance $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ remove things which remind addicts of drug, or stay with people who remind addicts not to use), cognitive (i.e., addicts tell themselves not to use), and distraction $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ do something else when tempted to use). These strate gies focus on producing cessation of the target behavior. Similar re covery approaches can be widely found in the substance literature. For example, nicotine replacement therapy is used for smoking cessation [62]. The study of Thurgood et al. [70] also provides a systematic review of cessation recovery approaches for substance abuse. However, recov ery approaches in the IT addiction context are more for reducing usage levels rather than complete cessation. Substance addicts in mindfulness-training programs are taught to be aware of external (i.e., contextual) cues associated with substance use. While an awareness whether or not to use ITs may be inappropriate for IT users. This is due to the difference in criteria for identifying relapse. Given the high likeli hood of relapse and severe consequences of addictive behavior, as well as the differences between IT addiction and substance addiction, it is imperative for IS scholars to further develop relapse prevention models and provide guidance for practical recovery approaches. In this study, we refer to the self-regulatory process for building our research model.

## 2.3. Self-regulation

Self-regulation means that people monitor and control their behav iors to achieve their desired outcomes or goals [71–73]. Deficient self-regulation is centrally conducive to addictive behaviors [74], such as Internet addiction [75], addictive smartphone behaviors [72], and online game addiction [76]. Addictive behavior has been treated as a self-regulation disorder [77]. Prior research also claims that the core of addiction is mismanagement of the urge for good hedonic experience [34]. The substance abuse literature further indicates that preventing relapse to addiction is also a self-regulation process [16, 18]. Training exercises to improve self-regulatory skills has been suggested by Killen et al. [78] for preventing smoking relapses. People may build their ca pacity for self-regulation, which can serve as an effective coping strategy for helping people reduce their risk of relapse in dealing with drug or alcohol problems [79].

Previous literature has discussed self-regulation from the perspec tives of mindfulness and self-control [e.g., 80]. Mindfulness and self-control can be treated as two aspects of self-regulation [81–83]. Mindfulness involves observing or monitoring of what is occurring at the moment, which captures the monitoring aspect of self-regulation [52, 53]. Self-control is defined as the capability for altering or restraining individual responses toward compliance with moral standards or social expectations, or toward the achievement of long-term goals [86], which reflects the controlling aspect [52, 53]. They are two distinct forms of self-regulation that can work together to improve results [80]. The self-regulation literature has highlighted that monitoring and control aspects are at the heart of all self-regulation models [87–92]. These two aspects have been widely viewed as critical components of self-regulation [81–83]. Thus, it is important for researchers to distin guish between these two types of regulation.

Self-control is a form of regulation that includes control functions, and it serves as the goal-directed agent of change [80]. It is marked by effortful, ongoing regulation of goals, and by focusing on achievements. An individual may exert self-control to inhibit urges and desires, to delay gratification, or to follow relevant rules [68, 69]. Although a few scholars have used the concepts of self-regulation and self-control interchangeably, research has noted that self-control is actually the effortful, conscious, and deliberate subset of self-regulation activities [86]. For dealing with IT addictive behavior, self-control is a valuable capability that makes people less susceptible to developing IT addiction (see Appendix B).

The Mental Health Foundation Report of 2010 describes mindfulness as a process of consciously bringing awareness to current experience, without making judgments [70]. Based on previous explanations of mindfulness [70], this study defines the term as a state of being conscious regarding current behaviors, emotions, and cognitions, with purposeful attention and a nonjudgmental attitude. Unlike self-control, mindfulness is not a tool to keep moving toward predesigned goals. The main feature of mindfulness is an abiding awareness of what takes place, regardless of whether that experience involves worries, intrusive thoughts, or negative feelings[80,135] .

Moreover, the subjective consequences of these two forms of selfregulation are likely to differ. Mindful regulation of behaviors is ener gizing and vitalizing [88, 90]. People who are trained to observe and accept negative emotions (the core component of mindfulness) tend to display fewer effects of energy depletion than the control group [97]. Self-controlled regulation, however, has been demonstrated to be en ergy depleting[96,98] . Therefore, it is imperative for scholars to notice the two distinct aspects of self-regulation when investigating addictive behaviors. To date, IS research that adopts self-regulation perspective merely investigates the role of self-control on IT addiction $[ \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ 72], while ignores the monitoring aspect of self-regulation. The existing IS studies mainly analyze mindfulness at the organizational level and advocate the view that greater mindfulness from decision-makers will change the way in which mechanisms for information processing and environment scanning are used. For instance, mindfulness is found to be positively related to organizational IT innovation [99], and high-quality managerial decision-making [100]. Appendix C reviews IS research on mindfulness. It further shows that the value of mindfulness as the monitoring aspect of regulation in preventing relapse to IT addiction has been largely overlooked. Given that self-regulation involves two critical components, along with the significant value of mindfulness in pre venting relapse to substance addiction, future research on IT addiction needs to make efforts to reveal the influence of both self-control and mindfulness.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Building upon previous research and existing theories of selfregulation, we develop a model to explain how relapse to IT addiction can be effectively prevented. Then, we investigate how two forms of selfregulation (i.e., self-control and mindfulness) operate to reduce urges related to avoidance and approach, which are treated as two principal reinforcements of relapse. Fig. 1 depicts our research model.

## 3.1. Reinforcement mechanism

The addiction literature indicates that addiction stems from rein forcement mechanisms in reward-based processes [43]. Reward-based processes include positive reinforcement (i.e., gratification and mood enhancement) and negative reinforcement $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ relief from negative emotions or withdrawal symptoms) [34]. Moreover, the reinforcement mechanism is also considered as an effective explanation of the relapse issue. For instance, sensory satisfaction-positive reinforcement and negative affect reduction (or negative reinforcement) have been used to analyze tobacco consumption among abstaining smokers [101]. Also, several studies have indicated the role of reinforcement in substance relapse, specifically showing that drug relapse occurs because addicts seek drug use to relieve their withdrawal feelings (negative reinforce ment) [102–104].

On the basis of reinforcement, urge refers to a strong state of desire that is felt to be almost irresistible [93,105], and can be classified into two types: approach urge and avoidance urge. Approach urge refers to the strong tendency for seeking desirable outcomes (i.e., positive rein forcement), and avoidance urge refers to the intense desire to escape from undesirable outcomes (i.e., negative reinforcement) [106]. Research on substance addiction indicates that there are two types of cigarette cravings/urges, namely the anticipation of pleasure, and the relief from withdrawal symptoms or negative affect [107]. The study of Tiffany and Drobes [108] also distinguishes smoking urge for positive outcomes from the urge for relief from withdrawal symptoms or nega tive affect. Therefore, this study uses reinforcement to analyze relapse and identify approach urge as a strong desire for positive reinforcement, which highlights the use of certain ITs for desirable outcomes (e.g., enjoyment). Meanwhile, avoidance urge is regarded as a strong desire for negative reinforcement. which focuses on the use of certain ITs for relief from negative emotions. These two types of urges may reinforce people’s tendencies for returning to previous levels of uncontrolled IT usage.

Urges/cravings have been demonstrated to be powerful causes of relapses. Their prominent role in relapse has been ingrained in the addiction field [109]. Studies suggest that urge accounts for 60% to 100% of relapse to drugs (e.g., cocaine) [110,111]. The importance of urge stems from its prediction of relapse, thereby making urge an ideal target for specific recovery approaches [112–117]. As urge is among the strongest predictors of relapse [118–123], many promising and effective recovery approaches for addiction have centered on managing or reducing substance urge [59]. Abstinent individuals with stronger urges to smoke tend to relapse by the next measurement period to relieve their negative emotions (e.g., sadness, anger, confusion, and anxiety) and psychosocial stress [124]. Smokers have been found that the impact of pain in their immediate situation is fully mediated by smoking urges [125]. Drug craving and decreased coping contribute to the increase in relapse risk [126]. Craving exerts a primary influence in the mechanisms of addiction[130], which can lead to relapses after attempts to quit smoking [127]. Craving has also been argued as a primary characteristic of opiate addiction, which triggers further opiate usage and relapse [128]. Cocaine relapse is often attributed to the intense cravings for euphoria or for reducing withdrawal symptoms [133, 134]. Cra vings/urges are also connected with various forms of IT addiction, such as online game addiction [131] or social networking addiction [132]. In such studies, urges are found to involve both desire for the pleasur e/reward of smoking and desire for relief from negative emotions. Therefore, we believe that both approach urge and avoidance urge will increase the likelihood of relapse to IT addiction.

H1a. : The strength of avoidance urge toward using ITs is positively related to relapse to IT addiction

H1b. : The strength of approach urge toward using ITs is positively related to relapse to IT addiction

## 3.2. Mindfulness mechanism

Self-control shows the capability for altering or restraining individ ual responses toward long-term goals [86]. In order to achieve these goals, self-control requires monitoring of the current reality prior to making control efforts [80]. Failure to monitor or keep track of one’s own behavior causes difficulties in control [133]. Mindfulness involves the capability to discern what occurs at the moment, which can enhance control capability to conduct oneself in congruence with consciously decided goals [94, 95]. Thus, mindfulness promotes higher self-control, which further works together to realize better outcomes [85]. Increasing evidence has suggested that mindfulness is beneficial to self-control [136,137]. For instance, mindfulness is an appropriate approach to counteract the depletion of self-control. It can be used as a method for curing addiction through self-control [138]. Therefore, we believe that if people have a better mindfulness state while they are using an IT arti fact, they will be able to exert greater self-control.

## H2. : Mindfulness is positively associated with self-control.

In theoretical research on mindfulness, emotional regulation has been repeatedly proposed as a central mechanism of self-regulation [139–143]. Research on the mechanism underlying mindfulness con firms its role in promoting the effective regulation of emotion[84], and especially the regulation of unpleasant emotion [143]. The usefulness of mindfulness in curing behavioral disorders is due to its benefits in two lines of emotional regulation, namely, the reduction of negative emo tions and increase of the tolerance of negative states [139,144]. Major research on how mindfulness functions in emotional regulation is listed in Appendix D.

Mindfulness has been widely shown to be a valuable approach for reducing negative emotions [145]. Evidence from many cases indicates the positive effect of mindfulness for reducing negative emotions such as distress, craving, depression, caregiver distress, and stress [146,147]. Due to its function in reducing negative emotions, the concept of mindfulness has been widely regarded as a crucial element in the context of addiction[60]. For example, a reduced negative affect has been indicated as a primary mediator between mindfulness and smoking abstinence [148]. Mindfulness meditation can improve emotion regu lation and reduce drug abuse [149]. Based on these studies on mind fulness, we propose that IT users with higher levels of mindfulness tend to experience reduced levels of negative affect, including less intense negative emotions or feelings induced by withdrawal.

## H3a. : Mindfulness is negatively associated with negative affect.

Besides reducing negative emotions, another function of mindfulness in mood regulation is to increase affect tolerance [150]. Affect tolerance refers to the capability to withstand unpleasant internal state [151]. Awareness and the cognitive technique involved in mindfulness can reduce hyperactivity of autonomic nervous systems. A nonjudgmental attitude toward emotions (which is a dimension of mindfulness), regardless of whether they are positive or negative, can promote the ability to allow for the experience of these emotions without reacting to them [139]. Due to its benefits in mood regulation, mindfulness can also serve as a therapy for curing and preventing addiction. For instance, mindfulness-based relapse prevention (MBRP) enables the decrease of substance use by helping people tolerate uncomfortable states such as cravings and negative emotions without having instinctive reactions [152]. Therefore, this study proposes that IT users with higher levels of mindfulness are more likely to gain increased tolerance for negative affect.

H3b. : Mindfulness is positively associated with negative affect tolerance.

## 3.3. Factors influencing urges

Based on the reinforcement theory and substance literature, the urges of using ITs in this study are explained by approach and avoidance urges. To inhibit urges, research on substance explores factors predicting urges and effective recovery approaches. Negative affect has been found to be an important mechanism activating smoking urge, which coheres with negative reinforcement models of addiction [153]. High levels of psychosocial stress, negative emotions (e.g., sadness, anxiety, confusion, or anger), and withdrawal are found to predict stronger urges to smoke [154]. Therefore, affect management intervention can be useful in attenuating smoking urge [153]. A conditioned withdrawal model has also shown that cravings are caused by withdrawal-related feelings of desire to avoid or prevent an aversive experience [119]. Such negative affect triggers a strong desire to avoid negative reinforcement. The greater the negative emotions or feelings, the greater the negative-affect cravings [155]. Increases in individual negative affect and anxiety are correlated with drug cue-induced and stress-induced cravings. Negative affect and deprivation-caused cravings appear to be important targets in developing new methods to treat cocaine dependence [156]. Ren et al. [128] pointed out that distress over pain (which reflects the emotional aspect of pain) significantly increases the intensity of cravings for relief. Therefore, we believe that if IT users have higher negative emotions, then they are more likely to experience strong avoidance urge toward escapism or relief.

## H4a. : Negative affect is positively associated with avoidance urge.

As discussed above, negative affect plays an important role in motivating urges for relief. When people have higher negative affect tolerance, i.e., they can stay with undesirable feelings, their urges for relief may be reduced. Affect tolerance has also been shown to signifi cantly influence individual urges. Previous studies have reported that pain-sensitive addicts have greater opiate cravings than pain-tolerant addicts [128]. The substance literature finds that relapsers show smoking urge in response to a lack of behavioral persistence when facing emotional discomfort [157]. If an individual has sufficient tolerance for negative emotions (i.e., they can accept negative emotions rather than seeking to escape or reduce them), then avoidance urge may become lower because this urge involves the desire to reduce or relieve negative feelings. Hence, when IT users have higher tolerance of negative affect, their avoidance urge toward escapism or relief may be reduced.

H4b. : Negative affect tolerance is negatively associated with avoidance urge.

Self-control focuses on overriding or inhibiting competing desires, urges, or behaviors [158]. Previous research has indicated that if self-control resources are depleted, then an individual will experience substantial impulse-buying urges [159]. Research has also confirmed that craving for drugs occurs under the influences of other factors such as the level of self-control [160]. Bayot et al. [161] showed that people with higher self-control can reduce their cravings for smoking better. Alcoholics can be trained in a self-control strategy to help them suc cessfully cope with cravings/urges [162]. Three self-control tactics, namely, postponement and distraction, avoidance, and substitution, all aim to reduce desires via undoing the shifts in reference-points [163]. Therefore, when IT users have higher levels of self-control, their approach and avoidance urges will decrease.

H5a. : Self-control is negatively associated with avoidance urge.

H5b. : Self-control is negatively associated with approach urge.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Procedure and sample

Smartphone game addiction has been treated as a prevalent form of IT addiction [164]. For instance, nearly 15 million users in Western countries are shown to be addicted to a smartphone game called Candy Crush Saga [15]. Addiction to smartphone games is often associated with detrimental consequences, such as psychology and physiology disorders as well as conflicts [15,165]. In this study, we examine our research model in the context of smartphone game addiction.

Relapse occurs after quitting for a period of time [166], and longi tudinal methods have been widely used in studies on relapse to addic tion [166–170]. In a similar vein, this study adopted a longitudinal survey design to understand the phenomenon of relapse to smartphone game addiction. Respondents were invited to complete two online questionnaires at two time points. At first, we collected responses related to all constructs except for relapse. After one month, we collected responses related to relapse. To ensure the sample quality, we used a market research company to conduct the survey. Invitation messages including the questionnaires’ URL were sent to potential respondents. Given that the focus of this study is relapse, it is necessary to distinguish people who may relapse from first-time addicts. Relapse is about the recurrence of addiction after a period of control and emerges with previous experience of recoveries [166]. For instance. Marlatt and

Donovan [18] indicated that relapse occurs to the people recovering from addictive behavior problems. Hence, in the survey design, screening questions were used to select those respondents who had previous experiences of successfully decreasing their smartphone game usage after addiction. Respondents who lacked this experience were not considered in the survey.

To examine the respondents’ prior addiction level, we used 7-point Likert scale items to measure smartphone game addiction and further selected questionnaires with averaged item scores over 4. Then, we asked about their experience of decreasing smartphone game usage after addiction. Eventually, we collected a total of 420 valid responses: 228 (54.3%) respondents were males and 192 (45.7%) were females. The majority of the respondents (77.1%) were aged between 18 and 30. Of the remaining respondents, 12.6% were aged from 31 to 40, and 10.3% were above 40 years old. Table 2 shows the demographic characteristics of the respondents.

## 4.2. Measures

Established measurements from prior research were used to measure the majority of the constructs, with minor modifications of wording to fit the smartphone game context. A 7-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (tagged with 1) to strongly agree (tagged with 7), was used for the items. Except for mindfulness, all other constructs were reflective. We followed prior studies on substance addiction [e.g., 171] to conceptualize mindfulness as a second-order formative construct. Compared to other measures used in the IS literature, where mindfulness is measured to emphasize individuals’ personal innovation [e.g., 99], it was more appropriate for this study to adopt the measurement of mindfulness from the literature on relapse to substance addiction. More specifically, mindfulness was measured with five dimensions: observing, nonreactivity to inner experience, acting with awareness, describing, and nonjudging of inner experience. These dimensions described the conditions in which the construct was realized [171].

Appendix E lists the measurements used in this study. Previous research has pointed out that people tend to under-report their negative behaviors or characteristics (e.g., addiction and stealing), as they may want to seem favorable to others [4]. Therefore, social desirability bias might exist in the survey study. We accordingly included the measure of social desirability bias [31] in our data collection.

## 4.3. Pretest

Given that the measures were in English, and our survey was con ducted in China, we translated the English questionnaire into Chinese. Then, we used the back-translation technique to ensure linguistic equivalence [173], and any inconsistencies were corrected. A total of 23 IS students were invited to refine the wordings, clarify the questions and instructions, and indicate any improvements needed. Most of these students were satisfied with our questionnaire, and we were able to improve the questionnaire based on their advice. Therefore, we believed that our measurements were appropriate, and the questionnaire was both operational and understandable.

## 5. Data analysis

## 5.1. Measurement model

We adopted SmartPLS 3.0 to estimate our research model[172]. Mindfulness was measured as a second-order aggregated construct, with factor scores generated from its first-order constructs. The resulting scores were used as formative items of the aggregated construct [174]. To analyze the validity of mindfulness, we assessed its items’ weights. We found that the items were all significant, except for “describing” (t = -0.032). Item weights reflected the relative signification of formative items [175]. Therefore, the dimension of describing might not be a key aspect of mindfulness and was thus removed in our context of smart phone game addiction. We noted that describing with words required skills in expression. Therefore, it was possible that respondents using mindfulness practices might be noneloquent regarding their situational self-regulation. Besides, our research model focuses on the role of mindfulness in emotion regulation. We attempt to investigate how mindfulness reduces negative emotions and increases abilities to tolerate negative feelings that people will face without coping/a voidance strategies. This is consistent with the work from Hayes and Feldman [176], which highlighted the content of mindfulness in having internal experience with acceptance, intensive self-observation without elaboration, and judgment or attempts to change the experience. Further, Hayes and Feldman [176] similarly focused on the dimensions like awareness, present-focus, attention, acceptance, and nonjudgment, rather than describing. In this regard, we concluded that mindfulness comprised the following dimensions in the data analysis: nonreactivity to inner experience, observing, acting with awareness, and nonjudging of inner experience.

Reliability, convergent, and discriminant validity were tested to analyze our measurement model. Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR) were two criteria for reliability [177]. To ensure suffi cient reliability, the values of Cronbach’s alpha and CR needed to be greater than 0.7 [177]. Meanwhile, average variance extracted (AVE) values should exceed 0.5 to ensure convergent validity[142,143,179] [, 1. After deleting NA3 and NA6 (in negative affect) due to their low factor loadings, our data achieved these requirements (see Table 3).

Discriminant validity reflected the degrees of difference between any two constructs. To achieve sufficient discriminant validity, item load ings for a theoretical construct needed to be higher than cross-loadings with other constructs [34]. The values of square root of AVE in each construct also needed to be above correlation values with other con structs [34]. Table 4 and Appendix F show acceptable results.

Moreover, we examined whether this study involved serious com mon method bias (CMB). First, Harman’s one-factor test was tested [180]. The results showed that no single factor was extracted, and no factor could explain the majority of variances. Second, we conducted Lindell and Whitney’s test [181]. The factor of intention to purchase virtual goods was considered to be a theoretically unrelated construct. CMB would be a concern if there were high correlations between the added unrelated construct and the items of principal constructs in the model. The results indicated that the average correlation among inten tion to purchase virtual goods and the principal constructs was r = 0.161 (average p-value = 0.438), reflecting weak correlations. Third, the correlation matrix in Table 4 did not show any highly correlated con structs (the highest correlation was r = 0.614), whereas the evidence of CMB should have correlations higher than 0.9 [182]. Hence, CMB seemed unlikely to be a serious concern in our study.

As multicollinearity was a potential threat, we analyzed the variance inflation factors (VIFs). The range of VIFs for reflective constructs was between 1.002 and 1.189, which did not surpass the threshold value of 3.3 [183]. The VIFs of the formative items of mindfulness were also checked [184], and the range was between 1.211 and 1.376, which was smaller than the suggested 3.3 threshold [185]. Therefore, the measures did not suggest a serious multicollinearity concern.

## 6. Discussion

This study aims to enhance our understanding toward preventing relapse to IT addiction. Given that relapse prevention is often a selfmanagement program [17], we apply a self-regulatory perspective to identify factors that help to prevent relapse. The proposed research model is analyzed in the context of smartphone game addiction, which is a common and representative form of IT addiction. Regarding the reinforcement mechanisms of relapse, our findings show that both avoidance urge $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ negative reinforcement) and approach urge $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ positive reinforcement) positively affect relapse to IT addiction. How ever, avoidance urge demonstrates a much more significant effect than approach urge. It suggests that the urge for avoidance or escape from negative affect and withdrawal symptoms appears to be the major cause for IT users to relapse into addiction. This finding implies that negative reinforcement plays a more powerful role than positive reinforcement in causing relapse.

Moreover, we examine how to inhibit the impacts of reinforcement from a self-regulation perspective. Mindfulness and self-control are two distinct forms of self-regulation considered in this study, which can work together to improve self-regulation [80]. In line with the effects of mindfulness noted in prior research on substance addiction, we find that mindfulness in using ITs can also reduce negative affect and increase tolerance for negative affect. When users have lower negative affect and higher negative affect tolerance, their avoidance urge toward IT usage is weakened. Moreover, self-control is found to be effective in reducing both avoidance urge and approach urge. We further demonstrate that mindfulness can improve the level of self-control. Our findings are consistent with those of prior research on regulation, which has shown that the monitoring aspect $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ , mindfulness) of regulation can enhance the controlling aspect (i.e., self-control) [80].

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

First, this study advances the existing IS literature on IT addiction. To date, little effort has been exerted in relevant IS studies to investigate the phenomenon of relapse. Previous research on relapse to addiction is mainly found in the psychology and substance abuse literature [e.g., 16]. Considering the severity and high rate of relapse to addiction, more IS research will be needed to understand how to prevent relapse. This study is one of the first efforts in the IS field to investigate the issue of relapse. Based on prior non-IS research on relapse [e.g., 11,55], we first conceptualize the concept of relapse in relation to IT addiction. There after, we show how it can be prevented in a self-regulation process. The regulatory process aims to prevent relapse by attenuating positive and negative reinforcements, which are the underlying mechanisms of addictive behaviors. This study implies that attenuating reinforcement can provide an effective approach for relapse prevention in IT addiction. Essential factors involving mindfulness and self-control are also exam ined as a means for undermining the influence of reinforcement. We believe that this study enriches and advances our knowledge of IT addiction, and it provides a foundation for future IS research to better assess the relapse issue.

A second implication of this study is that the value of self-regulation is demonstrated, specifically in relation to preventing relapse to IT addiction. Note that prior research on substance addition has shown the link between relapse prevention and self-regulation [16, 18]. Thus, it is reasonable to adopt a self-regulation perspective to address the relapse issue in the IT addiction context. Further, we find that mindfulness is a valuable method which can function as a monitoring strategy, and self-control can be treated as a controlling strategy. These two forms of self-regulation can function together to inhibit the influence of rein forcement. Previous IS research on IT addiction has acknowledged the important role of self-regulation [30]. However, existing IS studies have largely focused on the controlling strategy of self-regulation (i.e., self-control) [e.g., 72], while the monitoring side of self-regulation has been overlooked. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first to consider the role of self-regulation using a balanced perspective that includes both the controlling and monitoring aspects. Mindfulness can serve as an effective monitoring regulation for discomfort emotions. We believe that this study provides and articulates a new and more valuable approach to conducting self-regulation. Moreover, this study provides evidence that mindfulness is significantly associated with self-control. Self-control is a promising but easily depleted resource for treating addictive behaviors [86.194]. Self-control tends to deplete over time with repeated usage, as with a muscle that gets tired [195]. Further research to identify factors that facilitate self-control is still necessary, given that the findings in this area remain limited [138]. Hence, we provide new insights concerning how to replenish self-control, and these insights offer a deeper understanding of self-control. This study therefore enriches the existing IS research on self-regulation by explicating its two aspects and examining their relations. It provides insights into the mechanisms of self-regulation especially for future IS research on regulating behaviors of IT users.

Third, this study contributes to research on mindfulness. Existing IS studies mainly analyze the role of mindfulness in promoting innovation quality and organizational performance [196]. Although prior non-IS research has demonstrated that mindfulness is useful in treating addic tion and preventing relapse $[ \ e . g . ,$ 58], IS research on IT addiction has generally ignored the importance or usefulness of mindfulness. This study provides evidence indicating that mindfulness is a significant monitoring aspect of self-regulation. The mindfulness and substance abuse literature has recognized the effectiveness of mindfulness in treating negative affect. For IT relapse, one of the main drivers is to relieve negative feelings. Considering these, mindfulness appears to be an indispensable part of self-regulation in preventing relapse to IT addiction by reducing negative reinforcement of relapse. We therefore extend the preventing effects of mindfulness from substance abuse and psychological disorder contexts to the IT addiction context. We are also one of the first in the IS literature to investigate the role of mindfulness in relapse to IT addiction. We extend the mindfulness literature to the IT addiction context. This study also supplements the existing IS research on mindfulness which primarily focuses on regular IT usage [e.g., 62, 63].

## 6.2. Practical implications

This study also offers several contributions to practice. Considering the negative consequences of IT addiction and its high relapse rate, it is imperative to understand how to prevent relapse in advance. The find ings of this study can help to raise the awareness of IT users in managing their behaviors to avoid any relapse to addiction. Our empirical results further provide guidance to assist addicts themselves in maintaining their regular usage and preventing relapse to uncontrolled usage of ITs.

Specifically, our results confirm that reinforcement is an underlying mechanism of relapse. Therefore. it is imperative for users to learn how to tolerate their uncomfortable feelings, and perhaps to manage their negative emotions through engaging in other healthy activities rather than continuously using ITs. People should notice the potential risks of relapse if they continuously use ITs for the sake of relieving feelings of discomfort. An effective method is to maintain a state of mindfulness. Mindfulness can help people attenuate strong negative emotions and increase their capacity for tolerance. People can be educated or trained to monitor their experience and behavior. Furthermore, they can learn to accept their feelings without judgment or automatic reaction. In this way, the driving power of negative reinforcement to induce relapse can be inhibited effectively. People can also use mindfulness to strengthen their self-control in further restraining their urge to use ITs excessively. Health institutions may apply our findings to provide mindfulness classes for rest, stress-relief and healing. Considering the important role of mindfulness in preventing relapse to IT addiction, they may further design training programs by teaching IT users how to monitor their behaviors during using ITs, and helping them practice in class with professional guidance.

IT (e.g., online games or social networking tools like WeChat) pro viders and managers can also implement some of the strategies explored in this study to help reducing the worldwide social problem of IT addiction, though part of their business aim would be to encourage sustainable usage. For example, adding alerts or reminder windows may be helpful, so that people can easily keep track of their behaviors by referring to their usage time. IT providers can also integrate a screentime function to allow users to monitor their screen time and set timers for individual apps. Such modifications in application design may help users better regulate their behavior. It is also useful to develop mindfulness meditation apps which provide a series of meditations for creating a more mindful life.

Despite this study’s contributions, it also has several limitations.

First, we investigate the problem of relapse into IT addiction in a general way, without differentiating the types of ITs. As various types of ITs show different features, the process of preventing relapse to addiction may involve factors specific to certain types of ITs. Prior research has pointed out that IT addiction can be different across various types of ITs, such as Internet, mobile phones, and video games [197]. In this regard, future IS research can categorize ITs into different types and compare their possible differences with regard to relapse.

Second, our survey study is conducted in China. The respondents are all Chinese smartphone game players. Prior research indicates that culture can influence IT usage[149,178,198,199] . Therefore, future research may include participants of other cultural backgrounds to extend the research findings, or further explore how cultural factors can influence the self-regulatory process.

Third, our study focuses on revealing factors for preventing relapse into IT addiction. We suggest that future research may design or develop several different schemes of relapse prevention based on our research model, and further evaluate the effectiveness of these schemes in practice.

Forth, our data are collected with survey method. Although survey method shows noteworthy practical and economic advantages, it may suffer from self-report bias, especially in the addiction context. Thus, further research may consider the use of mixed methods. For instance, survey could be used with direct observations or case studies to achieve more accurate and detailed understandings of how and why certain results occur.

Last, our research model mainly focuses on the effects of the self regulatory process. Thus, we do not investigate the roles of other fac tors such as social influence during the usage of ITs. Note that social factors have been found to profoundly influence users' behaviors. Social influence is also shown to be important in shaping IT usage behaviors [200]. Therefore, future research may consider these factors when exploring how to prevent relapse into IT addiction.

## 7. Conclusions

The goals of this study are to understand the concept of relapse into IT addiction, and to empirically investigate how to prevent such relapse by identifying valuable prevention factors. We propose a research model based on the self-regulation perspective. Our longitudinal survey study collects data from two different time points for validating the proposed hypotheses empirically. Our results provide strong evidence that both negative and positive reinforcements can evoke relapse. Meanwhile, negative reinforcement has a more powerful influence than positive reinforcement. Furthermore, we show that mindfulness and self-control function as two aspects of self-regulation. Mindfulness is an effective approach for reducing the reinforcement of urges, which works by reducing negative affect and increasing tolerance for negative affect. Self-control can also effectively manage the urges of users, and mind fulness helps to facilitate self-control. These two regulation strategies can work together to diminish the reinforcements of addiction relapse. The most noteworthy contribution of this study lies in providing insights into how relapses to IT addiction can be prevented. Given that relapse to IT addiction is a new topic, future analysis is encouraged to pursue and enrich this line of research, thereby enhancing our understandings in this area.
