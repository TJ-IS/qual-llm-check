## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cyber-attacks Member attitude Perceived risk Privacy concern Social networking sites SNS use

## A B S T R A C T

This study focuses on how social networking site (SNS) users’ perceived risk is affected by perceptions of the duality of potentially harmful events (cyber-attacks and privacy breaches) on SNS and explores the potential threat sources that influence such perceptions. Further, it examines the effects of perceived risk on SNS members site use and the underlying mechanisms through which these effects are cast. The study finds that users perceived SNS risk affects site use behavior through attitude as an important mediator and users’ concern regarding cyber-attacks (security) casts a greater impact than the concern regarding privacy breaches in shaping perceived SNS risk.

## 1. Introduction

Social networking sites (SNSs) have proliferated in the last several years. Leading SNSs, such as Facebook, Twitter, and Pinterest, collec tively account for about 90% of all social network visits [1]. Particularly, Facebook accounts for 70% of internet users in the U.S. [2]. At a typical SNS, members<sup>1</sup> can create their profile pages to share their personal information about events, activities, interests, and histories [3,4]. Site use results in engagement in social interactions, such as profile browsing, comment exchange, and reciprocation of mutual favors [5]. Moreover, members who engage in site use are likely to share user-generated content (UGC), such as photos, videos, and blogs [6]. This contributes to the wealth of an online community [7].

Members’ use of SNSs is, however, not without threats that can cause loss. Loss may result in adverse forms ranging from harassment, loss of employment, damaged reputation, impaired career outlook, damaged data or networks, unintended content modification, intellectual prop erty theft, lost revenue or income, and loss of productivity [8–10]. Regarding events that introduce potential loss, cyber-attacks and privacy breaches have been recognized as two distinctive major types [8,11–13]. Cyber-attacks refer to “illegal computer-mediated activities that often take place in the global electronic network” [14]. They affect computer in frastructures, data assets (personal and non-personal), and information services. In contrast, privacy breaches concern unauthorized access, disclosure, or misuse of sensitive private information [15,16]. Cyber-attacks may not result in a breach of privacy unless private data are obtained by assailants; on the other hand, privacy breaches may be initiated by threat sources and events (e.g., an SNS provider selling members’ data to data brokers) that do not relate to cyber-attacks. Therefore, cyber-attacks concern issues that may be broader than those of privacy breaches in one sense while privacy breaches are associated with issues broader than cyber-attacks in another sense [17]. As a recent actual example, Facebook was found to leak personal in formation of 1.5 million users without user consent in April 2019 [18]. More recent examples of cyber-attacks and privacy breaches that affected SNS users are summarized in Table 1.

Several reasons account for the prevalence of these two types of in cidents. First, attackers are constantly attracted to SNS because of the ease of accessibility to the services as well as the wide connectivity with a large pool of potential targets [19]. Over the years, surveys have found that about three-quarters of online adults use SNS [20]. Most sites are free to use and place no validation on members’ backgrounds and identities. SNS, therefore, renders an ideal attack platform for attackers to target and exploit their prey. Meanwhile, members of SNS services share a vast amount of personal information and such information may be disclosed without consent or may be misused.

Although it is generally agreed that decision-makers perceive risk based upon their assessment of key threats, to date, it remains largely unclear as to how SNS users develop their perceptions of SNS risk and how this perception accounts for their behavior regarding SNS use. Early studies have explored the risks of SNS with a primary focus on privacy as “one of the core issues of research” [21–26]. However, there is a dearth of research that provides a systematic investigation of perceived SNS threats, ranging from the sources of threats to the events that imperil members, and to the likely consequences. Extending a three-pronged perspective of threat assessment [27], this study contributes to the literature by (1) explaining how perceived risk is affected by perceptions of a duality of potentially harmful events (cyber-attacks and privacy breaches) on SNS and exploring the potential threat sources that affect such perceptions; (2) examining the impacts of perceived risk on SNS members’ site use and, importantly, revealing the underlying mecha nisms through which these effects are cast; and (3) exploring the contention between cyber-attacks and privacy breaches to find out which one casts more impact on shaping perceived SNS risk.

In this paper, we answer two specific research questions. (1) What are the antecedents of cyber-attack exposure and privacy breach concern and how do they affect SNS users’ perceived risk? and (2) What are the effects of SNS users’ perceived risk on their site use behavior? Answering these questions can help us improve our understanding of the process of threat assessment and the consequences of SNS risk perception. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. First, we explore the risks of SNS use and introduce the theoretical underpinning of the research. Next, we pro pose a research model and explain a set of hypotheses. The subsequent section details the research methodology which is followed by an articulation of the analysis results. We conclude the paper by discussing the key findings, theoretical contributions, implications for practice, limitations, and future research.

## 2. Literature and theoretical foundation

## 2.1. SNS risks and major threats

The FBI [8] and the U.S. General Services Administration [10] have recognized SNS perils include, for example, access to age-inappropriate content, child abuse, and prosecution due to ones posting of offensive or inappropriate comments. Others have also identified potential hazards, such as targeted attacks, network vulnerability, unintended content modification, unnecessary infrastructure and maintenance costs, and productivity loss [9]. Since the inception of SNS, reports of cyber-attack and privacy breaches have been frequently documented [8,11–13].

Due to the wide adoption of SNS, millions of Internet users log into these platforms, making SNS a platform for cybercriminals to prey on targets. The core values of networking sites, such as openness, connecting, and sharing with others have given rise to increasing threats of cyber-attacks [22]. For example, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has charged SNS providers, such as Twitter for failing to safeguard their SNS against cybercriminals [28]. The use of SNS may expose a member to predators, scammers, and hackers. Cyber-attacks may take a wide range of forms, such as intrusion, malware infection, cyberbullying, and social engineering [9,10,29].

In addition, privacy breaches represent an important challenge in the SNS context because of the large amount of personal information that is shared and exchanged on SNS. The FTC suggests that privacy breach takes the form of unauthorized disclosure or misuse of private infor mation [16]. SNS encourage online users to share the information which is the key to strengthening social interactions among members. To attract new friends, individuals reveal personal information, such as education, affiliation, work location, personality, hobby, and travel sites in their profiles. To retain the interest of their friends, one may update private events through news posts, photos, and videos. The amount of private information shared on an SNS is greater than what is disclosed on traditional online platforms such as online retailing stores. The Consumer Reports’ 2010 State of the Net Analysis found that more than half of all users of social networks in the U.S. post private information (e. g., full birth dates, home address, photos, and names of children) [12]. Therefore, SNSs have developed into a “central repository of personal in formation” and many users reveal more personal information than they should, leaving them vulnerable to threats to privacy [30].

With the growth of accumulated private information on SNS, the potential threats and therefore odds of privacy breaches have increased for several reasons. First, there may exist problematic issues with SNS providers regarding security policy design and management framework implementation [31]. Second, SNS provider sites may purposefully share and/or sell their users’ information to unintended individuals and other business entities for purposes, such as advertising. Third, mali cious members may befriend an SNS user to collect private information for offensive purposes, such as cyberstalking. Fourth, friends may acci dentally disclose private data about a member without awareness and consent from them. In 2012, for example, the FTC charged Facebook with eight counts of unauthorized disclosure and misuse of member privacy information [32]. In July 2019, Facebook agreed to pay a record-breaking \$5 billion penalty to settle the 2012 FTC charges [33]. Once released to the public, private information is no longer under the control of the person and results in unforeseen loss [34,35]. Therefore, an SNS user may become wary and concerned about the loss of control over own privacy.

## 2.2. Three-pronged perspective framework and need to extend

A three-pronged perspective framework (TPF) of perceived risk, ar ticulated by [27] offers a holistic view of how decision-makers perceive risks. The first perspective is related to the identification of potential threat sources that are responsible for events that cause harm to the end-users [38]. The second perspective focuses on events that expose decision-makers to harm [37,39]. The third perspective builds an in ventory of the harms that decision-makers may suffer [40,41]. As a descriptive model, TPF recognizes the fact that harm-exposing events are induced by the presence of threat sources and that harmful events result in a spectrum of harms. However, the traditional TPF does not recognize the differences among threat sources.

Within the setting of SNS, in this paper, we expand on TPF by recognizing that SNS users are exposed to different types of threat sources. Some sources are non-context specific and post persistent risk to end-users in general – that is, these include threat agents, such as computer viruses, Trojans, as well as cybercriminals that propagate threats through the Internet. Others are context-specific (in our case,

SNS specific) and may pose potential dangers to SNS users – that is, these sources may be familiar to users of a particular system; for instance, they may have at one time been trusted but may introduce threats occa sionally through misuse, tampering of information or simply, careless ness that may compromise confidentiality. Threat sources of the first type, such as malware or hackers, commonly arouse concerns among SNS users. In contrast, threat agents of the second type, such as other SNS members and SNS providers, typically serve as important resources to fulfill one’s social needs despite occasional mishaps. The value they offer may normally exceed their probable negative consequences. To capture an online SNS user’s assessment of SNS members and SNS, we include positive perceptions, such as trust beliefs in a way to recognize that these two entities are potential threat sources, but are not neces sarily risky by nature, in contrast to the first type of threat agents, such as malware and hackers. We argue that the perceptions of these threat sources contribute to the SNS users’ perceptions regarding exposure to cyber-attacks. According to the Chief Privacy Officer of IBM, Christina Peters, “Privacy is a concept that emerges when it is under threat” [42]. So we contend that trust (or lack of trust) in other SNS members and the SNS<sup>2</sup>, along with the potential of exposure to cyber-attack, contribute to SNS users’ privacy breach concerns. Since the threat effect of malware and cybercriminals on member privacy is manifested through the po tential of cyber-attack exposure, a direct relationship between these threat sources and privacy breach concern is omitted in this study.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Through the theoretical lens of an extended TPF, we propose a research model to explore how SNS users assess threats. This model consists of the process of assessing perceived SNS risk along with its potential impacts on users’ attitude and use behavior. We contend that SNS users identify and, more importantly, assess risks from three per spectives as seen in Fig. 1. Along with potential threat sources, we expect that SNS users’ individual characteristics, such as disposition to value privacy help explain the individual differences in assessing harmexposing events [43–45]. Moreover, we posit that assessments of harmful events may be interrelated as the occurrence of one event may trigger the advent of another. Additionally, we measure the assessment of overall inclusive harm as a perceived risk because the discrete harms tend to associate with one another. The use of inclusive harm is consistent with recent literature on perceived risk [46–50]. Finally, we theorize that perceived SNS risk has an effect on both attitude and SNS use behavior.

SNS primarily comprises people, platform, and private (and public) information. In this study, we examine SNS users’ risk assessment in terms of the following: (a) trust in other SNS members (i.e., people), (b) perceived risk on the Internet (i.e., technology platforms) which is en dangered by cybercriminals or malware, (c) trust in SNS (i.e., technol ogy platforms), and (d) disposition to value privacy (i.e., private information), which are suggested and supported by literature as related factors [51–55].

The trust literature has advocated the use of trust in assessing the threats in a relationship. Hobbes viewed trust as one way of risk assessment involving the expected behavior of another person, in order to achieve the likely benefits of cooperation [56]. Sztompka and Uslaner have pointed out that trust is viewed as a special instance of rational risk-taking – the decision to trust another person is similar to placing a risky bet on the trustworthiness of another person [57]. That is, trust presupposes risk [58,59]. Thus, we examine SNS users’ threat assess ment of other SNS community members and the vendor site through the lens of trust. As discussed above, these risk elements jointly concern decision factors related to three important aspects: internal threat sources (i.e., SNS members and SNS provider sites), external sources (cybercriminals and malware that are manifested through the endan gered Internet), and an interpersonal trait regarding privacy (i.e., a disposition to value privacy; see Fig. 1). We detail each of these in the following subsections.

## 3.1. Direct and indirect effects of perceived sns risk on attitude and site use

We expect that perceived SNS risk influences user attitude and behavior. Despite some supporting evidence in the area of e-services and internet shopping [37,49,60], the literature on perceived risk has been mixed – in terms of the presence or absence of a consistent relationship between perceived risk and behavior choices in the forms of user intention and actual use of information systems [41,61–64]. Findings regarding perceived risk suggest the potential association between perceived risk and attitude [65,66] and between attitude and behavior choices [27,67]. In this study, we examine whether SNS member atti tude helps bridge the effect of perceived risk on SNS use behavior.

During site use, SNS users engage in activities such as information retrieval, communal activity participation, and personal networking. Following the literature on information system use [68], this study fo cuses on SNS use which refers to the regularity with which an SNS member uses SNS services during a designated period [69]. Site use indicates SNS users’ intensity of SNS usage and it is strongly correlated with the variety of site use [70]. Following PRT, SNS users perceive risk in SNS, which in turn affects their usage decisions [36]. In other words, when SNS users perceive uncertainty and possibly undesirable conse quences (e.g., online frauds, identity theft, cybercriminals, malicious attacks, etc.) of engaging in SNS activities, they are reluctant to use the SNS [71]. An increased level of risk that users perceive diminishes their use of the SNS. Therefore:

## H1a. : Perceived SNS risk is negatively associated with site use

Information systems literature has long considered attitude as a focal factor that bridges end-users perceptions/beliefs and behavior [72,73]. We. therefore, consider SNS user attitude in the exploration of the likely relationship between perceived SNS risk and site use. In this study, user attitude refers to an individual’s positive evaluative state toward using a given SNS. Attitude is developed based on subjective assessments about beliefs and values. It reflects the extent to which one likes an object [74]. The risk literature has advocated the likely association between endusers’ perceived risk and their positive attitude toward information systems [67,75,76]. The accumulated experience with a social networking service will lead members to develop judgments about the site and subsequently form their attitudes [77]. This projected effect of perceived SNS risk on attitude reflects the process of attitude formation when expected negative outcomes lead decision-makers to develop weakened attitudes [78]. When the risk of using an SNS is perceived to be high, a user may become cautious about the site and less likely to develop a favorable attitude to use it. As a result, we expect a negative relationship between perceived SNS risk and user attitude toward a site. Therefore, we posit that:

## H1b. : Perceived SNS risk is negatively associated with user attitude toward a site.

Prior research suggests that “the person’s attitude toward the target influences his or her subsequent actions toward it” [79]. Within the context of voluntary use of technologies where users have vested interests, studies have found that individual attitude is associated with their ac tions [[79], [80]]. Regarding SNS, attitude has also been theorized as a key antecedent to users’ behavioral choice [81,82]. We contend that users' positive attitude toward an SNS is associated with an increase in site use. That is, when a user is happy with a site, he or she is more likely to use it. On the contrary, an SNS user will tend to avoid using a site if he or she finds it unpleasant. Therefore:

H2. : User’s positive attitude toward a site is positively associated with site use.

In addition to the direct and indirect effects of perceived SNS risk on site use, we propose a mediating effect of attitude on the relationship between perceived risk and usage behavior. Several prior studies about SNS have questioned the direct association between perceived risk and behavioral choice. For example, an insignificant association has been found to exist between perceived risk and SNS use behavior and use intention [62,64,83–85]. Studies in non-SNS contexts have also sug gested that perceived risk may not directly influence end-users with regard to their behavioral choices [41,63]. Perceived risk reflects a belief about the risk and, as a consequence, it is akin to attitudinal phenomena investigated by social psychologists [86–88]. Recent liter ature has related perceived risk with feeling and affect [65,66]. As part of the intuitive process of risky decision-making, risks affect decision-- makers’ feelings and affect, which are important to drive their behavior [89]. Attitude. therefore, serves as an important facilitator that bridges perceived risk and one’s actions. Within the context of SNS, we argue that perceived risk may dismay a user and subsequently lead to reduced use of the site. Prior studies have shown that attitude connects to perceived risk as well as behavior [27,67]. Accordingly, we expect that user attitude toward an SNS may mediate the effect of perceived risks on site use.

H3. : User attitude toward a site mediates the relationship between perceived SNS risk and site use.

## 3.2. Relationships between harm-exposing events and harm assessment

Drawing upon prior literature [8,11–13], we pinpoint (a)

cyber-attacks and (b) privacy breaches as two critical types of harm that threaten an SNS user. Kılınc et al. suggest that it is not risk but dangerous exposure events that are directly sensed by individuals [90]. Glover and Benbasat point out that a focus on events that expose individuals to harm is the best way to inform practitioners [27]. We capture SNS users assessment of cyber-attack events in terms of their perceived cyber-attack exposure which is defined as one’s belief about the state of being exposed to harms by cybercriminals during the use of an SNS [91]. In addition, we capture SNS users’ assessment of privacy breach events in terms of privacy breach concern, which refers to one’s concern about a possible loss of private information as a result of privacy breaches [92, 93].

Cyber-attacks on SNS platforms are frequently recorded, affecting members of major sites, such as Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn [94–96]. FBI and Symantec have found that cyber-attacks on SNS may exploit human vulnerabilities (e.g., follower scams, impersonation of friends and celebrities, and Koobface), application and widget vulner abilities (e.g., embedded active codes and links to unrelated malware), malicious contents (e.g., infected profile sites, malicious links, and scripts), and social weakness (e.g., intimidating others and following up with victims for repeated terrorizations) [8,13]. Also, TrendMicro has identified the popular types of cyber-attacks that threaten members of leading platforms: (1) likejacking (clickjacking) attacks and rogue ap plications, and Facebook chat-based attacks for Facebook users and (2) spammed tweets, malware downloads, and Twitter bots for Twitter users [97]. In case, a member is victimized by cyber-attacks, he or she will suffer from a series of potential losses and damages (e.g., finance, reputation, emotion, and performance). With increased perceived exposure to cyber-attacks, an SNS user perceives greater risk, ceteris paribus. Hence:

## perceived SNS risks.

SNS offers a social environment where users may engage in inter personal interactions. Interactions include profile creation and browsing, sharing of photos and videos, exchange of comments, participation in groups and events, and ramifications, such as likes and retweets. Over time, members reveal a large volume of private infor mation to their SNS. Privacy management in SNSs is a complex phe nomenon as it differs from other online platforms. In traditional online retail stores, for example, individual privacy has a very clear boundary as user-provided information to an online merchant is in general regarded as private [98]. In addition, there exists a clear social contract between online users and merchants where user privacy is expected fully protected [99]. The management of member privacy in SNS, however, faces unique challenges [100]. First, the boundary of individual privacy blurs because members may change their desired privacy level during the course of social interactions; and, hence, the boundary of their pri vacy [101]. Second, the strength of social contracts among online users may become weak as some SNS members may not respect the privacy of their peers. Third, it is difficult for an SNS user to remove his or her private information that is disclosed by peers. As a result, users’ privacy is vulnerable to SNS. Private information disclosed may be used against an SNS member by others and result in losses, such as reputation loss, financial loss, and psychological stress. A member will become more concerned about privacy when he or she feels the odds of privacy breaches are high and anticipate loss stemming from the potential pri vacy loss events [102]. Thus, we expect:

H5. : Privacy breach concern is positively associated with perceived SNS risks.

Cyber-attacks may give rise to breaches of individual privacy. Culan and Armstrong [103] have suggested that end-users have two kinds of privacy concerns: (1) unauthorized access to personal data as a result of security breaches and (2) secondary use. Likewise, Hoffman et al. [104] have recognized major privacy concerns as “environmental control” and “control over secondary use of information,” where “environment control” refers to one’s worry due to threats to online security including fears of hackers and information theft. Cyber-attacks result in unauthorized access to protected data and lead to involuntary disclosure of private information. They, therefore, place member privacy in immediate danger. Within the SNS context, cyber-attacks such as data theft and hacking may result in privacy breaches of online users [95,105]. Ron Johnson, Chairman of Senate Homeland Security Committee has com mented that cyber-attacks are the biggest threat to individual privacy [106]. For instance, the Pony botnet attacked SNS, such as Facebook, and illegally attained more than 2 million user passwords, which allowed access to user profile pages [107]. In addition, Facebook scams have tricked thousands of members into giving criminals access to their personal pages by infecting unsuspected users with malware [108]. Therefore, we expect that exposure to cyber-attacks may lead to breaches of privacy. When a user perceives greater exposure to cyber-attacks, he or she is more likely concerned about privacy breaches. Therefore:

H6. : Perceived cyber-attack exposure is positively associated with privacy breach concern.

## 3.3. Relationships among threat sources, disposition to privacy, and assessment of harm-exposing events

In the following paragraphs, we discuss how threat sources including (a) untrusted public networks, such as the Internet, trust (or lack of trust) in (b) other SNS members and (c) SNS, and (d) disposition to value privacy) are related to individual members’ differences in the ham exposing event assessment process that may trigger perceptions of risk. We hypothesize the relationships between assessment of these threat sources and the duality of harm-exposing events, that is, (a) perceived cyber-attack exposure and (b) privacy breach concerns.

Cybercriminals prevail on the Internet and launch attacks on inno cent users on a 24/7 basis. Following prior studies [109–111], we cap ture SNS users’ appraisal of the non-context specific, persistent threats through associated threat agents (e.g., malware and hackers) on the Internet as Internet risk perception, which is defined as one’s uneasiness about using the Internet – this could include financial transactions, purchasing goods or services on the Web, and/or posting UGCs [51]. Perceived Internet risk is indicative of the potential threat of online criminals: a user perceives the Internet to be risky when he or she holds the belief that offenders patrol cyberspace. Offenders on the Internet often consider SNS users as one of the preferred targets. Facebook, for example, reported a daily 600,000 hacking attempts to member ac counts [112]. In November 2013, Trustwave researchers found that online attackers had compromised credentials for 300,000 Facebook accounts, over 20,000 Twitter accounts, and 8,000 LinkedIn accounts [113]. A compromised SNS account may also be exploited to spread malicious attacks to friends and friends-of-friends. The potential role of perceived Internet risk is an example of the experience- or association-based processing in decision-making [89]. When the perceived risk of the Internet is evaluated to be high based on previous direct and indirect experiences, a user may exaggerate the uncertainties in SNS use, exhibit unjustified confidence in his or her judgment, and consequently overrate possible exposure of being attacked during the use of SNS. In contrast, a member with low Internet risk perception may underestimate the exposure to cyber-attacks on SNS. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## H7. : Perceived Internet risk is positively associated with perceived cyberattack exposure.

SNS users trust their community members and site providers and subsequently interact with these entities, which makes users vulnerable at times – an unintended consequence of what should otherwise be a pleasant use experience. We capture SNS users’ assessment of contextspecific threats related to the SNS environment in terms of trusting be liefs regarding such agents. SNS community members bring in friend ship, share life skills, offer feedback and assistance, supply entertaining information, render support and comfort, and steer thoughts and influ ence behavior [114]. Yet, studies have shown that a percentage (e.g., 4.8% in the case of Facebook) of SNS community members may be nefarious [115]. Nevertheless, SNS users connect with others in large numbers to achieve more value from the community. Smith [54] reports that in the case of Facebook, 39% have 1–100 friends, 23% have 101–250 friends, 20% have 251–500 friends, and 15% have more than 500 friends. A significant portion of those contacts of an average SNS user is “loose ties” that are individuals other than family and close friends [52]. As mentioned earlier, following Hobbes’ view of trust as a way of risk assessment involving the expected behavior of another entity [56], we use the lens of trust to explore the potential risks embedded in a relationship [58,59]. We define trust in SNS members as an individual SNS user’s belief that the SNS members will behave in a manner consistent with his or her confident expectations even when the possi bility of opportunism is present. This trust is based upon the belief that other members follow appropriate rules of conduct and interaction behavior. Through the assessment of the community members, one de velops trusting beliefs about the peers on the same SNS. SNS members can potentially violate the positive trust expectations of the user thus bringing in elements of risk. In case one trusts community members, he/she perceives a reduced exposure to cyber-attacks that are plotted by and committed by peers. The more an SNS user trusts in SNS members, the less he or she perceives the exposure of cyber-attacks. Thus, we posit that:

H8. : Trust in SNS members is negatively associated with perceived cyberattack exposure.

We also expect that an SNS user’s perceived trust in SNS providers affects how he or she assesses the exposure of cyber-attack threats. SNS providers play a critical role in safeguarding their members against malicious attacks [116,117]. By adopting security systems and archi tectures, SNS may screen the attacks and reduce the exposure of users to cyber-attacks. Facebook, for example, has adopted many security tech nologies including the account protection security status bar, one-time passwords, and remote log-out of active sessions [118]. Facebook also actively cracks down on fake accounts that are exploited for nefariou purposes [115]. In this paper, we define trust in SNS as an SNS user’s belief that the service provider will behave in a manner consistent with his or her confident expectations. This trust is based upon the belief that the company follows appropriate rules in working with its users. For example, an SNS may promise the security of member information and fulfill this promise by safely handling the data. But the site may occa sionally fail to adequately safeguard its members. In 2010, Twitter was fined by the FTC for failing to safeguard its Web systems, leaving site members vulnerable to hackers. “When a company promises consumers that their personal information is secure, it must live up to that,” said David Vladeck, Director of the FTC’s Bureau of Consumer Protection [28]. When members assess an SNS provider site and develop a trusting belief, they expect the site to commit to protecting their users’ best interest: shielding their users’ account, system, and data from unwanted attacks – thus resulting in reduced cyber-attack exposure. Therefore, we posit that:

## H9. : Trust in SNS is negatively associated with perceived cyber-attack exposure.

We expect that the assessment of SNS members influences their privacy concerns about breaches. Relationships (e.g., with friends and acquaintances) are the fundamental building block in SNS. Friendship grants one member the privilege to access the private information of another member. For example, one may read the detailed profile data of another user, read private messages posted, and initiate personal chat sessions. That is, friendship relations enable privacy exchanges in that privacy access is only granted to friends [119]. Sometimes, friendship relations may themselves lead to incidents, such as privacy breaches. Disclosure may take forms such as dispersion of conversations, com mentaries, videos, and images, and it may be accidental or a result of retaliation. In the case of photos, for example, 35% of Facebook users are tagged by their friends in a photo [52]. However, 36% of Facebook users “strongly dislike the fact that others post things about them or picture of them without asking permission” [54]. When an SNS user perceives the other SNS members to be trustful, the expectation is that others will respect his/her privacy and avoid activities that may endanger his/her privacy – a reduced likelihood of privacy breaches. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H10. : Trust in SNS members is negatively associated with privacy breach concerns.

SNS providers play an active role in protecting member privacy. Leading sites have increasingly implemented complex privacy controls that help avoid member privacy breaches. For example, Facebook has offered numerous privacy features such as tools that remove member photos (both tagged and untagged) upon request, display complete visibility of member data through expanded activity log, and refined control on app permissions [120]. It has also partnered with the National Association of Attorneys General to launch consumer education pro grams that provide members with tools and tips for protecting privacy on Facebook [121]. Yet, incidents when SNS providers violate member privacy take place at times. In 2010, for example, Facebook and MySpace were found sending data, which could be used to find mem bers’ names and other personal details, to advertising companies without receiving permission from their members [55]. When users assess an SNS to be trustworthy, they expect a higher chance of the website protecting member privacy and properly handling their shared privacy. That is, users will expect reduced odds of privacy breach events.

Therefore, we propose that:

## H11. : Trust in SNS is negatively associated with privacy breach concerns

Furthermore, we argue that pertinent individual characteristics may affect the degree of privacy breach concern. The literature on privacy has found that individual decision-makers vary with respect to their disposition to value privacy [43]. It describes the extent to which an individual will preserve his or her private information [93]. Prior pri vacy studies suggest individuals place different weight on the impor tance of personal privacy [44,45]. This disposition may be impacted by culture and past experience of privacy invasion [122,123]. Disposition to value privacy is largely stable in an individual SNS user across different decision-making scenarios and it helps explain how one eval uates the threats to his or her privacy. At a high disposition to value privacy, members tend to be hypersensitive about privacy and therefore overestimate the likelihood of privacy breaches, resulting in an exag gerated concern about potential privacy breaches. In other words, SNS users with a higher disposition to value privacy, they are more likely concerned about the possibility of privacy breach. Therefore, we posit that:

H12. : Individual’s disposition to value privacy is positively associated with his or her privacy breach concern.

Finally, we consider a set of control variables including age, Internet use experience (years), length of SNS use (months), and perceived use fulness of SNS which have been suggested in the social media literature for their impacts on the principal constructs in the research model [124–126]. Specifically, perceived cyber-attack exposure, privacy breach concern, attitude, and site use are controlled by age, Internet use experience, and length of SNS use. In addition, attitude and site use are controlled by the usefulness of networking sites.

## 4. Research methodology

To test the proposed theoretical relationships implied by the research model and hypotheses, we used survey methods. In order to avoid the likely cross-site effects, rather than targeting members of multiple SNSs, we survey existing members of Facebook, the single largest SNS worldwide. The survey was designed to capture Facebook users’ as sessments of threat sources, individual disposition, harm-exposing events, harms, attitude, and their site use.

## 4.1. Survey measurements

To increase validity, measurement items of research constructs were adopted from previously tested measures of existing literature [127]. All items were measured on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). The survey instruments were also pretested with 60 users of an SNS to improve the psychometric prop erties of the scales [128]. Based on the feedback, several modifications were made to tailor the measures in the context of social networking services. Detailed measurements of the constructs and their sources are presented in Appendix A.

## 4.2. Survey administration

To validate the model, we collected survey responses from 849 stu dents in a large university in the Midwestern United States. The response rate is approximately 80%. Research subjects were recruited from un dergraduate courses, which served both non-business and business stu dents from multiple majors, and MBA courses. College students are a significant segment of Facebook users. The 2017 statistics found that worldwide men ages 18–24 make up the highest percentage of Facebook users by both age and gender at 18%, whereas women age 18–24 represent the highest percentage of female Facebook users worldwide [129]. Nowadays, college students often have work experience from working part- or full-time positions $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , interns). They are also targeted by cybercriminals, as education becomes one industry that has been bombarded by online attacks [130–132]. As a result, IS studies that concern cyber-security has used college students as research sample [133–140]. The student sample is, therefore, appropriate for the current research. Recent SNS studies have also considered college students [141–145]. Because the research subjects consisted of business and non-business students, these mixed student profiles introduced hetero geneity to the survey data that were collected, enhancing the general izability of the findings to report. Further, the use of student population, over whom the instructors have some control, minimizes the threat of survey bots that may randomly take surveys resulting in survey fraud [146].

We took both procedural and statistical measures to ensure high data quality [147]. Procedure wise, we designed the survey as both voluntary and anonymous. Participation in this study was completely voluntary and subjects could abandon the survey at any time without penalty or prejudice. Participants received a small class credit (0.7% of the total course credit); in contrast, those who declined the consent form had the option to collect the same credit by finishing an alternative task. The presence of an alternative option and the use of a small incentive alle viate the concern that research subjects were attracted to the study solely for rewards; they also reduce the odds that research subjects provided false information only to complete the survey and collect re wards. Also, the fact that the survey was designed as anonymous and that it did not contain questions on sensitive topics, reduces the likeli hood of subjects providing falsified data due to potential concerns such as social desirability. Out of the 849 responses, we removed 11 users that did not use Facebook as their primary SNS, leaving us a total of 838 responses. For robust statistical analysis, we screened the collected data to remove data of low quality: (1) we removed incomplete data (e.g., responders abandoned the survey during the process); (b) we removed data from research subjects who rushed through the survey process (e.g., finishing faster than one half of the median completion time); and c) we removed data from subjects who straight-lined the survey (e.g., selecting the same exact answer for every question). We retained a total of 822 responses of high quality at the end of the screening process. We compared early responses with late responses on attributes and found no significant difference: age (p=.16), years of experience in computer usage (p=.330) years of experience in Internet use $\scriptstyle ( \mathtt { p } = . 6 9 ) _ { \mathrm { : } }$ , and months of user experience on Facebook (p=.47). ## 6. Discussion

The empirical results of the study reveal several interesting findings. First, the effect of users’ perceived SNS risk on SNS use is significantly mediated by users’ attitude toward the site, when the overall research sample is considered. Subsequent a post hoc analysis on gender differ ence reveals that this mediating effect remains significant for male users but not female users, showing that perceived SNS risk is not significantly associated with the site use of female members. Despite a lack of direct effect on SNS use, perceived SNS risk still casts noticeable, indirect in fluence on female users’ site use through its influence on their attitude. Our findings, therefore, advocate the importance of perceived SNS risk in member behavior. More importantly, the results reveal that the effect of perceived SNS risk is not uniform between male and female users. We are, to the best of our knowledge, the first to report the presence of a mediating effect of attitude in the phenomenon of SNS risk and to un cover the significant gender difference on SNS risk. We attribute the observed gender difference to the different social roles that are associ ated with men and women. While we do not specifically hypothesize thi in the paper, Social Role Theory (SRT) suggests that men are agentic whereas women are communal [166,167]. As a result, behaviors of men largely focus on outcomes whereas those of women revolve around interpersonal relationships [165]. Per SRT, male SNS users may consider perceived risk a salient decision factor because it decreases the expected utilities (outcomes) in their site use. Female SNS users, on the other hand, may consider perceived risk an unimportant decision factor because they are committed to maintaining friendships and serving communities, even at personal costs.

Second, we find that cyber-attack exposure and privacy breach represent two focal harmful events that threaten SNS members and that the assessments of these threats affect members when they gauge the risks embedded in site use. More interestingly, SNS members’ perceived cyber-attack exposure is more influential than their concerns on privacy breach on their SNS risk assessment. In addition, the results show that perceived exposure to cyber-attack also increases members’ privacy breach concerns. These effects remain consistent between male and fe male research subjects. Furthermore, perceived cyber-attack exposure acts as the dominant predictor of perceived SNS risk as its effect $( f ^ { 2 }$ effect size: .25) is much greater than the effect of privacy breach concern. Since the inception of SNS, a large volume of media reports and aca demic studies have predominately focused on privacy breach and deemed it as a core issue that challenges SNS users (Hu and Ma 2010b; Jiang et al. 2013; Krasnova and Veltri 2011; Krasnova et al. 2012; Maitland et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2011. Our results, however, counters the media hype by revealing that SNS members weigh low on the perceived threat of privacy breach when they calculate the likely adverse consequence of site use. We suggest that this could be due to the development of privacy breach fatigue [168,169]. A 2014 report of Ponemon Institute suggests that online users might suffer from privacy breach fatigue [170], which is caused by “the continuing stream of news reports about breaches large and small, the notification letters that follow, and the repeated warnings and recommendations to individuals and busi nesses” [171]. Most consumers do not experience subsequent losses. For example, consumers often bear zero liability for fraudulent purchases and are issued new credit cards promptly at no cost. Perhaps, individuals who have grown accustomed to the idea of having their privacy breached are common in today’s digital landscape. They become less surprised by the events and are less motivated to beef up their vigilance or protective actions [168.169]. While SNS members consider it possible that personal information may be breached, they may not anticipate future losses incurred. This fatigue is likely produced when members hear endless reports of privacy breach incidents in the past, but rarely suffer personal loss from these events. As a result, members are inclined to consider the likelihood of the lost privacy data leading to serious damage will be small.

Third, SNS users’ perceived cyber-attack exposure is shaped by the joint effect of Internet risk perception, trust in members, and trust in SNS, among which Internet risk perception casts the greatest effect $( f ^ { 2 }$ effect size: .12). In addition, SNS users’ privacy breach concern is determined by perceived cyber-attack exposure as well as disposition to value privacy; between the two, perceived cyber-attack exposure casts more effect ${ \dot { \ f } } ^ { 2 }$ effect size: .12). Despite anecdotes that suggest a relevant role of peer members in affecting SNS members [28,54,115,172], our findings suggest that they are considered as significant threat sources of cyber-attacks by SNS members yet less a source of privacy exposures. The post hoc analysis reveals that the effects of trust in SNS members and in SNS on privacy breach concern are indeed mediated by perceived cyber-attack exposure. The lack of significant effect of trust in SNS on privacy breach concerns may also be attributed to the fact that trust in SNS does not fully capture one’s assessment of a site’s ability to safe guard user information. Trust in SNS may be due to branding or size effect and such effect is not related to one’s awareness of the data pro tection controls on the platform.

## 6.1. Theoretical contribution

This study makes a three-fold theoretical contribution. First, this study contributes to the literature on perceived risk by adapting and extending the TPF within the context of SNS. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first study to provide a systematic investigation of perceived SNS risk and its consequences from three pronged-perspective – that is, ranging from the sources of threats to the events that imperil

SNS members, to the perceived risk, and in turns to the attitude and behavior of SNS members as consequences. The proposed adaptations and extensions assist in developing a research model that offers a holistic view of the perceived risk phenomenon in SNS.

Second, more specifically, we identify cyber-attacks and privacy breaches as two concerning events for an SNS member. It is interesting to find that member evaluation of the perceived risk is weakly deter mined by the concern about privacy breaches, which have been consistently deemed as an emerging, core threat for SNS users [21–26]. We suggest that this observed weak effect of privacy breach concerns on perceived risk is due to the phenomenon of privacy breach fatigue [168, 169], which has not been evidenced in the literature on SNS research. Further, the study confirms that perceived SNS risk affects member site use and, more importantly, such an effect is mediated through member attitude toward a site. When considering gender differences, our study explains how perceived SNS risk functions to discourage female mem bers, while perceived SNS risk does not directly influence member site use, it casts an indirect effect through member attitude. This explains the observed insignificant relationships between perceived risk and SNS user behavioral choices in prior studies whose sample mainly consisted of female users [61,85]. For male users, on the other hand, both direct and indirect effects of perceived SNS risk exist. Differences in the social roles of men and women are suggested as a plausible cause of gender difference.

Third, as an insightful result of the study, we find that the cyber criminals endangered Internet platforms, SNS members, and SNS pro viders act as important threat sources in SNS whereas disposition to value privacy serves as a pertinent personal characteristic. Despite an ecdotes and early studies which underscore the potentials of SNS peers and providers in jeopardizing members [32,53–55,81,119,173,174], this study confirms a slight role of these potential threat sources in SNS users’ site use and, more importantly, reveal how they associate with harmful events on SNS. While the two threat sources affect the perceived cyber-attack exposure, they do not directly affect individual privacy breach concerns – that is, their effects on privacy breach concerns are mediated through perceived cyber-attack exposure. This interesting phenomenon has not been reported in the SNS literature to date.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Vendors of SNS are suggested to develop responsive schemes that reduce the amount of perceived SNS risks. First, they may take actions to inhibit the threat sources and curtail them from generating harmful events. For example, SNS providers may increase the investment in se curity technologies that prevent and mitigate cyber-attacks originated from or within their networking sites [175–177]. Second, since the re sults show the importance of trust-building between SNS members and social network sites (i.e., service providers), and among SNS members, some practical interventions or managerial practices would promote trust in SNS and trust in SNS members as well. The practical implication include providing a clear site policy, more convenient and effective privacy settings, and continuously enhancing trustworthy site reputa tion through third-party certification programs. For example, SNS pro viders may benefit from privacy certificate programs such as TRUSTe, BBBOnLine, and Trust Guard to demonstrate their compliance with a set of standard security and privacy assurance requirements to collect and handle personal data and further, make sure that users are aware of their success in the certification exercises. Third, communication and edu cation may help users establish a strong faith in service providers and consequently develop less worry about the threat sources and their related harmful events. SNS providers may employ an organizational level of security education and training program to improve their users understanding of the assessment of threat sources and harm-exposing events. This kind of effort is useful in educating SNS users with an ac curate assessment of the various risk elements, removing any exagger ations or false beliefs. Moreover, it can contribute to the enhancement of knowledge and skills in avoiding get victimized by SNS threats [179–181]. In addition, SNS providers may publicize their responsive efforts to increase understanding among their members through a wide range of media coverage (e.g., website, TV, magazines, and newspa pers). Which would be employed to educate users about provider practices that value customer interest as the core of their business values and missions [178].

## 6.3. Limitations and future directions

We recognize several limitations. First, this study proposed the concept of trust in a broad sense and measured it in a simple and general context. If this study considered trust in a specific situational context, such as cyber-attack exposure cases (e.g., online frauds, identity theft, cybercriminals, malicious attacks, etc.), the results may be different. Thus, it would be interesting to investigate the effect of trust on privacy breach concerns in a specific context. Second, as the main research method, this study conducted a survey to assess SNS users’ site use behavior. While the survev-based method reduces interviewer bias and reaches a large population, it has certain limitations such as a selfreported bias, less precision, and control in a survey [182,183]. Although prior studies have found a positive correlation between self-reported and actual data of members’ use of Facebook [184], future research may consider adopting multiple methodologies (e.g., experi ments) to collect objective data to assess the actual amount of user site use. Third, the current study utilizes a cross-sectional design and, therefore, causality cannot be inferred within such a dataset. While a longitudinal analysis is preferred, cross-sectional studies must first be established before future research can examine their viability over time [185]. Fourth, it is important to point out that not all SNS users may seriously consider perceived risks before using a site. For example, a teenager’s decision to use Facebook may be a result of other factors such as peer pressure, which makes site use no longer a voluntary choice. Fifth, in this study we did not fully track the non-respondents. Many faculty members helped recruit research subjects in their classes, and few did not share with us their entire class rosters. Future research needs to implement procedures that ensure the full visibility of the non-participants and conducts a comparison test between respondents and non-respondents. Finally, future studies may consider some other antecedents such as security awareness of users on the security measures and privacy policies provided by SNS. If users are aware of the measures and policies, they may have a lessened perceived cybersecurity attack exposure and privacy breach concern.

## 7. Conclusion

Along with the deep penetration of SNS in the world, online users have enjoyed the benefits of these social platforms while also suffered from the potential pitfalls that using SNS may entail. Specifically, in dividuals may be exposed to cyber-attacks and may also lose their per sonal data while they engage on SNS platforms [8–10]. It is, therefore, important to find out how members evaluate the harms on SNS and how their assessment affects the subsequent use of SNS platforms. Following three-pronged perspective framework [27], this study investigates the SNS threat sources, related individual dispositions, assessment of harm-exposing events, assessment of harms, and direct/indirect impact on member attitude and behavior. Survey data from a large sample lent support to the conceptual model and post hoc analyses further revealed interesting findings. Findings of the current study help scholars and practitioners better understand end-user interactions with SNS platforms.
