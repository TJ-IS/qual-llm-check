# Understanding value co-creation in virtual communities: the key role of complementarities and trade-offs

## Abstract

Virtual communities form the principal environment favoring provider-client interaction. However, questions regarding the way to manage these communities so that they might generate value have yet to be resolved. Following an inductive approach, seven Lego virtual communities, classified following their governance form and objectives, were analyzed. It is found that different kinds of virtual communities appear as complementary tools to favor value co-creation and enhance members’ performance by constituting a system of value co-creation. Moreover, these community features can help firms achieve a better adjustment between their strategies and their decision-making process relative to the use of social networks.

Keywords: Value co-creation, Virtual communities, S-D logic, Qualitative analysis.

## 1 Introduction

Virtual communities can be understood as “a group of registered members who use a platform or website to share information” [1]. Virtual communities are supported by a technological infrastructure, usually social network sites [2,3], that act as facilitators and enablers of social interactions between agents [4,5]. Social interaction is generated through the sharing of information, common interest, and emotional feelings among members [6,7]; these elements are key to the development of virtual communities and configure them as an appropriate environment for value co-creation [5,8–10]. In virtual communities, value is generated through the development of practices of collective value creation. Practices are routinized and implicit ways of understanding, saying, and doing things [11,12]. They are linked and temporal since evolution is what makes a practice appear and develop [12].

Virtual communities can be classified into three types depending on their governance form and goals: customer-managed virtual communities with informative objectives, customer-managed virtual communities with transactional and informative objectives, and firm-managed virtual communities [6,13–16]. Since practices are highly contextdependent [17–19], those factors should lead to diverse forms of interaction in each type of community, reflected in different value creation practices and, consequently, in different types of value.

Although there are studies that consider the existence of differences among types of communities [6,13,14], none of these works address the presence of different value cocreation practices or differences in the kind of value created from the practices in each type of community. In effect, recent works have highlighted that, despite the advances, more understanding is needed about the dynamics of value co-creation [20–22]. Hence, this article aims to answer the following questions:

\- How are different types of a virtual communities associated with different groups of practices of value co-creation?

\- How do the different practices present in a community strengthen or weaken each other?

\- How do different communities interact between themselves to generate value? From a theoretical point of view, this work focuses on how the context in which the interactions take place affects their development and the kind of value they foster, with the purpose of complementing the current virtual communities’ literature. The results shed light on the understanding of the dynamics in the interactions among virtual community members, showing the complementarities and antagonisms between practices, and the synergies between communities. From a practical point of view, knowledge on the functioning and the results of these networks will provide a useful guide for company decision-making concerning the use of these networks, and it will favor a better fit between pursued objectives and those that can be obtained through virtual communities.

## 2 Theoretical Framework

From the perspective of value co-creation, virtual communities can be classified according to the governance form in customer-managed virtual communities (CMVC) and firm-managed virtual communities (FMVC) [6,13,14]. In regard to the scope of the communities’ objective, it is possible to find CMVC oriented toward sharing information and conducting transactions [6,16]. As for FMVC, the orientation is usually based on transactions or the diffusion of commercial information [6,15]. Thus, three kinds of virtual communities can be distinguished: CMVC with informative objectives, CMVC with transactional and informative objectives and FMVC. The CMVC’s characteristics (Table 1) have been identified as essential elements for value co-creation in a virtual community. The importance of information exchange and shared knowledge has been particularly noted [3,5,23–27]. For its part, participation in FMVCs (Table 1) has also been shown relevant for value co-creation. However, references to these communities as value co-creators have had less impact, casting doubt on their positive impact on this aspect.

In virtual communities, value is generated through the existence of collective value creation practices. The company and the client participate in decision-making processes [35] and make way for the joint creation of value or value co-creation [5,9,36–39]. The different virtual communities’ characteristics should lead to diverse forms of interaction, reflected in different value creation practices and, consequently, in different types of value.

Regarding the practices, although there is no consensus on their relative importance and effects, the practices identified by Schau et al. [12] are one of main references. Following

Schau et al. [12], Brodie et al. [17], Laroche et al. [40], Lee and Hyun [41] and Luo et al. [18], four value creation practice groups can be identified: social networking (SON) practices, community engagement (COE) practices, brand use (BRU) practices, and impression management (IMM) practices.

## 2.1.1 Social networking practices

Social networking practices make reference to shared information, fundamentally personal, that creates social and emotional ties among group members [12]. These actions involve negotiations between these members that lead them to personal proximity, common objectives [41], and mutual understanding [12]. Practices linked to relationships generate social capital value [42,43]. It also includes the sense of duty or obligation to the community [44]. It creates a collective awareness of the rules of behavior. That is, members recognize what is right and what is wrong with the community. They appreciate the help of other members to make their consumption decisions and thank them for their flexible and reliable information [40]. Included in this group are behaviors welcoming new members [12] and socializing [45]; the development of norms, attitudes, behaviors, and language shared by the group [17], and expected behaviors [12], managing and governing [46]; sharing information and personal experiences with other members of the community [17]; and lending emotional and / or physical support [12], assisting [47], questioning and answering, translating, and dialoguing [46].

## 2.1.2 Community engagement practices

Community engagement practices make reference to the interactions taking place between the members of the community when their objective is learning, communicating experiences, knowledge, and skills [17,40,41,48]. These practices gather the knowledge value coming from a “win-win” pattern of information exchange between agents [49,50] and will create cultural capital [42,43]. This interaction helps community members acquire useful cognitive skills for decision-making [17]; it boosts confidence, satisfaction, loyalty [41], [51] and the intention of continuity in the community [40]. The behaviors related to these practices center on problem solving, knowledge reproducibility, and free sharing [12]; they aim to identify important community events by giving them meaning, documenting events [12], celebrating milestones [47], praising and marking distinctive members as references for their contributions and knowledge [12,46,47]. Likewise, the COE also mentions actions aimed at differentiating the community from other groups [44] and identifying sub-groups within the community that highlight internal communalities and inter-group differences [12,45].

## 2.1.3 Brand use practices

Brand use practices refer to behaviors regarding product use that describe the experiences of use [41] and show new and improved ways of utilizing said product [40]. These kinds of behavior are associated with community members’ intention to help each other [40], and they may be used for decision-making [41].

Community members may also participate in the modification of existing products to adapt them to particular needs [12] as well as to develop new products, services, and meanings [17]. They can intervene in the proposal of ideas, the realization of tests, and the launching of the product [11]. The knowledge externalization will help generate new knowledge through the reconfiguration and combination of the existing knowledge [52,53], and it forms part of the cultural capital [43].

However, these practices also refer to commercializing the product, i.e., to marketoriented actions, offering advice on the characteristics that will best adapt to the market or the uses that can be made of the product [12,46]. These practices, called “commoditizing” [12], refer to the more commercial character of a virtual community, to its profits, or economic value [42]. Economic value refers to economic capital and concerns the fact that economic opportunities can effectively induce clients to participate [49,50]. In some cases, this dimension has been exclusively associated with e-business [54], recognizing the possibility of creating value from economic elements only when these have a long-term orientation [12,49,50,55,56].

## 2.1.4 Impression management practices

Impression management practices are activities aiming to transmit positive information about a product, a brand, or an organization to the outside world [12]. This type of it encourages others to use the product or brand, share personal experiences [40]; and makes jokes and debates about the benefits of this element. This information favors the creation of a positive image [12] and increases confidence with respect to the object of the speech [41]. Likewise, it is associated with economic capital given their market orientation.

## 3 Methodology

We considered it appropriate to use an inductive approach to develop concepts capturing the object of study [57]. We adopted a contemporary focus that develops the theory throughout data collection [58–62]. Following an initial literature review [63,64], we find that data and theory evolve together in such a way that theory makes no restraint on the emergence of categories derived from the data [65,66].

The study was guided by a netnographic method centering on the analysis of community websites and the interactions between their members. Netnography uses content analysis based on a codification process to decode individuals’ interactions as well as to build and understand theories [67,68]. Following the work of Gioia et al. [57], a first-order codification process was performed in order to collect the kinds of member participation;

this step is carried out through open coding [58] using informant-centric terms. Then, second-order codes were developed to show the theoretical concepts associated with member participation; this second step corresponds to axial coding and uses researchercentric concepts [57,58], “to move the findings to a theoretical level” [69]. In a third step, a selective coding [58] was performed so that the theoretical categories were grouped to explain and understand the generation of value in virtual communities. Open, axial, and selective coding was performed using ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH version 8 (Berlin, Germany) for categorization.

The purpose of this analysis is to link contexts, interpret meanings, and converse with others experts [67] to propose second-order models that explain the actors’ behavior [70]. Kozinets [71], Brodie et al. [17], Cherif and Miled [72], Kannan, Chang, and Whinston [6], and Pongsakornrungsilp and Schroeder [66] use similar methods.

## 4 Data Sampling and Collection Design

The empirical framework of our analysis focuses on Facebook virtual communities. Facebook, the most viewed virtual social network [73], has grown enormously in the last few years [10,25,74]. It offers a user group that is highly committed to the community [75]. These facts have led companies to invest in creating corporate pages on Facebook [76] to seek business opportunities [23].

The criteria used to select cases was that a given community should fall within the category of group or community on Facebook, that the community be active, and that a rapport could be established with informants [77]. Furthermore, we selected communities centering on the same product – Lego – given that the type of product can influence consumer participation [78]. Lego was selected due to the strong ties of this company with its fan base and consumers and its commitment to the development of actions favoring collaboration with these agents [65,79–82].

Furthermore, in line with the objective of this study, we have selected communities characterized as having different objectives and forms of management, i.e., communities managed by the company, communities managed by users with informative objectives, and communities managed by users with informative and transactional objectives. The selection of the sample was performed using theoretical sampling and a constant comparative method [83]. New data were incorporated until reaching a point of saturation to ensure that all of the categories were thoroughly explored [68,84]. These techniques led us to include two groups managed by the company (FMVC1 and FMVC2), two groups managed by users with transactional and informative objectives (CMVC1 and CMVC2), and three groups managed by users with informative objectives (CMVC3, CMVC4 and CMVC5). This classification was defined during the process of analysis considering the content of the posts and the description of the purpose shown in the information of each Facebook group. The selected texts fall in line with the characteristics of completeness, representativeness, homogeneity, and relevance [85]. The raw data consisted of comments introduced by users on the seven pages of the Facebook groups during the months of April and May 2015, and these data were completed with the text of sites that were directly linked in the feeds (the use of multiple sources of data is recommended in qualitative analysis [68]). The period of analysis of each community depended on its interaction patterns. Thus, data were collected until no significantly different information was found.

The data analysis comprises several actions. In a first step, two expert researchers on the subject independently revised the categorization of posts and comments to ensure reliability and internal validity. Then they compared their analysis to check for discrepancies and discussed the disagreements, thereby achieving common understanding, greater clarity, and providing a joint codification [86]. In a second step, two semi-structured interviews were taken from members of the communities [65]; the answers revealed the validity of content analysis. Finally, in a third step, categories of meaning units along the sample were checked by an independent judge [9]. ## 5 Results and Discussion

The conducted empirical analysis allows us to identify three groups of member participations depending on its link to the creation of cultural, social, and economic values. In addition, our analysis shows that the patterns of the value co-creation practices are different in the three groups of communities (group of communities managed by the company, group of communities managed by users with transactional and informative objectives, and group of communities managed by users with informative objectives). Below, we describe the three types of value and the dynamics found in the communities under study based on the practices identified in each group.

## 5.1 Identification of practices and co-created value

The results of the codification process show the presence of 12 practices associated with the generation of three types of value. Cultural value emerges through the practices:

learning, socializing, externalizing, innovating, and openness. Social value emerges through the practices: empathizing, welcoming, dynamism, governing-social status, and governing-formalization. Economic value arises through the practices: commoditizing and evangelizing.

## 5.2 Comparison among virtual communities

The analysis shows that virtual communities develop different value creation practices according to their objectives and governance form. This, in turn, affects the dimension of the generated value.

Figure 4 graphically depicts the position of the three types of groups analyzed in terms of practices and value dimensions (the figure was made using the groundedness of codes obtained from the Atlas.ti software). We may also observe existing differences between relations (see Figure 1, Figure 2 and Figure 3). This allows us to characterize each type of community in terms of how appropriate they are for promoting each kind of value cocreation.

![](/api/attachments/BGPD3G8B/fulltext/images/ab2313b7e63294fd04b5b182e31f996f8eea18be2a2e0626caa9584bd309142e.jpg)  
Figure 4: Comparison of practices and value generated in the three types of communities

The CMVC with informative objectives show a predominance of the COE and BRU practices in terms of cultural capital; this predominance comes from the higher emphasis of these communities on generating and sharing reproducible knowledge, available to all and replicable [17,40,41,48,53]. SON practices, and therefore social capital, also acquire relevance; the results indicate the group’s strength in terms of value co-creation: a high number of new members increases sociability and social recognition, the flat structure and the lack of formalization [12,27] together with the existence of members with greater status, derived from their greater knowledge and resources, fosters group cohesion and knowledge generation [31], and the definition of the boundaries of the group strengthen group identity [44]. These communities present higher values in practices concerning cultural and social capital than the rest of the groups; they are the most balanced in developing practices. In addition, the relationship between these practices is more intense. Hence, these communities are in a better position to co-create value, especially cultural and social value.

The CMVCs with transactional and informative objectives are placed in an intermediate position in BRU, SON, and COE practices, as well as in the generation of cultural and social capital. The few references made to subjects linked to knowledge exchange are limited to information on new models for sale and at times they are limited to events; there is no consensus concerning the recommended level of formalization and social structure and the number of relationships between social and cultural capitals are considerably lower in these groups as compared to former groups. Therefore, the diversity of objectives does not appear to favor member interaction and, consequently, value co-creation from the perspective of knowledge [32]. However, BRU practices associated with economic capital are noteworthy in these communities, making them the ones that contribute the most value through transactions. Unlike other groups of O communities, these communities add value in the economic dimension. The FMVC show the least interaction and developed value creation practices. In this sense, they are also in a worse position with respect to created value. This result is consistent with the work of Wiertz and de Ruyter [27], who demonstrate that the intervention of administrators has a negative impact on interaction. However, SON practices and IMM practices acquire relevance because the value generated in this group of communities focuses on the improvement of the image of the product, the generation of emotional ties, and the attachment to the product [12,41]. Our results are in accordance with previous literature showing that social and cultural capital are the basic element for fostering value co-creation [48,94–96]. In addition, our results show the existence of three types of value (cultural, social, and economic), present at different intensities depending on the kind of virtual community. CMVC with informative objectives generate cultural and social value, CMVC with transactional and informative objectives favor economic value, and FMVC enhance social value.

Proposition 1: In virtual communities, value co-creation practices can be grouped into three dimensions, cultural, social, and economic, present at different intensities depending on the type of community.

Furthermore, cultural and social dimensions strengthen each other while they are incompatible with economic dimension. Therefore, a narrow-focused virtual community will be in a better position to generate value.

Proposition 2: To generate value, the goal of the virtual community should be narrow-focused. Cultural and social capital reinforce each other, but these dimensions and economic capital are mutually exclusive.

The different dynamics of virtual communities lead us to a second result that make explicit the complementarities between communities and the relevance of the ecosystem generated by the presence of different types of virtual communities. A service ecosystem is characterized as being a self-adjusting system, formed by actors connected by shared institutional logics that create mutual value from their interactions through resource integration [97,98]. Self-adjusting and shared institutional logics are inherent to virtual communities [44]. In addition, the integration of resources emerges in the cases under study, so that each type of community is linked to a dimension of value complementing the global generation of value; individual communities’ performance is tied to the overall performance of the ecosystem. Following the types of ecosystems proposed by Jacobides et al. [99], supermodular complementarity in production and consumption emerge given the existence of synergies on both sides. Furthermore, our results appear to associate a central role with self-managed communities focused on information sharing due to their better position to generate value; hence, their relevance.

Proposition 3: Cultural and social capital are the leading dimensions for cocreating value.

In addition, the practice of openness (slightly mentioned by Pera et al. [100], Wong et al. [89] and Hollebeck et al. [47]), present in all the communities, acquires a new dimension highlighting the benefit of the interrelations between communities because of their complementary resources. This practice emerges as a crucial element for enhancing individual and global value. As for individual value, it acts as an enhancer of communities’ specialization, which favors internal value creation; as regards global value, it emerges as an enabler of synergies between communities.

Proposition 4: Virtual communities are complementary. The presence of different types of communities generates positive synergies, and, as a result, a higher value than what would be generated in independent individual communities. The practice of “openness” becomes essential in this process.

## 6 Conclusion

Communities with informative objectives managed by their members show a greater presence and interrelation of joint value-creation practices that foster value co-creation. However, other types of communities appear as complementary groups that help generate economic value and enhance social value. The complementarity of the predominant practices in the three types of existing communities favors a higher value, which arises from the dynamic nature of these practices and the positive effect of their presence and interaction. In this sense, the three types of virtual communities behave like an ecosystem of value creation in which each actor contributes to increasing the total value, and the interaction makes the system evolve and adapt continuously. Although the results of this study highlight the importance of defining specific informative objectives and keeping a narrow scope so that virtual community members feel involved in that community, they also emphasize the differences and the complementarities among groups and the need to build value co-creation systems allowing for a greater engagement of each actor together with a better overall result.

## 7 Implications, Limitations, and Future Research

From a theoretical perspective, this study represents an advance in our knowledge about value co-creation in virtual communities. It uses the C-D paradigm, the practices of value co-creation [12], and the dimensions of value from the standpoint of knowledge management [49,50] to highlight differences between virtual communities. Thus, it cannot be affirmed that all virtual communities encourage the same dimension of value co-creation because this will depend on the community’s objective and the kind of management. The narrow focus of the community is conditioned by the complementarities between social and cultural practices, and the antagonism of these two regarding economic practices; predominant practices influence the community role inside the ecosystem. In addition, practices fostering links between groups emerge and acquire relevance as enablers of the benefits from individual focused groups and from interrelated complementary groups.

From a practical angle, the results of this study provide a clearer view on the likelihood of obtaining value through virtual communities. This large knowledge base will allow managers to improve the way they allocate resources and their strategic decision-making relative to the use of social networks as elements of consumer participation in product use, improvement and development. In this sense, our results point out the preference of member-managed communities with a narrow scope that focuses on information sharing, but also the positive effect of virtual communities with a transactional purpose and virtual firm-managed communities. Therefore, managers should take into account the map of existing virtual communities related to their products, the characteristics of these

communities and the remaining gaps to adjust their strategies toward the achievement of synergies from the construction of a value co-creation system.

Finally, this study also has several limitations. First, the analysis only centers on one product, and it is based on C-D logic and the value generated from interaction. It addresses no other virtual communities of other types of products and makes no assessment of any other benefits of institutional Facebook pages that merely derive from a company’s presence in the network. Therefore, the conclusions of this article are limited to the empirical framework it addresses. The analyses of other empirical frameworks would be of help to support or specify present results.

Second, the constant use of virtual communities to interact with consumers favors the company’s achievement of knowledge in this field. This learning process can promote the companies’ receptiveness [101] and thus its attainment of value in virtual companymanaged communities. Practices develop, so the generation of value should evolve accordingly and results may change over time. A longitudinal analysis on the effect of practice development could pose an interesting and useful issue as a future line of research.
