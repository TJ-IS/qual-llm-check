## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IT usage Beliefs Practical relevance Inference Broadness Specificity

## A B S T R A C T

There has been extensive research in Information Systems (IS) regarding the individual-level beliefs that are germane to technology usage. Theories like the technology acceptance model, user satisfaction, service quality, the diffusion of innovations theory, and others identify a wide variety of influential beliefs, such as those that target properties of the system itself (e.g., its features), the use of it (e.g., its ease of use), or other contextual aspects (e.g., the pressure from peers to use it). These theories have been invaluable for understanding one of the most fundamental phenomena of interest to IS researchers and practitioners: individuals’ adoption and use of information technology (IT). However, as valuable as these theories are, there has been growing criticism about the fact that several of their constituting belief constructs (e.g., usefulness or quality) do not lend themselves to prescriptions for actionable interventions, in particular those geared toward IT design. This is especially prob lematic when it comes to applving or extending them to the contexts of emerging technologies, where best design practices are still to be discovered and tested. We address this concern by developing a Broadness Reduction Methodology (BRM) that relies on Fishbein and Ajzen’s (1975) foundational work on the nature and formation of beliefs to help researchers develop belief-based theoretical models that are more relevant to IT practitioners. In order to illustrate the application of our methodology and demonstrate its validity, we apply it to the context of ebusiness. In doing so, we decompose the broad construct of supporting-service functionality, which has been shown to be a strong predictor of satisfaction and continued usage, into more specific constructs that are more amenable to actionable design recommendations.

## 1. Introduction

Explaining and predicting individual-level information technology (IT) usage<sup>1</sup> has been a core theme in Information Systems (IS) research [12]. The genesis of a large portion of this research is from the domain of social psychology, specifically the theories of planned behavior and reasoned action (TPB, [1]; TRA, [24]). A fundamental tenet of both TPB and TRA is that beliefs help predict an individual’s behavior; in other words, humans make use of the information available to them in guiding their decision-making and behavior [24].

As a result, many IS researchers have examined the beliefs that in dividuals have concerning a system and its use [90]. Notable examples include the technology acceptance model (TAM, [17]) and its extended incarnations like the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT, [80,81]), the diffusion of innovations (DOI) model [49], the IS continuance model [9], and the IS success model [18.19]. These theories rely on the similar “beliefs-lead-to-intentions-lead-to-behavior” para digm and have the same overarching behavior (i.e., technology use). Where they differ is in the attribute(s) being targeted by the beliefs they scrutinize, that is, in “what” an individual (often, a user) forms a belief about. For example, DOI holds that the more users believe an IT is compatible with their needs, the more likely they will adopt and use it. The user satisfaction model predicts that beliefs about the reliability and the accuracy of the information a system provides influence satisfaction with the system and affect the likelihood of its use. This stream of research has been and is likely to remain very lively because the un derlying logic of these theories can be used as a platform to examine the attributes that become salient to users in new emerging technology contexts, such as shopping using augmented and virtual reality [82,85], or interacting with AI-enabled systems [67].

The constructs that IS researchers define and operationalize to cap ture user beliefs, which we refer to as belief constructs, and the theories in which these constructs are ensconced, have been exceedingly valuable, enabling the development of a solid theoretical foundation toward un derstanding how and why individuals use IT. Yet, there has been a growing concern in the IS literature regarding the limited ability of these theories to generate actionable insights for IS professionals such as de signers (e.g., [3,7,22,28,73,86,91]). The primary criticism is that while belief constructs such as usefulness or service quality are strong pre dictors of usage intentions and behavior, they are often fairly broad, and as a result, conclusions concerning their effects are difficult to translate into actionable prescriptions in a specific context of application [73]. For instance, the contributions of a study highlighting that the useful ness of a new IT is more important than say its trialability [39], fall short of a most important next step of how to make that IT useful. While the research indicates that a useful IT will be used, what does that finding mean for developing a useful IT? How can this IT be developed to generate desirable usefulness beliefs, or in other words, what criteria associated with that IT encourage such perceptions? Current belief-based IT usage theories are mostly silent with respect to such criteria, a limitation that impedes the practical relevance of this important stream of work in the IS field.

To address this issue, we set out to investigate how to help researchers develop belief-based IT usage theories that have more practical relevance. Practical relevance is a frequently-discussed topic in IS as well as in other applied disciplines like business and management [8,48,50,60,70, 75]. Overall, this literature indicates that a large variety of tactics can be employed to make research resonate more strongly to audiences outside of academia (e.g., IS professionals, policymakers), including institu tional and project governance tactics as well as others more directly embedded into the research process.

Our focus is on what Benbasat and Zmud [8] call “applicable” rele vance (i.e., knowledge and prescriptions for application by practi tioners). and what Nicolai and Seidl [1011 refer to as “instrumental’ relevance (i.e., knowledge instrumental for action). Such view is con cerned with making research implications actionable by aiming for “re sults on which individuals could take practical actions based on the expectation that the actions could cause the intended effect" [54]. A number of generic approaches and guidelines to help develop IS theories that are more relevant to practice exist. For example, researchers can develop design theories [31] or contextualize their behavioral theories [35]. Because our target (beliefs-based IT usage theories) is much more circumscribed than that of prior work, we are able to tackle the practical relevance issue on the basis of a more specific, theory-driven framework: Fishbein and Ajzen’s [24] theory on the nature and the formation of beliefs.

Thus, using Fishbein and Ajzen [24] as a theoretical basis, we examine the integration and linking of constructs with lower levels of broadness<sup>2</sup> into belief-based IT usage theories, a practice that we expect to be conducive to research insights that are both theoretically useful and practically valuable. To implement this approach, we develop the Broadness Reduction Methodology (BRM), whereby a broad belief construct that is highly predictive of IT usage is decomposed into more specific belief constructs that can be more informative from an IT design standpoint. The method describes how the decomposition can be rigorously achieved and validated. Subsequently, we demonstrate the BRM with an example from within the context of e-business websites as the system of interest. The focal (broad) construct in this context is supporting service functionality (SSF). It is defined as the user’s belief about the extent to which a website uses IT to provide services that support a core product or service transaction, and has been proposed as an antecedent of usage behavior [13].

In sum, this article describes and illustrates how researchers can build on the often-overlooked theoretical foundations about beliefs types and the formation of such beliefs to develop belief-based IT usage theories that are more practically relevant. Note that the application of the BRM to the context of SSF is just one possible illustration as the methodology is meant to be generalizable to any given technology usage context and/or alternative usage constructs beyond SSF. Identifying the design-relevant antecedents to broad belief constructs is especially important in the context of emerging individual-use technologies [79]. Such technologies are endowed with new features that allow for the projection of a multitude of characteristics that eventually shape users evaluations of these technologies and their interactions with them. For instance, the broad perception of the usefulness of a robot advisor or a conversational agent like Amazon Alexa might be driven by a number of beliefs formed regarding its various characteristics and behaviors, such as its demographical similarity to the user [59], or its friendliness and positivity [4]. Hence, a theory-informed approach and its concomitant instantiation in the BRM are most needed to ascertain how design fea tures manifest in specific technology characteristics and how these shape broad perceptions of the technology. This quest might be even more critical in the context of technologies, such as mobile phones and social media, whose use is prone to perverse side effects $[ 2 , 2 7 , 4 2 , 5 1 ]$ . A tighter link between technology features and user beliefs in these usage contexts will provide a more effective basis for designers to craft in terventions that can alleviate the identified detrimental effects (as well as for policymakers to regulate the design of certain features).

The rest of this article proceeds as follows. In the second section, we present an overview of the main existing approaches to improve prac tical relevance in IS research. In the third section, we develop the theoretical foundations for our work and use them to derive the BRM. In the fourth section, we provide the details of an empirical illustration of the BRM in the context of SSF. In the fifth section, we discuss the im plications and limitations of this research.

## 2. Practical relevance and IS research

Practical relevance has been a highly discussed topic in IS research. In this section, we review the key literature that has helped to clarify the nature of practical relevance and the means to achieve it. We emphasize how the two research paradigms that characterize much of the IS discipline, behavioral science and design science, address practical relevance, and we situate the proposed BRM among this literature.

## 2.1. Key tenets of practical relevance in IS research

The practical relevance of many of the findings from IS research has suffered as a result of an arguably excessive focus on rigor [44] and theory [32]. Yet, as several researchers have previously noted, it is possible, and in fact necessary that research endeavors be both rigorous and relevant [8,31,65]. In other words, rigorous academic research needs to be consumable by practitioners [64]. According to Rosemann and Vessey [65], three types of avenues can foster practical relevance: (1) institutional efforts (e.g., guidelines from academic consortia or doctoral programs, [40]), (2) project governance initiatives (e.g., get ting research projects funded by industry partners), and (3) actions within the research project process that are more directly within the researcher’s control. In this latter category, researchers can become more engaged [33,34,77], seek to be more critical [44], conduct evidence-based research [83], interact with practitioners and focus on addressing problems pertinent to them [71,75], conduct applicability checks with practitioners [65], or publish their work in professional outlets and train their students to consume academic research, thus increasing the audience of reflective practitioners [16].

Despite the variety of views on practical relevance, the concomitant markers of practical relevance can be synthesized in three key di mensions: importance, accessibility, and suitability. Importance refers to whether the research addresses a real-world problem that matters to practitioners. Accessibility involves presentation style; it refers to how understandable the research outputs (e.g., manuscripts or talks) are. Finally, suitability captures the extent to which the research offers detailed insights into how the research findings and implications can be achieved in practice. Suitability is consistent with the idea that practical relevance is fundamentally linked to the production of research impli cations that are implementable, that is, “prescribed in a manner that could be put to use (to some extent) in practice to exploit an opportunity or to resolve a problem” ([8], p. 5). Because they both relate to content (vs. presentation) aspects, importance and suitability are often considered as the two critical conditions for producing relevant research [65].

## 2.2. Practical relevance in design science research

Design science seeks to extend the boundaries of human and orga nizational capabilities by creating new and innovative technology-based solutions that are intended to solve important (classes of) business problems [31,47,55]. Because the relevance of IS research is directly related to its applicability in design [8], practical relevance can be viewed as a defining characteristic of design science. Four characteris tics of design science research further delineate its tight relation to practical relevance. First, design science produces design knowledge, which takes the form of design guidelines embedded in design theories that prescribe actions to achieve goals [29], and the value of this knowledge is assessed against utility criteria like efficacy, effectiveness. efficiency, or ethicality [78]. Second, problem identification and solu tion validation are important components of the “relevance cycle” in a design science research project [30]. They are both conducted in close relationship with the relevant application domain in the real world Third, the practical relevance of design science can be further improved by blending it with an action research approach, yielding action design research [69]. This hybrid endeavor emphasizes the concerted efforts of researchers and real-world practitioners both situated in an organiza tional setting, thus the continuous feedback between research and practice throughout the phases of a design science research. Finally, it is noteworthy to mention that although the design science research para digm is fundamentally grounded in and focused on practical value, it also faces challenges when it comes to practical relevance. Lukyanenko and Parsons [45] have analyzed whether the design theories developed by scientists are readily amenable to application by practitioners. They highlight a chronic issue that cannot be fully mitigated, the “design theory indeterminacy issue”, which arises because multiple (design) instantiations may satisfy the prescription of a design theory. This absence of a one-to-one mapping, also known as “equifinality”, chal lenges the usability of design science research outputs.

## 2.3. Practical relevance in behavioral science research

The behavioral science paradigm seeks to develop and verify theories that explain or predict human or organizational behavior. The type of theories that the present research focuses on (beliefs-based IT usage theories) fall into this category. Although Hevner et al. [31] argue that behavioral-science is passive with respect to technology—often ignoring the IT artifact—we have identified two general approaches within that paradigm that have the potential to yield knowledge relevant to design practitioners: human–computer interaction (HCI) experiments involving the manipulation of a design artifact, and theory contextualization.

A first stream of IS behavioral literature that can claim to have produced practically relevant outputs include experimental in vestigations testing the link between concrete design factors and broad outcome constructs. For instance, Yi et al. [89] have manipulated the presence of social functionalities in an online store (product tags and social endorsement) to assess the effects of their presence on online shoppers’ perceived diagnosticity and serendipity (two proximal out comes ultimately leading to product decision satisfaction). Xu et al. [88] have manipulated the design of a trade-off transparency feature into an e-commerce recommendation agent and assessed its effects on users perceived enjoyment and perceived product diagnosticity (two proximal outcomes ultimately leading to users’ intention to reuse the recom mendation agent). In sum, this is “by design” that this literature has produced findings that are likely to score high on both dimensions of practical relevance: (i) importance, by offering theories that include dependent variables known to be of primary importance—for example, product decision satisfaction or intention to reuse the artifact, and (ii) suitability, by providing specific design prescriptions that could be implemented in practice in an effort to achieve the effects predicted by the theory.

Another means to generate more actionable practical insights is to develop and test theories that take the characteristics and usage contexts of the technology into greater consideration, a strategy sometimes referred to as context-specific theorizing [35] or theory specialization [73]. Three sets of tactics enabling deep contextualization [35] can be consid ered. First, researchers can add context-specific factors as antecedent constructs. For example, in a study about e-commerce adoption, Pavlou and Fygenson [53] have accounted for the particularities of the e-com merce context by specifying a set of new factors as antecedents to the constructs of attitude and perceived behavioral control from the TPB [1]. Second, researchers can add context-specific factors as moderators in their theory. For example, Fang et al. [23] examined the moderating effects of task type on wireless technology acceptance. Third, re searchers can decompose core constructs in their theory into contextu alized factors. For example, Taylor and Todd [74] decomposed normative and control belief structures in the TPB into multidimensional belief constructs. In the same vein, Karahanna [38] decomposed the compatibility belief construct into four distinct and more specific con structs (compatibility with preferred work style, compatibility with existing work practices, compatibility with prior experience, and compatibility with values).

The decomposition approach to achieve deep contextualization is consistent with the idea that the constructs that are employed in behavioral science can span different levels of broadness [52,72]. Although no broadness level can be characterized as universally supe rior,<sup>3</sup> the highest one is often most valued in behavioral research because of the assumed theoretical utility of more general notions: increasing the level of broadness of a construct makes it more encom passing and universal, provides it with higher predictive validity, and often allows it to transcend specific contexts and facilitate cumulative research [5,21]. This pragmatic preference may explain why the commonly identified influential beliefs in the IT usage literature, such as usefulness or quality, tend to be specified at a broad level. However, defining a broad construct can lead to overly vague characterizations and come at the expense of actionability [76]. Hence, the value of multidimensional conceptualization, that is, specifying sub-constructs as component elements of a broad, thus highly resilient, umbrella construct [21,72]. Multidimensional conceptualizations can increase realism in empirical models [58], leading to theories that are likely to be more usable by practitioners.

## 2.4. Synthesis and positioning of the BRM within the literature

As discussed throughout our review of the literature, several initia tives can help IS researchers produce more practically relevant findings. The conduct of research within the design science paradigm is particu larly interesting in that regard given its intrinsic focus on producing design knowledge. Yet, behavioral researchers can also implement strategies to augment the practical relevance of their research outputs (explanatory knowledge), and our work in this article is more specif ically focused on this latter research stream. To summarize, the behav ioral IS research process furthers practical relevance through situated practices (e.g., HCI experiments involving testing the link between concrete design factors and broader, important outcome constructs; constructs decompositions such as in [74] and [38]), and more generic methodological guidelines (e.g., [35]).

To specify the contribution that the BRM intends to make within this latter methodological guidelines category, as well as within a broader spectrum of methodological work in behavioral research, we position it in Fig. 1. We developed Fig. 1 to highlight four distinct areas of meth odological contributions based on their focal level of analysis (theoret ical or empirical) and their prioritized outcome (rigor or relevance).

The theoretical soundness sphere refers to guidelines on how to develop more informative and valid theories including how to specify clearer constructs (e.g., [63,72,84,87]). The theoretical applicability sphere, which includes the BRM as well as prior work on theory con textualization (e.g., [35,73]), focuses on enhancing theory (not mea surement) while maximizing practical relevance. The measurement validity sphere consists of procedures to enhance validity when it comes to specifying and measuring constructs—how to rigorously assign a (valid) value to an (often unobserved) attribute of interest (e.g., [14,15, 46,57,66]). Finally, the ecological validity sphere aims to enhance the practical relevance of empirical instruments (e.g., [6,43,61]).

In essence, the BRM intends to provide a detailed procedure to integrate, in a systematic and valid fashion, more specific design constructs into a particular class of theories (i.e., beliefs-based IT usage theories). Within the theoretical applicability sphere, the BRM is a much-needed addition to the generic guidelines for contextualizing theory (such as those proposed by [35]), mainly because the latter are limited in the extent to which they offer detailed guidance on how to systematically achieve construct decomposition via the specification, integration, and validation of more specific design constructs. After all, similar to constructs and theories, which can oscillate between precision and generalizability, strategies to encourage practical relevance can also be more or less specific (vs. largely applicable). As we will explain next, Fishbein and Ajzen’s [24] theory on the formation process of individual beliefs offers a perfectly well-suited theoretical logic to address the lack of availability of a more specific approach in the behavioral literature.

## 3. Developing the broadness reduction methodology

Three key components of Fishbein and Ajzen’s [24] theory underlie the development of the BRM; they address aspects of definition (i.e., what is a belief), categorization (i.e., what are the different types of beliefs and their formation process), and causation (i.e., how are beliefs related). We present each of these components subsequently and derive key premises from it to develop the BRM. We then present the BRM’s three-step process.

## 3.1. Theoretical foundations

## 3.1.1. Definition

Generally speaking, beliefs can be defined as “a person’s subjective probability judgments concerning some discriminable aspects of his world” ([24], p. 131). Such probability judgments are formed through the establishment of a link between two aspects of an individual’s world: an object (e.g., IT artifact) and the characteristics that the object pos sesses. Consider the following example regarding people’s beliefs about smartphones. Someone may believe that smartphones are omnipresent, thus establishing a link (in his/her mind) between the object “smart phones” and the property of being “omnipresent”; another person might believe that smartphones control our lives, thus establishing a link be tween the object “smartphones” and the concept of “control”. For simplicity, we will use the sole term “attribute” to refer to that aspect of an object that is being addressed in a belief, be it a concept, a property, or a value. Also, readers should note that an “object” does not neces sarily refer to a physical artifact. Fishbein and Ajzen’s [24] use this term inclusively to refer to a variety of targets, such as things, events, places, actions, and people. In other words, an “object” may refer to any “thing” that is evaluated by someone.

## 3.1.2. Categorization

Beliefs form through three different types of processes, yielding three categories of beliefs (Table 1). A primary source of information used in the formation of beliefs is the observation of reality (i.e., the physical and social world) through senses or actual experience with an object; judgments formed in this manner are called descriptive beliefs. For example, one may observe that someone is tall and has dark hair. Despite evident contingencies related to factors like the extent of time allocated to the observation or the occurrence of unexpected in terruptions during observation, Fishbein and Ajzen [24] note that such beliefs tend to be relatively accurate and held with high certainty (“they tend to be veridical”, p. 135).

A second category, inferential beliefs are formed through a process of inference by using cognitive mechanisms like assumptions, heuristics, probabilistic reasoning, logical deduction, or other mental inferential processes. This happens, for instance, when meeting people for the first time and forming beliefs about a characteristic that cannot be directly observed (e.g., their intelligence), or has not been observed. Such conclusion may be drawn using descriptive beliefs (e.g., these people are tall and have dark hair) as well as associations, more or less accurate, that have been learned in the past (e.g., height and hair color tend to predict intelligence). Of course, beliefs do not always derive from directly observable or inferred characteristics of the real world. Some times, we just accept and integrate the beliefs of outsiders like friends, colleagues, teachers, or media outlets, a process leading to the formation of informational beliefs.

Descriptive and inferential beliefs have a particularly interesting relationship. Beliefs that are referred to as inferential tend to addres attributes of an object that are harder to assess through the sole input of direct observation or experience compared to those referred to as descriptive. As a result of the difficulty of posing an accurate and confident judgment on such more abstract attributes, individuals rely on a variety of mental mechanisms ranging from cognitive shortcuts to more thorough reasoning. While the distinction between descriptive and inferential beliefs may suggest a strict dichotomy, a continuum is likely to be a more accurate representation, especially because most attributes, even the ones that could appear easily reckonable by most people (e.g., darkness, roundness, sourness), are concepts (i.e., abstractions) that had to be learned one day. Our interpretation of Fishbein and Ajzen’s conceptualization is that on the theoretical continuum of descriptive to inferential beliefs, the former address attributes having a greater degree of concreteness whereas the latter addresses attributes of a more abstract nature. Table 2 illustrates further the distinction between descriptive and inferential beliefs (i.e., between beliefs addressing more concrete vs. more abstract attributes) across three different types of objects (a per son, an IT artifact, and an activity). In the case of a website (an IT artifact), the Table suggests that a user should be able to easily and accurately assess a concrete attribute (e.g., page display speed) from directly observing and interacting with the artifact, but that forming a judgment about a more abstract attribute (e.g., system quality) will require more inference.

## 3.1.3. Causation

Because every inferential belief can eventually be traced back to some descriptive beliefs (or other inferential and informational beliefs), they are connected to the aspects of the reality captured in these descriptive beliefs, such as the design characteristics of an IT. Thus, if someone wanted to encourage new inferential beliefs about a certain IT and/or to change already held ones, not only would the characteristics of the IT need to be changed, but it would also be necessary that the user experiences and perceives these changes so that the underlying descriptive beliefs are updated too [3]. As an illustration of this linkage, think of an individual’s interaction with a laptop. During, or as a result of his or her interaction with the laptop, the individual may form descriptive beliefs about some of the laptop’s characteristics, such as its reliability, or the accuracy of the information provided by the software residing on the laptop. These fairly descriptive beliefs<sup>4</sup> may lead to in ferences regarding the system (or overall) quality of the laptop and/or the quality of the information provided; according to theory, the indi vidual is likely to use these inferences to evaluate the laptop’s usefulness and his or her intentions to use or continue using the laptop [18,19,56]. In other words, descriptive and inferential beliefs can be deemed to be causally related, from beliefs addressing the most concrete attributes of the objects relevant in the context of a particular interaction (e.g., the size and brightness of a laptop’s screen) to beliefs addressing more ab stract aspects of that reality (e.g., the adaptability of that laptop for completing work tasks).

According to Fishbein and Ajzen, the primary basis for the causal influence of descriptive beliefs derives from the probabilistic consis tency principle, which describes a logical, causal relationship between a known (descriptive) property and an unknown (inferred) property. For example, price (which can be easily observed) is often used by a buyer to infer the quality of an unfamiliar product (which is less easily and directly observable), as prices tend to correlate with quality [20]. ## 3.1.4. Summary

The review of Fishbein and Ajzen’s theoretical foundations in light of our research objective yields insights that can be crystalized into the following four premises that will act as guiding principles in developing the BRM. Although construct broadness is not part of Fishbein and Ajzen’s lingo, there is a noticeable correspondence between construct broadness (specific vs. broad), belief type (descriptive vs. inferential), and the level of concreteness of object attributes addressed in beliefs (abstract vs. concrete), which we highlight in premise #3.

Premise #1 (definition). the beliefs that individuals form about different aspects of an IT and its use are captured in constructs that are the key building blocks of belief-based IT usage theories.

Premise #2 (categorization). such beliefs may target attributes that are more or less concrete or abstract, and correspondingly, these beliefs can be viewed as more or less descriptive or inferential.

Premise #3 (construct broadness and belief type). the constructs that capture individuals’ beliefs about IT-related attributes in existing belief-based IT usage theories tend to be broad, encouraging theoretical utility but penalizing practical relevance—they are broad because they tend to capture inferential beliefs, which target attributes of an IT artifact that are quite abstract in nature.

Premise #4 (causation). inferential beliefs are informed by more descriptive beliefs, which target more concrete attributes of a same focal object.

## 3.2. The BRM: a three-step process

The four premises derived from Fishbein and Ajzen’s theory provide a basis for relating broad constructs (that represent more inferential beliefs about more abstract IT-related attributes) to more specific con structs (that represent more descriptive beliefs about more concrete ITrelated attributes). Importantly, they suggest that deriving more specific constructs from a broad construct of interest that is important and relevant in an IT usage context involves three main stages. First, the focal broad belief construct to be decomposed needs to be determined. Second, the abstract attribute targeted by this focal construct needs to be decomposed into more concrete attributes. Third, the more specific constructs capturing the identified concrete attributes need to be spec ified and validated against the broad construct defined at the outset. ## 4. Application of the methodology

Our application of the methodology, which is summarized in Table $^ { 3 , }$ focuses on the construct of supporting-service functionality (SSF) in the context of business-to-consumer e-business.

## 4.1. Applying step 1: defining and justifying the broad SSF construct

SSF is defined as the extent of the belief to which an IT provides services that support a core product or service transaction and help customers reach their goals [13]. Representative statements (i.e., mea surement items) for SSF include: “[the website] has tools and functions that provide services beyond just the product that I may be purchasing”, and “[the website] has helpful features which add value to my overall product experi ence". These statements convey the relatively broad nature of SSF. In addition, SSF has been employed as a construct within belief-based theories where there has been both theoretical and empirical support that a user who perceives that a website offers increased levels of SSF is more likely to have increased perceptions of service quality [13,102] as well as a greater likelihood of using that website [13]. In sum, as it is common to other belief constructs, SSF is fairly broad, which does not help identify which specific IT design criteria are conducive to high SSF and thus instrumental to mode distal desired outcome such as achieving better service and more website usage. This makes SSF a suitable construct to decompose.<sup>5</sup>

## 4.2. Applying step 2: decomposing the SSF construct

The goal of this step is to identify more concrete SSF-related attri butes, that is, functional characteristics of a website that are conducive to supporting a product or service transaction.

## 4.2.1. Ensuring a complete identification of more concrete attributes

To ensure a broad coverage of such functional attributes, we con ducted a series of related studies. In study 1, we conducted a review of the prior literature on IT-enabled service such as Ives & Mason [92] and Ives & Vitale [93]. This review generated a set of 43 attributes (we also refer to them as functionalities).

In study 2, ten graduate business students specializing in e-business were used to act as content experts to generate functionalities. We asked them to recall functionalities based on their own personal past experi ences using B2C websites, and to guide them in their task, they were shown a list of service functionality categories and their definition (see Appendix A). In other words, prior literature, such as the customer service life cycle (CSLC) [94], the Lovelock supplementary service model [95]), and others ([96,97] offered initial guidance. The above-mentioned frameworks specify a number of categories of service functions (e.g., specification, ordering, maintenance, replacement), which can be separately defined and described. For example, ordering refers to the services that help customers express what product and how much of it is desired from a supplier. Taking again the ordering service as an example, this attribute of a website (to provide features that can effectively support the ordering of a product or a service) can be enabled by a number of functionalities such as an electronic shopping cart for tracking desired purchases, or email order confirmations. Another example is the establish requirements service; this attribute (to help customers determine a product’s attributes and options and/or assist them in estimating the quantity of a product that is required) may be enabled by “virtual tour” functionalities; for instance, such functions on real estate websites, help customers determine the properties of a home they may want to purchase. After culling redundant functionalities, this second study generated an additional set of 56 functionalities, for a total of 99 functionalities generated from studies 1 and 2.

In study 3, we analyzed 21 well-established online retail websites across an array of retail categories such as clothing, books and elec tronics. We asked nine graduate students with expertise in e-business to content analyze these websites to identify the functionalities available at each site, again using the service functionality categories and their definition (shown in Appendix A) as a guide. Each website was inde pendently content analyzed by at least two judges. These judges were also free to suggest functionalities from other sites not directly involved in the study. A total of 244 functionalities were identified. We compared these functionalities with those previously generated and eliminated 37 redundancies. In total, the first three studies identified 206 functionalities.

## 4.2.2. Refining the set of concrete attributes

In study 4, we sought to winnow the 206 functionalities to a more manageable set. This would aid both in reducing participant fatigue in the subsequent validation activity (Step 3) as well as assuring general izability of the functions across an array of different retail contexts. We set two criteria for evaluation of each function; 1) its importance for shopping at retail websites and 2) its relevance to a variety of product and shopping contexts. To evaluate each function against these criteria, a study was conducted in which we recruited a panel of 60 e-business consumers to act as judges. The judges were 58% female; with an average age of 38; who had made an average of 10 online purchases in the past 6 months. To avoid respondent fatigue, we split the 206 func tionalities into three sets of 72 with an overlapping set of five func tionalities in each set for cross-group comparison checks. Twenty judges were assigned to evaluate each of the three sets. They were asked to evaluate how important the functionalities were to them on a 1 to 5 Likert scale ranging from “not at all important” to “very important”.

We were sensitive to the possibility that functionalities may have product category specificity so, in addition, we asked the judges to evaluate whether a function was relevant to one of five product cate gories: Books; Clothing; Electronics; Digital Media; and/or Other.<sup>6</sup> This product relevance measure was dichotomous. Each judge was asked to indicate whether the functionality applied to a given product category or not.

We evaluated the level of agreement among the three sets of judges in terms of their shared perception of a functionality’s importance. We applied James $\mathrm { R } _ { \mathrm { w g } }$ [37] as a measure of inter-rater agreement. This statistic compares the variance in judge responses against the variance of a uniform distribution across the scale used. For example, a 5-point scale would have a uniform distribution variance of 2. The inter-rater agreement is calculated as $\mathrm { r _ { w g } { = } ( V _ { u } { - } V _ { o } ) / V _ { u } }$ where $\mathrm { V _ { u } }$ is the uniform variance and $\mathrm { V } _ { \mathrm { o } }$ is the observed variance across judge ratings for a given trait, in this case, perceived importance of a function. The value of ${ \bf r } _ { \bf w g }$ will vary from 0.00 to 1.00 with the latter indicating perfect agreement. The desired inter-rater agreement according to James et al. is 0.60 or greater. The three groups of judges had ${ \bf r } _ { \mathrm { w g } }$ values of $0 . 9 8 , 0 . 9 6 ,$ , and 0.99 thus suggesting a very high level of inter-rater agreement among judges concerning the importance of each functionality. To assure that there were no unique effects associated with a particular group of judges, we evaluated the five functionalities common among the three groups. The average importance rating for each of these five functionalities were not significantly different between the groups $( \mathfrak { p } > 0 . 1 0 )$ and thus indicative that there were no group level effects among judges. These results support that the data gathered from the judges was a reliable and consistent measure of each functionality’s objective importance.

The concreteness of the 50 functionalities serves as an improved basis for design. Theory predicts that SSF will improve satisfaction and perceived website usefulness, but the level of abstraction employed in that theory only suggests that a better website provides higher levels of service functionality. This is akin to stating that more useful systems get used. Neither of these constructs prescribes much in terms of design. However, the set of 50 functionalities derived from decomposing the general attribute captured in SSF does help to inform design. B2C transactional websites should “help assess the condition of the product bought”, “offer secure transactions”, “provide detailed information about delivery time”, and so forth. The results of identifying more concrete attributes associated with SSF were incorporated into Step 3, which we describe next.

## 4.3. Applying step 3: specifying and validating a decomposed SSF model

In step 3, we proceeded to specify a decomposed SSF model and test its statistical validity. This required empirically assessing the relation ships between the broad belief construct of SSF and the newly specified constructs capturing beliefs about the functionalities generated prior.

## 4.3.1. Decomposed model specification

To specify these more specific constructs, we used a card-sorting exercise [49] aimed at categorizing each of the 50 functionalities into one of the three generic stages in the customer lifecycle (pre-purchase, purchase and post-purchase). Two members of the research team completed the sorting task independently. The results indicated a very high level of agreement between the two judges (exceeding 85%). Any differences were resolved after a detailed discussion of the conflicting assignments. The resulting research model used to test the validity of the decomposition is illustrated in Fig. 3. Based on previous research recognizing the role of service functionality in driving usefulness and satisfaction perceptions [13], we incorporated these two important e-business adoption constructs in the model to serve a nomological validity purpose.

Our choice of how to model descriptive belief constructs based on the pre-identified 50 functionalities was driven by our desire to answer a research question that relates to the relative effects of website functions that are concerned with the three main stages of the purchasing process. As described in Section 4.3.4 (Model Testing), we also employed two other approaches for categorizing the set of functionalities identified in Step 2: an approach that uses hierarchical cluster analysis, and a purely data-driven approach that utilizes principal component analysis. Each of these additional approaches can contribute to the answering of different research questions. It is also important to note that researchers could even decide to specify one belief construct on the basis of each of the functional design attributes identified during the decomposition stage (Step 2). In our case, this would yield a high number of descriptive belief constructs (50), which would each focus on a very concrete attribute [66]. In sum, the BRM offers significant leeway to researchers to decide (based on their own research question as well as theoretical context) what are the most sound, meaningful, and usable sets of descriptive belief constructs covering the more concrete attributes resulting from decomposing the broad construct of interest.

## 4.3.2. Constructs operationalization

We developed perceptual measures for each construct. We used prior literature to specify reflective items for the SSF, usefulness, and satis faction constructs (see Appendix C). We operationalized pre-purchase functionality, purchase functionality, and post-purchase functionality as formative constructs and incorporated each of the 50 functionalities generated during Step 2 into a single-item measure capturing the perception of the user of the extent to which a website offered that particular function (see Appendix B). For example, the functionality labeled as “compare delivery options” was used as the basis for the agreement scale measure “The tools provided by the site provide a way to compare delivery options”.

## 4.3.3. Data collection

In this last stage of applying the BRM, given our intent to use the target audience of e-business consumers to conduct a large scale testing of the decomposed model’s statistical validity, we conducted an online field study. Prior to the study, we pre-tested the survey instrument with a convenience sample of 22 undergraduate and graduate students to clarify item wording and assure proper functioning of the online collection instrument. With the field study, we gathered data from each respondent for a specific website, chosen by the respondent, with respect to the performance of that website on its functionality performance at each level of broadness. Invitations to participate in the study were sent via electronic mail to 4100 members of a marketing research firm’s nationwide panel. Individuals were provided a point-based incentive for their assistance in the study redeemable for various prizes made avail able through the marketing firm.

Measurement validity. Given the nature of the satisfaction, useful ness, and SSF as conventional perceptual constructs, we were able to assess the measurement properties of our items relative to the latent belief constructs that they represent.<sup>7</sup> To do this, we applied Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis using SmartPLS 3 [62] to calculate an item loading and cross-loading matrix [26]. As shown in Table 6, all items loaded highly on their intended constructs (> 0.70, [98]) and, impor tantly, more highly than on any other construct [26]. Next, we assessed the constructs’ internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and the average variance extracted (AVE). As depicted in Table 7, all constructs were well in excess of the recommended thresh olds for these statistics thus supporting convergent validity (>0.70, [98]). We evaluated discriminant validity by comparing the square root of AVE with the correlations between constructs [25,26]. The square root of AVE for a construct should be greater than the correlations with any other construct to support that the variance shared between a construct and its measures exceeds the variance shared by that construct with other constructs. All constructs met this test (Table 7).

Rather than categorizing the different functions into groups or clusters based on subjective evaluations of the judges, we performed a principal component analysis based on all 50 items. The analysis indicated that presence of 9 components of eigenvalues higher than one (this value being the commonly cut-off value). Using SPSS, we calculated the regression-based factor scores. The factor scores for the nine components were subsequently used the independent variables in a linear regression to predict SSF (a factor score for SSF was also calcu lated and used as the dependent variable in the regression). The results of the regression indicate that the nine components explain 49.8% of the variance in SSF, with seven of the nine components exerting statistically significant effects. For instance, applying the first approach allows for a direct comparison between the effects of website functions that are concerned with various stages of the purchasing process. The second approach enables a more direct comparison between the relative effects of the individual dimensions of the CSLC (Ives & Learmonth 1984). ## 4.4. Follow-up validation of practical relevance

Two primary provisions enhance our confidence that applying the BRM can yield mode practically relevant models for. First, we ensured that importance and suitability were both embedded by design in the method. Importance is an explicit requirement in Step 1, where the importance of the identified broad construct needs to be substantiated. Suitability is at the core of Step $^ { 2 , }$ which focuses on leveraging diverse sources (including consumers) to identify a set of concrete, actionable design attributes. Note that in this step, we also ensured that all generated functions were rated based on their importance and their applicability to various contexts. Second, practical relevance appears to have been satisfied in the empirical study at the core of Step 3. Indeed, the final model test, where the functionality attributes are grouped into dimensions and used to predict SSF, show that the perceived presence of the identified (and more actionable) concrete functional attributes drive perceptions of SSF, which drives perceptions of usefulness and satis faction (two important performance indicators of e-commerce website success). This provides some empirical support to the validity of the broadness reduction exercise conducted with the BRM in generating results that are both important and more suitable to practice.

## 5. Discussion

In this section, we highlight the study’s contributions to research and practice. We also discuss its limitations and the opportunities that it generates for future inquiries.

## 5.1. Implications for research

IT usage is a topic of high interest to IS researchers, and belief-based theories offer a valuable theoretical paradigm to investigate it, espe cially as it manifests in emerging, uncharted contexts such as shopping using augmented or virtual reality ([82]; White [85]) or interacting with AI-enabled systems [67]. This article’s primary objective was to address an issue associated with a lack of practical relevance associated with existing belief-based IT usage theories. The latter do not explain how design features manifest in specific technology characteristics and shape broad perceptions of the technology. That is, the belief constructs in these theories tend to be specified at a high level of broadness, and while it benefits theoretical utility, it also inhibits actionability. Against this backdrop, we set out to develop a systematic approach to help re searchers produce belief-based IT usage theories and research insights that are more directly informative to practitioners while remaining theoretically significant. The proposed approach relies on Fishbein and Ajzen’s [24] theory on the nature and the formation processes of indi vidual beliefs. In brief, it explains how the broad belief constructs that are highly predictive of IT usage can be used as starting points to identify causally related antecedent constructs that can be more informative from an IT design standpoint.

To illustrate and demonstrate the validity of the BRM, we applied it to the supporting-service functionality construct in the context of B2C ebusiness. SSF is a fairly broad belief construct that is not amenable to identifying which specific IT design criteria are conducive to it and lead to more distal outcomes that SSF influences (e.g., more satisfaction). The results of our application demonstrate that the BRM enables the rigorous decomposition of a typically broad belief into constructs addressing much more concrete attributes that are more design diagnostic. These attributes stand for functionalities that are specific, descriptive, and ultimately prescriptive in that they can be directly rendered in IT. In the specific case of SSF, the 50 newly identified supporting-services func tionalities, grouped in three categories of functionality beliefs, contrib uted to explain more than half the variance in SSF, which, in turn, predicted about 30% of both satisfaction and usefulness beliefs.

These findings come in support to our goal of offering a methodo logical approach for model development that unites the theoretically useful with the practically useful. In the specific SSF context, if we are interested in what gets a website user to return to that website for future business, we can use theory to tell us that satisfaction matters to such usage, that SSF is important to achieving satisfaction and usefulness, and that “allowing a customer to save items in a shopping cart between visits” and/or “allowing a customer to check availability of a product in a physical store” (functionalities) will increase SSF and thus ultimately satisfaction and usefulness. In sum, our study emphasizes the idea that descriptive beliefs about a website’s functionality (thus design) can play a role in theory. We found that user beliefs about specific design features help to predict the belief they hold about a more abstract attribute (SSF) of that website, as is consistent with the contention of Fishbein and Ajzen [24] that descriptive beliefs influence inferential beliefs.

An overall implication for IS research is that the constructs we employ in our behavioral theories need not be devoid of actionable and prescriptive guidance for IT design and practice. The theoretical rele vance of specific and actionable IT design properties addresses a key shortcoming of the broader belief models used in IS research: namely, that such models tend to lose sight of the IT artifact that should be at the core of our research interests [7,8,22,99]. We can theorize at length about IT needing to be useful, reliable, easy to use, of high quality, and so forth. But these prescriptions likely fall flat from a design perspective. At the same time, theory is vital as it identifies and explains whether usefulness, ease of use, etc. are important to users and why these factors are important. In the long-running debate of rigor versus relevance, the answer is that it is a false choice and that applying a BRM like that proposed in this article is one possible step toward bridging both objectives.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

The concepts and methodological approach introduced in this article can benefit practitioners too. Practitioners can apply our proposed broadness reduction approach in order to specify in a rigorous manner the (design) attributes that influence a particular inferential belief that they are interested in “manipulating”. Practitioners would first have to identify a relevant belief/construct of interest; the academic and pro fessional literature could guide such identification. Then, they will have to identify the more concrete attributes that relate to the more abstract attribute addressed in the focal belief/construct. Exploiting techniques used in our empirical illustration is certainly one approach $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . ,$ , elicit ing from users, literature reviews, etc.). However, we recognize that practitioners may prefer a less onerous process. The Ishikawa Fishbone quality method [36] may be adapted for such purposes. The relevant broad construct is recorded on a Fishbone diagram with a focus group consisting of relevant stakeholders (e.g., designers, users) gathered to discuss and brainstorm ideas on the construct. Each branch represents a more granular, lower level of broadness of the construct. Sub-branches can be used for more concrete design criteria until an appropriate level of specificity is met. Another approach is to use the analytical hi erarchy process [68]. This technique relies upon the decomposition of broader concepts into a hierarchy of increasingly detailed decision attributes, a similar process to decomposing a construct into lower levels of broadness. Evaluators are asked to make pairwise comparisons among attributes of various alternatives using a weighted scale. An algorithm is used to compute a final score to identify relative importance/desirability of a given alternative. Such an approach could substitute for the field study employed in our illustration for the validation of the decomposition.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

Certainly, there are limitations to our broadness reduction approach. Although the variance in the broad SSF construct to be decomposed was relatively high (more than 50%), it was not fully explained by the more specific belief constructs identified. A number of factors can explain this result. One is that users may be unable to identify some of the salient concrete attributes that could have an influence in their evaluation. We have strived to mitigate the risk of users missing out salient function alities by running multiple rounds of data collection using a diverse set of participants, but regardless, the inability of users to elicit some salient SSF features remains a possibility.

A second factor is the likelihood that some explanatory factors are not identifiable by our approach. It is indeed possible that inferential beliefs, such as those most commonly employed in belief-based IT usage theories, are influenced by other factors in addition to the descriptive beliefs that our approach focuses on. Indeed, the literature supports the idea that the attributes addressed in inferential beliefs cannot always be fully traced back to the more concrete salient attributes addressed in concomitant descriptive beliefs. The influence of other determinants is also likely to occur, such as residual effects of experience with other technology artifacts. As stated by Fishbein and Ajzen [[24], p. 133], “at the descriptive end of the continuum, a person’s beliefs are directly tied to the stimulus situation, and at the inferential end, beliefs are formed on the basis of these stimuli as well as the residues of the person’s past experiences; the continuum may be seen as involving [a range from] maximal to minimal use of such past experiential residues”.

A third factor is the possible occurrence of emergent effects, which our approach does not account for. Users may form inferential beliefs that are derived not only from the combined linear effects of descriptive beliefs but also from some interaction effects between them. For example, the marketing literature suggests that a customer’s assessment of a vendor’s service quality (arguably an inferential belief) is a nonlinear function of the customer’s beliefs regarding normative, pre dictive, and ideal expectations, as well as vendor performance [11].

Despite the role that the above-mentioned additional factors may play in explaining some variance of broad belief constructs, our illus trative study demonstrates that the proposed approach was effective in tracing more descriptive beliefs that addressed more concrete attributes, and in linking them to the more inferential belief captured in the tar geted broad construct to be decomposed (SSF in our study). The approach builds on Fishbein and Ajzen’s theory on the nature and for mation of beliefs, and therefore, it is designed to be generalizable to constructs that address such properties (beliefs). In addition, the approach is flexible with respects to its use of theory in conjunction with a more bottom-up, empirical approach. This manifests in Step 2 of the approach (decomposition), where we used theory (e.g., the CSLC) to guide the work of our judges and content experts in identifying the more concrete attributes (functionalities in the SSF study). Because we realize that in other contexts, theory may not be available, or the use of judges may be impractical or ineffective, we have articulated guidelines in a way that favors flexibility and that gives some leeway to those applying the approach.

## 6. Conclusion

In closing, we demonstrated how IT design can be an integral part of belief-based IT usage theories. The ultimate purpose of an IT and of IT design is to assist in the achievement of practical goals. The ultimate purpose of theory is justified truth. We hope that the broadness reduc tion methodological approach presented in this article has shown one way in which practical utility and justified truth can coincide within the same theoretical model.

## Appendix D. Structured Conceptualization Procedure

We used structured conceptualization to categorize the 50 design level functionalities generated in studies 1–3 and refined in study 4. As a first step, we made use of the card sorting technique used by Moore and Benbasat [49] and we invited 10 judges to perform the sorting. These judges were 80% female with an average age of 33, who had made an average of 12 online purchases in the past 6 months. The judges were provided with the list of 50 functions and asked to sort the functions, grouping like functions together using the service level functionalities dimensions as a guide. We also provided the judges with an “ambiguous” category to avoid forcing a categorization for a function if it was perceived to be vague or not clearly related to other functions. The ten judging rounds were conducted separately and no judge was aware of the results produced by any other judge. Since one of the judges placed more than half of the functions into the “ambiguous” category, it was evident that the person had difficulty with the exercise. We eliminated the data from this judge in our subsequent analysis.

As the second step in the structured conceptualization, we used hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA, [100]) to determine the groupings of functions that the judges consistently agreed upon. We consolidated the results of the nine judges into a 50 × 50 similarity matrix consisting of pairwise comparisons of each function. The elements of the matrix are the total count of times a judge put two functions into the same category. As a result, each cell in the matrix could vary in value from 0 (no judge thought the two functions were similar) to 9 (all judges thought the two functions were similar). We then analyzed this similarity matrix using the HCA agglomerative clustering procedure found in SPSS version 10. The number of clusters is inherently subjective as plausible numbers can range from 1 to the number of functions (50 in this case). Determining the best number of clusters can be facilitated by observation of the value of the fusion coefficient as fewer clusters are agglomerated. We created a plot of these values relative to the number of clusters. Such a plot resembles a scree plot used in factor analysis and, as in factor analysis, a sharp increase in the fusion coefficient (the “elbow”) can be used to identify the optimum level of clusters [41]. The agglomeration schedule suggested 6 clusters (see Fig. D1). We interpreted the nature of the underlving construct that the cluster represents by looking for patterns of similarity between the constituent functions of a cluster and matching these clusters with the most closely matching type of service. We also independently evaluated the interpretation of each cluster by referring to the frequency with which each judge identified a function to a particular type of service in the sorting process and determining the modal frequency. Both our interpretation and the modal frequency of the judge selections were congruent. The individual items in each cluster seemed to map well unto the dimensions of the lifecycle dimensions examined in Cenfetelli et al. [13]. Hence, for ease of identification, the resulting clusters were labeled as ordering, resell/return, evalugtion, obtain, specify, and payment.
