# What results in more sample downloads? The role of social learning and individual learning with software category

Fan Li <sup>a</sup>, Shengli Li <sup>b,\*</sup>, Xiaokun Wu <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> China Center for Special Economic Zone Research, Shenzhen University, Nanhai Ave 3688, Shenzhen, Guangdong 510060, China

<sup>b</sup> Department of Information Management, Peking University, No.5 Yiheyuan Road Haidian District, Beijing 100871, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Software free samples Individual learning Social learning Software category

## A B S T R A C T

To alleviate uncertainties prior to purchase, consumers may learn product information through social learning (online ratings) and individual learning (free samples) channels. Drawing on a dataset from CNET.com, we empirically examined how social learning and individual learning impact consumers’ downloads of software samples. We further demonstrated that product types (hedonic or utilitarian) moderate the influences of social learning and individual learning. Specifically, the influence of user ratings is significant for utilitarian software but not for hedonic software. In addition, consumers using utilitarian software prefer both time-limited and function-limited samples while users of hedonic software only prefer time-limited samples.

## 1. Introduction

Software is a typical type of experience good whose product infor mation cannot be obtained through searches [37]. As a result, con sumers may have severe uncertainties about software prior to its purchases. Such information asymmetry may reduce consumers’ will ingness to pay and is considered as one source of market failure [7]. To reduce consumers’ uncertainties, software firms may adopt various market tools to reveal product information to consumers before they make purchase decisions. One potentially effective design of informa tion channels is to enable consumers to “learn” software information through their own or others’ usage experiences. Most popular examples include online ratings and free samples. What these two information channels have in common is that they are both formed through usage experiences, but differ in the sources of users. For online ratings, con sumers obtain product information from others’ usage experiences posted on social media. We thus refer to it as a social learning process. In contrast, when using free samples, consumers learn the software infor mation based on their own usage experiences. In other words, con sumers’ acquisition of product information occurs through an individual learning process.<sup>1</sup>

As a social learning channel. online ratings refer to the evaluations of products or services that have been posted by others who have used them. Online ratings have been shown to have a great influence on consumers’ decisions on the adoption of software. For example, a survey conducted by Apptentive shows that approximately 96% of the study’s participants would download a four star app while only approximately 15% of the study’s participants would download a two star app [1]. Researchers have also discussed the influence mechanism of online ratings on consumers’ software adoption [31,38,48].

In addition, to offer free samples to consumers is also shown to be an effective strategy in the software products market and has already gained widespread popularity due to its zero marginal cost feature [26]. In contrast to online ratings, free samples provide consumers with first-hand use experience before purchase. Thus, free samples work as an individual learning channel through which consumers make more ac curate product assessments based on their personal usage experiences [18]. Free software samples (a.k.a. trial versions or demos) usually come with specific forms of limitations to avoid the cannibalization of sales of commercial versions [7,35,36]. Free samples are primarily designed in three dimensions in terms of limitations, which include functionality, availability and convenience, which correspond to the three types of free samples: function-limited samples, time-limited samples, and samples with unfriendly obstacles. The design of free samples is shown to play an important role to influence software firms’ profitability [6,7].

Free samples are crucial to reduce consumers’ uncertainties prior to purchase and may thus have great influences on consumers’ demands. Undoubtedly, greater adoption of free software samples from consumers may relate to the market success of a software product. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the factors that influence consumers’ adoption of free software samples. As mentioned above, a vast body of literature have shown individual learning and social learning to be effective in increasing consumers purchase intention. However, few studies have examined their influences on consumers’ free samples downloads.

Our paper aims to fill this literature gap and provides practical im plications to relevant market players. First, online ratings may influence consumers’ downloads of free software samples. Knowing this is conducive to software firms’ better understanding of consumer learning mechanisms before purchase, which facilitates a more effective mar keting strategy. Second, the designs of free samples may affect con sumers’ adoption of free samples. It is reasonable to conjecture that consumers may have preference for one design over the others, facing restrictions related to functionality, trial periods, or the convenience of free samples. If that is the case, an appropriate design of free samples will be essential in fostering market success of free sampling strategy.

More importantly, the effects of the social learning and individual learning information channels might be further complicated by the issue of software categories. Software can be categorized as utilitarian or hedonic according to consumers’ purpose of usage. Typical utilitarian software includes: developer tools, security software and browsers, while games, music players, and wallpaper software are deemed he donic software. Utilitarian goods are usually reckoned as necessary, functional, practical, and instrumental while hedonic items are related with thrilling, delightful, spontaneous, and sensorial consumption ex periences [3,15,32,43]. Thus, consumers’ intention to adopt the two types of software might be driven by different incentives. It is, therefore, reasonable to conjecture that the individual and social learning infor mation channel may have different influences on consumers’ sample downloading behavior for utilitarian and hedonic software. If this is true, software firms will need to design different information channels for different categories of software. Such a moderating role of software category, however, has not been examined by previous literature yet.

Therefore, we aim to address the following research questions in this paper.

1 What is the aggregate effect of social learning (including user and editor ratings) on consumers’ downloads of free software samples?

2 Which is the aggregate effect of individual learning (the types of free samples. including function limited. time limited. or with unfriendly obstacles) on consumers’ downloads of free software samples?

3 How does the software category, hedonic or utilitarian, moderate the impact of social learning and individual learning on consumers’ down loads of free software samples?

Based on a dataset drawn from CNET.com, our research leads to several important findings. First, user rating has a significantly positive aggregate effect on consumers’ download of software samples, while the types of free samples have no significant aggregate effect. Second, the effects of social learning and individual learning vary across utilitarian and hedonic software. In particular, the social learning channel con tributes to more sample downloads for utilitarian software but not for hedonic software. Furthermore, users of utilitarian software have a significant preference for time-limited samples to function-limited samples and samples with unfriendly obstacles, while users of hedonic software are indifferent among these three type samples.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature. Section 3 presents our theoretical framework and hypotheses. Section 4 describes the data, variables, and methodologies. Section 5 reports the results, discussions, and robustness checks. We then conclude the paper inSection 6.

## 2. Literature review

This research draws on the following streams of studies: (1) studies on the influences of online ratings, (2) studies on the influences of design of free software samples, and (3) studies with regard to utilitarian vs. hedonic product categories.

## 2.1. Studies on the influences of online ratings (social learning)

Online word of mouth (eWOM) has been shown to have significant influences on consumers’ purchase decisions. For example, Chevalier and Mayzlin [10] demonstrated that book sales increase with the average star rating given by previous consumers. Cui et al. [11] revealed the positive effect of user ratings on new product sales in the context of online shopping. Software is a type of experience good whose quality and function are more difficult to evaluate prior to purchase as compared to other goods. Thus, consumers of software rely heavily on eWOM when making adoption decisions. The significant influences of online ratings on consumers’ software adoption have been demonstrated in many previous studies [31,38,48]. Furthermore, in addition to online ratings posted by previous consumers, it is also common that experts are invited to post their evaluations. Previous studies have established that user ratings and expert ratings may have different impacts [25,27,49].

The influence of online ratings can vary in different contexts. Re searchers have examined the moderating effects of different factors on the impact of online ratings, such as product and consumer character istics (e.g., [50]), reviewer and review characteristics (e.g., [33]), and product types [41].

Our contribution to this stream of research lies in that we first examined how the social learning (online ratings) information channel influences consumers’ sample downloads, and we further explored how product categories (utilitarian vs. hedonic) moderate this influence.

2.2. Studies on the influences of free software sample designs (individual learning)

Although free samples are crucial to reduce consumers’ un certainties, they may also lead to the cannibalization of demand for commercial versions [8]. To mitigate such cannibalization effect, free samples usually have some restrictions. For example, they might be time-limited, function-limited, or have unfriendly obstacles. Many scholars have examined the design of free samples from the supplier side. This line of literature attempts to compare different designs of free samples and find the optimal one that maximizes firms’ profits in different market conditions (e.g., $[ 6 , 7 , 1 4 , 1 7 ] )$ .

Another line of pioneering work focus on the consumers side and examine individuals’ responses to different types of restrictions of free samples. For example, Yang et al. [45] conducted a field experiment to explore the subjects’ responses (rational thinking, action coping behavior, and willingness to pay) to the time restrictions and function ality restrictions of software samples. Yang et al. [46] studied the im pacts of the two restriction types on cognitive absorption and self-control outcomes in the context of a game software trial. Following these works, one may conjecture that consumers’ responses to different types of restrictions might lead to their differential preferences for free samples. However, few studies have examined this important issue. One exceptional work is Lee and Tan [25], which empirically compared the effect of freeware (a function-limited sample) and trial ware (a time-limited sample) on consumers’ sample downloads and found no significant difference between the two types of free samples.

We contribute to this stream of literature in two folds. First, in addition to the function-limited and time-limited strategies examined by Lee and Tan [25], we identify a third free sample strategy-unfriendly obstacles. Unfriendly obstacles strategy is becoming more and more popular but has not received due attention from the previous literature. It is, thus, valuable to include this strategy into the analysis. Second, in line with Lee and Tan [25], we also found that the consumers’ down loads of the three types of free samples are indifferent when we do not distinguish software types. However, consumers’ preferences are found to be heterogeneous when specific software type is considered (utili tarian or hedonic).

## 2.3. Studies regarding utilitarian vs. hedonic products

Extensive previous studies have demonstrated the important impli cations of the classification between utilitarian and hedonic products. One important line of research has examined consumers’ preferences over the two product categories in different contexts, such as different decision targets [32], the various natures of decision tasks [15], the single or joint presentation of the two choices [39], etc. In addition to the direct influence of product attributes of utilitarian and hedonic products on consumers’ choices, many studies also employ classification as an explanation mechanism to account for inconsistent findings by considering the moderating effects of product features. For example, several scholars have revealed the moderating effects of utilitarian vs. hedonic product types on the effectiveness of different sales promotion strategies [12,16,24,30,47].

More closely related to our research, several scholars have studied how product categories may moderate the effectiveness of consumers learning of product information. Ren and Nickerson [41] revealed the significant moderating role of hedonic vs. utilitarian product categories on the influence of online reviews on product sales. Cheema and Papatla [4] demonstrated that the influences of online and offline information sources on consumers’ purchase decisions vary for utilitarian and he donic products. Chen and Chang [5] investigated how product category moderates the framing effect on consumers’ online purchase intentions. In particular, they find that a negative framing message leads to a lower purchase intention for a utilitarian product than for a hedonic product.

Following previous studies, we aim to contribute to this stream of literature by examining the moderating effect of product categories (utilitarian vs. hedonic) on the influences of social learning and indi vidual learning on the number of software sample downloads.

## 3. Theoretical framework

Both social learning and individual learning are important channels of product information. It is not clear how they affect sample downloads when both of them are available, and therefore, we first examine the relationship between software ratings (editor ratings and user ratings) and consumers’ sample downloads (H1). Moreover, the limitations of free samples prevent samples from delivering additional information; therefore, we investigate various effects of limited functions, limited trial periods, and unfriendly obstacles to use on consumers’ sample downloading behavior (H2). Finally, the software category, hedonic or utilitarian, is related to consumers’ different incentives to make a pur chase decision, thus affecting consumers’ needs for information. Accordingly, we test whether the software category moderates the im pacts of social learning (H3a and H4a) and individual learning (H3b and H4b) on consumers’ sample downloads. ## 3.1. Social learning (software online ratings)

As an important social learning channel, software ratings can be directly accessed by consumers through website reviews. However, software samples require consumers to make additional efforts to download, install, and trial to gain their own experiences. In other words, learning software information through samples is associated with more costs. Focusing on these differences in the information acquisition through the two learning channels, Li and Li [27] suggest a two-stage information learning process for customers before they purchase soft ware when both product ratings and free samples are available. Typi cally, a consumer first simply reads software ratings that are posted by previous consumers or experts. After this direct social learning process, the consumer decides whether to download a software sample to further explore the product through his/her individual learning process. Low software ratings may prevent a consumer from downloading a free sample, while high software ratings may motivate a consumer to obtain his/her own first-hand experience by downloading and trying a sample. Lee and Tan [25] find that higher software ratings lead to more sample downloads.

Furthermore, although both user ratings and expert ratings are direct social learning channels that provide other users’ evaluations of prod ucts, they are quite different. Depending on lab tests or expert evalua tions, expert sources are assumed to possess the requisite knowledge and ability to deliver professional information [40]. However, some con sumers doubt expert sources’ credibility because expert reviewers might be intentionally controlled by sellers [44]. Unlike expert sources, con sumer reviews provide previous users’ experiences, which may be more trustworthy and suitable for consumers interested in learning about a product, but they may also lack objectivity and expertise. Thus, it is necessary to explore their impact on software downloads.

For the convenience of testing, we setup a hypothesis that the posi tive influences of user and editor ratings outweigh their negative in fluences due to lack of credibility, objectivity or expertise. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H1: Social learning (user and editor ratings) is positively related to consumers’ sample downloads.

## 3.2. Individual learning (free samples with various limitations)

Unlike consumer reviews and expert reviews, free samples serve as an individual learning channel by providing consumers with their own firsthand experience. Software is a type of experience good whose product characteristics such as functionalities and qualities are difficult to observe in advance. Thus, consumers are presented with uncertainties with regard to such products before adoption [29]. Previous studies (i.e., [6–8]) have found that individual learning through free samples can reduce consumers’ uncertainties and consumers would be more willing to adopt a free sample if it can disclose more product information.

Meanwhile, a free sample serves as a prototype of a commercial product; therefore, there exists competition between a free sample and its commercial counterpart. Specifically, some consumers may use a free sample without purchase of the commercial product. To reduce this cannibalization effect, sellers impose limitations on free samples such as trial period limitation, limited function limitation, or obstacles to use [28]. A time-limited sample can disclose full information on software’s functions and features, but the restriction on trial period may prevent extensive learning. A function-limited sample allows unlimited time. but the restrictions on the functions disclose partial information about software’s functions and features. A sample with unfriendly obstacles to use may apply watermarks, warning messages, or a low resolution. Although this type of sample has no restriction on time, the unfriendly setting may impede consumers’ precise learning of any function or feature. In summary, all of these samples have advantage and disad vantage in terms of providing using experience, while a time-limited sample releases more information about software function than a function-limited sample and a sample with unfriendly obstacles. Without knowing whether using experience or disclosure levels of function information is more valued by consumers, we may expect that consumers have indifferent interest in using different types of samples to realize individual learning.

Lee and Tan [25] found that a function-limited sample and a time-limited sample have similar levels of consumer software sampling. Extending their study, we compare consumers’ sample downloading behavior among three types of samples, function-limited, time-limited, and obstacles to use, and propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Compared with a function-limited sample, a time-limited sample or a sample with unfriendly obstacle leads to an indifferent number of sample downloads.

## 3.3. The moderating effect of software category on social learning and individual learning

There are two general categories of software, utilitarian and hedonic software.<sup>2</sup> The former one is often labeled “practical” or “necessary” while the latter is often labeled “frivolous” or “decadent” [42]. Relating to the inherent nature of these two software categories, consumers purchase motivations also differ. The purchase of utilitarian software is motivated mainly by the desire to satisfy a realistic need or accomplish a functional task, and the purchase of hedonic software is motivated by the desire for pleasure, enjoyment, and fun $[ 9 , 1 3 , 4 2 ]$ . Therefore, consumers’ attitudes toward utilitarian software are influenced by cognitive factors while their attitudes toward hedonic software are largely determined by emotional factors, where cognitive factors are usually measured by usefulness, efficiency, and convenience and emotional factors are usually measured by pleasure scales [2,22].

Because of different motivations to purchase, consumers’ needs for software information before purchase might be different [25]. Specif ically, for utilitarian software, consumers need to determine whether the software is able to achieve certain goals or solve practical problems as their purchasing decisions regarding utilitarian software largely depend on software functions and quality ([19,21]). Consequently, the infor mation channel providing appropriate learning on software functions and quality is attractive to consumers.

For social learning, when both user ratings and expert ratings are available, consumers may tend to believe the more credible and suitable one. That one is user ratings, which provides previous consumers evaluations of the software’s function and quality. Thus, user ratings may positively affect the consumers’ sample downloads of utilitarian software. However, editor ratings are less credible and cannot represent consumers’ attitude to the product; therefore, editor ratings may have insignificant effects. Meanwhile, free samples enable experience-based individual learning, and various types of samples deliver product in formation differently. In terms of software functions and quality, a timelimited sample can provide full information, function-limited samples can provide partial information, and a sample with obstacles to use prevents consumers from clearly learning any function. Accordingly, as a sample can disclose more information of software’s function and quality, consumers are more willing to download it to further learn whether the software can solve specific realistic problems. That is, for utilitarian software, when compared with function-limited samples, consumers are more incentivized to download time-limited samples and are less incentivized to download samples with obstacles. We thus test the following hypotheses for utilitarian software

H3a: For utilitarian software, user ratings are positively related to consumers’ sample downloads, while editor ratings are insignificantly related to consumers’ sample downloads.

H3b: For utilitarian software, when compared with function-limited samples, time-limited samples lead to a larger number of sample downloads while samples with unfriendly obstacles lead to a smaller number of sample downloads.

By contrast, consumers are more emotional and relaxed in making a purchase decision regarding hedonic software. The consumption de cisions are mainly made for the purchase of pleasure and leisure, and thus consumers are likely to make a purchase decision without sys tematically learning the product function and quality [23,42]. Instead, consumers may care more about whether the hedonic software fit their personal tastes or preferences. For example, a sport fan may prefer sport games such as Wii Sports, while a music fan would be more inclined to download games like Guitar Hero. Consequently, for one thing, con sumers are not as keen to learn quality and functions for hedonic soft ware as for utilitarian software; for another, customized experiences and personal feelings, rather than evaluations from previous consumers and experts, may drive consumers’ emotional response and purchase inten tion. In summary, unlike the purchase of utilitarian software where cognitive factors dominate, emotion plays more important roles in consumers’ purchases of hedonic software. According to Paul Marsden at Digital Intelligence Today [34], “sometimes a first-hand experience from a discounted trial . . . may trump both.<sup>3</sup> Social learning is great, but sometimes individual learning is better.” Although user ratings and editor ratings may provide other users’ evaluations about the product, they fail to provide consumers with their own customized experience or to strike a chord for consumers. Accordingly, consumers may not apply the social learning channel to determine whether they like the software

or not.

Moreover, although the levels of information disclosure on software quality and functionality are different among function-limited samples, time-limited samples and samples with unfriendly obstacles, all of these samples pose various restrictions to prevent consumers from friendly using. Specifically, consumers’ feelings are restricted by limitations on functionality, availability, and convenience, leading to a similar level of reluctance to use different samples. As mentioned earlier, consumers are not as incentivized to learn quality and function for hedonic software as for utilitarian software. Thus, the different level of information on quality and function released by various samples may not drive con sumers’ different willingness to download samples. By contrast, con sumers may weigh more on their reluctant emotion to use due to sample limitations. Thus, compared with a function limited sample, consumers may have similar willingness to download a time-limited sample or a sample with unfriendly obstacles. We thus test the following hypotheses for hedonic software.

H4a: For hedonic software, both user ratings and editor ratings are insignificantly related to consumers’ sample downloads.

H4b: For hedonic software, compared with a function-limited sam ple, a time-limited sample or a sample with use obstacle leads to an indifferent number of sample downloads.

## 4. Empirical analysis

In this section, we present our empirical analysis that tests the hy potheses proposed above. We begin with a detailed description of our data, which includes the collection process and summary statistics. We then discuss our empirical models and define the variables.

## 4.1. Data

CNET.com, a popular American media website, publishes tech product reviews, news, prices, videos, forums, and articles. The down load section provides free and legal software sample downloads by software category. Note that each sample on CNET.com has an associ ated webpage, which not only displays information on its corresponding software such as the software title, price, file size, ratings, and operating system, but also presents information on the sample such as the license model, the limitation, and the number of downloads (Fig. 2). Thus, before downloading a software sample, a consumer can learn about both the software information and the sample information.

The license models of free samples on CNET.com involve three types: free, free-to-try, and purchase. Free samples with a free license perform identically as commercial software. Samples with a free-to-try license can be used by consumers with certain limitations (e.g., time limitation, function limitation, or use obstacles). These limitations are set to reduce the cannibalization effect, which arises because consumers may use free samples instead of paying for commercial products [7,20]. Samples with a purchase license list the titles of the features or functions of the commercial software but cannot be used by consumers at all. In sum mary, samples with a free-to-try license not only disclose product in formation with the provision of personal use experiences, but also reduce the cannibalization loss; therefore, they are widely used as an information channel in the software industry to boost profits [28]. Thus, we restrict our attention to those with free-to-try license models and explore the effects of various limitations on sample downloads.

We collect data using a Python-based crawler from the download section of the website on February 2018. Overall, our data set includes 1897 ob servations of software with a free-to-try license on CNET.com. CNET groups all of the software into 21 types: (1) Games, (2) Educational Software, (3) Entertainment Software, (4) Business Software, (5) Utilities & Operating Systems, (6) Productivity Software, (7) Travel, (8) Drivers, (9) Home Soft ware, (10) Digital Photo Software, (11) Internet Software, (12) Browsers, (13) Desktop Enhancements, (14) MP3 & Audio Software, (15) Developer Tools, (16) Communications, (17) Video Software, (18) Security Software, (19) Graphic Design Software, (20) Screensavers & Wallpaper, and (21) Networking Software. Based on the functionalities and features, we consider the software in types (2), (4), (5), (6), (8), (11), (12), (15), (18), and (21) a utilitarian software; and the remaining are considered as hedonic software. Based on our broader classification, the former category is related to func tional, practical, and instrumental purposes such as software that is used to enhance internet connections and the latter category includes software used for fun, developing hobbies, and enjoying life such as video games and music players.

## 5. Results and discussion

## 5.1. Baseline estimation: the aggregate impact of social learning and individual learning

The regression results of Eq. (1) are shown in Table 2 with several interesting findings. First, the user rating coefficient is significantly positive while the editor rating coefficient is insignificant. Therefore, H1 is partially supported. This result, which indicates that on average, user ratings but not editor ratings shape consumers’ sample downloading behaviors, is consistent with previous studies [11,27]. Unlike editor ratings, user ratings provide product evaluations from previous con sumers; and therefore, they are perceived to be a more credible infor mation channel and more representative of current consumers’ opinions [44]. To purchase experience products, consumers care more about experiences of previous consumers than expert advice; therefore, the role of expertise is not significant. Moreover, in the online software market, sellers may intentionally select expert reviewers. CNET.com employs the expertise and technicians to conduct lab tests and statistical analyses on CNET Labs, which lead to the expert’s evaluation to the software. Because of experts' partnerships with the website, editor rat ings might be biased and incredible. Consequently, editor ratings fail to affect consumers’ purchase decisions, particularly when user ratings are available. The different impacts of user ratings and editor ratings reflect consumers’ different attitudes to information from various social learning channels.

Second, the coefficients of time limitation and obstacles to use are insignificant, which suggest that, when compared with function limi tation, imposing time limitation or obstacles to use on free samples does not significantly yield a different number of sample downloads. There fore, H2 is supported. Intuitively, these three type samples may release different levels of software information, while they all cause consumers reluctance to use due to various restrictions. Overall, various sample limitations (i.e., function limitation, time limitation, or obstacles to use) do not lead to different incentives to download these samples.

To test H3b and H4b (the moderating effects of the software category on the relationship between individual learning and the number of sample downloads), the interaction terms between the software category and sample limitations are also added to the baseline regression. ## 5.2.1. The impact of social learning moderated by software category

The results (Table 4) show that, for utilitarian software, editor rat ings affect the number of sample downloads insignificantly, while user ratings are positively related to the number of sample downloads. ## 5.2.2. The impact of individual learning moderated by software category

It is also interesting to explore consumers’ attitudes toward various ways of individual learning by software category. The results on sample downloads for utilitarian software (Table 4) show that when compared with a function-limited sample, a time-limited sample leads to a larger number of downloads while a sample with unfriendly obstacle generates an insignificantly different number of downloads. Therefore, H3b is partially supported. A function-limited sample releases partial infor mation on function, while a time-limited sample releases full informa tion on function. Consumers of utilitarian software would like to learn more information on function through a time-limited sample than a function-limited sample. By contrast, although a sample with unfriendly using obstacles hinder consumers from any clear study of any software function, it does not lead to significantly less downloads than a functionlimited sample. This mav be due to the information disclosure levels between these two type samples that are not sufficiently different to drive consumers’ dissimilar incentive to download them.

The results (Table 5) show that for a hedonic software, when compared with a time-limited sample, a function-limited sample or a sample with obstacles to use leads to insignificantly different down loads. H4b is supported. As mentioned earlier, although these three types of samples disclose different levels of information about software quality and function, they all set restrictions on using. For hedonic software, consumers care more about use experience that a sample provides than software information that a sample releases. This is because the former one is more likely to generate emotional response to increase purchase intention. Consequently, due to similar satisfaction from using various samples, consumers have indifferent incentives to download these samples.

Moreover, extending Tables 4 and 5, we have calculated the relative effect on weekly sample downloads of obstacles to use as compared to time limitation. We found that the relative effect is − 229.16 with pvalue of 0.09 for utilitarian software and 31.84 with p-value of 0.63 for hedonic software. This implies that compared with a time-limited sample, consumers are less likely to download a sample with obstacles to use for utilitarian software. This is because the former releases more information than the latter, satisfying consumers’ needs for acquiring information about function and quality of utilitarian software before purchasing. By contrast, for hedonic software, consumers’ preference is indifferent between a time-limited sample and a sample with obstacles.

## 5.3. Robustness check

The robustness test is performed to validate the effect of social learning and individual learning on sample downloads that has been supported by the data analysis. The dependent variable, which is the number of weekly sample downloads for the software, is a nonnegative integer. Poisson regression, a nonlinear estimation method, is often used for modeling count data; therefore, it might be appropriate for it to be applied as a rigorous test.

The results of Poisson regression Tables 6 and 7) are consistent with those of the baseline regression (Table 2) and the regression with moderating effects (Table 3). In addition, we include observations with zero software price to estimate Eqs. (1) and ((2) again using OLS esti mation. The results of this alternative robustness test are also similar as our main findings. Consequently, the outcomes of the robustness test strengthen the validity of the hypotheses testing results.

In our paper, we differentiated two information channels through which consumers’ uncertainly may be alleviated: social learning and individual learning. Social learning refers to the process through which consumers learn product information based on other users’ usage ex periences (online ratings). In addition, consumers can also learn product information through individual learning based on their own firsthand experiences (free samples). The first research issue drawing our atten tion is how the two information channels interplay. More specifically, how do online ratings (social learning) affect consumers’ downloads of free samples (individual learning)? Software firms may have different options in terms of restrictions when releasing their free samples. In this research, we identified three types of free samples including timelimited sample, function-limited sample, and sample with unfriendly obstacles. It is, therefore, interesting to examine whether consumers have preferences for specific types of samples. Furthermore, software categories may also play a role to influence consumers’ downloads of free samples. Thus, we categorize software as utilitarian and hedonic to show how software categories moderate the effects of social learning and individual learning.

To answer these questions, we conducted analysis using a dataset from CNET.com. Our analysis leads to the following important findings. First, in terms of aggregate effects, we find that user ratings (one way of social learning) are positively related to consumers’ downloads of soft ware samples, while consumers are indifferent to whether the free samples are restricted by time, function, or unfriendly obstacles. Second, software types are found to moderate the effects of social learning and individual learning. More specifically, we find that the influence of user ratings is significant for utilitarian software but not for hedonic soft ware. Consumers also have different preferences for the types of free samples among utilitarian and hedonic software. When using utilitarian software, consumers prefer time-limited free samples. In contrast, users of hedonic software are indifferent to different types of restrictions.

We make three main theoretical contributions. First, we distinguish social learning and individual learning and examined their impacts on consumers’ download of software samples. The result contributes to the literature on both eWOM and software free sampling. Second, we identified three types of free samples based on the imposed restrictions on usage, which include function limitation, time limitation, and un friendly obstacles. In particular, free samples with unfriendly obstacles are becoming popular and now accounts for an increasingly significant proportion of free samples. Despite its importance, few existing litera tures have examined this type of free samples. Our research included it into the analysis and compared it with the other two types of free samples. Results show that unfriendly obstacles may have different in fluences than usage time restrictions for utilitarian software. Such analysis can help obtain a better understanding about consumers preferences among different types of samples. Third, our categorization of software into utilitarian products and hedonic products leads to important findings on the moderating effects of software categories on the influence of information disclosure. This finding reveals the different influence mechanisms of online ratings (social learning) and free sam ples (individual learning) for two different categories of software. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to reveal these different mech anisms and include the samples with obstacles to use in the analysis of sample downloading.

Our research also provides important practical implications to the software industry. Online ratings and free samples provide consumers with access to software information prior to purchase through the social learning and individual learning processes. However, their effects might depend on the categories of the software. First, software firms should notice the different roles of online ratings for utilitarian and hedonic software. As a result, utilitarian software firms should put more efforts into boosting user ratings because they are related to more free sample downloads. For example, firms may use gamification or reward mech anisms to encourage satisfied users to rate. However, this strategy is not effective for hedonic software firms. Meanwhile firms should note that recurring experts to provide ratings might not work for either software categories. Second, the design of free samples is also important since a more effective design would yield more sample downloads. According to our findings, utilitarian software firms should consider time-limited free samples as their primary strategy to attract more downloads for samples. In contrast, for hedonic software firms, each type of free samples make no difference in terms of sample downloads.

This research can be extended in several directions. For instance, the ultimate goal of the software firms should be to increase their profits. However, due to the lack of sales data, the current research focuses on consumers’ downloading behaviors of free samples. The influence of consumers’ sampling behaviors on sales cannot be measured in this research. Future research can combine sales data and data on the downloads of free samples to analyze the implications of our findings on software sales.
