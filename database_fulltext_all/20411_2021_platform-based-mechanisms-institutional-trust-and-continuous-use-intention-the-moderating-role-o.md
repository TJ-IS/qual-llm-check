# Platform-based mechanisms, institutional trust, and continuous use intention: The moderating role of perceived effectiveness of sharing economy institutional mechanisms

Baozhou Lu <sup>a,\*</sup>, Zhen Wang <sup>a</sup>, Song Zhang b

<sup>a</sup> School of Business, Qingdao University, 308 Ning Xia Rd, Qingdao, 266071, PR China

<sup>b</sup> College of Quality & Standardization, Qingdao University, 308 Ning Xia Rd, Qingdao, 266071, PR China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Sharing economy Trust in the platform Continuous use intention Platform-based institutional mechanisms General institutional context

## A B S T R A C T

Trust plays an essential role in the sharing economy (SE), which has dramatically changed many industries, including tourism, hospitality, and transportation. As interpersonal trust is difficult to develop in the SE, this study focuses on a specific type of institutional trust at the platform level, namely trust in the platform and it role in shaping clients’ continuous use. Drawing on institutional theory, this study examines two constructs, i.e., the perceived effectiveness of the platform-based institutional structure (PEPIS), the perceived effectiveness of the SE institutional mechanisms (PESEIM), and their roles in trust in the platform and continuous use intention. A model is proposed to depict how trust in the platform is formed and operates under the boundary condition of PESEIM. Our findings suggest that PEPIS has a positive impact on trust in the platform, which leads to continuous use intention, and PESEIM negatively moderates the relationship between PEPIS and trust in the platform and the relationship between trust in the platform and continuous use intention.

## 1. Introduction

The sharing economy (SE) is an emerging socioeconomic system that allows individuals to engage with their peers in temporary disposition and acquisition of underutilized resources through digital platforms such as Airbnb, Uber, and Didi [48]. It has been experiencing tremen dous growth in the last few years, bringing dramatic changes to a variety of industries, particularly tourism, hospitality, and transportation in dustries [8, 38, 43]. For example, Airbnb, the largest sharing accom modation platform in the world, has over seven million listings worldwide, penetrating over 20% of the market in the accommodation industry [61]. In China, the sharing transportation revenue reached 248 billion RMB in 2018 and that for sharing accommodations reached 16.5 billion RMB, accounting for 10.3% and 6.1%, respectively, of the total revenue of their respective industries [58]. In particular, scholars envision that SEs will fundamentally change the future dynamics of many industries, including hospitality, tourism, and manufacturing [21, 59].

Despite having benefits such as the efficient use of resources and reduction of ecological impacts for societies [19, 55], the rapid growth of the SE is accompanied by some serious problems, including potential safety hazards, privacy breaches, data trafficking, etc. For instance, a female rider was brutally raped and murdered by a Didi (the largest Chinese online car-hailing platform) driver on May 5, 2018, which resulted in an extremely negative social impact and caused Didi to suspend its hitch-riding services nationwide and rectify the problems in operation and safety management. In the sharing accommodation in dustry, the privacy, property, and even personal safety of guests might be violated by potential malicious hosts. As illustrated in Table 1, while the e-commerce context does not necessarily require transactional parties to meet in person, the settings of SEs often involve both digital and face-to-face interactions [41]. These problems, if not properly addressed, would increase the perceived risk and uncertainty faced by clients, thus undermining their confidence and willingness to continu ously participate in the SE. Trust, particularly interpersonal trust, as an effective social mechanism mitigating perceived risk and uncertainty, is mentioned in both the academic and practical research to play an important role in client decision-making in the context of the SE (e.g., [28, 43, 50, 62]). However, in the SE context, clients and service pro viders rarely interact with each other before the exchange, and in some cases (e.g., sharing transportation and take-away service), unknown providers are automatically assigned to clients by the platform. Because institutional trust is tied to formal societal structures [57, 66], drawing on the perspective of institutional theory, we consider the following two institutional structure levels to investigate how trust in the platform is formed and how it takes effect in the context of SEs: platform-based mechanisms at the local level [16, 44, 56], which have a direct impact on trust in the platform and general institutional mechanisms at the macro level [14], which constitute the regulative boundary conditions under which trust in the platform operates.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Institutional trust in sharing economy

Institutional trust is defined as trust that is based on formal societal structures, such as guarantees and recommendations from institutions, systems, or third parties [57, 66]. Unlike interpersonal trust, which primarily builds upon social ties among individual actors e.g., frequent contacts and similar social and cultural backgrounds, institutional trust is established in formal social structures such as promises, regulations, guarantees, and legal recourses. Institutional trust is a solid basis for interpersonal trust and transactions because it enables shared expecta tions even among actors who have no mutual experience or previous interactions [42]. It then becomes particularly important for situations where two unknown parties need to make transactions and where interpersonal trust is difficult to form such as the SE context.

The increasing importance of institutional trust has been recognized in the marketplace-based e-commerce literature (e.g., [4, 34, 35, 44]). Pavlou and Gefen [44] posited that institutional trust is particularly suited for marketplace-based e-commerce in which buyers predomi nantly transact with new and unknown sellers. The marketplace, as the intermediary institution, provides a reliable and secure institutional context where fair values and benevolent behaviors are encouraged, and opportunistic behaviors and violations of the community’s rules and norms will be restricted or punished [34]. Thus, trust in the marketplace has been indicated as the most important type of institutional trust in the context of the online marketplace in the literature (e.g., [16, 44]). Trust in the marketplace has become an extremely important prerequisite for trust in individual sellers and continuous purchases in marketplace-based e-commerce [30, 32, 35].

Institutional trust also plays an essential role in the SE context [38, 56], in which the platform intermediates the service exchanges between clients and providers. Because on most occasions clients engage with unknown service providers (i.e., hosts or drivers), they have to rely on the platform to protect their interests and safety in SE transactions. Trust in the platform is then helpful to eliminate clients’ worries and concerns about the potential transgressions of providers [63] such as hidden in formation, hidden actions, privacy breaches, and even infringements on personal properties and safety. A trusted platform can then be expected to take the necessary measures to provide a reliable and safeguarded exchange environment, thus reducing the clients' perception of risks and leading to continuous purchasing behaviors [35]. The existing studies have revealed that trust in platforms can help to form interpersonal trust [43] and promote the continuous use of SE services [31, 56]. Based on these studies, we extend the understanding of one particular type of institutional trust, i.e., trust in the platform, by uncovering its effect on client continuous use and its boundary conditions. Following prior studies (e.g., [34]), we define trust in the platform here as the subjective belief by the buyer that the platform will institute and enforce fair rules, procedures, and outcomes in its platform competently, reliably, and with integrity, and if necessary, will provide recourse for buyers to deal with providers’ opportunistic behaviors [35, 44].

## 2.2. Platform-based mechanisms: antecedents of institutional trust

To enhance buyers’ online trust and mitigate their perceived risk when they engage in online transactions, online marketplaces and platforms have introduced a variety of institutional mechanisms [35, 44]. For example, online marketplaces, such as Amazon and eBay, have also established a set of market-driven mechanisms to form a coopera tive and safe online exchange environment, including escrow services, online credit card guarantees, privacy protection, feedback mechanisms, and dispute resolution [35, 44]. Some of the mechanisms established in traditional e-commerce, such as feedback mechanisms and payment security mechanisms, can also be suitable for SEs [25] and have been adopted by most SE platforms. Shao and Yin [56] further identified two new institutional mechanisms that are specific to the context of sharing transportation, i.e., driver certification and surge pricing. These marketplace-specific (or platform-specific) institutional mechanisms, which operate at the local level [14], not only enhance buyers’ trust in the community of online sellers [44] but are also useful in building trust inherent in the transactional relationship with the specific marketplace (or platform) [35, 56]. These local institutional mechanisms are then considered as key strategic resources and important characteristics of online marketplaces (or platforms).

To capture the nature of these marketplace-specific institutional mechanisms to provide assurances to online customers, Pavlou and Gefen [45] introduced the construct of the perceived effectiveness of institutional structures, defined as the extent to which a buyer believes that appropriate conditions are in place to facilitate successful trans actions with the community of sellers. By adapting this construct to the SE context, we then propose the construct of the “perceived effectiveness of platform-based institutional structures” (PEPIS) to capture the client’s perception of the effectiveness of the overall institutional structures of an SE platform. Following Pavlou and Gefen [45], we define PEPIS as the extent to which a client believes that appropriate conditions are in place to facilitate successful and safe service exchanges with the community of providers in an SE platform. This definition thus follows the previous research and views the effect of marketplace-driven mechanisms more largely in perceptual terms than in objective terms.

PEPIS, in this study, is then conceptualized at the local level [14], i. e., the platform-specific institutional environment. There are four most-adopted institutional mechanisms in SE platforms. These are (1) feedback mechanisms, (2) escrow services, (3) provider certification, and (4) urgent rescue<sup>1</sup>.

The feedback mechanism is the client-driven reputation system that accumulates and disseminates information (e.g., reviews, opinions, ratings, and recommendations) about service providers’ past behavior and performance [16]. Such a mechanism is likely to be effective only if the clients perceive that the feedback provided is accurate and credible and has not been manipulated by humans [35]. Accordingly, the perceived effectiveness of the feedback mechanism is defined as the extent to which a client believes that the feedback mechanism in the SE platform is able to provide accurate and reliable information about the service providers.

Escrow services, such as PayPal, Zhifubao, and WeChat payment, are third-party payment services in which a trusted third party authorizes payment only after the client has received and approved the purchased SE services [44]. Escrow services can address many uncertainty issues for clients, such as hidden information and actions, thus helping to build a more trustworthy SE environment. Accordingly, the perceived effec tiveness of escrow services is defined as the extent to which clients believe that escrows are able to guarantee that their exchanges with service providers in an SE platform will be fulfilled in accordance with their expectations.

Provider certification is the verification and approval system in which the SE platform guarantees qualified and eligible providers, e.g., hosts and drivers, through a set of rigorous procedures, including background checks, screening, and certification [25, 28, 56]. Provider certification is an important type of institutional mechanism for SEs because face-to-face interactions often occur in the context of SEs [43] such that unvetted providers might pose a potential threat to clients. Accordingly, the perceived effectiveness of provider certification is defined as the extent to which clients believe that effective and rigorous verification procedures are implemented by the platform to ensure that they only engage with qualified and eligible providers.

Urgent rescue is an emergency response system in which clients can share peer-to-peer service (e.g., sharing riding and sharing house) in formation with their emergency contacts and press one button to contact the police if they encounter danger in the process of a service exchange. Urgent rescue is the emergency mechanism that protects clients’ per sonal and property safety against the malicious behaviors of providers. Accordingly, the perceived effectiveness of urgent rescue is defined as the extent to which clients believe that the platform’s emergency mechanisms are able to protect their personal and property safety from the risk of illegal conduct committed by providers.

These four platform-based mechanisms are not expected to be highly interrelated but to combine to serve the same overall purpose to provide institutional guarantees and a basis for SE exchanges [45]. Following previous studies [16, 35], we propose PEPIS as a reflective-formative second-order construct, where the reflectively measured lower-order constructs ‘‘do not share a common cause but rather form a general concept that fully mediates the influence on subsequent endogenous variables” [3]. PEPIS fully mediates the influences of its lower-order con structs on the subsequent endogenous variable in this study [3], i.e., trust in the platform. The proposed model offers a parsimonious view that is theoretically meaningful (through the second-order construct that may relate to other variables) and managerially relevant (through the first-order constructs that online platforms can readily influence) [35, 45].

## 2.3. General institutional mechanisms: boundary conditions of institutional trust

Institutional mechanisms have been understood at two different levels in the existing literature: (1) at the general level, where institu tional mechanisms are independent from any specific online vendor or marketplace; and (2) at the local level, where institutional mechanisms are examined in a specific context, such as online vendors, marketplaces, or platforms [14, 35]. Marketplace-specific institutional mechanisms that operate at the local level, capture the extent to which these mech anisms provide assurances to online customers for their transactions within a specific online marketplace. These mechanisms are thus useful in building trust and eliminating the risks inherent in the transactional relationship with the specific marketplace. The general mechanisms include bureaucratic sanctions and safeguards that generalize beyond a given transaction and beyond specific sets of exchange partners [66]. Institutionalized to guarantee that all transactions will take place as promised, the general mechanisms are neither transaction-specific nor party-specific [66], as they are helpful to build trust at the general level [4, 51] and to reduce contextual uncertainties [57].

As depicted in Fig. 1, while platform-based mechanisms, such as the local-level institutional environment, are the primary antecedents of institutional trust, the macro-level institutional environment is actually the regulative boundary condition under which institutional trust is built and operates. Although the existing research has explored the moderating effect of the general institutional context on the relationship between interpersonal trust and online repurchase (e.g., [14]), we know very little about how institutional trust is formed and affects continuous use under the boundary condition of macro-level institutional mechanisms. Clarifying the moderating role of the general institutional context will not only help explain how platform-based mechanisms contribute to the formation of institutional trust under various condi tions of the general institutional environment, but also help specify the regulative conditions under which institutional trust has varying effects on repeat purchases, thus explaining the somewhat incongruent results of trust on client repurchases in the literature [14].

Following Fang et al. [14], we use the perceived effectiveness of SE institutional mechanisms (PESEIM) as a manifestation of the institu tional context. PESEIM is defined as the extent to which clients believe that general institutional mechanisms, regulations, and rules, such as bureaucratic sanctions, privacy, and data security, minimum insurance requirements, regular safety inspections, and health and safety re quirements [29, 37], exist to protect them against potential risks and illegal conduct in the context of SE. PESEIM is conceptualized to capture the clients’ perceptions of the vendor-independent SE institutional environment and is framed to highlight its ability to mitigate contextual risks in the environment beyond the platform providers’ control [14]. Thus, PESEIM is defined at the general level (i.e., vendor-independent), which differentiates PESEIM from PEPIS, which is defined at the local level (i.e., platform-specific). PESEIM could not only alleviate the relationship-specific risks resulting from the conduct of the particular trustee (e.g., the client-platform relationship) but could also mitigate the contextual risks that are not completely addressed by platform-specific institutional mechanisms [39]. We thus posit that PESEIM, which is vendor-independent, will moderate the effects of PEPIS on trust in the platform as well as the effects of trust in the platform on continuous use intention.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

Based on the existing studies on trust and institutional mechanisms (e.g., [14, 16, 35]), we develop a research model to explain the role of PESEIM in specifying the contextual condition under which (1) trust in a platform is formed based on the PEPIS and (2) trust in a platform affects continuous use intention in the general context of the SE (PESEIM), as depicted in Fig. 3. Specifically, PESEIM negatively moderates the rela tionship between PEPIS and trust in the platform as well as the rela tionship between trust in the platform and continuous use intention. The model also incorporates control variables known to affect trust and continuous use intention, including trust disposition, satisfaction, fa miliarity, education, age, gender, and personal expense. Next, we will explain each hypothesis in detail.

## 3.1. Trust in the platform and continuous use intention

As online transactions have grown rapidly worldwide, repurchases or continuous use has become a more important concern for online vendors, marketplace makers, and platform providers [14, 60]. How to retain existing customers such that they make repeated purchases is also becoming increasingly important for sustaining the development of the SE. Among the many influencing factors mentioned in the literature, trust has been found to be a key driving factor for customer retention and continuous use (e.g., [52]) because of its crucial ability to diminish the social complexity, uncertainty, and vulnerability that a buyer feels in e-commerce transactions ([39, 40]).

Because behavioral intention is the most significant predictor of actual behavior, we use continuous use intention to represent the actual action of repurchasing in the SE. Here, we define continuous use intention as the extent to which clients will repeatedly use the peer-topeer sharing services offered by an SE platform in the future [60].

Participating in SEs often requires clients to be exposed to the opportunistic behaviors of service providers [35, 46], who might be total strangers to the clients. Although the deals are digitally matched, face-to-face interactions are generally a necessary condition for SE [41], which might exacerbate the perceived risk and uncertainty due to the opportunistic behavior of the service providers. Interpersonal trust is often considered an effective social mechanism to overcome negative perceptions in an online context ( [39]). However, it is particularly difficult for a client to form trust toward an unknown service provider in the SE context. Under such circumstances, she/he has to rely on the institutions (i.e., platforms) that are responsible for intermediating and safeguarding these exchanges to protect her/his interests. A trusted platform can be expected to take solid steps and necessary measures to reduce client risk, such as identifying and removing problematic pro viders, taking legal action against fraudulent providers on behalf of the clients [44], or using necessary technologies and methods to prevent providers’ potential transgressions. Thus, if clients believe a platform is able to provide a reliable and safeguarded exchange environment, insufficient situational uncertainty will make risk irrelevant to these clients’ decision-making [16], thus increasing their willingness to repeatedly purchase from this platform. Trust in a specific platform will then drive the clients’ intention to continuously use the peer-to-peer services offered on the platform. Accordingly, we have the following hypothesis:

H1: Trust in a platform will have a positive impact on clients’ continuous use intention towards the services offered in a specific SE platform.

## 3.2. PEPIS and trust in the platform

An SE is a platform-based business in which platforms such as Airbnb, Uber, and Didi establish digitally enabled infrastructures and mechanisms to enable peer-to-peer service exchanges between clients and providers [48]. Institutional trust, such as trust in the marketplace, can be established through formal social structures such as promises, regulations, guarantees, and legal resources [66]. Therefore, these platform-based mechanisms, including feedback mechanisms, escrow services, credit card guarantees, information security, and dispute res olution [35, 45], are often viewed as key strategic resources for client trust building, jointly constituting the overarching institutional context, and providing the rational basis for cooperative buyer-seller trans actions [16, 66].

The perceived effectiveness of marketplace-based institutional mechanisms has been found to build buyers’ trust toward the market place in the context of e-commerce (e.g., [35, 44]). Similarly, in the context of SE, if these platform-based institutional mechanisms are perceived to be effective in facilitating and protecting service exchanges, the client will believe that the local environment is in an appropriate, well ordered, and secure condition for the continuous usage of sharing services. In other words, the client will be willing to depend on the platform to provide safeguarding procedures and measures to address the uncertainty issues related to the exchanges and to protect her in terests and safety. Thus, PEPIS should be a key antecedent of trust in platforms. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: PEPIS will have a positive impact on client trust in the platform in the context of SE.

## 3.3. Moderating effects of PESEIM

Trust is built and operates within the institutional environment [14]. In the context of SE, trust in the platform, as a specific type of trust at the local level, will be formed and operate within the macro-level of the institutional environment (refer to Fig. 1). Thus, while PEPIS is the key predictor of trust in the platform, PESEIM is indeed the regulative boundary conditions under which trust in the platform is engendered and has various effects on behavioral intentions. The perceived effec tiveness of e-commerce institutional mechanisms (PEEIM) has been found to play an important moderation role in both the formation and the operation of buyers’ interpersonal trust in online vendors in the e-commerce context [14]. In the context of SE, we thus posit that PESEIM, specifying the macro-level institutional environment, will have the same moderating effects on the formation of trust in platforms as well as its impact on continuous use intention.

In online repurchase contexts, such as e-commerce and SE, returning customers’ trust in an online vendor or an SE platform needs to be maintained and continually evaluated [52]. Returning customers who have previous transaction experiences will use experience-based, firsthand knowledge to reevaluate an online vendor’s (or platform’s) trust worthiness ([26]). When people tend to rely on their past experiences to inform their assessments of current decisions, the level of reliance de pends on the certainty of the context [14].

More specifically, when PESEIM is high, the transaction environment is characterized as having low uncertainty. In such a certain and stable environment, people tend to assume that the trustees, e.g., the platform, will comply with the regulations and rules; otherwise, they will be punished and sanctioned by the regulators, and thus, they do not need to recall or rely on their past experience with this platform, e.g., the platform-based mechanisms, to reevaluate the platform’s trustworthi ness. In other words, people give less weight to the perceived effec tiveness of the local environment (PEPIS) in certain and stable situations. In contrast, when PESEIM is low (i.e., the general SE envi ronment is uncertain), people have to rely on their own first-hand knowledge (i.e., platform-based mechanisms) to reevaluate the plat form’s trustworthiness. This implies a strong influence of the perceived effectiveness of the local environment (PEPIS) on trust in the platform. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3: A returning client’s perceived effectiveness of the SE’s institutional mechanisms (PESEIM) negatively moderates the relationship between the client’s perceived effectiveness of the platform institutional structures (PEPIS) and trust in the platform.

Trust in platforms has been found to be a key predictor of both initial online purchases and continuous purchases in both e-commerce and SEs (e.g., [30, 32, 56]). For the situation of continuous use, PESEIM serves to mitigate clients’ perceptions of contextual risks in the general SE envi ronment that are independent of a specific platform [14, 20]. Because trust enables online transactions by reducing social uncertainties [35, 36], PESEIM, as a contextual factor indicating the uncertainty level of the general environment, would change the necessity of trust in risk-taking behaviors, such as repurchasing [14, 54]. As general insti tutional mechanisms are perceived to be effective in mitigating contextual risks in the transaction environment, the need for customer trust in promoting repurchasing decisions is diminished [13, 36]. This implies that when PESEIM is high, clients will perceive the general SE environment to have less uncertainty, and thus, they may rely less on trust in a platform to promote their continuous use intentions [14]. In contrast, when PESEIM is low, clients will feel that the general SE environment is full of risks, and thus, they need platform-specific con fidence to manage those risks to increase their repurchase intention. As a result, SE clients tend to rely more on trust in the platform to obtain the necessary assurance for engaging in risk-taking behaviors [39]. Thus, we have the following hypothesis:

H4: A returning client’s perceived effectiveness of SE institutional mech anisms (PESEIM) negatively moderates the relationship between trust in a platform and the continuous use intention toward the services offered on the platform.

## 4. Research methodology

Following the prior studies on institutional mechanisms (e.g., [14, 16]), we used the survey method to test the proposed research model. The survey method was chosen because it is suitable to obtain personal opinions, beliefs, and perceptions, and it is also able to fulfill the purpose of this study for theory development and testing.

## 4.1. Measurement development

The measurement scales were adapted from the literature and tailored to the context of SE. As the original items were in English, we used the following procedures to ensure the translation validity. First, a researcher whose native language is Chinese forward translated the items into Chinese. Next, another researcher independently backward translated the items into English. Subsequently, the two researchers compared and discussed the two English versions to develop the first Chinese version of the items. The preliminary instrument was then reviewed by three doctoral students in the e-commerce field to ensure the content and face validity. Minor revisions were made based on their feedback. Finally, the two initial translators checked this version together and finalized the Chinese instrument.

All items were measured on standard Likert-type scales anchored at 1 = “strongly disagree,” 7 = “strongly agree,” and 4 = “neither agree nor disagree.” A pretest of the survey instrument was performed with 156 subjects. The items were modified following the procedures recom mended by Churchill [10]. The final scales and their references are presented in Appendix A.

## 4.2. Data collection

The data were collected from clients of Didi, which is the largest sharing transportation platform in China. Selecting a reputable SE platform was a deliberate design choice [16] because reputable platforms have solid institutional structures and an adequate number of clients who could offer opinions with adequate variance. The survey was distributed both offline and online. The offline survey was distributed at locations, such as college campuses and malls, where crowds congre gate, and the respondents were asked to complete the paper question naires. When conducting the online survey, we distributed a research invitation that contained a link to the e-questionnaire through social network tools (e.g., WeChat). The survey used a recall method [14, 15] to solicit respondents’ perceptions about a recent sharing transportation experience with Didi. The recall method also helped simulate the pro cess through which people form their perceptions about the continuous use of SE services. To encourage participation, the respondents were offered a small monetary reward and a summary of the results. Finally, out of the 746 questionnaires distributed, 438 valid responses (117 paper questionnaires and 321 e-questionnaires) were received after removing invalid responses, such as those containing more than five missing values, those with a very short completion time (less than 90 s), and those with the same answer to all questions, resulting in a valid rate of 58.71%.

## 5. Results

Partial least squares (PLS), a component-based structural equation modeling technique that enables path analytical modeling using latent variables [9], was used for data analysis. PLS is regarded as an appro priate statistical tool for theory exploration [17], which is the case in our study. In addition, PLS is well suited for this study because it allows for latent constructs to be modeled with formative indicators [3]. PLS then allowed us to examine the formative second-order construct, i.e., PEPIS, which had four underlying dimensions. We estimated the model by using SmartPLS 3.0 [53].

## 5.1. Measurement model

The full measurement model including the formative second-order constructs was assessed with confirmatory factor analysis of the PLS. The repeated indicator approach was used to estimate the formative second-order constructs $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , PEPIS) in this study. The high-order construct was estimated using Mode B, where the repeated indicators were treated as formative items for the high-order construct [3]. The inner path weighting scheme was used for the PLS algorithm. Re finements to the model were made by dropping items with low loadings one at a time.

We first evaluated the measurement model for our reflective constructs. The results are presented in Tables 3, 4, 5, and 6. The internal reliability of reflective constructs was verified with both composite reliability scores and Cronbach’s alpha in Table 3, which were above the recommended 0.7 cutoff. Convergent validity was established by having adequate internal reliabilities (>0.70), high AVE (average variance extracted) scores (>0.50), and significant item loadings (>0.70) on their respective constructs, as illustrated in Table 6.

The discriminant validity of reflective constructs was assessed with four approaches, including the Fornell-Larcker method, the crossloading method, the interconstruct correlations, and the heterotraitmonotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT) approach [67]. First, as shown in Table 4, the square root of the AVE for each construct (diagonal term) exceeds the correlations between the construct and other constructs (off-diagonal terms), which suggest that the Fornell-Larcker criterion has been satisfied. Second, a low to moderate correlation coefficient (<0.80) (in Table 4) is also often considered as an evidence of discriminant validity. Third, all of the items loaded well onto their own construct and poorly onto other constructs (in Table 6). Finally, the HTMT approach suggests that discriminant validity is established if the HTMT value of two constructs is below a threshold value of 0.85, the most conservative value indicated by the literature. Illustrated by Table 5, the HTMT value of any two reflective constructs of this study is below 0.85, which establishes discriminant validity.

The validity of the second-order formative construct – PEPIS – was evaluated with the following two approaches: the significance of the indicator weights and the indicator multicollinearity [22]. First, Table 7 demonstrates that all the weights of the formative indicators (e.g., perceived effectiveness of the feedback mechanism) are statistically signif icant (T-statistic value >1.96 and p<0.05). As shown in Table 7. the VIF values for these formative indicators are below the threshold value of $^ { 3 , }$ which indicates the absence of multicollinearity [22].

In summary, these results for both reflective and formative con structs together offered strong support for the reliability and validity of our measurement.

## 6. Discussion

This study offers several major findings. First, the results indicate that trust in platforms has a positive and significant effect on continuous use intention, even allowing the effects of the controlling variables, thus confirming the important role of institutional trust in enabling contin uous participation behaviors in the SE. Transactions in SEs indeed occur in a triadic relationship that includes clients, providers, and the plat form. The peer-to-peer service exchanges between clients and providers are then fully intermediated, governed, and guaranteed by the platform. Therefore, given that interpersonal trust (i.e., clients’ trust in providers) is difficult to form in the context of SE, clients’ continuous use intention to a large extent depends on their confidence that the platform can protect their interests and safety against the opportunistic behaviors of SE providers [35]. Institutional trust (i.e., trust in the platform) becomes a necessary and essential prerequisite of both interpersonal trust [44] and continuous purchasing behaviors [60]. This study, thus, not only supports the claim that institution-based trust is essential for e-com merce activities [30, 35] but also extends this perspective of institutional trust to the new context of the SE [32, 56].

Second, the results further show that the level of clients’ trust in a platform depends largely on their perceived effectiveness of the institutional structure established by the platform. The perceived effectiveness of each underlying platform-based mechanism (i.e., feed back mechanisms, escrow services, provider certification, and urgent rescue) contributes significantly to the formation of trust in the platform through the formative second-order construct – PEPIS. This study then discloses that these platform-based mechanisms are the major source of institutional trust in the SE context [35, 44]. In addition, the results uncover that although all contribute significantly, these four platform-based institutional mechanisms contribute unequally to the overall institutional environment; the perceived effectiveness of pro vider certification has the strongest impact, followed by the perceived effectiveness of feedback mechanisms, followed by the perceived effectiveness of escrow services, and followed by the perceived effec tiveness of urgent rescue.

Third, the findings suggest that the clients’ perceived effectiveness of the general SE institutional context (PESEIM) not only negatively moderates the relationship between PEPIS and trust in the platform but also negatively moderates the relationship between trust in the platform and clients’ continuous use intention. In doing so, this study not only examines the role of platform-based institutional mechanisms but also examines the role of general institutional mechanisms in trust formation and their subsequent effect on clients’ repurchase behaviors [14]. In the context of SE, while platform-based institutional mechanisms are the primary antecedents of trust in platforms, general institutional mecha nisms indeed act as regulative boundary conditions under which trust in platforms is built and operates.

## 6.1. Research implications

Based on these findings, this study makes several contributions to the existing research. This study first examines and confirms the essential role of institutional trust (i.e., trust in the platform) in underpinning peerto-peer transactions in the context of the SE. While the importance of institutional trust has been argued and examined in the e-commerce literature (e.g., [27, 35, 44]), it is rarely studied in the SE context. By clarifying the triadic relationship among clients, service providers and the platform in the SEs, our study argues that special attention should be given to the platform – the endorsing institutions for the sharing service exchanges – and a specific type of institutional trust associated with the platform, namely trust in the platform [35]. Trust in the platform, as a specific type of trust at the local level and a relational characteristic of the client-platform relationship, plavs a particularly essential role in facilitating transactions in the context where interpersonal trust (microlevel trust) is absent or difficult to develop, such as marketplace-based e-commerce [44], community-based transactions [34], and a platform-based SE. This study complements the existing studies on trust by focusing on institutional trust and by extending this perspective of institutional trust to the relatively new research context of the SE.

Second, drawing on previous studies (e.g., [33][44]; [56]), this study introduces two new types of platform-based institutional mechanisms and thus, proposes a new set of institutional mechanisms that are rele vant to the context of SEs, including feedback mechanisms, escrow services, provider certification, and urgent rescue. All four mechanisms significantly contribute to building clients’ trust in platforms if they are perceived to be effective, but their effects on trust building are different. The preventive mechanisms that are designed to constrain the oppor tunistic actions of providers (i.e., feedback mechanisms and escrow services) and exclude unqualified providers (i.e., provider certification) in advance seem to be more helpful in engendering trust in platforms than the emergency responsive mechanisms (i.e., urgent rescue) that are designed to protect clients’ property and safety when providers are committing illegal acts. Our results thus indicate that all four platform-based mechanisms should be viewed as important strategic resources that lead to competitive advantages [14, 35]. However, plat form makers (e.g., Didi and Airbnb) should be aware of the differences in these strategic resources in building institutional trust and, thus, invest in these trust-building resources according to their differences. This study thus contributes to the SE literature [38, 43, 56] by proposing and testing a new set of platform-based mechanisms and by arguing that these platform-based mechanisms should be considered as strategic as sets for platform makers.

Third, trust is influenced by both the local institutional environment (e.g., marketplaces and platforms) and the general institutional envi ronment [14, 35]. The existing studies have examined how trust oper ates under the boundary condition of the local-level institutional environment in the context of marketplace-based e-commerce (e.g., [16]) and how interpersonal trust has various effects on repeat pur chases under the boundary condition of the macro-level institutional environment in the context of general e-commerce (e.g., [14]). How ever, we know very little about how institutional trust is formed and operates when both local-level and macro-level institutional environ ments are present. This study then complements prior studies by examining the effects of both the local institutional environment and the general environment on trust in platforms in the context of SEs, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the building and operation of institutional trust. PEPIS, as a construct that represents the effectiveness of the overall local environment, is the major direct source of trust in the platform and PESEIM, as a construct capturing the effectiveness of the overall SE regulatory conditions, negatively moderates the relationship between PEPIS and PESEIM. PESEIM then substitutes the need for PEPIS in the formation of trust in the platform. That is, when PESEM is high, clients will not need a high level of perceived effectiveness of the local environment to develop their trust toward this local environment. This implies that platform-based mechanisms can be an important source of institutional trust production only under the condition of low but not high PESEIM. Our study thus deepens our understanding of institutional trust building [14, 16] by theorizing and empirically verifying the roles of both the local instructional context and the general institutional environment in affecting institutional trust in existing peer-to-peer ex change relationships [56].

Fourth, although repeat purchases have been examined in both the ecommerce context (e.g., [32]) and the SE context (e.g., [56]), the existing research has rarely taken the effect of general institutional conditions into account [14]. The present study adds a new under standing of continuous use intention by introducing the construct PESEIM and examining its moderating role in the relationship involving institutional trust (i.e., trust in the platform) and continuous use inten tion. Our study confirms that PESEIM negatively moderates the rela tionship between trust in a platform and continuous use intention such that the positive effect of trust in a platform on continuous use intention is weaker when PEEIM is higher. In response to the call to examine the conditions under which trust operates [68]; [14], the present study advances our understanding of the conditional effect of a specific type of institutional trust (trust in the platform) by explicitly specifying the SE institutional context as an important boundary condition to assess how institutional trust influences continuous use intention. Our study also complements the existing research by illustrating that general institu tional mechanisms act as the boundary condition not only for inter personal trust (e.g., [14, 16]) but also for institutional trust (e.g., trust in the platform).

## 6.2. Practical implications

The present study also offers several managerial implications for both SE platform makers and public policy makers. For SE platform makers (e.g., Airbnb, Uber, and Didi), our study first confirms the essential role of institutional trust at the platform level in encouraging repeat transaction intention with the platform as interpersonal trust is difficult to form in the SE context. Thus, when there are a variety of platforms in the same industry, institution-based trust at the platform level will become a key antecedent for clients’ continuous use intention and should be considered a strategic tool to gain competitive advantages over their competitors. Our study further discloses that platform-based institutional mechanisms, which are considered strategic resources for trust building at the platform level, contribute differently to forming trust in platforms. Preventive mechanisms (e.g., feedback mechanisms, escrow services, and provider certification) are more effective than responsive mechanisms (e.g., urgent rescue) in engendering clients trust in platforms. Platform makers should carefully evaluate the im pacts of these different mechanisms in trust building and invest in their trust building resources based on the actual results of this evaluation. Our study also suggests that while it is important for SE platform makers to focus on building institutional trust with clients and this clientplatform relationship, they should more strategically allocate their trust-building resources according to the level of the existing SE insti tutional mechanisms. They should assess the PESEIM of their (potential) clients. If they operate in an SE institutional environment that is perceived to be relatively ineffective (PESEIM is low), they should commit more resources to building clients’ institutional trust at the platform level. However, if they operate in an SE institutional environ ment that is perceived to be relatively effective (PESEIM is high), they could allocate fewer resources to maintain client trust at the level of a business necessity for ongoing operation. Finally, our study suggests that platform makers should apply contingency thinking to trust building and allocate their trust-building resources according to both the level of the existing SE institutional mechanisms and the importance of these different platform-based mechanisms.

Our study also suggests that SE policy makers should commit themselves to building a more effective institutional infrastructure for the entire SE environment such as privacy and data security measures, minimum insurance requirements, and health and safety requirements for service providers. Because SE is a new, rising socioeconomic phe nomenon, the general institutional infrastructures of SEs have lagged behind and have been imperfect and ineffective. A perceivably safer and effective SE environment would help platform makers (e.g., Airbnb, Uber, and Didi) invest less in trust building at the platform level and allocate their strategic resources to other key areas for development, thereby contributing to the prosperity and sustainability of the SE in their respective industries, such as tourism, hospitality, and transportation.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research directions

The present study has a number of limitations that can be addressed in future studies. First, our sample was drawn from a typical platform in a single SE industry (i.e., sharing transportation). Though we have conceptualized our constructs in the general SE context, generalization of the results from a single industry to the general context of the SE should be cautious. Future research can take this investigation further by drawing research subjects from more diverse SE industries. Second, this study was conducted mainly in Chinese cultural contexts, and thus, the results might not be simply generalized to other cultural contexts. The effects of platform-specific institutional mechanisms might be different in another broader institutional, societal, and cultural environment. Third, we controlled for the effects of some important variables on the dependent variables; however, this paper cannot address all these possible antecedent factors such as surge pricing, website quality, reputation, and branding. Additional research is also needed to deter mine the effects of these variables. Fourth, although the effect sizes of the interaction effects are acceptable in comparison with previous studies, their statistical significance might be questionable given the relatively small sizes of the moderation effects of PESEIM. Future research can then continue to explore the moderating effects of PESEIM by including more data sets from other SE industries. Finally, the recall method used in this study might induce memory recall bias. Future research could employ alternative methods, such as experiments, and adopt more objective measures to address memory recall bias.

In addition to the aforementioned suggestions, there are several more directions that future research can pursue. First, this study exclusively focuses on instructional trust as a relational characteristic of the client-platform relationship. However, the client-provider relation ship might also be relevant for continuous use intention in the SE context, and thus, future research can add new perspectives to the proposed model by including trust in the community of providers [34] and trust in specific service providers (i.e., interpersonal trust). Second, we have only examined four platform-specific institutional mechanisms. Thus, examining the effects of the institutional context by including other important institutional mechanisms will add new perspectives and understandings to continuous exchange behavior in the context of SE. Third, this research focuses on the formation and operation of institu tional trust under the boundary condition specified by the general institutional mechanisms. However, both the platform-based institu tional structure and the general institutional mechanisms could directly impact client behaviors. Future research can then examine how client behaviors are influenced by these two levels of institutional mechanisms and their interactions. Finally, we have framed the general SE institu tional environment from the positive effectiveness perspective; future research may apply negative risk framing [14] to the general SE insti tutional context and attempt to quantify the practical impact of thi framing effect in the SE context.

## 7. Conclusion

This study extends our understanding of the role of a specific insti tutional trust (i.e., trust in the platform) on clients’ continuous use be haviors in the SE context by introducing two constructs based on the perspective of the following institutional mechanisms [14, 16]: PEPIS at the local institutional environment level and PESEIM at the general institutional environment level. PEPIS is found to have a positive and significant effect on trust in platforms, which in turn leads to clients continuous use intention. PESEIM is found to negatively moderate the relationship between PEPIS and trust in the platform as well as the relationship between trust in the platform and continuous use intention. PESEIM and PEPIS then function as substitutes in the production of trust in the platform. These research findings not only add new understanding to online trust by confirming the important role of institutional trust at the platform level for SE research but also reveal the regulative boundary conditions under which trust in platforms is built and oper ates. Collectively, these findings show that both platform-based and general institutional mechanisms are relevant for understanding repeat usage behaviors and set a starting point for future research to further explore these two perspectives in the SE context.
