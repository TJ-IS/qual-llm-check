## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Blockchain Capability Service-dominant logic Social exchange theory Supply chain finance

## A B S T R A C T

The objective of this study is to explore the blockchain-enabled value creation process in supply chain finance (SCF). We analyze the roles of participants (who?), motives (why?), resources (what?), and practices (how?) through the lens of service-dominant logic and social exchange theory. We conducted an exploratory case study of a blockchain-based platform that offers SCF solutions for supply chain partners and interpreted the results through a series of qualitative analyses. The results prove that the blockchain-driven SCF solution provides services to its customers by applying key resources and conducting corresponding practices, which create value for the participants through meeting their motives.

## 1. Introduction

Supply chain finance (SCF) has been utilized for capital optimization and cost reduction in many industries and has piqued growing attention from both academia and practice [1]. SCF mechanisms such as reverse factoring, payable financing, and dynamic discounting are implemented for supply chain partners to jointly create value through aligning financial flows, optimizing cash flow management, and alleviating financial risks [2–4]. SCF provides an innovative approach for cash-constrained suppliers and has enjoyed mushrooming growth since the financial crisis of 2008 when credit to small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) was restricted.

In recent years, more and more large retailers (e.g., Walmart, Jing dong) and leading suppliers (e.g., Unilever and P&G) have adopted SCF through collaborating with financial institutions to streamline payment procedures for their suppliers to receive loans [5]. Theoretically, the SCF solution is attractive to SMEs in emerging markets because they can borrow at a favorable interest rate based on their buyers’ superior credit rating. However, in most cases, the reality is not a win-win situation per se. Despite many reports about the promising results in SCF, business suppliers are still hesitant to embark on the SCF platform [6]. Pertaining studies have found that SCF might not always be adopted by suppliers because large buyers may aggressively extend the payment terms to suppliers and leave the suppliers’ benefits questionable. In addition, credit can only be passed between core enterprises and their first-tier suppliers without covering the whole value chain, thus impeding financial flows in the financial service industry [7,8].

Recently, a few studies have attempted to investigate how to improve the efficiency in the SCF platform [9,10]. Blockchain, as a digital, autonomous, and distributed network of real value would be applicable to enhance the efficiency of a countless number of financial processes and services such as SCF [11]. Accordingly, the number of blockchain-driven projects for enhancing SCF efficiency is growing rapidly, and companies from developing countries such as China and India have collaborated with blockchain technology service providers to improve SCF effectiveness [12]. However, both conceptual expositions and empirical evidence are relatively deficient in the existing body of knowledge, such that robust theoretically grounded and empirical evi dence on the phenomenon of blockchain technology vis-a-vis \` SCF is still scarce and prior studies have revealed various gaps in our understand ing. First, there is a lack of investigation of how blockchain technology improves SCF efficiency, as theory-driven empirical studies are starkly missing in the literature. In addition, SCF is also such an embryonic concept in business management that the pertaining literature lacks an underlying theoretical foundation, which therefore induces a disparity between SCF theory and practice [13,14]. Furthermore, blockchain, as an emerging technology for creating transparency, automation, and trust with the ability to provide the necessary infrastructure for financial systems, has been still largely experimental in deployment. Little attention has been paid to understand the role blockchain technology plays in business value creation in the financial service industry. The lack of scientific knowledge in this growing yet important field no doubt warrants further investigations.

To resonate with the recent call for further research on blockchain innovation in the financial ecosystem, this study strives to investigate how blockchain-driven technologies create business value by analyzing the key aspects of an SCF platform [15,69] in a bid to fill the scientific knowledge gap in this growing yet paramount field. In essence, we make an early attempt to fathom the possible blockchain-driven solutions to technological impediments on the processes of value generation, such as the lack of payment automation, poor visibility into services and pro cesses, and insufficient transparency or timely information. In doing so, we presuppose that blockchain technology can provide relevant IT re sources and capabilities to eliminate the impediments and improve the effectiveness of services, thereby creating value for all interested constituents.

As prior studies have starkly remained in theoretical and conceptua discussions, we endeavor to advance this line of research by scientifi cally investigating a theory-grounded exploratory case study of a blockchain-driven platform that offers SCF solutions for supply chain constituents through a series of qualitative analyses. In particular, we respond to recent inquiries for further insights into the interplay be tween blockchain technology and business management. To achieve ou research obiectives, this study addresses the theoretical and empirical gaps and further contributes to the blockchain literature in the following ways: (1) As our following comprehensive literature review reveals the predicament that both theoretical and empirical research on how blockchain technology functions and performs in financial systems i scarce, this study is one of the first to probe the surfacing phenomenon in depth, and it provides a more penetrative understanding of this appli cation in an underexplored scientific domain. (2) As the research gaps in pertaining SCF studies have been demonstrated, current research con siders either financial institutions or buvers as essential roles within an SCF solution and focuses on only partial components in the SCF scheme, such as payables and receivables or inventories. We hereby define SCF as an intercompany activity that facilitates the increase of the value of al the participants, and thus the focus of blockchain-driven SCF solution is to provide services designed to improve the effectiveness of the supply chain and create value for all interested stakeholders. Therefore, we endeavor to explore the roles of participants. motives, resources. and practices vis-a-vis \` blockchain-enabled value creation processes in an SCF system by embracing the service-dominant logic (S–D logic) and draw ing on social exchange theory as the basis of our theoretical framework to explore our understanding of the role of blockchain technology in SCF, which is yet to be uncovered by previous studies. (3) This study is a novel supplement to the current SCF research. Prior research was mostly dominated by econometric models, which may not reflect the actua interplay between blockchain technology and SCF that our study mea: sures. In addition, prior studies focused primarily on one-level or two level of trade credit and rarely gauged the coordination among various actors of SCF [3]. (4) Our study sheds new light on blockchain application, as an IT resource, that enables credit transmission and co-operation among multilevel participants and proves the crucial role of blockchain technology in SCF through an explorative case study. To the best of our knowledge, explicit consideration of blockchain-related issues has been scant in extant SCE studies

## 2. Related literature

## 2.1. Value creation in SCF

Scholars of logistics and finance focus on the innovation adoption of SCF and explore the value creation of the SCF process. Extant research subsumes the benefits or impacts of SCF solutions on supply chain per formance (i.e., the drivers and impediments of the value creation pro cess in the SCF context) and investigates how SCF service providers coordinate in the supply chain and create value for both individual companies and the entire supply chain. The pertaining literature that explains the value creation of SCF solutions presents the analysis from two primary perspectives: the “finance-oriented” perspective and the “supply chain oriented” perspective. The “finance-oriented” perspective interprets the value creation of SCF solutions as a set of financial de mands and objectives, i.e., financial cost minimization [16,17]. Specif ically, the suppliers that engage in SCF are motivated by the potential rate arbitrage [18], liquidity requirement or shortening of cash-to-cash cycle [2,13], or alternative source of finance [17,18], the buyers (in most cases the core firms) are motivated by payment extension or operational decisions such as order quantity and wholesale prices [19, 20]. The suppliers and buyers reach their financial demands and thus, realize their value through participating in the SCF activities. From the financial perspective, financial institutions are essential components in the SCF scheme and focus on payables and receivables rather than in ventories. Additionally, studies that address SCF from an even stricter perspective, which regards SCF as a set of buyer-driven financial solu tions, are often modeled as an evolved form of reverse factoring [2].

On the other hand, the “supply chain oriented” perspective extends the framework of working capital optimization to include inventories [7], which analyze the value creation achieved through a generic shifting of inventory among supply chain players. Notably, the described benefits might be achieved in a specific financial solution provided by a buyer to optimize the inventory decision [21], or by a fund lender for financial hedging of operational risk [22–24] or for coor dination/collaboration among supply chain partners [25,26]. As a general trend, these studies that take this perspective tend to provide holistic analyses of the SCF approach without describing only financial institutions or buyers. However, as the elements that trigger SCF solu tions in the trade process surface, the articles from this perspective neglect the most important events in SCF solutions such as resources, practices, and how the participants interact in the trade process.

Some articles are primarily concerned with the technological improvement of the SCF solution, which, in comparison to traditional reserve factoring, allows for the provision of capital to a greater number of suppliers at a lower rate and the involvement of more market players [2,17]. But this perspective is taken into account in only a limited number of articles [27]. More articles claim for the technological ob stacles of current SCF solutions, including the lack of automation of payment, poor visibility into services and process [28], and insufficient transparency and timely information [29,30].

As shown in Table 1, the main drivers of SCF value creation include the benefits from two aspects: financial cost minimization, and supply chain strategy optimization. The impediments, on the other hand, are classified into three aspects: finance, supply chain, and technology.

## 2.2. Blockchain use in SCF

There are a significant number of studies about blockchain use in financial systems. These articles depict that on a permissioned block chain, which refers to the blockchain that is only accessible for selected participants who have permission to engage in [31], financiers and firms with access approval can sign in to the system and view informational flows of multitier partners recorded on the blockchain, which enhances mutual trust and reduce uncertain risks in supply chain network [32–36]. In contrast to the traditional trust mechanism where a central party (e.g., an insurance company, a central bank, or a government) is needed, blockchain is a trust-free solution because the underlying techniques assure the transactions not to be altered as long as they are logged on the blockchain. The most paramount aspect of blockchain technology is that it makes the financial system inherently secure because no transaction will take place if data are changed [37–39]. Though the application of blockchain technologies in the logistics and supply chain management (L/SCM) sector is still at its initial stages, it is considered likely to lead to disrupting transformations to traditional relationship models, mainly because of the disintermediation of the transactions, i.e., without any central authority to validate and offer credibility to the transactions [40,41]. A few studies have attempted to investigate how blockchain can improve the efficiency in the SCF plat form [9,10], and how a digital, autonomous, and distributed network of real value would be applicable to enhance the efficiency of a countless number of financial processes and services such as SCF. But little is known about the technological and managerial implications of block chain for SCF solutions even though in practice business service pro viders (e.g., IBM and PWC) have launched a blockchain-based system to enable various parties in an SCF platform to act on a mutually shared ledger. Specifically, the existing literature does not explain how block chain transforms the participants’ relationships and interactions in the SCF trade process and what resources (both technological and mana gerial) and practices blockchain would provide to SCF solutions to trigger the value creation among supply chain players. This study, therefore, is an early effort to employ robust, theoretically grounded methods to scientifically uncover the mechanisms of blockchain tech nology for value creation in SCF.

## 2.3. Research gaps

The research gaps show that the current research has starkly considered either financial institutions [16–18] or buyers [2] as essen tial roles within an SCF solution. However, from the service-related literature, value is co-created by all participants and shareholders and depends on the intercompanies’ interactions and the quality of their relationships. Thus, to build up the theoretical framework for SCF value creation driven by blockchain technology, we should take into account all the participants (buyers, suppliers, financial institutions, and other service providers) in an SCF scheme and the participants’ interactions and relationships [42,43]. On the other hand, previous articles focus on only partial components in the SCF scheme, such as payables and re ceivables or inventories [19–24]. However, as the elements that trigger SCF solutions in the trade process should be identified, we should consider the most important events in SCF solutions, such as resources, practices, and how the participants interact in the trade process. Finally, as prior studies have only focused on theoretical and conceptual dis cussions [9,10], methods of empirical study (e.g., qualitative analysis) need to be employed to investigate the value creation process of blockchain-enabled SCF systems.

## 3. Theoretical framework

## 3.1. Conceptual components

There is a plethora of research on the value creation process in the field of SCF. To fill the research gaps identified in our comprehensive literature review, we build our analysis in a theoretical framework that takes into account all the participants (buyers, suppliers, financial in stitutions, and other service providers), the participants’ interactions and relationships [42,43], together with the key events in the trade process (e.g., resources, practices, and enablers). From the service-related literature, service value is created in supplier-customer interactions and depends on the quality of the relationship. Scholars have employed S–D logic to conceptualize business as networked sys tems in firm-to-firm settings, where all participants are motivated to partake in the services to create value [44]. According to Vargo and Lusch [45], the central concept in S–D logic emphasizes the critical role of resources and implies that the resources are exchanged in the service process for the benefit of other parties.

Departing from the pertaining studies of SCF [4,7,16,17,46], we define SCF as an intercompany activity, which embodies a set of tech nological and financial services aiming to facilitate the optimization and integration of the physical, informational, and financial resources, to increase the value of participants (i.e., suppliers, buyers, and service providers). SCF value creation involves a symbiotic relationship be tween a core company and its primary stakeholders [47–49]. Therefore. the focus of the blockchain-driven SCF solution is to provide services designed to improve the effectiveness of the supply chain and create value for all interested stakeholders. Among the various theoretical perspectives for supply chain value creation, we consequently embraced the S-D logic perspective and drew on the social exchange theory as the foundation of our theoretical framework.

Within the S–D logic, the service represents the foundation of all economic exchanges [45], the service provider creates and offers value propositions for the benefit of other participants, and the service customer perceives the value of such propositions through experiences of the interaction [45,50]. The process of service provision is to support the customer’s value-creating processes with service offerings by applying innovative tools [45]. As such, we consequently position our study within these foundational premises. According to the social ex change theory-related literature $[ 1 5 , 5 1 , 6 9 ]$ an interaction in business-to-business (B2B) settings can be described along with the four core constructs: the participants (who) – with whom the entities want to interact; the motives (why) – why the participants interact and what they expect from the interaction; the resources (what) – what the participant exchange; and the practices (how) – how the participants interact.

Therefore, to further advance our understanding of the role of blockchain technology in financial systems, we endeavor to leverage the conceptual lens of the S–D logic and the social exchange theory to explore the roles of participants, motives, resources, and practices visa-vis \` blockchain-enabled value creation processes in an SCF system. We propose a primary research question to guide our study:

Why and how do participants in SCF service systems exchange resources and create value through blockchain technology?

## 3.2. The role of the enablers

While the application of resources within business partners leads to service provision, value generation is de facto subject to contextual factors [52]. In an effort to understand the relevant factors, we reviewed the B2B exchange literature with a focus on value creation through supplier-buyer interactions and the emerging literature on Information Systems (IS) and blockchain applications. The B2B exchange literature delineates several categories of mechanisms to address B2B exchange issues while increasingly drawing on relational governance mechanism for explaining B2B relational exchange and focusing on the relationship between the exchange parties and the governance mechanism (e.g., [53, 68]).

In the B2B exchange literature, scholars have confirmed that inter organizational trust is critical to the success of supplier-buyer relation ship governance efforts and that the perception of value will be positively influenced by the level of interorganizational trust [54,68]. Also, in the blockchain literature, the blockchain application in an L/SCM context associated with transactions’ disintermediation implies disruptive transformations to traditional relationships involving collaboration and trust among members [41]. Therefore, we presume trust as an enabler for value creation in the blockchain-driven SCF ser vice system.

An emerging body of IS literature suggests the importance of IT capability as a key enabling factor in B2B relational governance. IT capability refers to an organization’s ability to combine IT resources and direct practices toward achieving business objectives. IS scholars have attempted to address value creation activity at the interfirm level, have indicated that IT capabilities have a stronger influence on performance than IT resources, and are widely characterized as an enabler for the ability to create value such as organizational agility [55,56]. The blockchain application literature also emphasizes the importance of the capabilities driven by blockchain applications as providing an under lying infrastructure for the entire supply chain ecosystem, which ensures the trade status information to be conveyed securely through multitier partners [57,58]. Therefore, we presume IT capability as another enabler for value creation in the blockchain-driven SCF service system. ## 4. Methodology and data

## 4.1. Research design

As our comprehensive literature review reveals that the value crea tion process of blockchain-enabled SCF systems is under-investigated, an inductive, theory-building approach using explorative case studies is a comparatively appropriate research strategy [59,60]. In essence, to differentiate from other prior studies primarily driven by econometric or conceptual approaches, this study deploys a theoretically grounded case study using Yinuo Finance in China. The reason why we chose Yinuo Finance to conduct a single case study is that Yinuo Finance is a highly representative case of blockchain application in the SCF context. While most of the deployments were just in their initial stages whose effec tiveness still needs to be tested, Yinuo Finance launched its first SCF project early in 2017 and has achieved good results since then. Yinuo Finance started to implement the Yinuo Financial system in September 2017, and it has been running the blockchain-driven platform success fully for over one year. By the end of August 2018, Yinuo Financial platform had more than 7 billion registered assets. It began with the business of writing-off account receivables for the upstream of its supply chain and solved the problem of information asymmetry. By the end of June 2018, 30 stable consensus nodes have been developed in the un derlying system, with business partners, including five overseas firms, seven financial institutions, nearly 100 core enterprises, and more than 600 suppliers. In this study, we endeavor to adopt an exploratory case study on this representative company and highlight how blockchain techniques affect value generation in an SCF scenario. We strive to provide the implications of this case to the practitioners who are plan ning to apply advanced information technologies to their SCF solutions to improve efficiency and performance.

## 4.2. Methodology

Because of the lack of empirical evidence in extant IS research, an exploratory case study was adopted as an approach to understand how blockchain techniques affect value generation in an SCF context. Also, according to the literature, an exploratory case study can highlight how all of the different components of a particular SCF solution, e.g., the cash-to-cash cycle, can be managed in a collaborative way by the supply chain players involved. Finally, a case study is appropriate for exam ining complex or new phenomena and processes and for addressing “how” and “why” questions. Therefore, an in-depth case study of the Yinuo Financial platform was carried out and led to discovering how and why the blockchain technology, as an emerging IT artifact, is applied in SCF solutions on value generation for different motivated parties guided by the S–D logic framework.

## 4.3. Data collection and analysis

The purpose of this research is to understand why and how partici pants in SCF service systems exchange resources and create value using blockchain technology. Therefore, first, we attempted to understand who the current participants were and why they participated in the blockchain-driven SCF service. Second, we strived to find out the prac tices and resources in the service. Finally, we were interested in iden tifying facilitators in the service. We identified individuals who understood and were able to explain the whole process in the blockchain-driven SCF platform. We focused on one successful case of Yinuo Finance, in which a small rubber company (known as R) gained loans of 10 billion Yuan with a 7% rate from a commercial bank (known as B) within 10 days based on the repayment ability of the core com pany, an automobile manufacturer (known as A), and all the processes were completed with the assistance of the blockchain services provided by Yinuo Finance. We conducted interviews with five (5) representatives of Yinuo Finance, two (2) representatives of the supplier R, two (2) representatives of the core company A, and two (2) representatives of the bank B (as shown in Table 2). The interviews were conducted during the past 2 years, from November 2018 to May 2020, either face-to-face or on the telephone.

Data collection was through semi-structured interviews. The inter view questions were adjusted accordingly based on the feedback of initial responses during the interview. Approximately 15 major initial interview questions for each participant were developed based on the factors identified in the S-D logit and social exchange studies (as shown in Appendices 1-4). There were sub-questions below each major ques tion, and new questions were asked whenever new ideas were presented by the interviewees. Additional questions were also asked to probe deeper into the responses received or to get clarification on some issues raised. In this study, credibility was established by repeating a summarized version of their response. Interviewees were asked to confirm if that was the message they were trying to convey.

Open coding and selective coding were performed after the interviews. Open coding was performed on each of these transcripts, and the process was repeated multiple times. From the open codes identified in each interview, we selected core codes. Then, we searched for similar themes across interviews. We began looking for themes that appeared in more than one interview. These were aggregated into categories or super categories. ## 5.1. Participants (who?)

Our findings indicate that participants representing the service cus tomers performed three distinct roles during the blockchain-enabled value creation processes, which is depicted as the coding map in Fig. 3.

The first participant is the core company, which is generally largescale enterprises with strong capital domaining in the center of the in dustrial chain. In this case, Yinuo Finance provides services for core enterprises in industries such as steel, transportation, and logistics, and also has connections with some financial institutions (Y4,A1, and A2). The core company is viewed as a target customer for traditional financial service providers because they can get the financial credit (Y3 and B1). The second participant is multilevel suppliers of the core company, which have limited sources of financing or the financing is often too expensive and requires significant collateral (Y3, R1, and R2), and whose survivals are dependent on the industrial supply chain (Y3 and R1). The third participant is the financier, which refers to the in stitutions such as banks or nonbank financial organizations (a factoring, trust, asset management company, etc.) that provide financial services to SMEs through industrial funds or own funds (Y3 and B1). In this case, Yinuo Finance helped the financier to serve more customers than the present by providing such blockchain-based services and allowing the financier to be able to confirm the repayment ability of the supply chain in a more efficient way (Y3, B1, and B2). The blockchain solution pro vider, in this case, is Yinuo Finance who provides the underlying blockchain infrastructure for the SCF instruments. The open coding result is shown in Table 3.

## 5.2. Motives (why?)

In this case, we investigated that most participants have the moti vation to solve the problems of information asymmetry and data inte gration, along with the desire to enhance their financial services, as depicted in the coding map below (see Fig. 4).

Being able to rely on a single source of aggregated data to eliminate the problem of information asymmetry is the desired objective of most large institutions, but the data standardization and distribution to build a view of real-time positions are challenges with complexity and expensive implementation cost. Interviewees of the core company called that although the company has its Enterprise resource Planning (ERP) system, it has only registered the relationships with its first-tier sup pliers. If each company on multiple levels through the supply chain has purchased and docked the ERP systems, it takes about 2 to 3 months to launch the trade (Y4 and A1). Furthermore, the interviewees were concerned whether the information has been tampered with or its true validity cannot be guaranteed through a centralized system (Y3,A2,R2, and B2).

Another mostly concerning issue is that the credit of core enterprise cannot be transmitted through higher-tier suppliers. Hence, the main participants for SCF are limited to the first-tier suppliers or direct dis tributors of the core enterprises because their credit transmission is difficult to reach the second-tier or even further suppliers who have financing needs (Y4,R1, and B2).

## 5.3. Practices (how?)

As our investigation unfolded, the practices of providing services in the blockchain-driven SCF system can be categorized into four facets: standardization, mutual confirmation, visibility and automation, and risk control as shown in Fig. 5.

To meet customers’ needs for near real-time solutions and to mini mize the implementation cost, it is a key requirement to adapt the practices to a paradigm where information is shared between trans action partners rather than exchanged through messages [61]. Besides, according to the social exchange theory, standardized norms increase the efficiency of B2B relationships by agreeing to the manner of in teractions and thus reducing the degree of uncertainty [62]. Therefore, standardized norms and protocols are required to be applied to trans actions to increase the efficiency of B2B interactions.

In this case, the platform provides a standard for writing data in accordance with a protocol. Each participant is a node, who writes data to the blockchain following the same standard, while other participants directly read the node (Y3,A2,R2, and B1). The system uses the Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (pBFT) as a consensus algorithm, whose best advantage is the high speed for operation (Y2). “For example, PoS or DPoS is electoral, as a few representatives are elected to verify the node. Then in pBFT it is 3F + 1F; that is, one node fault in the whole four nodes can be tolerant. Therefore, in the so-called permissioned blockchain, in which speed is critical, pBFT can ensure that TPS can be high.” (Y2).

To start the service process, first, the core company registers an in voice of account payable in the blockchain, together with the basic trade information (e.g., the purchase order, bills of lading, and instructions of the delivery). Once the invoice is mutually approved by all the share holders, a contract is written, mutually confirmed, coded, and “stored” in a blockchain structure. A contract is triggered when it satisfies the conditions of the negotiation of the invoice. After that, cash is trans ferred according to the contract without relying on an intermediary (Y1 and Y5). One obvious characteristic of the platform is that the account payable invoice are confirmed by the system and the credit can be transferred on the multitier supply chain through the distributed ledger technology in the form of payment commitment letter and reaches SMEs that need financing (A1,A2,R1, and B2). On the other hand, transaction in a multitier SCF scenario require higher settlement speed and adequate security guarantees [10]. The automatic liquidation based on a smart contract, in fact, is to assure that the entire payment execution is safe, visible, and timely. Compared to the traditional solutions, the manual work for liquidation is not needed as it is a process of splitting and transferring the digital vouchers according to smart contract in structions (Y2,Y5,A1,R1, and B1) as noted: “…compared to ERP system, the qutomatic liquidation is cost-saving and secure. The qutomatic liquidation, in fact, is to assure that the entire payment execution is safe and visible; the second is timely and effective. You don’t need your financial staff to hold the pile of documents and to do it manually because it is a process of splitting and transferring according to the smart contract.” (Y2)

The core com pany, which pays at the end of the chain, initiates the process. First, it raises a digital invoice based on the account payable and passes it to the first-tier supplier. When the first-tier supplier signs the invoice, a trade relationship is established, and the transaction information is recorded as a block on the blockchain. ## 5.5. Blockchain-driven trust as an enabler in value creation

Based on the social exchange theory, a B2B relationship refers to the degree of trust, commitment, and shared vision between supply chain partners [64]. Without the foundation of effective interorganizational relationships, most efforts to manage the flow of capital or materials across the supply chain would tend to be ineffective [65]. Blockchain technology surfaces to free people from the necessity of implementing mechanisms to signal or convey trust [66], and as a class of distributed ledger technologies, it guarantees that archived information is not falsified. In this case, we uncovered that the blockchain-driven SCF system built a trust-free mechanism, which emphasized the trans parency of transaction information and credit transmission from the core company to suppliers, and at last led to cooperation among partners and an established (new) relationship on the supply chain as depicted in the following coding map (see Fig. 8):

Blockchain technology ensures transparency, security, and trust without intermediaries, thereby reducing processing time, eliminating the manual implementation costs, and fundamentally improving inter organizational cooperation through providing efficient financial ser vices for the core companies and suppliers and helping participants enhance financial performance and perceive their value. For the core enterprises, there is approximately a 20–30% reduction of procurement costs, which improves financial performance. In the traditional scenario, credit loans need a commitment letter from the bank plus a 30% deposit. Through the blockchain-enabled solution, the SMEs just need to submit the digital payment commitment letter for financing without deposit, which may save 30% procurement costs for the entire supply chain. (Y2, A1, and A2). For the bank, the platform prevents the problems of false seals or fraudulent trade offline. Thus, bad financial debts will certainly be less. In addition, the cost of loan approval of the bank has been reduced by at least 3 days. Whereas it costs at least 3 days for the core company to confirm the supplier and to apply for bank lending in traditional scenarios, the blockchain-enabled solution can complete the process within 1 h. Hence, the platform obviously shortened the period of the supply chain financing to better meet the liquidity requirement of SMEs, thereby substantially improving the efficiency and cooperation among various participants (Y1,Y2,B1, and B2).

Theoretically, when the suppliers reduce cash flow volatility, they also reduce their risk perception; and therefore, improve their capital sourcing cost and its weighted average cost of capital [67], which en ables a higher enterprise value and motivates the suppliers to join for the benefits of rate arbitrage and shorter cash-to-cash cycle:

“The interest rate for the suppliers has been reduced at least by three points. Actually, many suppliers turn to credit loans rather than factoring, and their previous interest rate was basically 12–15%. Now this blockchain solution reduces the interest rate to around 10%.” (Y2)

“It prevents the problems of false seals or fraud trade offline, and then the bad financial debts will certainly be less. Now the online loan definitely im proves efficiency since you can get loans on the day.” (Y1)

Through the blockchain-enabled solution, different participants along the multitier supply chain, who may not trust or even know each other, are given confidence because the data of financial transactions is guaranteed by the distributed trust-free systems from being tampered with [31,38]. ## 5.6. Blockchain-driven capability as an enabler in value creation

Among the critical IT capabilities that mediate performance and resources is IT Business Process Integration, which refers to the ability with which a firm links its IT to business helping the information ex change and collaborative relationship governance. Our detailed inves tigation of this case indicated that the blockchain technology facilitated an effective collaboration among SCF partners based on an efficient exchange of information and supported the service process by auto mating business processes across the value network, thereby promoting the integration of the blockchain technology and industry (Y3 and Y4).

Core enterprise on Yinuo Financial platform can reconcile the account online. When he signs the invoice to the supplier and the latter signs it, the accounting period is completed. It is consistent with the trade (A1, R1, and B1). As noted:

“After the registration of the invoice, the smart contract is written onto the blockchain. After the signing and mutual approval of shareholders, the con tract written is done, and then it is executed according to its execution con ditions on the due date. It is a real business system scenario, including deep integration of the blockchain and industries.” (Y3)

Therefore, the blockchain-enabled seamless data interchange and integrated business solutions are applied for optimizing the working capital and thus create financial value for the partners involved. In addition, another capability driven by the blockchain technology for value creation is IT management, specifically the IT-related cash flow management in this case. IT management refers to the firm’s ability to effectively implement IT-related activities. In this case, the combination of the ledger being distributed and validated across the network enables the platform to assign an asset to any user, and the fact that a single asset cannot be sent more than once makes it possible to trace the ownership of a certain asset at any location and to prevent double-spending assets [31], which helps achieve real-time automated reconciliation, thereby reducing the costs related to manual inquiries and enhancing customer’s experience (Y1 and Y2). Hence, the smart contract of blockchain enables the working capital to arrive basically in seconds, and the liquidation list is formed after the smart contract is created till the bank transfers money. (A2,R1, and B1). As noted:

“…and then through smart contract technology, after the payment of the core enterprise is made, the funds quickly complete the automatic liquidation among multiple suppliers, ensuring the payment is on time just as agreed by the transaction participants, securing repayment sources, and making financial management more convenient.” (Y1)

In this case, the blockchain technology brings the benefits of real time transaction handling and status update and visibility into pay ment transactions tracking, which supports the IT-related liquidity monitoring and reconciliation. The blockchain-driven self-enforcing automatic liquidation ensures that the entire payment execution is more secure, timely [to seconds], and effective than the previous mechanisms, thus meeting the need to reduce implementation costs and creating value for customers. Any supplier that needs financing on the supply chain can enjoy the high-quality credit of the core enterprise and reduce the financing cost of the entire supply chain.

## 6. Contributions and limitations

## 6.1. Contributions

This paper deepens the understanding of how blockchain can be applied to SCF by exploring the basic frameworks of blockchain-driven SCF scheme. This paper establishes the SCF process model through a qualitative perspective, provides new evidence based on the existing literature, and further fills the research gaps by empirically responding to the following scientific inquiries: what kind of internal motivations will generate the company to launch SCF? What resources and practices will cause the company to gain benefits from SCF solutions? How is SCF’s value creation for all the participants influenced by blockchaindriven enablers?

Specifically, our study offers several major contributions to the growing body of knowledge in SCF, S–D logic, and B2B exchange the ories. First, our study adds to SCF research by uncovering that the im pediments of value creation mentioned in the current SCF literature (e. g., lack of automation of payment, poor visibility, and lack of trans parency or timely information) can be eliminated by the blockchainenabled IT resources and capabilities. Our findings prove that the blockchain-enabled effective data interchange and integrated business solutions facilitate the optimization of the working capital and the promotion of financial performance. Additionally, the blockchain technology supports IT-related cash flow management through bringing benefits of real-time transaction handling and status update and visi bility to payment transactions tracking, meeting the need to reduce implementation costs, and creating value for customers, thereby sub stantially improving the effectiveness of SCF services and creating value for all interested stakeholders.

In addition, our study adds new insights to S–D logic theories for financial services and IS (information systems) research, where the practices of the blockchain-based SCF program facilitate the creation of service targets and are contingent on the motives of the participants for eliminating information asymmetry and improving financial services. Finally, our study also provides contributions to the B2B exchange paradigm. In essence, the quality of the B2B exchange relies on coor dination, cooperation, and information sharing, for which the supplier needs to have a well-developed and long-term relationship with the buver to assure interest for both sides [6]. The decentralized trust mechanism driven by blockchain revolutionizes the B2B relationship governance for supply chain partners, facilitates value creation, and transforms the traditional relationship models in the SCF context, because the disintermediation of the transactions is guaranteed by the distributed trust-free systems from being tampered with [31,38].

Managerially, our study provides implications for SCF practices. In traditional SCF scenarios, SCF is viewed as a set of buyer-driven financial solutions and is often applied as an evolved form of reverse factoring [2]. In this study, the evolution by blockchain is proven to be able to realize the technological improvement of SCF solution that makes it possible to transfer credit to higher tiers of suppliers. This embryonic mechanism allows high-risk suppliers to mitigate their credit risk level with that of their high-quality buyers, thus reducing their cost of debt and increasing their level of access to liquidity. The findings of this study imply that it is appropriate for those companies that are located in the complex and multilevel supply chain to adopt blockchain for SCF solu tions. Because through the core technologies of blockchain such as smart contract and consensus algorithm, blockchain offers benefits to SCF by allowing the convergence of the physical, financial, and document for the complicated supply chains, enabling collaborative documents drafting, reducing errors, maintaining data integrity, and enabling automation of key processes such as generating purchase orders and invoices, document comparisons, and sanction checks. This convergence allows banks and companies to reduce the complexity of SCF, improve working capital management, and reduce operational costs. As SCF re quires participation across the supply chain, the benefits increase with the size of the network. Therefore, on the other hand, the companies that have simple relationships on the supply chain or the supply chain has fewer levels, are not necessary to use blockchain in the SCF context, because the blockchain-driven SCF solution is a complex project and requires simultaneous change across industries, e.g., manufacturing, transportation, and banking, which is expensive and energy-costly.

## 6.2. Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of its limi tations. First, our theoretical framework is restricted in the S-D logic and social exchange theory that serve as the theoretical foundation. While this study explores the role of blockchain technology in financial ser vices, the relationship between the key roles and the contextual factor of value creation remains vague. Future research could draw on other potential perspectives to further enrich and generalize the theoretical framework. Second, this study used one case to examine the conceptual model. Future studies are expected to employ multiple cases with various participants (buyer,supplier, and financier) to measure the impact of blockchain on SCF value creation more comprehensively.

In addition, there are still a number of significant challenges to blockchain application in the SCF context. In the first place, blockchain technology could entail high risks due to potential problems associated with data portability, scalability, key securities, and user collision and control. Hence, it would also be useful to explore failure cases and prove blockchain technology on a larger scale. Furthermore, it must be sup ported by the acceptance by relevant government departments such as customs and financial regulators. Even within an organization, contracts and process changes must be made across several departments. For corporates, this change management includes trading, finance, execu tion or operations, and freight departments. For banks, it will have operational impacts across documentation teams across various prod ucts and branches. Future research is expected to leverage multiple case studies to investigate those factors that impede the deployment of blockchain-driven solutions.

## 6.3. Conclusion

The research gaps in previous SCF studies show that the current research has starkly considered either financial institutions [16–18] or buyers [2] as essential roles within an SCF solution. In this study, with the objective of better understanding the benefits emanating from blockchain-driven SCF solutions, we attempted to fill the research gaps of previous studies by exploring the value creation process through determining customer categories, their target motives, and the practices of resource exchanges for this innovative service from the theoretical lens of S-D logic [44,45], which is uncovered by previous studies. On the other hand, as previous articles focus on only partial components in the SCF context [19–24], we also considered two contextual factors, inter organizational trust and IT capabilities as the enablers for the value generation through improving B2B relationship governance among multiple partners, to fill the research gaps of the previous articles which neglect how the participants interact in the trade process. We employed

an explanatory case to prove our literature-based preunderstanding of the value creation process in the blockchain-based SCF program and further interpreted and gauged the results through qualitative analytical methods to fill the research gaps that prior studies have only remained in theoretical and conceptual discussions [9,10]. Our findings shed light on the managerial implications of current SCF practices and indicate that blockchain technology is in an extremely effective position to provide SCF customers with a viable solution as a value-created service. We discovered that buyers, suppliers, and financial institutions all benefit from SCF programs by optimizing financing terms or they would otherwise not have the motivation to participate. The traditional SCF programs, however, are unclear as to whether they could be imple mented successfully to benefit all participants. The existing differences, coupled with the lack of prior research, highlight the need to examine how traditional business practices in financial systems (e.g., SCF pro grams) can be galvanized by blockchain technology. Thus, this research is an early attempt to scientifically illustrate how value creation in the SCF platform can be revolutionized by blockchain technology, which has recently emerged as a promising approach for alleviating the drawbacks of traditional SCF programs.
