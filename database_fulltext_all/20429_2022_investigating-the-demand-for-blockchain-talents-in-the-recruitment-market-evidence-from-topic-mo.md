# Investigating the Demand for Blockchain Talents in the Recruitment Market: Evidence from Topic Modeling Analysis on Job Postings

Chunmian GE, Haoyue SHI, Junhui JIANG <sup>\*</sup>, Xiaoying XU

School of Business Administration, South China University of Technology, 381 Wushan Road, Guangzhou, P. R. China 51064

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Blockchain talent IT professional skill job posting topic modeling recruitment market

## A B S T R A C T

The demand for blockchain talents has surged in recent years, and there is a pressing need to understand what core skills and essential qualifications the blockchain talents demand and how employers value them in recruitment. This paper aims to reveal the crucial facts and patterns regarding the status quo of the recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs in China and clarify the heterogeneous nature of professionals demanded by blockchain-related jobs. We propose a data-driven approach by combining descriptive statistics, topic modeling, and regression analysis based on large-scale job posting data collected from a nationwide Chinese recruitment website. By this approach. we provide an overall picture of the landscape of the recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs and identify 11 skill sets that are most in-demand for blockchain-related jobs. On this basis, the skill sets of Blockchain Development and Cryptocurrency have emerged to be the most valued ones that affect salary levels: the talents mastering these two skill sets can obtain 21.4%, 10.4% higher average salaries, respectively, than those without these skills. The analyses also provide evidence that employers could attach importance to job candidates with T-shaped characteristics. Drawing on the findings, we also provide implica tions for blockchain practitioners.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The concept and prototype of blockchain can be traced back to 2008 and was first proposed by a mysterious user named Satoshi Nakamoto as the solution to double-spending in cryptocurrency [1]. After more than a decade of continuous development, the concepts and applications of blockchain have gone far beyond cryptocurrency and demonstrated great potential to reshape various industries [2]. However, the devel opment of blockchain and its application continues to face many ob stacles. In particular, the successful implementation and broad penetration of blockchain technology in various organizations and in dustries largely depend on the supply of talents with the necessary knowledge and skills, which works with the technology [3]. Seeking and having competent professionals in the field has become one of the most salient challenges faced by the adaptation and deployment of blockchain in industrial applications, and the demand for blockchain knowledge and skills has surged in recent years [4, 5]. According to a report by Zhaopin.com, a nationwide recruiting website in China [6], the coun try’s demand for blockchain talents surged by 636.83% from Q3 2017 to Q2 2018.

As an emergent general-purpose technology, blockchain is bound to bring substantial changes in talent demands and skill requirements [7]. The underlying technologies used to develop various blockchain appli cations may appear similar, while integrating and utilizing the appli cations can vary across different business domains, demanding talents with relevant knowledge, skills, and qualifications. Therefore, it is crucial to understand what qualifications and skills the talents should possess for blockchain-related business. For either start-up firms or existing enterprises intending to initiate or expand their blockchain-related business, a better understanding of this issue will facilitate their effective adoption and utilization of blockchain tech nology. By knowing the actual demand of the market, employees will also find it easier to cope with the accelerating pace of technological changes and secure their careers through learning the relevant knowl edge and skills or obtaining the necessary qualifications.

While there is a pressing need to understand what core skills and essential qualifications are necessary for blockchain talents and how employers value them when recruiting blockchain talents, little is known in the existing literature. As Zhao et al. [8] concluded by reviewing the research on blockchain adoption in business, most existing studies focus on the conceptual level that conceptualizes blockchain innovations in business and in the prescriptive level that outlines business applications of blockchain, rather than the descriptive level that provides observational facts, empirical regularities, and ex planations underlying blockchain phenomenon. They highlighted that “there is a need to ascertain the economic and social validity of block chain applications”. In the literature concerning the demand for IT professionals’ skills, it has been long recognized that IT professionals must face ongoing changes in technology and business practices [9], and it is increasingly crucial for them to keep their skills up to date by knowing what skills are in demand [10]. Given the vital role played by professionals and skills in the implementation and adoption of block chain technology as well as the lack of research concerning the issues in the literature, a primary goal of this research is to investigate the de mand for blockchain skills and talents at the descriptive level.

Specifically, this study aims to reveal some crucial facts and patterns around the status quo of the recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs and clarify the heterogeneous nature of professionals demanded by blockchain-related jobs. Notably, we propose a data-driven approach by combining descriptive statistics, topic modeling, and regression analysis based on large-scale job posting data collected from a nationwide Chi nese recruitment website. First, a series of descriptive analyses are car ried out to unveil the employers’ demand for blockchain-related jobs concerning temporal and geographical distribution, job categories, average salary, educational qualification, work experience, and popular majors. By doing so, we provide an overall picture of the landscape of the Chinese recruitment market for blockchain talents. Second, by applying LDA-based topic modeling techniques to analyze the job de scriptions in job postings, we identify 11 essential skill sets required for blockchain-related jobs. Furthermore, we utilize regression analysis to investigate how employers, in terms of the salaries offered in their job postings for blockchain talents, value the skill sets and qualifications. Our results show that Blockchain Development and Cryptocurrency have emerged to be the most valued skill sets that affect salary levels.

This study could enrich the literature on IT labor and jobs by enhancing our understanding of the blockchain-related recruitment market and talents. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first attempts to address the concerns raised by blockchain practitioners on the skills and qualifications required to facilitate the adaptation and deployment of blockchain in industrial applications. The findings could also contribute to blockchain literature by enhancing our understanding of the adoption and diffusion process of the technology from the perspective of talent recruitment. Moreover, this study contributes to the research on human resource management by demonstrating a novel approach to identify the core skills and qualifications demanded by jobs related to emerging technology. In addition, we also draw important implications for blockchain practitioners based on the findings of this study.

## 2. BACKGROUND

## 2.1. Research on Blockchain Adoption in Business Context

While blockchain technology was born with Bitcoin, its applications have gone far beyond Bitcoin or digital currency and are believed to have the potential to revolutionize various industries, such as account ing, auditing, managing properties, and protecting copyrights [8]. For instance, faced with fierce competition, the financial services industry has always been keen to seek innovative solutions to reduce transaction costs and improve efficiency and security, and blockchain technology may provide some of the most feasible options: The technology has demonstrated significant advantages in clearance and settlement of financial assets, payment systems, smart contracts, and operational risks in the financial market [11, 12].

Despite its enormous impacts on many industries, research on the implementation and adoption of blockchain technology is still scarce.

The few studies on blockchain adoption in a business environment, ac cording to Zhao et al. [8], can be roughly divided into three levels, including conceptual, prescriptive, and descriptive levels [13]. Most existing studies by then were found to focus on the conceptual level that conceptualizes blockchain innovations in business and in the prescrip tive level that outlines business applications of blockchain rather than the descriptive level that provides observational facts, empirical regu larities, and explanations underlying blockchain phenomenon. The focus of more recent studies on blockchain technology in businesses remains to be on the conceptual level (e.g., [14]) and the prescriptive level (e.g., [15-18]), with only a few exceptions that look at the descriptive level (e.g., [19, 20]). As Li et al. [21] conclude in their scoping literature review on blockchain technology in business organi zations, most of the research in this field are conceptual and focus on “how” blockchain technology works or “what” its potential applications are in the business context, while few studies are concerned about “whom” questions that address people affected by the blockchain technology and “why” questions which investigate the incentives for adopting the technology.

This research focuses on the descriptive level and investigates “whom” involved in implementing and adopting blockchain technology in industries, i.e., the demand for blockchain talents and skills in the recruitment market.

## 2.2. Blockchain Technology and Demand for Professions

The incredible power of blockchain technology will be achieved mainly through “its ability to support trustworthy transactions via net worked computation in place of human monitor and control.” It may eventually result in millions of jobs phased out because of blockchain based automation [8, 22]. However, before that happens, organiza tions must first create blockchain jobs and hire employees with the necessary skills and knowledge for working with the technology [3]. For all organizations, the conversion or change from raw materials into goods and services depends on employees’ effective use of necessary skills, knowledge, abilities, techniques, tools, and other equipment [23], and the organizations engaged in blockchain business are no exception. In other words, to harness the power of this new technology and even lead the way with blockchain-related business innovations, an organi zation must have talents who master the relevant qualifications and know-how to utilize blockchain technologies in an innovative way for a specific business. For instance, Khwaja Shaik, IBM’s Thought Leader, has stressed the utmost importance of blockchain skills and called the lack of blockchain skills “a top concern and significant inhibitor to blockchain adoption in companies across a variety of industries” [24].

With successful applications of blockchain technology in business by early adopters and increasing clarity of the roadmap for utilizing blockchain technologies in industries, more organizations will look to seize the opportunities and put forward their demands for blockchain talents and skills. Therefore, as a disruptive innovation, the adoption and utilization process of blockchain technology in industries will further spur the demand for professionals and skills in related fields. According to LinkedIn Learning, an educational portal of the world’s largest professional social network, blockchain emerged as one of the most in-demand hard skills in 2020, with growing demands from most businesses and very few supplies in the labor market [25].

While there have been reports from the industry, which indicate a great demand for blockchain talents and the supply is relatively insuf ficient [26], the current literature does not inform much about the de tails and patterns of such demand. In the field of IT labor research. the critical skills and knowledge necessary for IT professionals, in general, have received attention from the literature since the initial introduction of information technology into business. IT professionals must face ongoing changes in technology and business practices [9], and it is increasingly crucial to keep their skills up to date by clearly knowing what skills are in demand for the jobs [10]. In this strand of research, a seminal work by Todd et al. [27] was to track the change of IS skills over time. This study examined the skill requirements for three specific IS job positions: programmers, systems analysts, and managers, over two de cades as specified in job postings in four focal newspapers. Another seminal work on IS professionals’ critical skills and knowledge re quirements was done by Lee et al. [28]. In particular, they investigated anticipated changes in the IS profession and the impact of the changes on the skills and knowledge required.

Since then, with the penetration of information technology in various industries and the continuous emergence of new forms of technologies over decades, the division of the IT profession, together with the required skills and knowledge, has evolved and refined much further. In line with the change, researchers have begun to look at the subsectors of IT technology and explore the skills required for employees engaging in specific IT domains [29]. In their pilot work on new job positions and recruitment related to blockchain technologies, Luki´c et al. [30] summed up some of the new jobs created and required by the applications of blockchain technology, such as Blockchain Developer, Blockchain Project Manager, Blockchain Designer, Blockchain Quality Engineer, Blockchain Legal Consultant, Blockchain Concept Developer, Blockchain Analyst, and Blockchain Research Scientist. However, the qualifications and skills that blockchain talents are expected to master are much more complex than a list of new job titles. In one of the few studies, which concerns the specific skills required for blockchain pro fessionals, Bosu et al. [31] gave close attention to the group of software developers. They conducted an online survey among 1,604 active blockchain software (BCS) developers to analyze BCS versus non-BCS development. In their study, beyond those required for non-BCS devel opment, the knowledge of secured programming, networking, cryptog raphy, and math skills have emerged to be the unique skills benefiting the persons considering to be BCS developers. As Al-Jaroodi & Mohamed [4] pointed out, “to create fully integrated blockchain-based industrial ap plications, the design and development teams need to be knowledgeable in various areas” [4].

However, the actual demand for blockchain talents and skills can be far beyond the scope of software developers. As an overarching concept, blockchain relates to various technologies and applications which are still in rapid development and diffusion in various industries. Based on anecdotes or personal experience, industry experts have drawn up a long list of skills to become blockchain talents (e.g., [24]). However, there is still a lack of rigorous analysis and empirical evidence on the issues in academic literature to help us better understand the particulars of the demand for blockchain talents and skills.

## 2.3. Topic Modeling

As a popular text mining technique, topic modeling refers to a group of algorithms for capturing hidden structures in large-scale text data [32]. Using this technique in a given set of documents, we assume that each document consists of topics with a probability distribution, and each topic can be represented by probabilistically distributed words [33]. The topics identified by topic modeling are essentially semantic clusters representing word co-occurrence in a document [33].

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) is one of the most classic topic modeling algorithms [33] and has been widely used in analyzing free text such as user-generated content [32, 34, 35, 36] and corporate annual reports [37]. As an unsupervised algorithm, given a text corpus, LDA can automatically identify potential topics based on word fre quencies and co-occurrence frequencies in documents without defining labels in advance [38, 34]. In LDA, words in a document are randomly assigned and then semantically clustered using a repetitive probabilistic process based on a Dirichlet distribution. Bayesian estimation tech niques are used to identify topics of a given set of documents, per-document topic distribution, per-topic word distribution, and other hidden structures [39]. Given its effectiveness in analyzing user-generated content [40], we use it in this study to analyze

blockchain-related job postings.

With the recent development of labor market intelligence, researchers have realized the value of a topic model to analyze labor market requirements. Various LDA-based methods have been proposed to model job opportunities and the required skills. For example, given that the skills needed by big data-related careers were not clear, De Mauro et al. [35] mined nine groups of big-data skills that companies needed based on big-data-related job postings using LDA. Gurcan and Cagiltay [29] used LDA on big-data-related job postings to identify the knowledge domains and skill sets needed by big-data software engi neers. Xu et al. [41] proposed a novel approach based on the topic model and analysis of job postings to model the popularity of job skills and further proved that employees with more popular skills could quickly get hired. Colace et al. [42] proposed a novel approach to classify Labor Market Intelligence based on the topic model. These existing studies have shown the potentials of the topic model in analyzing the labor market while scant attention has been paid to the emergent blockchain area. Moreover, from a methodological perspective, there is a lack of research that utilizes both topic modeling and econometric analyses to investigate the actual demand for blockchain skills and talents. There fore, we introduce this novel approach, i.e., integrating econometric analyses with topic model outputs, to better identify blockchain skills emerging in job postings and examine the market demand for the skills.

## 3. METHODOLOGY

To better understand 1) the status quo of the recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs; 2) what qualifications and skills the jobs de mand; and 3) how an employer values these qualifications and skills when recruiting blockchain talents, we follow a data-driven approach by combining descriptive statistics, topic modeling, and regression analysis based on the blockchain-related job postings from a nationwide Chinese recruitment website. Specifically, the objectives of our methodology are to 1) reveal the crucial facts and regularities based on descriptive analysis; 2) identify the skill sets demanded by blockchain-related jobs by applying a topic modeling algorithm, LDA; and 3) further analyze the relations between skill sets, qualifications, and salary through regression analysis. The methodological procedure consists of three steps, i.e., data collection, data preprocessing, and data analysis, the details of which are given as follows.

## 3.1. Data Collection

We develop a job posting crawler to collect all job posting data from a nationwide Chinese recruitment website since February 2017. The website was launched in 2011 and had more than 59 million registered users as individual members, and served more than 620,000 firms as employer members by 2020. Before posting up its job postings on the website regularly, a firm needs to register as an employer user formally. The website will conduct a series of checks for the firm’s qualification, including its business license, official seal, email, and physical address, to ensure that the firm and the recruitment are authentic. The website also provides a structured template containing essential recruitment information such as job titles, job descriptions, job requirements, work locations, and salary ranges for employer users to facilitate their drafting of a job posting. Such measures make the job postings of the website a relatively reliable and complete data source of the recruitment infor mation for blockchain talents.

Among over 30 million job postings for the period from February 2017 to January 2020<sup>1</sup>, we have screened out those irrelevant job postings based on whether a job posting contains the keyword “blockchain” in its job title, job description, or job requirement, and managed to identify 18,028 job postings concerning the recruitment of blockchain talents, which constitute the sample for our further analysis.

## 3.2. Data Preprocessing

The original information of job posting mainly exists in plain text; therefore, the next step is to use the data preprocessing techniques for text mining to process the text data for further analysis [43]. The process of data preprocessing is initiated with the following four steps that are executed by the “tm” package in R: (1) Word segmentation (for Chinese): split successive text contents into separate words; (2) Character con version: convert all English letters in the text to lowercase and all traditional Chinese characters to a simplified version; (3) Character deletion: remove punctuations, weblinks, private tags, and useless numbers; and (4) Words deletion: remove stop words and irrelated words.

After being processed through the above steps, the generated job requirement documents will be used to build a document term matrix. First, we define each document as a word vector whose dimension is the frequency of words in this document. Later, through combining all the word vectors, the textual data are converted into a two-dimensional matrix, the so-called Document-Term Matrix (DTM), with the rows representing the documents and the columns representing the words. In the matrix, each number represents the frequency of the focal word in the documents. Created by a word-bag-based approach, DTM can pro vide information about word frequencies regardless of word order [29]. Finally, through the above process, we obtain a DTM with 18,028 rows and 1,550 columns, which contains 18,028 job requirement documents and 1,550 unique words. The matrix obtained will then be an essential input for the subsequent topic analysis.

## 3.3. Data Analysis

## 3.3.1. Descriptive Analysis of Job Posting Data

To provide an overview regarding the status quo of the Chinese recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs, through utilizing the job posting data collected online as introduced in Section 3.1, we use basic statistical methods and keyword indexing techniques to describe and reveal some important facts and regularities from the job posting data. In particular, we calculate and present the month-by-month changes in the total numbers and the average salary for blockchain-related job recruitment. In addition, we use a keyword index to analyze the composition structure of talent demand by the blockchain recruitment market in terms of its job categories and qualification requirements (i.e., education level, work experience, and major field of study).

## 3.3.2. Implementation of LDA

To address what qualifications and skills the blockchain-related jobs demand, LDA is implemented to identify skills described in the job postings based on the matrix obtained from the previous data pre processing step. Specifically, we use the LDA algorithm implemented by the “topicmodels” package in R to identify skill sets from DTM.

The main inputs to LDA are DTM obtained by the above process and the number of topics. The number of topics, represented as $k ,$ is a parameter manually specified to control the granularity of the identified topics. More fine-granular topics will be identified if the value of k is set larger; otherwise, the identified topics will be more generic. To deter mine the appropriate value of $k ,$ we follow two criteria, namely the metrics of perplexity and topic coherence. Perplexity measures the predictive power of the LDA topic model for new documents from the perspective of model generalization ability [39]. Smaller perplexity in dicates the better potential of the model in generalization, or in other words, better predictive ability of the model. This metric can evaluate the effect of models comprehensively. Specifically, perplexity is defined as:

$$
\text { perplexity } (D) = \exp \left(- \frac {\sum_ {d = 1} ^ {M} \log (P (w _ {d}))}{\sum_ {d = 1} ^ {M} N _ {d}}\right)\tag{1}
$$

where D is a test set with M documents in the corpus, $N _ { d }$ is the length of document d, $w _ { d }$ represents a word in document $d ,$ and ${ \mathrm { P } } ( w _ { d } )$ is the probability of $w _ { d }$ in document d.

The other metric is topic coherence. Topic coherence measures the quality of a topic. The higher the topic coherence, the more cohesive and interpretable the topic is [44]. Letting F(v) be the number of documents with at least a word $\nu , F ( \nu , \nu ^ { ' } )$ be the number of documents containing at least a word v and $\nu \ ' .$ . The topic coherence of a topic is defined as:

$$
C (t; V ^ {(t)}) = \sum_ {m = 2} ^ {M} \sum_ {l = 1} ^ {m - 1} \log \frac {F \left(v _ {m} ^ {(t)} , v _ {l} ^ {(t)}\right) + 1}{F \left(v _ {l} ^ {(t)}\right)}\tag{2}
$$

where $V ^ { ( t ) } = ( \nu _ { 1 } { } ^ { ( t ) } , . . . , \nu _ { M } { } ^ { ( t ) } )$ is a list of M words that have the highest probability of appearing in this topic.

As the value of k varies from 2 to 50, we obtained the corresponding perplexity and average topic coherence of the model, as plotted in Figure 1 and Figure 2. In particular, starting from $k = 1 1$ , perplexity becomes relatively stable instead of dropping dramatically. Meanwhile, when $k = 1 1$ , topic coherence is also close to its maximum value. When we follow the criteria recommended by previous studies [39, 44], we believe $k = 1 1$ can be the most appropriate specification for the LDA model. To further ensure the feasibility of this value, following the approach recommended by Blei et al. [33], we introduce a manual evaluation to see whether the identified topics by k = 11 are meaningful and interpretable. Specifically, we compare the outputs by varying the k value from 2 to 50 and confirm that when k is 11, the identified topics can be relatively meaningful and interpretable. Therefore, we specify the value of k as 11 in the LDA model.

The outputs of LDA include 1) a set of topics, namely 11 skill sets in our case, represented by keywords from tokenized documents, 2) the topic-word distribution, indicating the probability of each word appearing in each topic, and 3) the document-topic distribution, rep resenting the probability of each topic appearing on each document. To obtain more accurate and objective names and interpretations of these skill sets, we recruited eight Ph.D. students familiar with management information systems research and assigned them to independently label the 11 skill sets based on the top 20 keywords appearing in each skill set. To ensure that the labels of each skill set they provide are coherent, we gave a list of related skill names for them to choose. However, if they think none of the names given are appropriate, they were free to describe and name the skill sets by themselves. Eventually, we named each skill set with the label that the students consistently chose for the skill set. Eleven skill sets with clear names were identified through the procedure, including Database, Finance, Network Programming, Oper ation, Marketing, Testing, Big Data, Web Development, Blockchain Development, Cryptocurrency, and Product Design. They comprehen sively capture the core aspects of skills demanded by blockchain-related jobs.

## 3.3.3. Regression Analysis

Topic modeling based on LDA has informed us of what skill sets employers highly demand when recruiting blockchain talents. On this basis, we aim to understand how employers, in terms of the salaries offered in their job postings for blockchain talents, value the skill sets and qualifications. Particularly, we introduce regression analysis to examine the relations between the identified skill sets and average salary offered in postings, a practical approach used in extant studies [45, 46]. In addition, extant literature in human resource management and labor economics has also identified a series of antecedents that can affect employees’ salary levels, such as one’s educational background and working experience [47-49]. Therefore, we include them in the subsequent regression models as control variables.

Furthermore, the key for blockchain practitioners to effectively cope with the challenges and solve complex problems in the field is believed to lie in the recruitment of T-shaped professionals who master the skills and knowledge not only for blockchain technology but also from a di versity of disciplines [24]. In general, T-shaped professionals refer to the professionals deep in one area (represented by the vertical stroke of the “T”) and also with good knowledge, skills, experience, and complex communication abilities across many other areas (represented by the horizontal stroke of the “T’") [50]. Most of the extant studies on T-shaped professionals focus on the cultivation of T-shaped professionals and their impacts on firm knowledge or innovation, while few studies have explored whether employers really value such talents in recruitment. Given this gap and their potential importance in practice, we also examine whether the T-shaped characteristics of blockchain pro fessionals would influence their salary levels in job postings by the regression analysis. Specifically, the educational qualification of a doctoral degree is taken as a proxy of the vertical stroke of the “T” while the number of skill sets required for each blockchain-related job in a posting is taken as a proxy of the horizontal stroke of the “T” for T-shaped blockchain talents.

## 4.2.1. Core Skill Sets for Blockchain Talents

The above descriptive analysis provides an overall picture of the status quo of the recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs and reveals some essential qualifications for blockchain talents demanded by employers. To better understand what core skills are required for blockchain talents, we apply a topic modeling algorithm, LDA, to identify the demanded skill sets of blockchain talents in the job postings. Table 3 shows the 11 core skill sets we identified through the LDA for blockchain talents, including Blockchain Development, Cryptocurrency, Testing, Database, Big Data, Web Development, Network Programming, Product Design, Finance, Operation, and Marketing. The top 10 words (translated into English) for each skill set are also listed.

In addition, the results also show that the demands for blockchainrelated skills are diverse. The demands are not limited to technical skill sets such as Database, Network Programming, Testing, Big data, Web Development, Blockchain Development, Cryptocurrency, and Product Design, but also emphasize nontechnical skill sets such as Finance, Operation, and Marketing. Moreover, it is worth noting that among the 11 core skill sets, only the skill sets of Blockchain Develop ment and Cryptocurrency are unique to the blockchain technology; the other core skill sets are relatively generic and are shared by industrial areas. It indicates that the current development of blockchain technol ogy depends on the development of other related technologies and in dustries. Meanwhile, we can also infer that talents for emergent technologies or industries may come from other well-established tech nology fields and industries. Therefore, the competition for talents in the field of emergent technology can be cross-technology and cross-industry competition.

It should be noted that in Table 3, some top words appear to be identical between different skill sets, e.g., Big Data and Database, but they differ in their importance to their corresponding skill sets, as measured by the topic-word probability. For example, the probability of Big Data in the skill sets of Big Data is 0.09, while in the skill sets of Marketing is 0.01.

## 5. CONCLUSION

## 5.1. Summary of Findings

The study aims to reveal the crucial facts and patterns regarding the status quo of the recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs in China and clarify the heterogeneous nature of professionals demanded by blockchain-related jobs. We propose a data-driven approach by combining descriptive statistics, topic modeling, and regression analysis based on large-scale job posting data collected from a nationwide Chi nese recruitment website. The main findings are summarized as follows.

First, based on 18,028 blockchain job postings available on the website from February 2017 to January 2020, we carry out a series of descriptive analyses to unfold the employers’ demand for positions, salary, job title, major, education background, and experience in their recruitment for blockchain talents. By doing so, we provide an overall picture of the landscape of the recruitment market for blockchain related jobs. Second, by applying LDA-based topic modeling techniques in analyzing the job descriptions in job postings, we identify 11 skill sets that are most in-demand for blockchain-related jobs, including Block chain Development, Cryptocurrency, Testing, Database, Big Data, Web Development, Network Programming, Product Design, Finance, Opera tion, and Marketing. Further, we use regression analyses to investigate how employers, in terms of the salaries offered in their job postings for blockchain talents, value the identified skill sets and essential qualifi cations. The skill sets of Blockchain Development and Cryptocurrency have emerged to be the most valued skill sets that affect salary levels. In addition, the qualifications regarding academic degree, graduated uni versity, working experience, and the number of skills, are found to significantly affect salary levels. Notably, it is found that the require ment for both a doctoral degree and more skill sets are positively related to the average salary for blockchain talents, supporting the argument that organizations attach importance to the T-shaped characteristic when evaluating job candidates in recruitment.

## 5.2. Contributions and Implications

This paper makes the following contributions to the existing litera ture. First, this study enriches the literature of IT labor and jobs by enhancing our understanding of blockchain-related jobs’ recruitment market and talents. Despite the critical role played by the professionals and their skills in the implementation and adoption of blockchain technology, there is a lack of research concerning the issues of block chain talents and skills in the literature. As mentioned, the literature concerning IT labor and skills has long recognized that IT professionals must face ongoing changes in technology and business practices [9], and it is vital to keep their skills up to date by learning what exactly the skills and knowledge are in demand for specific jobs. In our case, the changes brought by blockchain applications in the business context create new demands on the jobs of IT professionals and new expectations about the blockchain-related jobs within organizations. To our best knowledge, this study is among the first attempts to address the concerns raised by blockchain practitioners on the skills and qualifications required to facilitate the adaptation and deployment of blockchain in industrial applications. Particularly, we have revealed the crucial facts and pat terns around the status quo of the recruitment market for blockchain-related jobs and made clarity of the heterogeneous nature of professionals required by blockchain-related jobs, i.e., what and how the essential qualifications and core skills the employers demand in their recruitment. In addition, the findings also provide preliminary evidence for the views emphasizing the importance of T-shaped professionals.

Second, the findings could also contribute to blockchain literature by enhancing our understanding of the adoption and diffusion process of the technology from the perspective of talent recruitment. Either in practice or theory, locating the position or phase of blockchain tech nology on its diffusion of innovation adoption curve at the industry level is of great significance yet full of challenges, which must be examined systematically from many aspects [55]. In this sense, this study provides a novel perspective for researchers and practitioners to look at the adoption and diffusion of this new technology and helps better under stand the current stage of blockchain technology in its innovation adoption curve in the industry.

Third, this study contributes to the research on human resource management by demonstrating a novel approach to identify the core skills and qualifications demanded by jobs related to emerging tech nology. Notably, we leverage a topic modeling analysis on job postings to extract and identify the core skill sets most in-demand for blockchainrelated jobs. As technological changes are getting increasingly faster, the traditional way of defining and classifying skills can be inaccurate or inefficient to capture the dynamics and cutting edge in skill re quirements. Instead, a more automated and intelligent approach com bined with big data techniques may be a better choice to excavate and classify skills in the context of new and fast-changing technology.

The findings of this paper also provide important implications for blockchain practitioners. First, by providing crucial insights into the recruitment market for blockchain talents, such as the distribution of blockchain-related jobs and essential qualifications required for block chain talents, the findings can inform policymakers in formulating more pertinent policies to promote blockchain-related industries and talent cultivation. Second, by providing the clarity of what qualifications and skills are most expected and highly valued by employers for blockchain talents, the findings can help potential employers, particularly start-ups, identify a qualified blockchain workforce. In addition, with a better understanding of the demand for blockchain professions and the core skill sets, current employees and job seekers for blockchain-related jobs may enhance their skills and knowledge or obtain necessary qualifica tions accordingly to have a better chance of success in their jobs.

## 5.3. Limitations

There are a few limitations to this study. First, the data analyzed in this paper were collected from a nationwide recruitment website in China, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the recruitment market for blockchain talents in developed countries. Future research may consider using the data of some international recruitment websites covering multiple regions to verify the findings or carry out comparative studies among different regions. Second, the core skill sets identified by applying topic modeling techniques to the job postings in this paper are coarse-grained to some extent. The purpose of the current study is to unveil the status quo of the Chinese blockchain recruitment market, and we take all job posting data collected from the website during the observation period as the subject of analysis without manually defining its substructure. Future work may make a more fine-grained distinction among blockchain-related jobs and identify specific skill sets for each type of job. Third, some inferences we made to interpret the underlying reasons for the observed facts and patterns emerging from our descrip tive analysis call for more systematic and rigorous future studies by incorporating related context and data. Until then, any further specu lation based on the inferences should be made with caution.

Dr. Jiang’s research interests include information systems adoption, human capital management, entrepreneurship, and innovation. His work has been published in leading journals such as Information & Management, International Journal of Information Management, and IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management.
