# Herd behavior in information security decision-making

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Popularity information Imitation Herd behavior Information security Coping appraisal Bounded rationality

## A B S T R A C T

Current information security behavior research assumes that lone individuals make a rational. informed decision about security technologies based on careful consideration of personally available information. We challenge this assumption by examining how the herd behavior influences users’ security decisions when coping with security threats. The results show that uncertainty about a security technology leads users to discount their own infor mation and imitate others. We found that imitation tendency has a more substantial effect on security decisions than the personal perceived efficacy of the security technology. It is essential for researchers and managers to consider how the herd behavior effect influences users’ security decisions.

## 1. Introduction

Information security threats continue to plague organizations and home users alike, with the number and magnitude of security incidents (e.g., identity theft, phishing, and malware) increasing dramatically. According to the Online Trust Alliance’s latest report [71], cyber in cidents nearly doubled from 82,000 in 2016 to 160,000 in 2017, showing that data breaches and cybersecurity attacks continue to grow dramatically. Kaspersky [50] reports that about half of all security breaches can be traced back to an individual making a poor security decision. Hence, one of the key goals of information security research is to find ways to improve information security decision-making and to motivate users to protect information assets effectively. Therefore, un derstanding users’ decision-making process about adopting proper se curity practices is important for both research and practice. Many studies have explored the effectiveness of deploying persuasive mes sages called “fear appeals” that warn about a specific threat and describe safeguarding measures to encourage the users to engage in a specific protective behavior ([14]; Johnston et al. 2018). An effectively crafted persuasive message triggers two sequential cognitive processes. First, the recipient recognizes the potential threat, and second, he or she as sesses the proposed security response designed to mitigate the threat [29].

Dominant theoretical lenses for investigating information security behaviors are primarily grounded on assumptions of lone individuals rationally responding to information related to security threats [23]. A common assumption in past information security research is that an individual computer user has sufficient personal information and expertise to assess the efficacy of security technologies and is motivated to invest the necessary cognitive effort in making the security decision and to cope with a threat. However, most users are constrained by bounded rationality. in which their ability to act is limited by factors such as their information, cognitive restrictions, and available time [79, 94]. Likewise, Dennis & Minas [23] argue that most of the users security-related decisions are not based on deliberate, rational cognition but on non-conscious automatic cognition that is immediate and reactive.

In many cases, users are uncertain about the efficacy of new security technology, either because their information is incomplete or they lack the ability or motivation to understand the technology’s capabilities. This situation may motivate users to find ways to cope with the resulting uncertainty [11]. In such circumstances, the observation of other users decisions and learning about the popularity of alternatives can play important roles in influencing users’ decision making. In other words, when users are uncertain about their next actions, they may simply follow the herd and imitate others. Herd behavior is defined as the phe nomenon in which a user follows other users in making decisions, even when their personal information might suggest a different course of action [13].

Thanks to the Internet and various online social media platforms, users can easily observe other users’ decisions pertaining to the adoption of security technologies, making it easy to learn how popular these technologies are. Thus, understanding the role of herd behavior in making security decisions and coping with security threats is crucial because it may significantly affect information security and assurance. Understanding the herd behavior phenomenon is important because it can have a positive effect by speeding the adoption of superior security technologies [59], but it can also create unrealistic expectations, groupthink, and distortions of the truth. Particularly, when users’ per ceptions are largely influenced by herd mentality, their decision making can be manipulated, with dramatic consequences at the individual, corporate, and social levels [68].

Even though the effects of popularity information on users’ behavior have been studied in various contexts such as electronic commerce [24, 56, 60, 91, 97, 102], tourism [78, 100, 104], and software use [26, 85], we know little about the influence of popularity information and herd behavior on security-related decisions. Therefore, because information security research has been silent on how herd behavior can influence security decisions and cope with security threats, we seek to answer the following research question: Are users more likely to follow the herd and develop a higher level of protection motivation when aware of the popularity of a security technology?

This paper examines how herd behavior influences users’ security decisions in a voluntary context. The results show that in uncertain circumstances when users are informed about the popularity of security technology, imitation tendency becomes the dominant factor in deter mining their protection motivation. Specifically, individuals place a higher value on the decision of the herd than their personal assessment and discount their initial judgment. Thus, herd behavior plays a signif icant role in security decision-making, and when the herd signals the adoption of security technology, users are more likely to adopt it.

The contribution of this research is also consistent with the call to increasingly consider the role of context, challenge the boundary as sumptions of the paradigms, and control for individual and situational factors that might play a significant role in explaining the phenomena under investigation [10, 21]. Burton-Jones & Gallivan [15] and Burton-Jones & Volkoff [16] advise information systems (IS) researchers to account for contextual factors and different circumstances in theo rizing and examining IS phenomena. Furthermore. consistent with the call for examining users’ actual behavior instead of behavioral in tentions [22], we measured our participants’ actual adoption behavior and found that it matched well with their stated decision to adopt or not.

This paper proceeds as follows. First, we discuss the relevant theo retical foundations. Then, we propose our hypotheses and the research model. Next, we explain the methods and the results. Finally, we discuss the theoretical contributions and practical implications of this study.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. Protection motivation theory

Messages warning about a security threat and advocating the adop tion of security technology to mitigate it (commonly called “fear ap peals”) and their influence on user behavior has been a popular area of research in the information security field [21]. Framing persuasive messages and their effects on behavior were initially investigated by psychology researchers during the 1950s [44]. In 1975, Rogers syn thesized this research in the protection motivation theory (PMT), which marked a significant turning point that led to research in many other areas. Subsequently, researchers in different fields, such as marketing [25, 70], communications [99], healthcare [54], and information se curity (e.g., [4, 14, 39, 47, 48, 95]) further investigated the capability of using persuasive messages to prompt the appropriate level of awareness about a threat and consequently improve individuals’ attitudes and behaviors in response to protecting themselves from threats.

According to past behavioral information security research, when individuals are exposed to a persuasive message that advocates a response to a threat, two cognitive mediating processes arise that shape protection motivation behavior: 1) the appraisal of the threat, and 2) the assessment of the individual’s capacity to cope with that threat [46]. Threat appraisal encompasses evaluating the perceived severity of the threat and the perceived susceptibility to the threat. Coping appraisal involves an individual’s assessment of how effective an advocated response is (response efficacy) and whether he or she has the requisite capabilities to organize and execute courses of action required (selfefficacy). It can be summarized as “This threat is severe, you are vulnerable, but this response works, and you can do it.” An effective persuasive message must include details addressing all four constructs in a balanced presentation to avoid a maladaptive response [64, 74]. When individuals receive a persuasive message, they evaluate both the threat and the recommended response. Individuals’ perceptions of threat severity and their susceptibility to it trigger assessments of response efficacy and self-efficacy to determine their level of protection motiva tion behavior [14, 46, 47]. If the threat is perceived to be severe and likely, if the recommended response is perceived to be effective, and if the recipient believes he or she can successfully implement the response, then the motivation to engage in protective behavior increases. As a result, an individual is expected to engage in the recommended response.

Numerous information security studies leverage persuasive messages and PMT to investigate security behavior in online activities, such as phishing attacks [96] and while using social networking sites [90]. Some information security research using PMT has focused on a few key fac tors (e.g., [46, 47]), while other research [14] argues for including many factors from the full nomology. In the current context, self-efficacy and response efficacy have been found to have the most significant influence on intentions to adopt protection-motivated behaviors [55]. For example, in the security context, self-efficacy and response efficacy were strongly influential in online account password management behavior [103]. Because the goal of this research is to investigate how users cope with security threats in uncertain circumstances and when popularity information about a relevant security technology is present, we focus on coping appraisal constructs and how they interplay with the other constructs of interest.

## 2.2. Behavioral security research, PMT, and bounded rationality

The foundational theories used to explain security-related decisions (e.g., PMT, General Deterrence Theory, etc.) are based on the assump tion of rational choice. However, extensive research has shown that, based on numerous factors, decision makers often violate the strict te nets of utility maximization [32, 37]. Simon (1967) defines this phe nomenon as bounded rationality, which occurs when the rationality of individual decision makers is limited by factors such as incomplete in formation, time constraint, or cognitive limitations. If users’ rationality is bounded, then the logical application of clear rules is impossible because incalculable uncertainty prevents the quantification of the probability of future events [8].

The bounds of rationality can be further understood through Simon’s (1976) distinction between substantive and procedural rationality. Substantive rationality is a situation in which people concentrate on achieving objective goals within given constraints. If a person is sub stantively rational, he or she will form quantifiable expectations of the future and make decisions using constrained optimization techniques to balance marginal benefits with costs, thereby maximizing utility. Thus, if different individuals have access to the same information, on average, they will have similar expectations about the probability of outcomes.

Conversely, procedural rationality concerns a reasoning process rather than the achievement of goals, which may lead to satisficing $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ sticking with the current situation because it is convenient even if it is not optimal). Procedural rationality may reveal more about the actual learning behavior of boundedly rational agents than the analysis of substantive rationality because realistically, many decisions are not based on evaluating all alternatives, and they include a certain level of

As uncertainty increases, it could be procedurally rational to imitate what other people are doing, thus following the crowd [2]. In the classic economics literature, Keynes [53] argued that when a person’s infor mation is sparse, he or she should follow what others are doing because the behavior of the majority might be based on more comprehensive information. Following the majority is rational if the individual has reason to believe that other people’s judgments are based on better in formation. Consequently, he or she will incorporate other people’s ac tions into his or her decision-making.

In the context of security behaviors, decisions are made in a complex and multidimensional environment that may include contradictory goals, such as choosing a facile but ineffective safeguard versus a com plex but highly effective safeguard. Therefore, understanding how security-related decisions are made by individuals in uncertain cir cumstances is crucial. In the next section, we will discuss herd theory, which explains how users’ decisions can be influenced by the informa tion they receive about the actions of other people. Recent research in the reference disciplines has empirically examined and confirmed the link between bounded rationality and herd behavior (Christofferson & Stæhr, 2019: [38, 72, 80]).

## 2.3. Herd theory

In a wide variety of social and economic situations, people are influenced in their decision-making by the behavior of those around them. For example, we often choose restaurants based on how popular they seem [11]. Similarly, Keynes [52] suggested that investors in asset markets often make decisions based on current trends. Decisions involving fertility choices (e.g., how many children to have) were sub stantially influenced by observing the similar behavior of people in the same geographic area [11]. The phenomenon of herd behavior, defined as "everyone does what everyone else is doing, even when their private information suggests doing something quite different" ([11], p. 798), can also occur in other contexts, such as citizens’ voting patterns, “hot” topics that researchers choose to investigate, and the decision to join a particular social network. In addition to personal information. herd behavior is based on the individual’s observations of the actions or be haviors of others; the most likely decision is a combination of both these factors [11]. Possibly, all people will make the same choice by imitating each other, though it remains unlikely because it requires that everyone disregards their personal information. Avery & Zemsky [7] showed that financial agents often trade on the differences between their personal information and public information.

When faced with a decision, a decision maker can invest time and effort into forming his or her own assessment of the situation. This process often involves information search and the evaluation of the products of the search [28]. Information technology is often complex, meaning that an accurate assessment requires technology knowledge [26, 45] and the investment of time and energy. A lack of knowledge about technology can create uncertainty over its appropriateness [85].

An alternative strategy to investing one’s own time and effort is to simply follow the herd. This approach presumes that the herd has gone through the search and assessment processes and has determined that adopting a certain technology is a reasonable decision. As uncertainty about a decision increases, an individual will have an increasing ten dency to discount their own beliefs and imitate others’ decisions [85]. The discounting occurs when the information about the popularity of a certain decision is not consistent with own previously-held beliefs; therefore, receiving popularity information might not necessarily lead to discounting own information (Sun, 2103). Ultimately, the individual will incorporate the behavior of others (i.e., popularity information) into their own set of prior information and make a herd behavior-based de cision [53].

In IS research, the influence of herd behavior has been examined using two different complementary theoretical mechanisms: 1) “dis counting own information” (i.e., the degree to which one disregards personal beliefs about technology when making an adoption decision) and 2) “imitating others” (i.e., the degree to which one follows previous adopters in choosing a certain form of technology) [85]. Studies of herd behavior have focused mainly on discrete decisions, such as whether to invest in a particular project or to adopt or reject a technology [76]. For example, Salganik et al. [75] and Hanson & Putler [36] reported that individuals followed others in their online behaviors, reacting to pub lished information about others’ adoption decisions. Moreover, Duan et al. [26] found that Internet users’ choices of software fluctuated significantly when the download rankings of the software products changed, which indicated that users were likely to be influenced by the decisions made by previous adopters. The fact that many users have adopted a specific technology may signify that it is popular; therefore, useful to others. Thus, the number of current users of technology may send signals (i.e., adoption or rejection signals) that influence a user to adopt or not adopt a technology.

To summarize, herd behavior is primarily triggered by an in dividual’s uncertainty about alternatives. For instance, a user becomes aware of a security threat and is uncertain about how to cope with it. The user may have two sources of information about the coping alternatives (e.g., different security technologies to use). These are 1) his or her own limited information, and 2) popularity information. Own limited infor mation forms the personal assessment (i.e., response efficacy), and popularity information results in imitation tendency. If the popularity information contradicts the user’s personal assessment, he or she may be inclined to discount the importance of personal assessment, place more emphasis on popularity information; consequently, imitate the majority (i.e., the herd). Ultimately, imitation tendency (with a strong effect) and personal assessment (with weaker effect) result in a decision that is influenced by herd behavior.

Despite having some conceptual overlap, herd behavior is distin guished from the concept of subjective norms in three important ways. First, subjective norms emanate from one’s reference group, consisting of those individuals who are important to them who are often a small group of known individuals, such as family members, co-workers, or close friends [46]. In contrast, herd members are typically unknown strangers.

Second, individuals influenced by subjective norms often want to please or satisfy the “important others” who exert that influence. For example, individuals care how the use of a recommended technology will influence their image in their own social circle [67]. In contrast, with herd behavior, the members of the large anonymous group will not know or care about the individual’s choice.

Finally, herd behavior and subjective norm differ in how information is acquired. Herd behavior is triggered by the observation of other people’s behavior (i.e., receiving popularity information), whereas subjective norm usually depends on messages received from significant others (Triandis 1980; [88]). That is, the subjective norm is often an espoused opinion of proper behavior, while herd behavior is an enacted behavior lacking any normative content. A more detailed and compre hensive elaboration of this comparison is provided by Sun [[85], p. 1017].

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

Based on the previous discussion, we propose a research model as depicted in Fig. 1. It conceptualizes the effect of herd behavior on in formation security decision making. Consistent with the herd theory, we argue that as a user’s uncertainty about a decision increases (defined as the inability to predict outcomes accurately because of incomplete or not fully understood information [79]), they are more likely to consider what other people are doing in their decision making; that is, they are more likely to discount their own assessment and emphasize what others have done instead of making decisions based on their own limited in formation. In the security context, it implies the user recognizes that because of high uncertainty, he or she cannot predict the effects of adopting or not adopting the advocated security technology. Perhaps the user lacks the necessary knowledge and skills to access the response and the consequences of adopting it. That is, he or she does not fully un derstand the advocated technology and how it will be used and thus cannot accurately assess its usefulness [85]. In such circumstances, a reasonable strategy could be to discount one’s own information and its interpretations as insufficient [6, 87]. Therefore:

H1: Users’ uncertainty about adopting a security technology positively influences them to discount their own information.

Discounting own information can increase the likelihood of users imitating the actions of others instead of making a decision solely based on their own information/beliefs. As one reduces the use of one's own information and opinions, where else is there to turn except to the ac tions of others? In circumstances when a user has discounted his or her own opinions, a reasonable strategy is to imitate the actions of others [6, 87]. Therefore, we argue that when a decision maker’s uncertainty in creases, imitation becomes a reasonable alternative strategy through discounting own information because users may believe that others have better and more complete information regarding that security technol ogy. Thus, we argue that:

H2: Discounting of own information positively influences users’ tendency to imitate the behavior of others.

A rational strategy for imitation is to adopt the behavior of others [11], which in this case, is to choose to adopt a popular security tech nology. Research in finance suggests that some investors imitate the investment decisions of professional investment managers to avoid being considered incapable if there are low returns on investments [76]. In fact, individuals may prefer the odds of being wrong, along with everybody else, to the risk of providing an atypical prediction that is the only correct forecast [34] and may find imitation as an efficient strategy to cope with uncertainty [27]. Likewise, in the information security context, the tendency to imitate others indicates that even when a se curity technology that is adopted by a herd is inefficient, this situation is more acceptable than the circumstance in which a user is the only one who makes the wrong decision not to adopt an efficient IT security technology and then suffers reputational damage. Therefore:

H3: Imitating others who have adopted a security technology positively influences behavioral intention.

Similar to the process in which users cognitively evaluate the efficacy of a response, they also assess the ability to execute the recommendation [99]. In this regard, self-efficacy is regarded as an element of intent concerning a recommendation to address a threat [47]. If users believe that a recommended response is effective, they still need to know whether they can successfully implement the solution. Even if they have high uncertainty, users need to have a certain level of self-efficacy to imitate others successfully. Furthermore, according to PMT, moderate to high levels of response efficacy perceptions are associated with the positive tendency to engage in protection from the threat by adopting the recommended technology [46]. Users evaluate the capabilities of such technology (based on own judgment) and form a positive percep tion of the recommended response. From this argument, we offer the following hypotheses:

H4: Self-efficacy positively influences behavioral intention.

H5: Response efficacy positively influences behavioral intention.

Discounting one’s own information means that users rely less on their own beliefs in making technology adoption decisions. It is reasonable to argue that the more discounting, the less important per sonal beliefs are in making such decisions, demonstrating a weak anchoring effect of these beliefs [85]. Therefore, discounting own in formation can negatively moderate the relationship between perceived response efficacy and adopting a security technology. Hence, we hy pothesize that:

H6: Discounting own information negatively moderates the relationship between response efficacy and behavioral intention.

Theoretically, receiving popularity information about a particular technology increases the likelihood of herd behavior ([11, 13]; Rao et al. 2001; [85]). For instance, Duan et al. [26] reported that Internet users choices of software fluctuated significantly when they observed the download rankings of the software products, indicating that users were likely to be influenced by the decisions made by previous adopters. Similarly, we believe that when users become aware that emerging se curity technology is rapidly becoming popular, their imitation tendency may significantly grow. Therefore, they may assume that the pre decessors have adequately analyzed all alternatives they have in front of them and then converged on the same decision. Thus, we hypothesize the following relationship:

H7: Receiving popularity information about a security technology posi tively influences imitation tendency.

Bandura (1977) argued that assessments of self-efficacy could be influenced by four main sources of information: (1) performance ac complishments, (2) vicarious experience, (3) verbal persuasion, and (4) physiological cues. In this study, we focus on the effects of vicarious experience. Vicarious experiences happen as people watch others perform a task and feel confident that they can complete the same task successfully with favorable outcomes [105]. Popularity information is a form of vicarious experience. Specifically, knowing that a large number of people are already using a security technology may influence people to conclude that many people have the self-efficacy to use it; otherwise, the adoption would have been limited to a low number of people with high self-efficacy [69, 98]. Thus, if many people have sufficient self-efficacy to use certain security technology, then they are more likely to feel confident that they have the needed self-efficacy. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H8: Receiving popularity information positively influences the perception of self-efficacy.

The observation of predecessors’ decisions may also affect a user’s personal perceptions. According to herd theory, users’ decisions when they are uncertain are based on a combination of limited personal in formation and herd behavior, which is based on observations of the actions or behaviors of others [11]. In other words, users are likely to adjust their own perceptions of security technology by inferring addi tional popularity information about it and, thus, may make security-related decisions through the synthesis of several factors. Based on this argument, we argue that receiving popularity information can positively affect their own limited perceptions. Specifically, such infor mation can influence individuals’ perceptions of response efficacy, which is one of the primary constructs of coping appraisal. Logically, if a person notices that security technology is being used by a vast number of users, he or she could infer this popularity as a quality signal [36]. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H9: Receiving popularity information positively influences the perceptions of response efficacy.

## 4. Method

We conducted an experiment to test the hypotheses described in the previous section. Descriptions of our participants, their experimental task, the popularity information treatment, and measures are presented in this section. The experimental procedures are described in Table 1.<sup>1</sup>

## 4.1. Participants

We recruited participants from Qualtrics, a professional panel of working adults in the United States with diverse educational and pro fessional backgrounds. Our technology of interest was a password manager, so we used a filter question so that only users with little experience and familiarity using password managers were qualified to participate in the experiment<sup>2</sup>. Participants were randomly assigned to either of the two treatments (control or popularity information group). After applying the qualifying filter and removing participants who failed the attention-check item (34 total), we recorded 290 usable responses from individuals with an average age of 43 years, including 209 female and 81 male participants. There were 135 responses from the control group participants and 155 responses from the popularity information group.

Task: The participants read a rather short narrative designed to encourage them to use a password manager (instead of following traditional password practices like devising and memorizing long, complex, and unique passwords for each important online account). The narrative provided information about the value and affordances of password managers and introduced Dashlane as an example. Then, participants in the treatment group received information about the increasing popularity of password managers among users. All partici pants then reported their intention to use the recommended password manager, which is a commercial password manager that has received high ratings.

A password manager was chosen as the focal security technology because there is still a high degree of uncertainty among IS users about this tool. Adoption of the password manager software is lower than other security technologies such as antivirus or backup software. Moreover, many IT users have a poor understanding of how password managers work. We asked more than 100 business undergraduate and graduate IS students at a large university in the U.S. whether they have ever used a password manager and less than 2% said they had used a password manager. Therefore, this IT artifact was a good fit in the context of our subject population because we could investigate whether providing popularity information influenced adoption.

## 5.2. Testing for common-method bias

Common-method bias (CMB) refers to the spurious variance that is attributable to the measurement method rather than to the constructs that the measures are assumed to represent. An equivalent definition is the systematic error variance shared among variables measured with and introduced as a function of the same method or source [73]. To reduce this bias, we used the procedural remedies, such as randomizing the order of items and ensuring participants of the survey anonymity before data collection, as well as a statistical remedy after the data collection. We used the common latent factor (CLF) technique, which is the use of a latent variable that has a direct relationship with the in dicators of each construct. The common-method factor represents vari ance across the constructs, which is caused by the potential method bias. This method is more effective than traditional methods such as Har man’s single factor in detecting common-method bias [77]. After per forming the confirmatory factor analysis, we checked the model fit. We then performed a chi-square difference test to determine the presence of bias by checking the model’s chi-square score and degrees of freedom. Next, we compared these results to the chi-square of the original model without the CLF. The degrees of freedom should be a difference of one degree because of the constrained relationships in the common-method test. The results showed no statistically significant change in chi-square after adding the CLF. Specifically, the original model’s chi-square, which was 377.340, changed to 374.820, the degree of freedom was reduced only by one point $( \mathrm { d f } = 1 9 2 ) ;$ , and the model fit statistics remained the same (CFI =.96, RMSEA = .05). Thus, there was no evidence of sub stantial common-method bias in this study.

## 6. Discussion

Within the IS context, “most of the interesting behaviors occur from the way humans physiologically process information and make de cisions based on that information.” ([35], p. 596). Correspondingly, decisions by individual users are critical to the security of information [21], and such decisions are influenced by various sources of informa tion. While IS security research has extensively studied the influence of purely rational factors (e.g., [14, 46]) and other factors such as habit [93] and rationalization [92] on security-related decision making, no study has empirically analyzed the impact of herd behavior on such decisions. Users often remain uncertain about the efficacy of security technologies, as they often lack the ability or motivation to truly un derstand how these technologies work. Therefore, this research examined how receiving popularity information about herd behavior influences security decisions. When users are informed about the popularity of security technology, they are more likely to imitate others. In such circumstances, individuals tend to discount their perceived response efficacy (i.e., personal assessment) as it becomes less influen tial in developing protection motivation. Discounting own information, coupled with receiving relevant popularity information, can trigger a chain of events that leads individuals to imitate the behavior of others. We proposed a conceptual model that tests the effect of herd mentality on secure behavior. As summarized in Table $^ { 6 , }$ our hypotheses were supported, and a high variance in behavioral intention was explained.

We observed that imitation is the most influential antecedent of behavioral intention by a large margin. The standardized path coeffi cient from imitation to protection was .627, whereas the same scores for the traditional secure behavior constructs such as self-efficacy and response efficacy were only .396 and .179, respectively. Therefore, imitation had a much more substantial impact than the users’ percep tions of the security threat or the advocated response. Similarly, our comparison of the partial model to the full model showed that including popularity information in decision making significantly and meaning fully increased the amount of explained variance in behavioral intention.

Furthermore, our findings showed that receiving popularity infor mation about a security technology positively influenced users’ herd behavior by increasing the imitation tendency. This effect was stronger than the effect of response efficacy and self-efficacy, indicating a strong influence of the experimental treatment on users’ herd behavior, which subsequently led to developing a higher level of protection motivation. These findings indicate the importance of examining the circumstances under which users make security-related decisions.

Another interesting finding of this study is the moderating role of discounting own information on the relationship between response ef ficacy (which is a users’ personal assessment of security technology or action) and behavioral intention. We hypothesized that as discounting increases, personal beliefs become less important, demonstrating a weak anchoring effect of these beliefs. Thus, discounting own information can negatively moderate the relationship between response efficacy and adopting a security technology (H6).

Overall, the current research shows that in the IS security context, users are likely to discount their own information and decide to follow the herd when they are uncertain about security technology. Specif ically, providing information about herd behavior increased the likeli hood that users would adopt the recommended security technology. This finding suggests that incorporating information about the number of users who have already adopted a security technology may be an additional tool to influence users. Our finding is also aligned with Xu & Warkentin [101] proposition that from the perspective of the elabora tion likelihood model, herd theory can explain how popularity cues and security argument persuasiveness impact users’ cognitive attitude and imitation tendency, subsequently affecting their protection-motivated behaviors. However, unlike Xu & Warkentin [101], the current paper includes a more extensive and modular conceptualization of herd behavior (e.g., perceived uncertainty, discounting own information, imitation tendency, and popularity information) and employs an experimental design to empirically test the proposed model by collecting and analyzing data.

## 6.1. Limitations and future research

The generalizability of our findings could be constrained by the limitations of our method. Field experiments lack the precision of a controlled laboratory experiment, though they place the participants in a more realistic environment [65]. Different research designs could reveal complementary findings on herd behavior in security contexts. Specifically, the temporal aspects of herd behavior dynamics should be explored in the information security context to determine the continu ance characteristics of herd mentality. Specifically, it is crucial to investigate whether herd behavior persists over time or if it influences only the initial use of security technologies. In the present study, the broad range of participants contributed to the generalizability of the results. Besides, the survey panelists were drawn from diverse de mographic backgrounds, which is common in behavioral security research [22]. However, the results obtained from our sample may differ from those obtained from a specific population. Salient sub-populations should be studied to surface findings of importance to our understanding of the decision process of key subgroups.

We examined individual adoption of a consumer security technology (a password manager), but herd behavior may also influence behavior in corporate settings. Likewise, we tested only the positive effects of popularity information. Would negative information about few adop tions also have an influence? Popularity information not only influenced behavior but also included concepts from herd behavior in our theo retical model improved the model’s ability to explain behavior, as evi denced by the large effect size of herd behavior on protection motivation. Little prior research has examined herd behavior in the adoption of security technology, yet information security is a complex area; therefore it may induce much uncertainty for non-technical users. We believe that our results should be supported by more security research that includes herd behavior so that we can better understand to what extent herd behavior influences security decision making.

Future studies should analyze the effects of different herd charac teristics on users’ decisions to employ security technologies. We pro vided popularity information about the herd (see Appendix A), but several characteristics of a herd might increase or decrease the effects of imitation and herd mentality in general. In the current study, the popularity information we provided mostly focused on the herd size, but would a larger herd have a greater impact, or is there a threshold beyond which the size of the herd would have no additional influence? Does the identity of the herd matter? For example, are herds that are primarily comprised of experts more likely to have more effect? Sun [85] found that herd identity did not matter in the context of using a wiki system, though it mattered in a more complex information security context.

Furthermore, to reduce the branding effect, the popularity infor mation we provided to the treatment group was focused on generic password managers. We recommended using password managers (as an efficacious security response) to a security threat and introduced Dashlane as an example. What if the popularity information about the recommended response pertains to a specific product? Likewise, what if the product was associated with a very well-known, moderately-known, or relatively unknown brand? Future research can investigate the effect of popularity information on security decisions by employing. For example, ${ \textbf { a 2 } } \times { \textbf { 3 } }$ factorial design to understand the extent to which brand familiarity can influence imitation-based protection motivation.

Another avenue for future research is that of individual differences. The effect of cognitive, personality-related, and situational variables in technology use context may be of great importance [41, 48, 66]. In the herd behavior context, some users may intentionally avoid joining a technology herd to maintain their independence or unique identity. Similarly, some individuals and organizations seek to differentiate themselves from others (especially from competitors) and avoid using a technology just because it is “too popular” in the industry [1]. Users may feel that adopting “fashionable” technology may make them look average [85]. This motivating factor may be especially prominent among individuals who have highly individualistic cultural values [40, 84]. In contrast, individuals who share collectivist values (which pro motes conformity) an emphasis on “being similar” may increase herd behavior. Therefore, an important future research question is what and how different cultures and personality traits impact users’ tendency to follow (or avoid) an IT herd.

To ensure parsimony and to manage the scope of this research, we focused only on the coping appraisal constructs of PMT. Future studies should study the effect of other PMT constructs such as response cost and maladaptive reward on herd behavior. Response cost, which is associ ated with taking adaptive coping response, may significantly affect an individual’s inclination to join or not join a herd [29]. Maladaptive re wards, defined as undesired behaviors intended only to decrease fear of a threat, as opposed to danger control [14], is another construct worth measuring. By using a multistage design, future research can analyze whether individuals recall their fear or perceptions of maladaptive re sponses after the completion of the study to investigate the effect of these rewards in highly uncertain circumstances. Consequently, a mal adaptive response could be either joining or avoiding a herd, based on the context.

Because we employed a cross-sectional research design, we could not investigate whether the intention to adopt Dashlane would translate into actual adoption over time, but we were able to measure the actual adoption by asking the participants whether they were willing to immediately adopt Dashlane by downloading a free version. In measuring the actual adoption behavior, we found that only a limited number of participants downloaded Dashlane (20 out of 290). This immediate adoption prompt must have significantly reduced the number of actual adopters. This situation is understandable because in most cases when an individual becomes familiar with specific security technology and is convinced of its efficacy, it normally takes a while until the actual adoption occurs naturally. Future research can longitudinally measure such actual behaviors by employing a multistage data collection design.

Even though our analysis indicated discriminant validity between imitation and behavioral intention (BI), there were two instances of cross-loadings between the items of imitation (IMI1 and IMI3) with behavioral intention items (Table C1). Arguably, imitation has consid erable conceptual overlap with behavioral intention. In this research, we regarded BI as a combination of both imitation tendency and users’ own assessments Hence. BI (i e, protection motivation) is influenced by both user’s own assessment of the security technology (i.e., response efficacy) and imitation tendency, which is the outcome of receiving popularity information. Like this study, other studies that measured both imitation and behavioral intention in the same model also established discrimi nant validity (e.g., [81, 82, 85]). Nevertheless, we believe that mea surement items of imitation (which we adapted from [85]) should be more distinct from the BI items because the imitation items are mostly geared toward behavior, rather than a tendency. For instance, the items such as “It seems that Dashlane is a widely-used password manager, there fore I would like to use it too.”, “I follow others in deciding to use Dashlane.” and “I would choose to use Dashlane because many others are already using it.” are essentially related to an intended behavior. This behavioral focus of the imitation items can be problematic, and we believe it is possibly the reason why there were two instances of cross-loadings between the items of imitations and behavioral intention. It should also be noted that imitation and BI could have a part-whole relationship instead of cause-effect and that imitative behavior can be conceptualized as a contextualized and special type of BI. In other words, arguing that a special type of BI can lead to BI is problematic. From a conceptual point of view, however, tendency and intention could be two distinct con cepts. If imitation is regarded as a tendency, we address an individual’s openness or disposition to a specific behavior. In the context of our study, this could be a person’s openness to incorporating popularity informa tion in his or her decisions. BI, on the other hand, primarily addresses the actual plan that this person has in trying to perform a behavior ([3]; p. 182) (in our case, adopting a security technology). Therefore, theo retically, this openness can lead to planning for the adoption of security technology.

Nonetheless, to improve the measurement validity of this construct, future research should follow rigorous scale development procedures to re-operationalize this construct by distinguishing tendency from behavior and develop items that are focused on imitation tendency instead of being worded directly toward a behavior. Drawing on rele vant key papers in reference disciplines is a good starting point to rec onceptualize, operationalize, and contextualize the construct of imitation tendency in the IS context (e.g., [17]; Jacob et al. 2010; Tanner et al. 2007).

## 6.2. Implications for practice

The results of this study indicate that individuals are influenced by the acts of others. Hence, both IT managers and software security ven dors should consider framing their communications (e.g., security training) to better inform users that a large number of other users are following recommended security procedures [83]. Consistent with the success of the so-called “nudges” used in behavioral economics appli cations, an employer may be able to increase the overall security of the organization by providing information about the behavior of others. For example, Barlow et al. [12] reported that a message providing infor mation about the compliance of others increased the likelihood that users would comply with security policies.

This research also suggests that fostering herd effects can enhance technology adoption [59]. A technology herd is unique in that early adopters determine the option upon which followers tend to converge. Early adopters (the herd leaders) are not necessarily similar to late adopters in terms of sophistication level, organizational tasks, etc. Therefore, to promote herd effects in using security technologies and complying with IT security policies, managers should emphasize the similarities between the early adopters and later adopters. Managers frequently imitate decisions made at other organizations, sometimes adopting best practices, “keeping up with the competition,” or converging on industry standards, including the adoption of new tech nologies, methods, and standards. They may simply think that their competitors’ choices of technology and systems are based on more quality information and awareness [59]. This behavior can lead to poor outcomes if early movers have erred and sent the wrong signal to the late movers (or followers). Managers must be careful not to blindly follow the herd of competitors in this case.

## 7. Conclusion

Theories in the current IS security literature have been grounded on conventional boundary conditions, which assume that users have suffi cient information to make a purely rational, informed decision regarding whether to adopt a recommended security technology or behavior. The present research contributes to IS behavioral security literature by investigating the effect of herd behavior on users’ coping with security threats. This research shows that when a decision maker is uncertain, they are more likely to discount their own information, and imitate others. Therefore, our study challenges the widely-held assumption in IS security literature that an individual computer user has the necessary personal information and expertise when assessing the efficacy of se curity technologies. This study advances the current understanding of users’ secure behavior by showing the significant effects of receiving popularity information and herd behavior on protection motivation and can be a basis for further analysis of users’ security behaviors under uncertain circumstances.

Definition: the degree to which one follows previous adopters in adopting a certain form of technology [85].
