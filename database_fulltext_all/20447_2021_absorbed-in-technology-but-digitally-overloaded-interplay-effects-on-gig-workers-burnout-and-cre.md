# Absorbed in technology but digitally overloaded: Interplay effects on gig workers’ burnout and creativity

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Gig work Creativity Cognitive absorption Technology overload Burnout

## A B S T R A C T

Technology has facilitated platform-mediated work, and individuals are often encouraged to engage with technology using various digital solutions. However, for platform workers, the use of technology can lead to digital overload, which may negatively impact their burnout and performance. In this paper, we examine the interplay between individuals’ cognitive absorption in technology and technology overload in predicting gig workers’ creative output. Using empirical data from 263 Amazon Mechanical Turk platform workers, we show that cognitive absorption positively predicts gig workers’ creativity. However, moderated-mediation analysi shows that in the case of high technology overload, cognitive absorption with technology leads to burnout and reduces creativity levels of the workers

## 1. Introduction

Digital transformation in modern workplaces and organizations has become a norm and expectation [146]. For example, employees in both digitalized and traditional work settings are increasingly required to be familiar with and use digital tools at workplaces [104]. In addition, employees often desire to adopt and use modern information technology (IT) to complete various tasks at work [103]. Continued use – typically conceptualized as an individual’s employment of technology features, learning, and exploration activities necessary for successful IT-supported task accomplishment [150] – and patterns of non-use, particularly in relation to people who adopt IT and then stop using it [172], are topics of increasing interest in the information systems (IS) literature. In organizational settings, digital skills have been shown to lead to several positive consequences for different employees and their organizations [111, 174], the use of collaborative technology in orga nizations is increasing [36, 163], and IT has been shown to enable innovation, growth, and new business development [115, 141, 165].

Understanding the nuances of the human-computer symbiosis (Jain, Padmanabhan, Pavlou, & Santanam, 2018) is even more important in the context of digital work for individuals performing digitally mediated work. The academic literature does not provide a clear consensus on how online outsourcing via Internet platforms should be developed [123, 167]. However, in this paper, online crowdsourcing work is referred to as gig work, and individuals who perform these digital tasks are referred to as gig workers [88]. These individuals are platform-mediated, though often self-employed and engage in either solving complex problems or performing menial routine tasks online [118].

Context-technology fit (cf., Vaidya & Seethamaran, 2011) becomes even more important in such settings. In particular, individuals’ atti tudes toward new IT significantly impact user acceptance and usage behavior [51, 87], as do the indices that represent IT usage behavior [4]. Individuals’ cognitive absorption in IT use represents individual atti tudes and behaviors toward information technologies [5], which importantly predict the extent of IT use [94, 161]. Furthermore, cognitive absorption is described as a state of deep engagement with technology [4]. The subjective experience of such individuals leads to positive attitudes toward greater exploratory use of IT [65], which has been shown to be beneficial for stimulating learning [136], using virtual communities [95] and active participation [130].

Based on these properties and results, cognitive absorption in IT could significantly predict individuals’ creativity in experimenting with new technologies [5]. Considering that creative cognition represents mental processes supporting idea generation [23], this study defines creativity as the generation of novel and potentially useful ideas [8], which is a critical stepping stone for individuals in their subsequent innovation performance, groups, and organizations [114]. In a non-digitized environment, cognition – defined as how individuals receive, process, accumulate, and use information – importantly predicts creative behavior [47, 93]. However, available research suggests that individuals who are part of digitally flexible, virtual, or crowd work may feel burdened by technology and less equipped with cognitive skills to cope with digital demands [1, 108]. Therefore, an important question remains unanswered – how to foster creativity among gig workers, as our understanding of their working conditions remains vague and limited [92, 100, 117, 140].

Despite the generally positive impact of technological advancements on economic and business indicators, the innovation imperatives com bined with increased demands in terms of productivity and competi tiveness have accelerated work to the point where many gig workers are overwhelmed. In the literature, this phenomenon is referred to as the dark side of IT use (Tarafdar, D’Arcy, Turel, & Gupta, 2015), which includes phenomena such as information overload [157]. In particular, we argue that such a “dark side” occurs when the encroaching digitali zation, i.e., technology’s intrusion into individuals’ work and lives [31], is too strong. This increases the cognitive IT overload of employees, making them feel overwhelmed and unable to cope with all the demands and encroachments that digitalization imposes on them [145], which in turn can lead to decreased creativity. Based on these arguments, we pose the following research question: Does IT overload act as a double-edged sword that prevents individuals on online platforms from being creative?

To answer this question, we apply the theoretical lens of the social cognitive perspective (Bandura, 1986). Consistent with this frame work, several internal and external stimuli influence individuals behavior, in our case gig workers’ technology use. Rapidly evolving technology requires learning and processing various pieces of informa tion at a rapid pace [126], while the social-cognitive perspective argues that personal (i.e., cognitive abilities) and environmental (e.g., the encroachment of digitalization) factors influence individual actions related to learning and creating [149].

Applying this theoretical lens, this study intends to contribute to the existing literature by bridging the IS literature on perceptions of adop tion and use of IT [112, 158] and the micro creativity/innovation management domains within the organizational behavior/psychology domains (e.g., [38, 52]). We study this by focusing specifically on the cognitive dimension of the adoption and use of IT, referred to as cognitive absorption (cf., [4, 15, 29, 136]) and highlighting the negative aspects of overwhelming digitalization when juxtaposed with digital workers’ innovation imperative, essentially exploring the “dark side” of innovation demands in tandem with IT [60, 117].

In this way, we first aim to extend existing research on the negative aspects of digitalization (see [25, 85, 135]). This stream of research has identified negative effects on people doing digitally mediated work, such as technostress, and emotional and cognitive overload. We take a step beyond these attitudinal factors and seek to conceptually and empirically assess digitalization’s impact on burnout – a state of emotional, physical, and mental exhaustion that excessive and pro longed stress causes [107, 121] – and, consequently, its impact on gig workers’ creative performance [85].

In doing so, we also aim to help advance existing research on digita work platforms [25]. We aim to do this by examining negative phe nomena that arise from digital requirements overload and can lead to burnout [102], particularly when individuals are cognitively absorbed with IT leading to fewer creative ideas. This issue represents a key issue when dealing with people who perform digitally mediated work to maintain their creativity in a humane manner [25, 46]. Comprehensive reviews on creativity generally distinguish two main types of creativity predictors: individual and contextual factors [143, 177]. Existing research on gig workers’ creativity either focuses on IT aspects of plat forms that enable collaboration and provide input for collaborative ideation and creation ([97]; Perjanen, Hennala & Konsti-Laakso, 2012), or examines online workers’ characteristics that foster creative perfor mance ([13, 83]; [169]). In the current paper, we focus on both personal and contextual factors that may determine gig workers’ creativity on digital labor platforms. Similarly, cognitive absorption has been asso ciated with IT/IS use (cf. [4, 89, 132, 136, 176]), which adds to the stream of research in IS examining the interplay between creativity and technology use (see [76]; Yeh, 2015), but not creativity in the context of platform workers.

By exploring the potential risks and consequences of digital tech nology use on creativity, we also aim to further inform the IS field that has explored creativity and IT/IS use. In particular, some researchers have argued that several conditions may play an important role in determining the relationship between creativity and digital technology use – cognitive absorption, information overload, and mental strain [34, 117, 136]. Although research indicates a possible link between the above conditions and creativity, the cumulative effects have not yet been investigated [117]. We add to these streams by applying a socio-cognitive perspective to examine how gig workers’ perceptions and attitudes toward technology use may predict their burnout and tangible creative performance. In what follows, we present hypotheses underlying our moderated-mediation research model, report results from a multi-source study of 263 gig workers, and discuss their implications.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Creativity and IT/IS

Creativity represents generating novel and useful ideas across a range of creative domains [58] and is an important driver of organiza tional innovation [43]. Creativity and IT are critical to organizational success [12, 24]. Numerous studies specify the relationship between these two areas. The IT/IS and management literature explores the way how various emerging technologies offer many specific benefits to the organization; however, in the context of creativity, three benefits seem to stand out: the improved ability to connect and empower employees, the improved ability to arrange the organization’s knowledge base, and the organization’s improved ability to transcend boundaries. IT connects employees horizontally, increases individual participation in social networks [45], and provides new space for personal initiative. Collaboration across levels and departments in the organization is necessary to stimulate creativity [59], suggesting that the ability of IT to overcome traditional communication patterns in hierarchies benefits creativity. Previous studies also suggest that IT enhances creativity by improving the domain-relevant knowledge that an employee can draw upon in problem solving and decision making [57]. IT may play an even greater role in employee creativity in the long run. In the absence of domain knowledge, individuals must quickly acquire relevant information. Taking a long-term view of the entire creative process from problem identification to an outcome, cumulative learning occurs over time among organizational members, which influences creative efforts [56]. IT plays an important role in these processes by providing employees with flexible access to internal and external information and facilitating problem solving, thus promoting learning (e.g., [73, 101]).

IS researchers have long recognized that employees’ use of IT does not always have a direct, positive effect on performance [37, 170]. In the field of organizational creativity, in which employee creativity is fragile and needs to be nurtured and protected, researchers have attempted to define the characteristics that either facilitate or constrain creativity (Ford, 1996; [117]). Muller and Ulrich [113] note that the creativity research in IS has improved in examining the social and technical factors that influence creativity in the IS context, but they call for additional explorations of the workplace and personal factors that influence employee creativity.

## 2.2. Creativity and gig work

Digital platforms are becoming increasingly popular [25], capital izing on the widely accepted notion of the “wisdom of crowds” that allows customers or businesses to obtain many different perspectives and solutions to challenges for which they seek ideas [153]. Indeed, the gig economy values creative performance [105], pushes the boundaries of creative thinking by allowing ambivalence in considering novel conceptual relationships [11, 62], and supports a variety of activities that include inventing and developing new products and services [22]. Digital platforms are a form of technological innovation [72] and are considered an important and growing source of organizational innova tion [26].

In terms of the process by which gig workers produce creative ideas, the existing literature shows that the rapid and complex cognitive assimilation of information required for their creativity is heavily dependent on their self-regulated creative processes and outcomes, though being exposed to a lack of external inputs [25, 78]. New labor market forms [88] challenge new ways of thinking, including an imperative for gig workers to generate various important creative out comes and move beyond the already established ways of performing their work [13, 142].

Studies suggest that computing devices can facilitate idea generation through networking and assistance from others in producing creative ideas [16, 32, 56]. Similarly, when responding to various task requests on the platforms, gig workers through technology have great access to knowledge and information, which can facilitate imagination, connect ideas from diverse sources, and foster novel and useful approaches to creative problem solving [32, 116, 128]. Diverse information derived from multiple sources can also help gig workers overcome potential “creative blocks” as barriers to their creative output [84, 152]. In addition to knowledge sharing, electronic communication tools enable interactions with people from different backgrounds, providing diverse expertise that can further facilitate the development of novel and useful ideas. Similarly, digitally mediated platforms enable employees to network and collaborate creatively, which can enhance creativity and ensure the quality of services provided [75].

Moreover, the gig economy is described as “non-standard employ ment” [81], which significantly changes many aspects of creative work [138]. This new approach to work is attractive because it opens up new creative possibilities for workers in terms of autonomy and flexible working hours [171] by giving them the freedom to accept or reject job offers [20]. However, engaging on digital work platforms poses certain risks that gig workers often experience [25]. For example, individuals in the digitally distributed labor setting (Wood, Graham, Lehdonvirta, & Hjorth, 2018) have to continuously convince their clients to hire them for their services [105], while often lacking social incentives in the form of performance feedback on their creative output [166]. Similarly, the excessive availability of information can lead to an inability to integrate new ideas due to multiple information overloads, resulting in higher stress and thus lower creative performance [34, 98]. Furthermore, although flexible working arrangements can contribute to gig workers freedom and creativity, such non-stop connectivity possibly increases one’s level of stress and burnout, leading to lower creativity [117].

Taken together, both the domains of creativity in gig work and the IS field highlight a crucial role of social context in evaluating, integrating, and implementing gig workers’ creative ideas and show how IT plays an important role in raising awareness of the information required by the creative process. Consistent with our overarching theoretical approach – the social-cognitive perspective (Bandura, 1986) – we restrict our focus to gig workers’ technology use as a medium for learning, processing information, and creating, in combination with personal factors (i.e., cognitive absorption) and contextual factors (i.e., IT overload) [149]. Keeping this in mind, we examine intertwined processes of gig workers technology use and their creative performance impacted by cognitive and external factors, cognitive absorption in technology, and the

growing burden of technology use.

## 2.3. The role of cognitive absorption in technology

Cognitive absorption is an individual experience while using digital technologies [136]. Cognitive absorption, a state of individual deep involvement with information technology, is derived from the concepts of flow – a state in which individuals are so involved in the task that nothing else matters [48] – cognitive engagement [168], and intrinsic motivation underlying one’s beliefs about using IT [4]. Building on these foundations, Agarwal and Karahanna [4] identify temporal dissociation or the inability to register the passage of time while interacting with technology; focused immersion or the experience of absolute engage ment when ignoring other potential demands; and enhanced pleasure, which describes the pleasurable qualities of interacting with technology, as different dimensions of the cognitive absorption construct.

IS research has already identified cognitive absorption as an important factor influencing employee engagement with technology [4]. Studies have shown IS research to be an important predictor of learning in a variety of contexts related to new IT [5], such as online virtual communities [39, 95]. Highly skilled workers who engage with digital platforms must mentally engage in creative outcomes, take risks, try new things, explore the boundaries of IT, and figure out what they can achieve once they use them [142]. In this sense, intrinsic motivation plays a key role in relation to individuals’ interest and engagement in the creative task [94, 96]. Intrinsic motivation is linked to creativity [63, 178], as people are more likely to generate truly creative ideas when inspired by their interest, satisfaction, enjoyment, and the challenge of the work [9]. Characteristically, this is a flow experience that leads to increased learning, an exploratory mindset, and better implementation of creative ideas [21]. Intrinsic interest and immersion in the work and technology could thus be an important factor in the necessary human-computer interaction for gig workers to turn an initial idea into an actual creative output.

Similarly, cognitive engagement relates to the state of playfulness [168], which supports the notion of constant change and uncertainty in the gig work world [13]. Playfulness is associated with learning and imaginative engagement in creative thinking [64]. Some of the intrinsic reasons why people choose gig work are the desire to have fun, to participate in challenging problem solving, and to develop creative skills [30]. Cognitive absorption in IT may be particularly important for gig workers who work remotely and do not have face-to-face communica tion available, but their expertise depends on networks and online learning that can further stimulate their creative initiative [110]. Similarly, the use of technology and the Internet can provide gig workers with the most interesting access to information where new ideas can emerge [91], and thus increase creativity. Therefore, we expect that sincere and intense engagement in technology use will enhance infor mation flow experiences, intrinsic motivation, and cognitive engage ment (from which cognitive absorption is derived) in creative thinking. By this rationale, we hypothesize the following:

H1: Cognitive absorption with IT is positively related to gig workers creativity.

## 2.4. Technological overload: Key boundary condition leading to gig workers’ burnout and decreased creativity

Despite their widely recognized benefits, informationcommunication tools also bring challenges [44]. Employees’ reluc tance to adapt to digitized work is well recognized in the digitalization versus humanization debate [13]. Digital transformation has enabled gig workers to control when, where, and on what they work, and thus (theoretically) better balance their work with their personal lives (Schmid-Druner. 2016). However, few studies confirm that workers are aware of their trading security and benefits for flexibility [7, 33]. Indeed, while the autonomy associated with gig work may be attractive, research shows that too much freedom and choice is associated with negative consequences, such as cognitive overload and distress [74, 102]. Compared to traditional workers, gig workers tend to experience a wider range of emotions, from joy to stress and anxiety, which gig work characteristics mainly cause, such as uncertainty, frequent commuting, and financial insecurity [122]. Moreover, competition between gig workers forces people to work very long hours and give up a high degree of flexibility to earn money [55]. As a result, there are increasing con cerns about gig workers’ health in the workplace [139].

Being immersed in technology for hours at a time can result in developing distractibility, difficulty concentrating, and reduced learning abilities [32]. In addition, the inability to limit time spent online can contribute to chronic stress and burnout over time [66];[144]. A review of the IS research on job burnout shows that gig work draws heavily on general organizational behavior theory (on parsimonious models that can be generalized across a variety of occupations and social contexts, such as online platforms). Most of the IS research on burnout follows the global approach and seeks to examine generic stressors, symptoms, and outcomes of burnout in the IT context [6, 86, 144]. Scholars have advocated supplementing the general theory of organizational behavior with an occupation-specific approach that incorporates factors and concerns specific to an occupation/work environment to increase explanatory power and relevance [120], such as digital work platforms.

In the specific online platforms’ work environment, gig workers often need to challenge their cognitive skills to persist and thrive [13]. As suggested, one of these personal skills is referred to as cognitive ab sorption, a state of deep involvement that gig workers experience with IT [4]. However, the rapidly changing demands on digital work plat forms require too many different IT solutions or work tools via tech nology from gig workers, leading to higher technology dependency and acceleration of data entry for processing, which is referred to a techno-overload [127, 145].

In IS research, technology overload has generally been associated with a range of undesirable workplace outcomes, such as higher stress levels, burnouts, work interruptions, and lower productivity and crea tive thinking ([2], 2018; Chandra et al., 2019; [41, 82, 127, 155]). Mahapatra and Pati [99] and Yu et al. [175] confirmed the positive relationship between technology overload and burnout. For online platform workers, technology overload may be particularly present as workers nowadays have constant and continuous access to communi cation and IS (e.g., email, messaging services, platform accessibility via apps, etc.). This connectivity can lead to a perception of extra work, “on-call” during non-working hours, and a general sense of technology-induced work overload [67]. Cao and Sun [35] suggest that excessive technology use can lead to overload, which can have negative psychological (i.e., exhaustion) and behavioral (i.e., IS discontinuance) consequences. Techno-overload decreases work productivity [162], in creases work-life imbalance [54], leads to various health problems such as addiction, anxiety, and sleep disorders [79], as well as increases distraction [133]. This is particularly pronounced in the digital work environment, where work outsourced through platforms has led to the development of the 24-hour economy across different time zones [25]. The constant accessibility of such platforms indirectly increases the length of one’s working hours [19], which increases feelings of overload [50] and escalates stress and cognitive burnout [18]. Specifically, we expect that greater work required in using IT (i.e., techno-overload) combined with gig workers’ experience of fully engaging with this IT (i.e., cognitive absorption) will lead to cognitive overload and burnout. Furthermore, we propose that these overload consequences will also lead to negative outcomes for gig workers, particularly in terms of their creativity.

Thatcher et al. [159], Tarafdar et al. [156], Addas and Pinsonneault [2], Haug et al. [68], and Cochis et al. [42] provide evidence of the negative effects of technology overload on employee creativity, such as the aforementioned arguments that lead to experienced cognitive overload and burnout. New technologies force employees to do more work and faster [156, 159], and individuals feel the need to connect in order to be reachable anytime, anywhere [42, 68]. Similarly, the ongoing changes that technological advancements impose mean that people must constantly spend time and effort updating their skills or seeking technical help when faced with a problem that may require a creative solution [2].

The above arguments may be even more relevant in the context of digital work platforms where skilled gig work is required in various crowdsourcing micro-tasks [154], where the compulsion to be creative can cause workers to have high stress levels [63]. Stressors can nega tively impact the ability to think and integrate information from mul tiple sources, which additionally hampers the gig worker’s engagement in the creative process. Finally, as outlined in the introduction, the available literature speculates on possible negative relationships be tween stressors and creativity [34, 117], but does not conclusively test the cumulative effects of their interaction with technology-related fac tors. Moreover, these findings come from the traditional work context, not digital platform-mediated work. Therefore, we propose:

H2: Technology overload among gig workers moderates the strength of the relationship between cognitive absorption and creativity, medi ated by burnout in such a way that the mediated relationship is stronger when respondents’ technology overload is high.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. Sample and data collection

Working professionals (n = 263) were recruited via Amazon Me chanical Turk (MTurk; a crowd working platform). All subjects gave written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Hel sinki. Ethics approval was not required for our study according to our institution’s guidelines and national regulations because the current research did not involve vulnerable groups; it focused on employee behavior, which is purely observational (non-invasive and noninteractive); the resulting data is completely anonymous; it is not possible to identify participants from the resulting reports, and the use of the data (or the data collection) could not harm or cause distress to the participants.

The mandatory requirement for study participation was that the participants be employed and have a Master Worker status on MTurk. Of the respondents, 68.1% were male and about 53.2% were under 35 years of age. The assignment time took approximately 20 minutes to complete. The majority of participants had acquired a bachelor’s degree (58.6%), and most of them were from the U.S. (47.9%) with more than 13 years of work experience (34.6%). They were from the following countries: United States (48%, with 0.4% from Alaska), India (47%), Canada (0.8%), Turkey (0.8%), Ireland (0.4%), and Australia (0.4%). They primarily worked in the following work domains outside MTurk: tech nology (44.1%), finance (13.7%), education (10.6%), service (9.9%), health (6.8%), and government (3.4%). On average, they had 34 months of MTurk tenure (SD = 23) and spent 20 hours a week (SD = 15.48) doing crowdsourcing work (via MTurk or other platforms – on average they engaged with three crowdsourcing platforms for task assignments).

## 3.2. Measures

We used a structured questionnaire with 5-point Likert-type scales with the anchors of “1 = strongly disagree” and “5 = strongly agree” to measure all of the constructs in this study, aside from the controlled variables. To alleviate potential concerns related to applying a Likert type online questionnaire, we applied several checks and balances. We included a control question (i.e., “Please choose response ‘4’ here”) and omitted responses that failed to respond correctly; we examined re sponses for potential flat-lining; and examined the amount of time each respondent took for answering, omitting those that took less than ten minutes. The complete list of items about our focal constructs in the model can be found in the Appendix. It includes final items adapted from original sources for our studied context.

## 3.3. Design and procedure

The online questionnaire notified participants that the research aimed to generate ideas for a new platform; a worker-friendly envi ronment alternative to the currently available platforms.

The first part of the survey included the assigned task to assess the participants’ creativity, which was constructed following the logic that Ritter, Van Baaren, and Dijksterhuis [131] applied, along with the following instructions:

“You are tasked to design a framework to improve the platform for workers. You need to come up with novel and potentially useful ideas.

Using the text below, please take at least 5 minutes to think carefully and write down a list of ideas for building a great platform for ONLINE (tailored to the participants, such as MTurker) workers.”

The second part of the survey included cognitive absorption, tech nological overload, burnout, and demographic information. Measured cognitive absorption referred to the computerized task of generating novel and useful ideas that we put forth for the participants (crowd workers), whereas technological overload and burnout referred to the crowd workers’ general perception of their work on MTurk. Each in dividual’s creative output was rated for novelty and usefulness on a scale of 1 = not at all novel/useful to 5 = very novel/useful by two inde pendent raters who were blind to the purpose of the study. One rater had ten years of professional experience in creativity and innovation man agement, and the other had a Ph.D. in organizational psychology and conducted research in the creativity field. In general, suggestions for improving the platform fall into the following categories: pay (e.g., higher compensation, Bitcoin), non-monetary rewards (e.g., promotion, skill levels), software (e.g., mobile-friendly), platform reputation (e.g., efforts to increase trust, social media marketing), user interface/ graphics (e.g., holograms, colors), and social collaboration (e.g., chat boxes, video conferencing with the crowd).

An example of an idea rated high in novelty included:

1) Examine each person’s strengths; 2) see who works well together; 3) get feedback from the workers; 4) set realistic goals; 5) create a strategy to complete those; 6) highlight strong and weak areas; 7) assess what areas need more direction or experience; 8) listen to workers’ in sights and concerns; 9) treat all workers with mutual respect; 10) create an atmosphere of teamwork; 11) ensure that each worker feels heard and respected – happy workers are more productive, and 12) develop an endpoint that is both strong and realistic.

Whereas ideas high in usefulness included:

Having a worker auditing department to routinely inspect worker quality, giving tests to workers to screen for specific types of work, paying workers an hourly wage instead of by assignment, workers being able to communicate with people from both the platform and the customer, transparency in operating practices, low fees to attract higher volumes of business, some sort of loyalty or perk program for high performers, clean interface, hiring workers as employees instead of passing off work as self-employed, using the platform to test the boundaries of digital work (producing its own projects if/when needed to bolster platform), and pitching platform jobs (digitizing public/gov ernment records, flagging social media, etc.).

An example of an idea rated low in novelty included: “Opportunity is very important,” whereas an example of an idea low in usefulness included: “Safety makes for a good process and facilities should be provided.”

## 4. Results

The means and standard deviations of the focal variables and the correlations among them are presented in Table 1. Next, we observed the factor structure of the focal variables using confirmatory factor analysis procedures in AMOS software version 21. The expected four factor solution (cognitive absorption, burnout, technology overload, and creativity) displayed a good fit with the data [chi-square (265) = 408,912; CFI = .947; SRMR = .0777; RMSEA = .046]. The standardized factor loadings ranged from .15 to .82 for cognitive absorption items, from .73 to .83 for technology overload items, from .70 to .82 for burnout items, and from .91 to .93 for creativity items.

## 4.1. Hypotheses testing

To test our hypotheses, we used the PROCESS macro [70], which applies bootstrapping – a non-parametric test that does not violate as sumptions of normality. It repeatedly involves randomly sampling ob servations with replacement from the data set to compute the desired statistic in each resample. Computing over hundreds or thousands of bootstrap resamples, provides an approximation of the sampling distri bution of the statistic of interest. The PROCESS macro provides point estimates and confidence intervals by which one can assess the signifi cance or non-significance of a mediation (or moderated mediation) ef fect. An alternative approach could apply stepwise regressions and only testing the mediation and moderation separately, which was done as part of the moderated-mediation analysis anyway, or structural equation modeling – which in the case of single items (such as our dependent variable) does not hold much additional value above PROCESS and is not as concise in testing moderated-mediation hypotheses (for a comparative discussion, see [69]).

We first examined the mediation of burnout between cognitive ab sorption and creativity. We followed standard procedures to examine this mediation using a bootstrap approach [125]. Drawing 5,000 random samples using replacement from the full sample. we constructed 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals for the hypothesized indirect mediating effects. The direct effect of cognitive absorption of IT on creativity, before the inclusion of mediator, was significant $( \mathbf { b } = . 1 4 , p <$ .01), supporting hypothesis 1, as shown in Table 2. Next, the moderated mediation Hypothesis 2 was expected to show that technology overload moderates the relationship between cognitive absorption and creativity, mediated by burnout. All steps of the moderated mediation are also shown in Table 2. ## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

Our research contributes to the existing literature by adding another “stone” to the bridge of IS literature with individual fields of creativity and innovation. Many of the claims the organizational psychology/ behavior areas of creativity research make in the contexts of “tradi tional” organizations may hold good in the digital environment as well; however, important nuances need to be considered, and studies con ducted to show the generalizability of these findings. To capture in dividuals’ responses to IT, the IS literature has considered the importance of individuals’ perceptions of the adoption and use of IT as well as technology/systems features that influence the creative process. By focusing on creativity as an increasingly important outcome of working in a digital or traditional environment (cf., [71, 124]), our findings add to the IS literature by answering Muller and Ulrich’s (2013) call to additionally examine workplace and personal factors that influ ence employee creativity. We specifically focus on the cognitive dimension of IT adoption and use (cf., [80, 136]), and show that gig workers' cognitive absorption with IT contributes to the quality of their creative work. This finding goes hand in hand with findings on flow and the state of optimal experience, which has similar characteristics to cognitive absorption, where demands and resources or stresses and as sets are perfectly balanced to create an environment conducive to creativity (cf., Csikszentmihaly, 1997; [147]). Because these studies were conducted in traditional organizational settings and experimental laboratories. respectively. the contribution of our study in this sense is both empirical and contextual, in that we extend these findings to the milieu of digital, platform-mediated work. Our work thus extends pre vious studies on the effects of cognitive absorption to examine its impact on employee creativity in the platform workers’ context. This also complements research streams in IS that examine the interplay between creativity and technology use (e.g. [76]; Yeh, 2015), and the relationship between cognitive absorption and job performance [40].

Our study has not only captured the benefits of IT for creative work but also highlighted the negative aspects of exuberant digitalization when juxtaposed with digital workers’ innovation imperative. This approach has allowed us to tap into the “dark side” of innovation im peratives (cf., [27, 63, 134]) and helped extend this line of research by exploring its interplay with IT. Although research has speculated on the side effects of information overload from digital technologies and stress on creativity, the negative relationships between these conditions remain untested and empirically examined [117]. Thus, we add to the existing research on the negative aspects of digitalization (cf., [28, 85]) in terms of its effects on people doing digitally mediated work also. Being absorbed with IT contributes to individuals’ output in terms of creativity. However, technology overload that generates technostress (cf., [155]), combined with its cognitive absorption (both at low and high levels), leads to burnout and individuals’ reduced creativity. Therefore, a cognitively overwhelming design of digital work leads not only to a detrimental effect on individuals’ stress and burnout at work, but also a significant decrease in output (in the form of creativity) for the platform or end customers.

We also contribute specifically to the literature on digital and gig work. We have shown that a key issue in managing individuals doing digitally mediated work is not to overload gig workers with digital re quirements, even when individuals are cognitively absorbed in IT. Our study thus further challenges existing gig work research with our finding since the technological nature of the platform and work setting is important. Our findings highlight technological strain and overload as key components of the platform context that is detrimental to promoting the positive outcomes of gig work; their creative output. This comple ments studies on conducive factors for digital platform work, such as beyond autonomy [171], relational and emotional sustainability [13], feedback and learning orientation (Wong et al., in press), and platform workers’ intrinsic and extrinsic motivational factors (Rockmann, Jacob & George, 2018). Beyond the technological elements of platforms and the job design characteristics of gig workers, individuals’ cognitive ca pacities to handle technology overload on digital labor platforms should be crucially considered in the research and practice of algorithmic sys tems, platform work, and the gig economy.

## 5.2. Practical implication

Generally speaking, to achieve more creativity in digital work, we need to ensure that digital workers are absorbed in technology. There fore, platforms and digital work environments should design the IT infrastructure such that individuals can achieve the state of optimal experience in terms of cognitive absorption in their task and the tech nology, thereby unleashing their creativity. IS designers should continue to focus on richer and more engaging user interfaces so that the resulting system is efficient, effective, and supports experiences that are intrin sically motivating, i.e., pleasant (Agarwal & Karahhana, 2000; [77]). A congenial organizational environment should be created that encour ages exploration, experimentation, and exploitation of new technologies and creative ideas, whether in the context of traditional organizations or online platforms. This will allow individuals to immerse themselves in and enjoy their creative tasks, leading to superior creative outcomes while providing an enjoyable experience for the gig worker.

Additionally, digital labor platforms should consider how to improve and manage the platform-mediated community by making it a better place to work at. We showed that, in general, cognitive absorption in technology use can contribute toward gig workers’ creative perfor mance. However, when they become immersed in technology too much, due to cognitive information overload, gig workers experience stress and burnout, leading to reduced creativity. Given that in recent years liter ature is suggesting a novel and exciting stream of research linking creativity and artificial intelligence [10, 109], one of the possible ways to prevent gig workers from burning out would be to use machine learning and AI benefits in tracking one’s job satisfaction and happiness level at work [129]. In such a way, digital platforms to some extent could prevent burnout and maintain a healthy platform-mediated community.

As digital and social technologies advance, organizational processes and the nature and meaning of work evolve, making work increasingly granular, modular, and decontextualized [14, 151]. It is important to also recognize the downsides of increasing technology use for focused work, close relationships, and effective collaboration in order to examine the formative conditions and effects of increasing technology use by a digital workforce, and also to guide how technology can be best used in service of organizational goals [44]. An important implication is that we should also be humane and not overburden gig workers when performing digitally mediated work. The current study also notes that technological overload puts gig workers on the “dark side of technology use,” contributing to their burnout, thereby reducing their creativity even as they become absorbed in technology. This applies to various technological options and solutions in terms of the availability of tech nological features associated with platforms, limiting the amount of work they can do on platforms, providing individuals with not just an opportunity but also a requirement to disengage from digital encroachment and be able to take time away from the potentially un consciously overwhelming platform work.

## 5.3. Limitations with future research directions

This study is not without limitations. Because it is a cross-sectional study, we cannot infer causality for the relationships we tested, despite using data from multiple sources. Reverse causality in the hy pothesized associations, although lacking a theoretical foundation, is therefore plausible. Thus, although the scale and practical limitations (e. g., cost, the difficulty of linking responses across multiple time points) associated with data collection via MTurk prevented us from doing so, further experimental or longitudinal studies should be conducted to test the links’ true direction between cognitive technology absorption and creativity. In particular, it would be interesting to investigate how to achieve an optimal balance between cognitive absorption and technol ogy overload so that one does not tip too far into the other. Alternatively, exploring the proposed relationships in the context of more or less digitized settings in “traditional” organizations with less flexible ex changes between workers and managers/owners also represents an interesting avenue for further study, as IT research in the context of organizational behavior is still in its infancy [13, 61, 148].

Future research should also examine individual differences of gig workers in more detail. While our central findings held up even when including several individual differences as control variables (e.g., additional analyses that included the individuals’ two different creative thinking styles – experimental versus conceptual, and their task-related optimism did not yield conflicting results in terms of statistical signifi cance tests and directions of hypothesized relationships), further studies should not continue to mistakenly view gig workers as an amorphous set of interchangeable, cheap labor rather than a community of skilled and valuable individuals. Each digital worker is unique, and their specific individual desires, values, and motivational factors should be consid ered when researching their work. Deng et al.’s concept [53] of value-sensitive design of gig worker research, as well as platform set tings, could be valuable, especially by connecting it to digital workers character and creativity. Thus, there must be further research to un derstand gig workers, their characteristics, and digital contexts to ensure not only optimal gains in terms of their performance but also to design their tasks and work environments in a humane way.

## 6. Conclusion

A work environment’s design is an essential aspect of IT platform mediated work. This study explored how to make digitized work more humane by examining the antecedents of gig workers’ creativity and highlighting its connection to cognitive absorption with IT and its boundary conditions. Drawing upon the IT/IS, creativity, digital work, and gig work literature, we developed research hypotheses and a con ceptual framework that demonstrated how, in the context of microwork platforms, technology overload can prevent cognitive absorption from contributing to gig workers’ creativity, thereby leading to their burnout and diminished creative output. We hope this study will spark future attempts to elaborate on our findings.
