## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Perceived information integration Perceived fulfillment integration Showrooming motivation IS success model Cue congruence theory Response surface methodology

## A B S T R A C T

Although omni-channel retailers have made substantial investments in online-offline channel integration, they face the challenge of how to best present these valuable systems to retain customers. Based on cue congruence theory, this study examined the congruence and incongruence between perceived information integration and fulfillment integration on customer retention, and further considered the moderating role of showrooming motivation. Results showed that incongruence was negatively related to customer retention, whereas congruence had a nonlinear effect. Additionally, an asymmetrical incongruence effect was observed, and showrooming motivation was found to amplify the influence of congruence and incongruence. Theoretical and practical im plications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid development of new technologies, various channels such as websites, mobile apps, physical stores, and internet kiosks are being integrated to retain customers through omni-channel businesses [1, 2]. A hybrid commerce service delivery system known as online-offline channel integration (OOCI) has thus emerged as a competitive necessity for those working in omni-channel businesses [3, 4]. Although retailers have made substantial investments in advancing the OOCI [5], neglecting the presentation of OOCI can lead customers to detect limited cues and thereby abandon using the system. Information systems (IS) research has long recognized the importance of systems information presentation because users rely on detected cues to evaluate a system’s quality and make decisions [6, 7]. Thus, customers’ acqui sition and reaction to OOCI cues are the determining factors in the success of an omni-channel business [5, 8]. Users’ attention, however, is limited when processing cues within a system [9–11]. As it is difficult for consumers to observe all OOCI cues, retailers face the challenge of effectively prioritizing various OOCI cues to retain customers.

The IS success model emphasizes the importance of both information and service quality in the transmission of IS value to end-users (Xu et al., 2013). This concept has been explored in the literature on OOCI, wherein, high-quality information and convenient service have been shown to constitute retailers’ OOCI value propositions in the co-creation of value with customers [3, 12, 13]. Accordingly, perceived information integration (PII) and perceived fulfillment integration (PFI) have been identified as the detected cues used to access OOCI value [14, 15]. The former indicates customers’ perceptions about a retailer’s provision of integrated access to and information about offline and online channels, while the latter reflects customers’ perceptions about a retailer’s offer of integrated service support in offline and online channels [16]. Though extant literature has demonstrated the independent effect of PII and PFI on customer attitudes and decisions [15, 17–19], the fit between them is under investigated. IS scholars have realized the importance of balancing evaluation toward systems’ various components because each system interacts with users as a whole, and system weaknesses devalue their merit [7, 20–23]. Specifically, the fit between PII and PFI reflects the fact that customers can experience continuity of information and services when using OOCI, which in turn determines the success of omni-channel businesses [15, 24, 25]. The misfit between PII and PFI can cause customers to abandon OOCI use as it cannot effectively serve them in the whole process [26]. In such cases, it is crucial to empirically investigate the influence of PII and PFI congruence (i.e., whether PII and PFI are at equally high or low levels simultaneously), and their incon gruence (i.e., the degree of discrepancy between PII and PFI) on customer retention.

Cue congruence theory has been widely used to understand how individuals react to the congruence and incongruence of multiple cues. This theory suggests that, when detected cues are consistent, they reinforce each other and enhance individual decision-making [27, 28]. In contrast, the inconsistency makes individuals focus on either the discrepancy or the negative cue, thereby negatively impacting the decision-making process [29]. Based on cue congruence theory, this study argues that congruence between PII and PFI can improve customer retention, while incongruence between PII and PFI can detrimentally affect customer retention.

IS scholars have further demonstrated that users’ characteristics play an important role in information system evaluation [6, 7]. Specifically, users’ motivations influence how they process a system’s cues for making judgments [30]. In omni-channel businesses, the proliferation of mobile devices engenders customers’ motivation to leverage them in transferring between online and offline channels [31]. Similarly, showrooming reflects customers’ motivation to search on mobile de vices while shopping in physical stores [1, 32]. Customers’ showroom ing has been considered a contingency factor that can influence their perceptions of and reactions to OOCI [2, 33]. In particular, customers with high showrooming motivation systematically processed OOCI cues as they cared about the transmitted value, whereas customers with low showrooming motivation spent little cognitive effort trying to under stand OOCI cues [30]. Yet, despite the acknowledged importance of customer showrooming in omni-channel businesses [34, 35], limited studies have attempted to understand how customers with showrooming motivation react to the balanced and imbalanced presentation patterns of OOCI. This study thus addresses the call of Verhoef et al. [1] to examine how showrooming motivation regulates the impact of congruence and incongruence between PII and PFI.

This study used IS success model as a general framework and applied it to the context of omni-channel businesses to investigate the effect of OOCI’s information and service quality on customer retention decisions. We specifically used cue congruence theory to account for the congru ence and incongruence between PII and PFI. A heuristic-systematic model was then used to explain how showrooming motivation regu lated these relationships. Based on cue congruence theory, we proposed that incongruence between PII and PFI stemmed from the reception of inconsistent cues for evaluating OOCI information and service quality. This would confuse consumers or lead them to overemphasize negative cues, which negatively influence retention decisions. And that congru ence between PII and PFI at a simultaneously high level would result in a positive judgment of OOCI’s quality and thus enhance customer reten tion decisions. We used the heuristic-systematic model to argue that showrooming motivation reflects a customer’s desire for OOCI. As such, it was predicted that customers with high showrooming motivation would systematically process OOCI cues and deliberately evaluate the consequences of congruence and incongruence between them, while others would apply a heuristic processing approach and pay limited attention to the relationship between PII and PFI. Therefore, this study contends that showrooming motivation not only amplifies the positive role of congruence between PII and PFI but also strengthens their negative incongruence effect. This study further compares the nuanced effect of two cue incongruence conditions (i.e., PII is higher or lower than PFI) and argues that customer retention is lower when PII is lower than PFI.

This study makes the following contributions to the extant literature on OOCI. First, extant research has only focused on the direct effects of PII and PFI, hence, the present study enhances this line of research by examining the various congruence effects of PII and PFI, and identifying differing outcomes generated in two incongruence scenarios (i.e., when PII is either higher or lower than PFI). Second, as showrooming moti vation has been increasingly emphasized in the omni-channel businesse context, this study extends existing studies by testing the moderating role of showrooming motivation in OOCI’s congruence and incongru ence effects. Third, this study extends cue congruence theory by considering the boundary condition of individual motivation and empirically examining it in the omni-channel businesses context. Finally, this study provides actionable practical implications for omnichannel retailers about how to better present OOCI.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. OOCI and IS success model

OOCI, a hybrid commerce service delivery system, is often a precondition for transforming omni-channel businesses to meet cus tomers’ needs for a consistent and seamless shopping experience [3, 4]. OOCI is effective for this because it provides an integrated conduit for consumers to interact with information, human resources, and technical resources [3]. For instance, integrating online and offline channel prices and warehouse management systems allows customers to obtain offline information (i.e., price, inventory level, and promotion activity) on the website. It also provides internet kiosks for acquiring website informa tion and advertises retailer websites in physical stores. Moreover, through the integrated payment and billing system, it allows customers to make payments, obtain products, and return or change purchased products through their preferred channels.

Importantly, customers’ perceptions of OOCI can determine the success of an omni-channel business [5]. In particular, the updated IS success model suggests that system quality, information quality, and service quality are key factors in consumers’ evaluations of these sys tems [12]. System quality has been defined as a user’s evaluation of the system’s technical capabilities and usability; information quality cap tures the content provided by the system, and service quality reflects a consumer’s perception of the overall support delivered by a service provider [12]. However, scholars have demonstrated that system quality is already considered in consumer evaluations of both information and service quality because problems with system quality will directly degrade the quality of information presented and services delivered [36]. Extant literature has further emphasized that OOCI evaluation can be classified from the perspective of information provision and service fulfillment [3, 13]. As an individual’s perception toward information systems is a proxy way to conceptualize IT artifacts [37], this study tends to understand the OOCI from the dimension of perceived online-offline information integration and perceived online-offline fulfillment inte gration [38]. The former reflects the evaluation of OOCI's information quality regarding the extent to which retailers provide integrated access to and knowledge of offline and online channels [16]. The latter reflects the evaluation of OOCI’s service quality regarding the degree of inte grated service support through offline and online channels [16]. Indeed, scholars have investigated the influence of PII and PFI in terms of system evaluation, adoption, and usage experience [14, 15, 17–19, 39]. How ever, previous studies have largely ignored their congruence effect. As cue congruence theory has emphasized how the congruent and incon gruent cues influence individuals’ decision-making, this study adopts it to understand how the congruence and incongruence between PII and PFI influence customer decisions in the omni-channel businesses context.

## 2.2. Cue congruency theory, congruence, and incongruence between PII and PFI

According to cue congruency theory, individuals detect cues to make evaluations and tend to maintain consistent patterns among multiple cognitive elements [28, 40–42]. Consistent patterns among cues are therefore more useful than inconsistent ones when rendering judgment [27]. When the detected cues are consistent, they are more likely to be used jointly in evaluation, whereas inconsistent patterns can cause in dividuals to focus on the discrepancy or negative cues, which negatively influences individuals’ evaluations, subsequent engagement, and purchase decisions [29]. In this study, PII and PFI are conceptualized as customers’ detected OOCI cues that are used to evaluate information and service quality. In other words, this cognition describes customers reception of the presented OOCI characteristics. Hence, we contend the logic that cue congruence theory can address how the congruence and incongruence between PII and PFI influence retention decisions.

Cue congruence theory has been used by scholars in marketing and IS areas to study various cues’ congruent and incongruent effects [27, 28, 42–47]. These studies have described cue congruent effect as the rein forcement of customers’ perceived diagnosticity of cues when cues are consistent $[ 4 2 ,$ 43], while cue incongruent effect has been described as individuals’ doubt of cue reliability and distinct focus on negative cues when multiple cues provide disparate conclusions [27]. As most previ ous literature has only empirically examined the cue congruence theory in various online website contexts, the question remains about this theory’s applicability in the omni-channel businesses context. Different from online websites, omni-channel business is more complex, as it in volves multiple channels $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , physical stores, mobile channels, online websites, internet kiosks, and other touchpoints) with heterogenous cue presentation ways. Therefore, PII and PFI are regarded as hybrid cues from multiple sources. Compared with single source information, in dividuals typically use a more diligent and enhanced way to proces multiple source information and rely less on the relationship between various cues, which finally regulating the cue congruent and incon gruent effect [48, 49]. Therefore, this study seeks to advance our un derstanding of how the congruence and incongruence between multiple source information (i.e., PII and PFI) alter customers’ decisions in the omni-channel businesses context. In addition, although the cue congruent and incongruent effect has been widely demonstrated, in dividuals may have heterogeneous ways to process cues. For example, scholars have revealed that individuals’ motivation influences the way they receive, store, and process cues [50, 51], which indicates the po tential role of motivation in processing congruent and incongruent cues. However. detailed investigations that examine the interaction between congruent or incongruent cue processing and individual motivation remain relatively rare. Because of the different reactions toward OOCI given customer heterogeneity $[ 2 , 4 , 1 5 ]$ , this study considers how in dividuals’ motivation alter the impact of congruence and incongruence between PII and PFI in the omni-channel businesses context.

## 2.3. Heuristic-Systematic model and showrooming motivation

The heuristic-systematic model posits that individuals’ motivations influence how they process information and that there are two modes of information processing: systematic processing and heuristic processing [52, 53]. Systematic processing is a comprehensive way of processing information whereby individuals pay careful attention to various pieces of information that they then evaluate and integrate for decision-making [52]. This kind of processing requires high motivation in individuals to allocate cognitive resources to obtain accurate and sufficient informa tion [52]. Those with low motivation prefer heuristic processing, as it requires less cognitive effort and uses a single informational cue for quick conclusion [30, 53–56]. This theoretical model has been applied by several scholars to understand how individuals process information and make decisions in various contexts, such as online reviews [53], website acceptance [57], online communities [58], product labels [59], and so on.

Showrooming motivation refers to customers’ desire to use mobile devices while shopping in physical stores to compare products for po tential purchases [32]. It generally reflects customers’ desire to reduce shopping uncertainty and enjoy flexible shopping experiences by leveraging various channels [60]. As OOCI can fulfill consumers’ needs with regard to showrooming motivation, an increasing number of scholars have suggested the practice of using OOCI to retain customers with showrooming motivation [60-62]. Although Li et al. [2] have made initial efforts to empirically examine the role of showrooming on regu lating the influence of OOCI, their findings are limited to explaining how customers with showrooming motivation react to various presentation patterns of OOCI. As the heuristic systematic model has revealed the important role of individual motivation in regulating the way to process information, we extend this stream of research by empirically validating showrooming motivation as a critical boundary condition of the balanced and imbalanced presentation pattern of OOCI.

## 3. Hypothesis development

## 3.1. Impact of incongruence between PII and PFI on customer retention

Cue congruency theory suggests that, when confronted with incon sistent cues, customers focus on cue discrepancy or negative cues [29]. In omni-channel businesses, customers may receive inconsistent cues from a focal retailer’s OOCI owing to the retailer’s heterogeneous orientation or the customer’s limited attention. Inconsistency between PII and PFI has two negative effects on consumer retention decisions. First, customers might focus on the discrepancy between PII and PFI, which causes confusion and suspicion regarding the reliability of the retailer’s OOCI [63]. For example, a high level of PFI signifies a cus tomer’s high evaluation of the OOCI’s service quality because an OOCI with high service quality can provide customers with a flexible and convenient process for acquiring and returning products. Specifically, customers can enjoy online orders, pickup offline services, and the op tion to return online products to offline stores. By contrast, a low level of PII means customers perceive OOCI information quality as low. This tends to happen when retailers provide limited or inconsistent infor mation across various channels $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , prices, assortment, inventory level, promotion activity). Owing to the importance of consumer perception of OOCI information and service quality, such cases can lead customers to lose trust and decrease OOCI’s reliability. Moreover, customers have been shown to focus on negative cues when multiple cues present con tradictory signals [27]. In other words, under these conditions, positive cues may be ignored, while negative cues may be emphasized. There fore, the positive impact of a high degree of PII (or PFI) may be weak ened because of the existence of a low degree of PFI (or PII). Given this, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Incongruence between PII and PFI is negatively associated with customer retention.

Moreover, PII-PFI incongruence offers two scenarios (i.e., PII is higher or lower than PFI). When PFI is higher than PII, we expected the incongruence effect to be more detrimental to customer retention. Though a high PFI can be a positive cue reflecting high OOCI service quality, a low level of PII can offset this positive effect. Scholars have shown that low PII often indicates conflicting product information among channels [64], which can make customers feel more uncertain and reduce trust toward the focal retailer [65]. The literature has demonstrated that customers’ distrust can decrease their evaluation of a retailer’s other offerings [66, 67]. Although a high PFI suggests that customers might enjoy seamless shopping experience in the fulfilling stage, the value of PFI may be weakened because of low PII.

In contrast, when PII is higher than PFI, low PFI often signifies low service quality, which requires the customers to exert additional effort to receive and return products. However, a high PII can offset this negative effect because it reflects that retailers provide customers with consistent and integrated ways of presenting product and retailer information [16]. For example, physical stores’ locations and inventory levels are pre sented in online channels, and product prices and assortments are generally the same in both online and offline channels. The relevance and completeness of this information can help customers decide how to obtain and return products in relatively convenient ways. Additionally, a high PII also means the retailer provides access to other shopping-related information, such as usage specifications and other customers’ product reviews. This valuable information can decrease the risk of purchasing inappropriate products, which in turn reduces the likelihood of returns. We thus expect that, when PII is higher than PFI,

## 3.2. Impact of congruence between PII and PFI on customer retention

OOCI cues can be at high and low levels, simultaneously. Customers can receive cues that a retailer’s OOCI has a low degree of information and service quality. In such cases, although there does not exist a cue incongruent effect, customers’ perception of poor information and ser vice quality can hinder their retention [68]. For example, a low level of PII implies inconsistent information regarding a retailer’s offerings in different channels [69, 70] and a low level of PFI requires customers to exert additional effort or wait for the delivery time to obtain ordered products [69]. Thus, when confronted with OOCI’s poor information and service quality, customer retention is limited. Nevertheless, when PII and PFI are at a high level simultaneously, it signifies high infor mation quality and service convenience [71]. Literature has demon strated that consistent cues reinforce each other, which further enhances customer’s decision confidence [27]. Therefore, when PII and PFI are at a high level simultaneously, they can mutually confirm the OOCI’s quality, which will increase customers’ decision to retain. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following:

H3. The degree of customer retention is higher when PII and PFI are both at a high level, as opposed to at a low level.

## 3.3. Moderating effect of showrooming motivation on the influence of PII–PFI congruence and incongruence

Motivation influences how individual’s process received cues and make decisions [30]. Those with high motivation systematically process relevant cues, including the steps involved in carefully attending to, evaluating, elaborating, and understanding such information [54]. In contrast, those with low motivation prefer a heuristic processing approach, such as using a subset of available information to make de cisions [30, 54].

Customers’ showrooming motivation reflects their internal desire to reduce uncertainty and enjoy a seamless transition from online to offline channels [60]. As information integration can decrease product uncer tainty and fulfillment integration involves the shopping flexibility in the fulfillment process [3], customers with high showrooming motivation are likely to systematically process OOCI cues. Customers would thus deliberately evaluate the potential outcomes generated by the incon gruence between PII and PFI, such as in cases lacking a seamless tran sition between online and offline channels. Furthermore, they risk purchasing products at high price points when PII is low. Customers with low showrooming motivation, however, would likely use a heu ristic approach to process these cues. As customers are apt to only focus on the existence of PII or PFI, regardless of whether these cues are congruent or not, the negative effect of incongruence between PII and PFI would likely be stronger for those with high showrooming motivation.

Similarly, the simultaneous high level of PII and PFI tends to indicate high information and service quality in online and offline channels [3]. As customers with high showrooming motivation desire seamless tran sitions between channels, they are likely to regard the simultaneous high levels of PII and PFI as important cues for making retention decisions. In contrast, customers with low showrooming motivation are likely to not regard the high level of PII and PFI as useful cues for making retention decisions owing to their tendency to shop in only one channel. Hence, the positive effect of congruence between PII and PFI should be stronger for customers with high showrooming motivation. Therefore, we pro pose the following:

H4. The negative effect of incongruence between PII and PFI on customer retention is stronger when the customer’s showrooming

motivation is high.

H5. The positive effect of simultaneous high PII and PFI on customer retention is strengthened when the customers’ showrooming motivation is high.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Sample and data collection

This study used an online survey to collect data and test the hy potheses. Participants were recruited through the Sojump platform, a popular online survey platform in China. Sojump has a considerable number of active members from diverse backgrounds and facilitates the invitation of eligible respondents to join investigations. Thus, we employed a random sampling technique for recruiting participants. Data were collected in China because it is becoming the largest retailing market in the world and the Chinese retailers continue to play an important, pioneering role in omni-channel transformation [72, 73]. In addition, showrooming has been frequently occurring in physical stores in the Chinese market [74]. As participants were Chinese speakers, we did back-to-back translation to convert questionnaires from English to Chinese. To ensure the questionnaire’s readability and clarity, we first presented it to two professors and several doctoral students majoring in business and asked them about its format and wording. Revisions and improvements were made based on their suggestions. For example, hard-to-understand words were modified without losing their meaning, while repeated words were deleted to ensure conciseness. The survey consisted of three sections. In the first section, we introduced the defi nition of omni-channel retailers (i.e., “retailers who have integrated mul tiple channels for customers to purchase products”), the goal for this questionnaire, and the statement about privacy protection. The second section was designed to measure key constructs. Only participants who confirmed purchasing from omni-channel retailers in the previous three months were permitted to continue answering the related questions. The third section collected participants’ demographic information.

The survey took approximately 10 min for participants to complete. We announced that participants who completed our survey would be given a cash reward of 10-yuan. After two weeks, we gathered a total of 320 responses, 70 of which were excluded because some participants did not answer key questions (i.e., whether they perceived a retailer’s in formation or fulfillment integration) or because they did not meet the minimum baseline for time (i.e., questionnaires were completed in less than five minutes). ## 4.2. Measurements

The measurement for all constructs in this study was adapted from prior research with slight modifications to fit our context. Specifically, the PII measurement was adapted from Bendoly et al. [38]. PFI was determined by combining the measurement of perceived physical inte gration from Bendoly et al. [38] and the measurement of integrated order fulfillment from Oh et al. [64], as the former only contains two items. Customer retention was measured with items adapted from Bojei et al. [76] and showrooming motivation was measured with items adapted from Rapp et al. [32] and Ripp´e et al. [77]. For the control variable, we measured the sample’s demographic information (age, gender, education, personal income). As scholars have emphasized the influence of alternative attractiveness (i.e., “consumers’ perceptions regarding the extent to which competing alternatives are available in the marketplace”) [78] on customer retention toward the focal retailer [79, 80], this study adopted measurements from Jones et al. [78] and regarded alternative attractiveness as an additional control variable. Aside from the demographic variables, other measures were obtained using a scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

## 4.3. Common method bias

The procedure outlined by Lindell and Whitney [81] was used to test common method bias through correlated marker variable methods. In particular, we selected the construct of social site identification as a method variance marker. Social site identification refers to the sense of identification a customer feels with respect to interactions with other customers through the e-WOM system on the same online shopping site [82]. As social site identification has low correlations with all variables in this study and does not theoretically relate to the phenomenon under investigation, it was regarded as a marker variable. Comparison of the correlations above and below the diagonal in Table 2 indicate that, with the exception of two correlations, the remaining 18 correlations were significant after adjustment. Scholars have suggested that if a study aim to test the interaction effect, common method bias cannot account for any statistically significant interactive effects because it is implausible that participants would consciously theorize an interacting effect when answering questionnaires [83]. Thus, common method bias was not a serious problem in this study.

## 4.4. Measurement model

Each construct’s reliability and validity were evaluated using SPSS. Table 2 shows the mean, standard deviation, and correlation among the variables. Cross-loading of each construct was performed because of a strong correlation between PII and PFI. Table 3 indicates that the crossloadings of all constructs were above 0.6, which suggests discriminant validity was achieved. Table 4 indicates that all of the items’ loadings were higher than the benchmark of 0.6; thus, convergent validity was satisfied. Composite reliability ranged from 0.827 to 0.902, which was also above the benchmark value of 0.7. In addition, the scores for the average variance extracted ranged from 0.515 to 0.572, which were higher than the benchmark value of 0.5. The estimates of Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.718 to 0.874 and were above the benchmark value of 0.7. These results indicate that the measurement model exhibited satisfactory convergent validity, discriminant validity, and internal reliability. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values were then calculated to assess potential multicollinearity problems among constructs. The re sults showed that the highest VIF value was 2.130, which is below the more stringent threshold of three, suggesting multicollinearity was not a concern in our study.

Specifically, this approach allows scholars to compare the results of two predictor variables’ congruence and incongruence on the outcome variable (H1) and can be used to test whether incongruence in one direction has a more nuanced effect than incongruence in the opposite direction (H2). It shows how congruence on simultaneously high versus low levels of the two predictors influence outcome variables (H3) and enables further moderation analyses that can provide profound insights into how congruence and incongruence effects are regulated by conditional variables (H4, H5).

This new method has been employed in cases where the balancing of two components is an important consideration [87]. For example, IS researchers have used this approach to understand how the congruence and incongruence between users’ expected performances and perceived performances influence their information system usage [88–91]. Meanwhile, scholars from other areas of study have used this approach to analyze employers’ and employees’ value incongruence [92], per sonality incongruence [86], and the incongruence between firms various capabilities [93, 94].

Descriptive information regarding the occurrences of congruence and incongruence was presented before the analysis. Participants with a standardized score on PII that was half a standard deviation above or below the standardized PFI score were considered to have discrepant values [85]. $$

$$

where CR refers to a customer's retention decision. PII refers to a customer’s perceived information integration, and PFI refers to perceived fulfillment integration. SHM refers to showrooming motivation, which was included as a control variable. The $\mathrm { P I I } ^ { 2 }$ , PII\*PFI, and $\mathrm { P F I } ^ { 2 }$ were included in Model 2 because estimating the congruence and incongru ence effects requires the higher-order terms of two predictor variables (e.g., the squared terms of PII and PFI, as well as their product) [95].

When the coefficients of any of the higher-order terms are signifi cant, the relationship of the two predictor variables on the outcome variable is curvilinear, and response surface methodology can be used to further reflect the response surface’s shape. However, when all higherorder terms are insignificant, the two predictor variables relate line arly to the outcome variable. Table 6 shows that the interactive term

PII\*PFI significantly influenced customer retention $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 8 , p <$ 0.05), which signifies that the relationship among PII, PFI, and customer retention was curvilinear. Moreover, the $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ of the second-order equa tion (Model 2) was higher than that in the first-order equation (Model 1), necessitating consideration of the influence of the interactive term on customer retention. We then used the response surface methodology to further describe and test the features of the surfaces corresponding to the second-order equation (Model 2). A significant, positive (/negative) curvature of the surface along the line of incongruence indicated that the incongruence between the two predictor variables had a positive (/negative) effect on the outcome variables, while the insignificant curvature implied that the effect of incongruence was not salient. The slope of the surface along the line of incongruence indicated the asym metry effect of the two incongruence effects: a significant and positive (/negative) coefficient indicated a lower (/higher) outcome variable when Y (i.e., PFI) exceeded X (i.e., PII), rather than vice versa. In observing the congruence effect, a significant and positive (/negative) curvature of the surface along the line of congruence indicated that the level of congruence between the two predictor variables had a U-shaped (/inverted-U shaped) relationship with the outcome variable. If the curvature of the surface along the line of congruence is insignificant and the slope of the surface along the line of congruence is significant and positive (/negative), there exists a positive (/negative) linear relation ship between the congruence of the two predictor variables on the outcome variable.

Further comparison of the two surface responses (Figs. 3a and 3b; Figs. 4a and 4c) yielded the same findings—the congruence between PII and PFI had a linear relationship under the condition of high showrooming motivation, while a nonlinear relationship existed under the condition of low showrooming motivation. The above results thereby supported H5, which suggest that showrooming motivation strengthened the positive effect of PII and PFI congruence on customer retention.

## 5. Discussion

This study is one of the first to use polynomial modeling and response surface methodology to investigate customer retention in omni-channel businesses. It was designed and conducted to develop a deeper under standing of how to prioritize OOCI cues by investigating the effects of PII and PFI congruence and incongruence on customer retention. Drawing on cue congruence theory and the IS success model, we theorized PII and PFI as the detected cues for evaluating OOCI information and service quality respectively, and analyzed the effects of their congruence and incongruence. We further compared the nuanced effect of the two cue incongruence conditions—i.e., when PII is higher or lower than PFI. Based on the heuristic systematic model, the present study proposed a comprehensive understanding of the boundary condition on both PII and PFI congruence and incongruence by considering the moderating role of customers’ showrooming motivation.

## 5.1. Key findings

The results demonstrate that PII and PFI incongruence was nega tively related to customer retention, highlighting the importance of remaining consistent presentation patterns of information and fulfill ment integration. This coincides with prior research that has empha sized the necessity of balancing evaluations of a system’s various components [7, 15, 20, 21, 24, 25]. In addition, our results indicated an asymmetrical incongruence effect between PII and PFI. The study spe cifically showed that, when PII was lower than PFI, customer retention was lower compared to when PII was higher than PFI. Because infor mation integration provides consumers with accurate and relevant in formation to conduct their shopping processes, PII can be used to mitigate the negative influence of a low degree of PFI. Thus, this study is consistent with existing findings on the subject that show the impor tance of presenting information integration cues in omni-channel busi nesses [16, 38].

Moreover, counterintuitively, we found that PII and PFI congruence was not linearly related to customer retention. In other words, customer retention initially remained unchanged but then sharply increased with a high level of congruence. The reason for this may be that OOCI can be regarded as an “attractive” attribute in retailers’ provisions. Research has suggested that increasing the performance of “attractive” attributes can generate high levels of customer satisfaction, while low degrees of such attributes would not cause customer dissatisfaction [96, 97]. Therefore, though retailers might not necessarily lose customers when both PII and PFI are at low levels, and customer retention can be improved when both are at high levels. This nonlinear relationship ex tends prior OOCI literature that has only demonstrated the positive, linear effect of OOCI on customers’ attitudes and decisions [2–4, 14–16, 98].

In addition, this study found that customer showrooming motivation functioned as a double-edged sword in the context of omni-channel businesses. Thus, showrooming motivation strengthened the negative effect of PII and PFI incongruence on customer retention. However, it amplified the positive role of PII and PFI congruence. Taken together, these findings illustrate that customers with high showrooming moti vation cared about OOCI and deliberately evaluated the outcomes of congruence and incongruence between PII and PFI. The results com plement those of Li et al. [2] by showing that showrooming motivation may not necessarily be beneficial for strengthening OOCI effectiveness, because it can also have a negative effect when PII and PFI are incongruent.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

This study has important theoretical implications for the design and implementation of OOCI. First, drawn on the IS success model and cue congruence theory, this study establishes an explicit distinction between PII and PFI, and empirically examines their congruence and incongru ence effects. The present study extends the OOCI research by revealing that one cue of OOCI influences the likelihood for the other cue to persuade customers. Previous studies have suggested that both di mensions of OOCI cues are beneficial for cultivating positive attitudes toward retailers [15, 17–19]. However, these studies have overlooked the interaction effect between PII and PFI. Our results show that the effect of OOCI on retaining customers depends on the balance and imbalance between PII and PFI. Specifically, the higher degree of incongruence between PII and PFI is detrimental to retaining customers. Moreover, a fine-grained examination of the asymmetrical incongruence effect reveals that PII is more important than PFI in terms of retaining customers. These findings provide a deeper understanding of the cus tomers’ interaction process with OOCI. More importantly, they indicate the importance of balancing the presentation of several subdimensions of information system cues for increasing users’ adoption intentions.

Second, by using the heuristic-systematic model, this study further understands how showrooming motivation influences customers’ re actions to OOCI with balanced and imbalanced presentation patterns. Prior studies have realized the detrimental role of customer show rooming on brick-and-mortar stores [32, 99–101]. Although scholars have suggested the practice of using OOCI for retaining customers who have showrooming motivation [1], limited studies empirically examine customers’ reactions toward balanced and imbalanced presentation patterns of OOCI. Our results suggest that showrooming motivation is a double-edged sword in omni-channel businesses. It can be regarded as an opportunity for physical stores only when retailers have presented a simultaneously high degree of information and fulfillment integration. Otherwise, showrooming motivation can amplify the negative incon gruence effect of PII and PFI. This study deepens the present under standing of the role of showrooming motivation in omni-channel businesses. Additionally, it urges scholars to incorporate users’ charac teristics for understanding users’ information system interaction processes.

Third, although cue congruence theory has been used by scholars in marketing and IS research disciplines, few studies have investigated its applicability in processing multiple source cues [28, 29, 42]. To address this gap, we contextualized PII and PFI as multiple source cues in the omni-channel businesses context and examined their congruent and incongruent effects. Furthermore, based on the heuristic-systematic model, this study extends cue congruence theory by taking showroom ing motivation as the moderating variable influencing how individuals process congruent and incongruent cues in omni-channel businesses. Therefore, this study highlights the importance for researchers to adequately account for the role of individual motivations in regulating their congruent and incongruent cues processing.

## 5.3. Managerial implications

The present study yields important insights for retailers on how to manage OOCI cues. First, omni-channel retailers should be aware of the methods to present cues of information and fulfillment integration in online and offline channels. Specifically, online store managers can add a physical store’s information (i.e., location, promotion activity) on their homepage. In addition, they can label a product’s offline price on the websites for showing that the same type of product is sold in online and offline channels with the same price. Besides, they can announce the provision of integrated services $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ online order, offline pickup, or offline return) through the retailer’s social media websites (i.e., official accounts and microblog). They can also directly give the option of buying online and picking up in a store when customers are deciding their online product fulfillment process. Furthermore, offline store managers can directly print products’ online price information near the offline product label, or they can add QR codes to the shelf for customers to easily search online information. Besides, store managers can place an internet kiosk at the store door, which has the potential to attract new customers to retailers’ offerings and can help existing customers who want to place orders for out-of-stock items. Further, offline store man agers can also print the integrated fulfillment services (i.e., online order offline pick up or return) in conspicuous locations in the store or on product packages. Cashiers can also remind customers to consider several valuable fulfillment services for future purchases.

Second, omni-channel retailers should take care in balancing the presenting of information and fulfillment integration. For example, on websites, it is better to allocate the same space, location, and color in presenting information and fulfillment integration. In physical stores, offline store managers are advised to simultaneously promote informa tion and fulfillment integration. To avoid overemphasizing just one aspect, store managers can invite customers to complete a survey or interview regarding a retailer’s OOCI. Managers can then draw on customers’ cognition and understanding to decide whether they need to emphasize certain integration practices. Although maintaining a bal ance between the presentation of information and fulfillment integra tion is the best way for omni-channel retailers to reap the benefits of OOCI, some retailers are only able to implement one type of integration due to resource constriction. As such, the results of this study suggest it might be better for retailers to first and foremost emphasize information integration. For example, on their websites, retailers can use a salient location and color to present offline store product information (i.e., price, promotion, and inventory level) and retailer information $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ physical store location and telephone number). Moreover, in physical stores, retailers can present their website’s QR code and use internet kiosks for customers to directly browse information about their online products.

Third, offline store managers should adjust to effectively addressing the showrooming phenomenon in accordance with their OOCI presen tation. Retailers who have implemented and presented a high degree of both information and fulfillment integration can encourage customers in-store mobile device usage behaviors by providing free Wi-Fi for cus tomers to compare online product information. Moreover, when cus tomers are hesitant about purchasing, salespeople can prompt customers to search online information and can even actively show the same product’s online information $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , price or review) to reduce customer uncertainty. Nevertheless, for retailers who present heterogeneous de grees of information and fulfillment integration, their customers’ instore mobile device usage still threatens to adversely affect their phys ical stores. Under such a condition, retailers should adjust their OOCI presentation to be more balanced, predictable, and consistent. To do so, they can increase the presentation of the less conspicuous dimensions of OOCI or decrease overemphasized aspects. They can also create more comfortable shopping environments $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . ,$ , suitable color schemes and lighting, orderly product placement, and sufficient fitting room accommodations) for customers to be immersed in, and thereby, reduce customers’ showrooming motivation.

## 5.4. Limitations and future studies

While this study makes several contributions to the extant literature on OOCI, it has limitations that should be addressed by future research. First, we employed a cross-sectional design, which can lead to difficulty in identifying causal relationships. Future studies might thus use a longitudinal design to allow customers’ reception of OOCI cues to be measured ahead of customer retention. Second, this study considers only the contingency factors of customers’ showrooming motivation. As we cannot ignore the existence of other factors that might potentially in fluence the role of perceived OOCI, future studies should explore the impact of other customer-level characteristics, such as personality, culture, and cognition. Third, although this study found a significant congruence and incongruence effect between PII and PFI, it focused only on OOCI information and service quality. Future research might there fore extend this study by combining the role of other dimensions $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ system quality) in relation to customer retention.

## 5.5. Conclusion

Overall, this study aimed to understand how congruence and incongruence between customers’ PII and PFI influence their retention decisions. It further examined the regulating role of customers’ show rooming motivation in affecting how congruent and incongruent cues are processed. We found that incongruence between PII and PFI nega tively influenced customer retention, whereas PII and PFI congruence had a nonlinear effect. An asymmetrical influence effect was also iden tified, whereby customer retention is lower when PII is lower than PFI under the opposite condition. Our results showed that customers showrooming motivation can function as a double-edged sword in the context of an omni-channel business that can emphasize the positive effect of PII and PFI congruence but can also amplify the negative aspect of their incongruence effect.
