# Understanding how the semantic features of contents influence the diffusion of government microblogs: Moderating role of content topics

![](/api/attachments/ZNX7R58F/fulltext/images/e86b637d35f323095c9aef9206595115aa05e6fc98562e95bef23b625d3a00d9.jpg)

Xiaodong Feng , Kangxin Hui , Xin Deng , Guoyin Jiang

School of Public Administration, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, Chengdu, Sichuan 611731, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Government microblogs Information diffusion Emotional words Content similarity Content topics

## A B S T R A C T

Understanding users’ behavior mechanism of information diffusion on government microblogs is helpful for formulating effective strategies to promote public participation. However, limited effort has been made to examine the effects of extensive textual features and their differences on different topics. Therefore, on the basis of the elaboration likelihood model, we develop a model to explain how the extensive semantic features will influence the diffusion of government microblog posts with different topics. A model test with real data from Sina Weibo demonstrates the promotional effect of positive words, city names, adjectives/adverbs, and dissimilar contents, and that negative words will hinder the diffusion. The effect of city names for political news is greater than that for living information, while the influence of positive words for living information is greater than that for political news. Contributions to the literature and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Recent years have witnessed the ubiquitous development of online social media, such as Facebook, Twitter, and Sina Weibo, which attract a huge number of users to engage themselves. Government microblogs, as an application of social media by government agencies, have been developing rapidly since they first appeared in 2009. The number of certified government accounts on Sina Weibo reached 177,437 by December 31, 2020<sup>1</sup>. As a communication bridge between the govern ment and the general public, government microblogs have certain ad vantages in promoting public participation [1]. They can not only improve citizens’ satisfaction with the government, but can also help accelerate the construction of a service-oriented government [2]. In recent years, when many public sectors continue to promote the development of new government affairs media, government microblogs have become increasingly prominent. They have been deeply integrated into social governance and have become the most important means for government agencies at all levels to optimize people’s livelihood ser vices, to consolidate social consensus, and to innovate social gover nance. Thus, microblogs are changing the way government agencies function and interact with the public regarding public affairs.

Specifically, equipped with a strong ability for information proliferation, microblogs are convenient tools for users to disseminate messages, as well as public opinions. In government microblogs, users can participate by forwarding, replying to, and liking posts published by government accounts. Among these behaviors, forwarding/sharing/ retweeting of a post can expose many other people to it, forming the process of information diffusion. The instant sharing ability allows government agencies to release up-to-date information for various public events and activities. A prominent example is that the poll for the 2016 US election is correlated with the public opinion on Twitter [3]. In almost all cases using social media to achieve some goals, trying to make published information spread more widely, such as gaining more retweets and comments, is one of the most critical goals. However, a tremendous amount of information is disclosed on microblog platforms every day. Thus, it is a big challenge for government agencies to make their published contents more attractive, and spread more broadly among the public. For this purpose, understanding the driving factors behind the dissemination of online posts is of great significance, and thus attracts much attention from the research field. Previous studies were devoted to finding the factors influencing the propagation of online content. They were focused mainly on social influential features of the publishers (such as the number of fans) and structured features extracted from posts (such as the length of the posts and the presence of images/mentions/defined hashtags) [4–8], and few studies focused on the impact of topics and the sentiment of the content [9,10].

However, in addition to these structural and textual features, extensive semantic features contained in the posts will also be critical for their attractiveness. For example, the emotional words in the posts will be important signals to attract users, and especially how the positive and negative words contribute differently to the diffusion of online content is also unclear. Some meaningful entities in the content should not be ignored since these will make the post more elaborate, such as city names, which are highly related to government-related contents. More importantly, posts that are more distinctive and different from other posts will also show greater competitiveness. Unfortunately, little research pays great attention to the above-mentioned semantic features implied in the content. Thus, it is still not clear how the outcome of diffusion of government microblog posts is affected by the rich semantic features of their contents.

To fill in these research gaps, greater investigation of how the extensive textual features in the content of government microblog posts will affect the diffusion outcome is the objective of this study. Typically, information diffusion is a complex sociocognitive process, which is a behavioral decision made after exposure to and assessment of the particular information. To explain the process of attitude changes and acceptance of online messages, the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) [11] has been widely applied. In the ELM, a message can influence people’s attitudes and behaviors from central and peripheral routes depending on the type of information processed by recipients (e.g., issue-relevant arguments or simple cues), allowing us to examine the information diffusion outcomes of government posts as the accumula tion of diffusion decisions made by individuals.

For the central routes, the argument quality and content elements of the message itself [12,13] are critical factors when recipients attempt to evaluate information. Accordingly, we delve into more textual factors in the government post contents that would make a difference for the spread and diffusion by users. This is also consistent with the purpose of information disclosure by government agencies as local governments would spontaneously publish information to match the expectations of the public, which would greatly help to gain social legitimacy [14] and increase the degree of transparency perceived by the public. The high quality of information with more detailed contents and persuasive sig nals would be perceived with more transparency by the public.

Specifically, we focus on extracting extensive sematic features from the dimensions of emotional words, richness in parts of speech (POS), government-relevant content measured by the appearance of city names, and heterogeneity of the texts measured by the similarity with others. Also, we examine the different effects on different content topics and consider the particular context of government microblogs rather than general social media when extracting features from texts. Thus, we build a theoretical model containing these factors and conduct an empirical study on our collected dataset of 76,619 posts by 29 govern ment accounts on Sina Weibo. The results indicate that positive words in the content have a positive effect on the diffusion of government posts, which is more important for living information in government posts, while negative words have the opposite effect. Government affairs–relevant content (measured by the number of city names) can also increase information diffusion, especially for political news in government posts. Also, the richness and heterogeneity of the texts will affect the overall outcomes of information diffusion. The findings will provide guidance for government microblog operators on how they can make their published posts spread more widely and get more attention from the public they serve. In conclusion, the current research makes several important contributions to both the literature and practice.

The rest of this article is organized as follows. Literature related to information diffusion on social media is briefly reviewed in Section 2. In Section 3, the theoretical foundations and research model are developed by our proposing associated hypotheses, followed by a description of the data collection and methods in Section 4. Section 5 provides the empirical results, and discussions on theoretical and practical implica tions are provided in Section 6.

## 2. Related work

The main features and benefits that government officials can gain from the use of social media are that public citizens can be well moti vated to participate in public affairs by diffusing posts. Thus, it is essential to understand the mechanism of online information diffusion on social media. Here we refer to “information diffusion” on social media as the spread, dissemination, and broadcast of online posts by users with the individual behavior of sharing, forwarding, and retweeting a post or tweet. These different names of information diffu sion behavior are defined on different platforms, but the functions are the same (i.e., making the information visible for more users) since the social media platforms usually have a mechanism to display what a user originally published or has forwarded/shared/retweeted from others).

On the basis of the different granularities of information diffusion, current studies can be grouped as a microview of individual’s informa tion diffusion behavior and a macroview of the overall information diffusion outcomes.

## 2.1. Individuals’ information diffusion on social media

From the microview of information diffusion of microblogs, the post is diffused (retweeted/forwarded) by one by one individual users on social media. Such studies usually exploit the theories of social psy chology to model the individual information diffusion intention or behavior as a social communication process. They empirically test the model mainly by the methods of survey and experiment, and a few use online real data. On the basis of the social presence theory and infor mation adoption model, it was found that information quality, source credibility, and perceived enjoyment significantly determine the per ceptions of individuals regarding online tweets and thus their diffusion intention [15]. From the perspective of social relationships, an empirical study found almost 25.5% of tweets are retweeted from friends’ blog spaces [16]. Other theory-oriented empirical studies based on ques tionnaire or online data examined the effects of social tie strength [17–19], perceived attitude homophily [20], and users social network characteristics [21] on individuals’ information diffusion intention. In addition to the direct effect, social tie strength can mediate almost 50% of the effect of value homophily on individual retweeting behavior [22].

Individual heterogeneity and preferences also play a key role in in formation diffusion behavior. For example, the needs to be individual istic and altruistic have positive impacts on an individual’s information forwarding behavior [23], and the messages fit with a user as well as the message content will determine the user’s rebroadcasting of a message [24]. On the basis of theories of personal branding, users’ retweets tend to focus on the topics they address in their self-produced tweets, instead of adding new topics [25]. From the perspective of affect, a study [26] based on the affective response model and a survey found individuals positive emotion will subsequently promote their urge to share infor mation on microblogs. In addition to individual information diffusion in general contexts, emotional support, external political efficacy, rumor control, civic skills, and mobilization significantly impact the informa tion sharing and commenting behavior on government social media accounts during crises [1]. Another empirical study [27] built a model based on uses and gratification (U&G) theory and showed that altruism was the most significant factor that predicts the individual sharing of fake news on COVID-19.

Although these studies do not directly correlate with our study for the exact same research target, the related social theory and insights will help us design related features from the content of posts. In turn, our research will feed back some theories, such as the information adoption model, the affective response model, and U&G theory, by providing more evidence by investigating textual features from the contents of

social media information.

## 2.2. Overall information diffusion outcomes on social media

From the macroview of information diffusion, it can be represented by the aggregation of individuals’ information diffusion behavior, such as the number of forwards, shares, or retweets. Such studies usually collect online social media data and examine how the multiple features will influence the overall information diffusion outcomes. The features are usually extracted from the publishers as peripheral routes and from information content as central routes, according to the ELM [11].

## 2.2.1. Publisher-specific factors

More often, on the basis of the theory of social capital, social network ties measured by the number of friends or followers in the online com munity are the most influential factors that will influence the popularity of user-generated content in the brand community [5], travel commu nity [6] and social Q&A (questions and answers) community [28]. In addition to the number of social ties, viral ads published by users with higher trust scores by a computational method on social networks will significantly spread faster and more broadly [29]. Also, studies have paid attention to the types of publishers. For example, cancer-related posts published by the American Cancer Society received more likes, comments, and shares than other source categories [30]. In the context of government social media, local governments with different public administration styles gained different levels of citizens’ engagement measured by fans, likes, comments, and shares, where engagement levels by citizens were higher for more open local governments [31].

## 2.2.2. Structure of posts

For post-specific factors, the effects of the structural features of on line contents on their overall popularity have been widely investigated. The ratio of multimedia elements and external links is found to have positive effects on the breadth of online interactivity on government social media [8]; the appearance of pictures, videos, headlines, location information, mentions, hashtags, emoticons, etc. as well as release time and length could explain the number of comments from readers on the news [4], responses to social questions [28], retweets of information during social crises [12], and replies to travelogues [6].

## 2.2.3. Topic of the posts

Besides the structural features of posts, many studies have also examined the effect of the content topic of online posts on the outcomes of information diffusion in different online communities. For example, different content topics (such as environment, health, education, and housing) of posts can gain significantly different levels of shares on local governments’ Facebook pages [31]; cancer information related to per sonal experience and cancer prevention will gain many more comments and fans [7]; firm posts that convey firm credibility and organizational achievements are associated with more likes [32].

## 2.2.4. Extensive textual features

Besides the attention to the general topic of online content, recent studies have tried to extract more textual features from the content, especially about the emotional signals and sentiment tendency. A prior study found the affective dimensions of Twitter messages (positive and negative sentiment) associated with political parties or politicians are related to the number of triggered retweets and the speed of retweeting [33]. An empirical study on three selected political topics from Twitter found moral emotion vocabulary has a significant impact on the reposting of the online tweets [34]. For government accounts on Twitter, feeds including references to both positive and negative emo tions are more likely to receive high levels of citizen attention [10]. Rather than the overall sentiment, emotional activation has a positive impact on the spread of rumor information on social media, while emotional pleasantness and imagery are not significant factors [35];

user-generated contents with more negative valence and complaints would be more prevalent on Facebook business pages [9]. In addition to the textual features, visual content features extracted from images by deep learning techniques, including complexity, similarity, and consis tency measures, can also play important roles to predict the popularity of online posts [36].

## 2.3. Review and research gap

Summarizing the related researches, we find that although studies have been devoted to how the content of online posts will affect their attractiveness and popularity, deep mining of semantic features from contents in different contexts is still necessary. Specifically, the senti ment of content has been widely examined, but the separate impact of positive and negative words is not clear in the field of government microblogs; the specific content features of government microblogs for the purpose of information disclosure and publicity should be noted; and whether the uniqueness of content compared with that of posts pub lished by other government agencies would result in a higher level of diffusion also needs to be tested. Thus, we try to fill these gaps by building a theoretical model and performing an empirical analysis on data collected from China’s local government microblogs on Sina Weibo.

## 3. Research evidence and hypotheses

## 3.1. Theoretical foundations: Elaboration likelihood model

From the perspective of public users who wish to diffuse government information, diffusion of social media posts can be regarded as the process of information processing and evaluation by users, which is a subsequent result of persuasion, where public attention has become a scarce resource in the digital age [37]. The ELM [11] was proposed to address the process of persuasion toward messages, and provides a theoretical framework for understanding how information is treated by users. As a “dual-process” theory, the ELM posits two routes to explain the complicated process of persuasion: the central route and the pe ripheral route.

The processing of online information on microblogs can potentially be influenced through both routes depending on an individuals elaboration pathway. The central route refers to the process where a person changes his or her attitude by thinking critically about issue-related arguments in a message, which involves mainly a series of characteris tics related to information quality, such as the novelty and objectivity of information content. For information diffusion on social media, struc tural richness, topical relevance [22], information completeness [13], and text sentiment [38] have been included as central routes that can affect recipients’ perceived information usefulness and acceptance of the information. Therefore, the central route involves a high level of information elaboration, which requires focusing on the content of the information, reviewing and evaluating the content, and reflecting on issues regarding the information. The peripheral route refers to the process of changing attitudes by a superficial, automatic, and uncon scious way of thinking without considerable thinking and effort. Instead of performing a lot of cognitive work, the information recipients rely on multiple cues that have little to do with the views or contents of the information to make a quick decision. In the context of information diffusion on social media, peripheral routes are mainly concern infor mation publishers and the length of information [38], where publisher-related characteristics include the credibility, attractiveness, and authority of the publisher [22], as well as social relationships with the publisher [19]. Obviously, these cues do not require recipients to examine the information argument carefully, so less cognitive effort is involved.

From the perspective of government agencies, according to the the ory of legitimacy [14] and stakeholder theory [39], local governments will spontaneously publish information to match the expectations of the public, which will help greatly to gain social legitimacy. This kind of information disclosure is also called “transparency,” and information diffusion on social media also offers a way of providing accountability and enhancing transparency. To promote public participation through information diffusion on government microblogs, effective use of social media in the public sector requires a clear strategy and ability. Specif ically, not only what content is published and diffused on social media but how it is written and communicated must be considered by gov ernment agencies when they are operating on social media. High quality of information with more detailed contents and persuasive signals will be perceived with more transparency by the public. Accordingly, the strategy considered by government agencies should be how to improve the quality of published information (central route) given their social influences (peripheral route).

Thus, although both routes in the ELM make differences in changing people’s attitudes, the central route, which cares about the quality of the information-related argument and requires more effort, is stabler and more influential than the peripheral route [40]. In government micro blog platforms, many cues from the content of online posts are implied as the central route. Despite more evidence on how content features [7, 10,28,36] can predict information diffusion, the literature has not fully embraced the natural language process method to depict the argument quality of online posts. Moreover, the different impact mechanisms on posts issued by government agencies with different purposes are not clear. Therefore, to interpret the variance of overall diffusion outcomes of government microblog posts (i.e., number of forwards), we seek to fill this gap by mapping textual cues to four factors (i.e., emotional words, content richness, government-relevant content, and homogeneity of texts) as well as the moderating role of the content topic (see Fig. 1).

## 3.2. Research hypotheses

## 3.2.1. Emotional words and the diffusion of government microblog posts

As an external behavior, the public’s diffusing of government posts is affected by various internal and external factors, including the charac teristics of the contents and individual perception. Clore et al. [41] pointed out that emotions serving as an information scent will drive attention and attitude change, directly connected to decision making. Accordingly, many studies on weblogs, online news portals, online fo rums, social network services, or other contexts found that the emotional representation of information (both positive and negative sentiment) may result in more attention, cognitive participation, and higher levels of arousal, which in turn would affect reciprocity and feedback [42,43]. Emotional words can be seen as affective appeals of the information, which are also important as they influence the impacts of persuasive messages from an affective perspective. Thus, emotional words are a key factor of central routes in the ELM, and will play an important role in the message, stimulating the diffusing behavior. When government posts are published, the emotional words that are related to a particular emotion tendency in the text can cause changes in the mood of the audiences, thus affecting the decision of whether to pay more attention and to forward them. This process can also be interpreted by the affective response model [26], whereby emotional words could be naturally seen as affective cues that will generate arousal and action tendencies.

Positive words and negative words are two different kinds of emotional words. In the particular context of government social media, the followers and publishers are mostly government officials or citizens with high social status. They care greatly about how others judge them by what they publish or forward, so they want to show a positive outlook and value for others by publishing or forwarding positive posts. Positive emotional words will convey a positive signal, which can increase the public’s perceived usefulness and will highly meet the public’s psy chological needs. Thus, the public is more likely to diffuse such posts after reading them. For the effects of negative sentiment words, previous research found that negative posts are significantly more prevalent than positive posts on business Facebook pages [9], and negative messages are found to spread faster than positive ones on general social media application [44] or public opinion issues [34,44]. However, in the context of government microblogs, as discussed, differently from gen eral social media applications, many of the users are government offi cials or authenticated users. They care greatly about the consequences of forwarding negative information that may cause agitation as govern ment administrators usually do not want negative information to spread widely [45]. This point is supported by evidence from a study that in formation sharing during social crises is positively associated with positive emotional language style but is negatively related to an angry and informal style [12]. Thus, the increase of negative words may inhibit the diffusion of posts rather than promoting this process. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1.1: The number of positive emotional words in the text has a positive impact on the diffusion of government microblog posts.

H1.2: The number of negative emotional words in the text has a negative impact on the diffusion of government microblog posts.

## 3.2.2. Content richness and the diffusion of government microblog posts

According to the ELM, when people are dealing with information through the central path, information quality is the key factor that af fects the persuasion effect [46]. Information with high quality can bring analytical materials, which show strong persuasiveness, while low-quality information content is scarce, which makes individuals unable to perform an effective analysis. Information quality usually re fers to the features related to the content, such as novelty, accuracy, richness, clarity, and objectivity. Among these features, richness is a general term, including a group of message cues reflecting whether the information is sufficient, specific, and analytical [12]. It has been applied in the context of social networking sites, and it was found that the richness of messages can result in higher perceptions of electronic word of mouth and channel credibility [47].

One such cue of richness is the amount of information, with the assumption that more information equates with better quality [11]. Previous efforts focused mainly on structural content richness charac teristics, such as the number of URLs/mentions/hashtags/multimedia and the length of the messages [4,8,30]. However, besides these signal-based structural features, other features of information richness can also be influential factors. It has been shown that most of the posts by government agencies on microblogs are to provide news about administrative matters, such as policy decisions, traffic situations, and financial reporting [31]. These posts are used to state the objective af fairs, so the richness of words contained in news will be a key factor to make the news have high argument quality and easy to communicate [48].

From the perspective of linguistics, a sentence contains many words with different kinds of POS, such as nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, pronouns, prepositions, and conjunctions [49]. These various POS can be divided into two groups—notional words and function words—according to whether a word has a practical meaning [50]. Among these two groups, notional words include mainly nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs, which will contribute more to the richness of the text by conveying a high-quality message [51,52] from the evidence of information richness theory [53]. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H2.1: The number of nouns and verbs in the text has a positive effect on the diffusion of government microblog posts.

H2.2: The number of adjectives and adverbs in the text has a positive effect on the diffusion of government microblog posts.

## 3.2.3. Government-relevant content and the diffusion of government microblog posts

The content factors in social media have been widely examined in previous discussions, suggesting a framework for the factors driving information diffusion in general settings. However, a field-specific model is more critical, which could offer definite guidelines for the strategy in the particular context of social media. In the online com munity of cancer prevention [7], cancer-related content factors, such as personal experience–related and cancer prevention–related topics in the messages and discrete emotions (fear and hope for the cancers), are found to be much more important to determine the attractiveness or vividness of user-generated cancer information. Investigating the rela tionship between topics and information diffusion outcomes could provide insight into the foci of public attention regarding which topic is most prevalent and thus can be most widely diffused. Usually, these studies apply automatic text mining tools [54] or manual coding [31] to detect popular topics in tweets and find how they contribute differently to information diffusion.

Among the different administrative levels of local governments, the city is the most important unit of the national governance system. Thus, municipal-level local governments are the most representative govern ment microblog operators and mostly influence the public online, and are frequently selected as the unit of samples in empirical studies [55, 56]. In this study, when we are focused on local government microblogs of municipal-level agencies, the microblog accounts we selected are the most influential ones with the most followers in a city area. These ac counts are usually operated by comprehensive government agencies. When focusing on the specific function of the posts by these agencies, the information disclosed is more about the whole city itself or comparing the city with other cities. Thus, the city names will be the key signals to determine the relevance of the published posts with government affairs. The more city names a post includes, the more it is definitely relevant with government affairs and with its goals of operating microblogs. Also, these kinds of posts will be more likely to have more government transparency and meet the expectations of public followers, according to the theory of legitimacy [14] and stakeholder theory [39]. Therefore, the quality of the message will be thought to be higher and will be thought to be more definite, which will stimulate the users’ willingness to pay more attention and then diffuse the information. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3. The number of city names in the text has a positive effect on the diffusion of government microblog posts.

## 3.2.4. The homogeneity of content and the diffusion of government microblog posts

The information published by local government agencies includes mainly news, public services, current affairs, and politics. Most previous studies [8,10,31] focused only on how the target posts themselves will determine how they gain attention. However, the posts are competing with each other to gain attention from users, as the total attention is scarce. In social media, we have limited time, so it is impossible to read all information carefully and spread it. Instead, we may prefer to pay attention to fresher information that would be much different from other information, as it may provide unique information. A recent study [57], as one of the few studies investigating the effect of comparison of con tent across different posts, found that divergence in content strategies brings about greater online engagement and attracts more new followers for focal firms.

Because of the similarity of their functions, some posts by different government agencies have similar contents. For example, most of them will forward policy documents from the central government. For public users, the information transmitted by similar posts will be redundant and have high homogeneity, and correspondingly the public’s willing ness to spread the information is relatively low. Usually, government posts with a high degree of similarity and homogeneity with other posts are likely to be templated texts, and it will be difficult to attract the public’s interest. However, original and fresh government posts always convey information in a peer-to-peer style, which could be seen as a dialogue, where users will pay more attention [8]. This can be explained by dialogic communication theory [58], whereby social media provides citizens with a channel for “dialogic communication” with government agencies to exchange ideas and opinions through negotiation under the guidance of intersubjectivity and the desire to achieve mutually satis factory results [10]. Thus, the following hypothesis is given:

H4: The homogeneity of content has a negative impact on the diffusion of government microblog posts.

## 3.2.5. The moderating effects of content topics

In government microblogs, because of different intentions, the posts by government agencies will be very different regarding what the posts are talking about, resulting in various content topics of these posts. Some previous studies suggested that the topic of the post has an important impact on its diffusion. For example, different content topics of health promotion programs are typically differently diffused via social media and the response of the users is different [59]; content analysis of Facebook posts concludes that there is no special topic that guarantees success in terms of discussion frequency, and messages about leisure activities and news about administrative matters seem to be of interest [60]. A similar study on business social media also indicates significant differences in information diffusion for the content topics of information provision and advertisement [61]. By coding of the content topics of microblog posts into 16 categories, the most engaging topics are “public transport” and “housing,” meaning that the content topic is relevant for explaining the different levels of citizens’ engagement [31].

Although these studies have examined the different information diffusion outcomes of different content topics, very limited research exists about how the impacts of determining factors are different for information diffusion of different topics. In government microblogs, public agencies will publish various types of messages for different purposes [31]. Different topics of the online posts will influence the followers differently, and semantic features of the messages may show different effects. For example, a large number of posts on government microblogs belong to political news that reports national activity, po litical activity, the development of politics, the economy, and culture, and incarnates the attitude of the government. Followers are seeking more objective and detailed information when reading this political news, and the use of name entities that contain specific meanings in the content can improve the objectivity and richness. Thus, among the four factors mentioned above, the city names in the content would make the political news more specific and thus get more attention. By contrast, another big group of government microblog posts tend to introduce some living information, such as the weather forecast, lifestyle tips, and entertainment news. When public followers are reading these messages, they are more relaxed and likely to be stimulated by the emotional words, as they can describe the living information more vividly.

Thus, if we classify the content of government microblog posts into two topics, political news and living information, we have the following hypotheses:

H5.1: The effect of emotional words on the diffusion of government microblog posts with the topic of political news will be less than that on the diffusion of government microblog posts with the topic of living information.

H5.2: The effect of city names on the diffusion of government microblog posts with the topic of political news will be greater than that on the diffusion of government microblog posts with the topic of living information.

## 4. Data collection and processing

## 4.1. Collecting data

As one of the most well-known social media platforms in China, Sina Weibo has built a complete operation mechanism and has real-name authentication of government agencies. Therefore, we collected data from Sina Weibo to ensure the integrity and reliability of the dataset. For all capital cities of provinces or subprovincial cities<sup>2</sup> in mainland China, we crawled through the posts published by the government microblog accounts that have the largest number of followers in each corre sponding city, and filtered out accounts that had published fewer than 10,000 posts since the account had been opened, resulting in 29 ac counts being collected. As the total number of posts was too great, we sampled the posts published on only the first 7 days of each month from 2016 to 2018. For each published post, we collected the content, account information, release time, and number of forwards. After removing unreasonable data and duplicate data, we finally got 76,619 posts, and to ease the reproduction of our results, we have made them publicly available on Github<sup>3</sup>.

## 4.2. Extracting extensive features

Firstly, we preprocessed the texts of posts as follows to prepare them for further analysis. As the various unmeaningful symbols in posts could influence the extraction process, we first used regular expressions in Python to remove a number of nontext symbols such as punctuation. With the Chinese word segmentation tool jieba<sup>4</sup>, the sentences were accurately divided into different words. Considering stop words such as prepositions and conjunctions in the texts that have no actual semantics and would interfere with subsequent analysis, we deleted the stop words in the parsed text on the basis of a public dictionary<sup>5</sup>.

Secondly, we extracted the content features of the posts processed by the prior steps, defined in Table 1. Following the hypothesis, the se mantic features to be extracted include the number of positive words, negative words, nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and city names in each post, and the similarity among the texts. Specially, we used a freely available dictionary<sup>6</sup> to recognize positive and negative words from the content. For the identification of nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs, a Python package for the Language Technology Platform (LTP) (Pyltp li brary<sup>7</sup>) was called to tag the POS of a sentence.

Computing the similarity between posts, we first extracted the term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) [62] vectors of all posts and adopted the cosine similarity measurement. Assuming the TF-IDF vectors of two posts are $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 } ,$ , the similarity sim(t , t ) between t and $t _ { 2 }$ is computed as follows:

$$
\operatorname{sim} \left(t _ {1}, t _ {2}\right) = \frac {t _ {1} \cdot t _ {2}}{\| t _ {1} \| \cdot \| t _ {2}}.\tag{1}
$$

The aggregate homogeneity of a given post t is represented as the average similarity between $t _ { i }$ and all other posts published in the same month as $t _ { i } { \mathrm { : } }$

$$
\text { similarity } (t _ {i}) = \frac {1}{k} \sum_ {t _ {j} \in M (t _ {i})} \text { sim } (t _ {i}, t _ {j}).\tag{2}
$$

To be clear, similarity(t ) represents the homogeneity of $t _ { i }$ and $M ( t _ { i } )$ denotes the set of posts published in the same month as post $t _ { i } ,$ where k is the number of posts in this month and $t _ { j }$ is a member of $M ( t _ { i } )$ .

## 4.3. Coding the two content topics

Another important variable in our research to construct is content topics. We first coded 6260 government posts randomly extracted from the whole dataset into two topics, political news and living information. To ensure the accuracy of coding, the process was conducted manually by three research assistants after training. Thus, we got a labeled data set, of which 4411 posts are labeled as the “living information” and 1849 posts are in the category of “political news.” “Living information” refers to online posts that provide some information about everyday life, such as the weather forecast, lifestyle tips, and entertainment news. “Political news” refers to information published about national or regional events, public policy, the status of the economy, international relationships, etc. Examples of posts on these two topics are shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

Then we trained a classification model by Text GCN [63]<sup>8</sup> to classify whether a post belongs to “living information” or “political news” for our 6260 coded posts (Text GCN is a newly developed text classification model that uses a graph convolution network and can produce robust results). Before the final classification on uncoded posts, we firstly performed model selection on a training set of 3326 posts and a vali dation set of the remaining 2934 posts. The precision, recall, and F-measure [64] for “living information” for the validation set are 94.77%, 79.87%, and 86.68%, respectively; for the topic of “political news,” they are 78.25%, 89.13%, and 83.33%, respectively. For the training set, they are 98.74%, 91.35%, and 94.90%, respectively, for “living information” and 82.90%, 97.30%, and 89.53%, respectively, for “political news.” We then applied this model to classify other uncoded posts into the two topics.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Main findings

We have investigated mainly the effects of the extensive semantic features from the content on the diffusion of government microblog posts, including the individual impact of each feature and the difference among different topics of posts, shown in Fig. 10. Several interesting findings are reached by empirical analysis on a real data set from Sina Weibo. The results indicate that government microblog posts containing more subjective words as well as positive emotional words and city names are more likely to receive more forwards from the public. How ever, public users tend to reduce the possibility of forwarding posts that contain more negative words or are similar to other posts regrading the content.

We also find a difference between different content topics in that the diffusion of “living information” posts is more sensitive to the positive emotional words in them. In contrast, the significant negative effect of city names implies that the diffusion of “political news” is more influ enced than the diffusion of “living information” by the appearance of city names. Unexpectedly, the effect of nouns and verbs on the diffusion of government microblog posts is negatively significant.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

This research provides several insights from analytical results and contributes to theoretical studies in the following ways.

Generally, exploring information diffusion of government micro blogs on social media raises a different research question. Public engagement on government microblogs is very different from general information interaction. In general social media campaigns, users’ be haviors are more casual, while on government social media, they are more concerned about the outcomes of their behaviors as they are involved in the more formal environment [45]. Specifically, most prior studies focusing on the factors driving information diffusion relied on general factors, such as the number of publisher’s followers/followees and the presence of urls/sentiments/mentions/hashtags in the content [6]. In this work, we care more about the government affairs–specific content factors. Thus, our research contributes to the information diffusion and government social media literature and provides insights regarding what factors induce users to diffuse posts on government microblogs.

Firstly, for the effect of sentiment signals of contents, both positive and negative emotions can promote information diffusion outcomes in the general social media context as both of these kinds of sentiment can stimulate users, or even negative emotions are found to be more sig nificant for promoting the participation of business communities [9]. However, users are more cautious about publishing or sharing negative information in the field of government microblogs, and negative emo tions in the content published by government agencies are used more to describe objective things than to express extreme views on e-business applications [66] or in fields of public opinion [34]. Thus, negative words in this study are found to have a negative impact on information diffusion, while positive words have a significant positive effect, which provides a new understanding of the effects of emotional signals in the particular context of government microblogs.

Secondly, although the difference in information diffusion of general content topics has been well examined [31], it is necessary to recognize more government affairs–specific topics from the content and see how they would impact information diffusion. Thus, we propose extracting government affairs–relevant content by counting the number of city names in the posts. Moreover, instead of examining only the effect of the content of target posts themselves, we try to investigate how the content similarity or difference of the texts compared with other posts will impact the information diffusion outcomes, as the functions of different government microblogs are similar and they will attract more audiences by distinguishing them from others.

Thirdly, information richness as an important factor for information diffusion on social media has been widely studied, but mostly structured features, such as the number of images, videos, mentions, hashtags, and links, are extracted to measure the content richness [4,8,30,31]. How ever, some unstructured features can also be mined to measure the in formation richness. Specifically, we propose identifying the POS in the content and counting the POS tags of notional words, including nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs, which is the first attempt to understand the effects of information diffusion as far as we know. The results indi cate the positive effect of adjectives and adverbs, but not nouns and verbs because posts with many nouns and verbs are usually redundant with similar information as shown in empirical study. Also, this finding provides new evidence to understand the variables in related theories, such as the argument quality as central routes in the ELM [11], infor mation quality in the information adoption model [15], and perceived gratifications in U&G theory [27].

Finally, although the different diffusion outcomes of government microblog posts on different topics were examined in previous research [31], only the separate impact of content topics was the focus. In this study, we have contributed to the research community by examining the different effects of emotional words and government-related content on the diffusion of government microblog posts with different topics. Thus, this study provides a deeper theoretical insight into how the diffusion mechanism is different for different content topics-related online in formation. For living information, the public is easily moved by the exquisite and attractive language of the content by use of positive emotional words. However, the public cares more about the specifics in the content of microblog posts so that more detailed information will be revealed when reading political news and to decide whether to diffuse it.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

The findings of this study also provide important managerial guidelines for government microblog service providers, operators of government microblog accounts, and other microblog users.

(1) For microblog service providers. Because of high competition and low switching costs, generating revenue and platform attractiveness requires a stable but active user base as an important prerequisite for microblog service providers, such as Sina Weibo. To enhance the adhesiveness of users, more platforms are using recommendation sys tems to push information that they think users will be interested in and attracted by [67]. In this case, this study offers them insights into how to recommend more effective results that will attract much attention from users. According to our findings, microblog service providers could consider recommending government microblog posts that contain more positive emotional words, city names, and subjective words. If they wish to recommend political news published by government agencies, it is suggested they select those posts with much government-relevant con tent, such as city names. Also, recommending living information by government microblogs with more positive emotional words would also be a good strategy.

(2) For government microblog operators. Our results show that positive words in the content are more likely to arouse the public’s intention to share the government information, while negative words have an insignificant impact. Thus, social media operators of government microblog accounts should consider publishing more positive informa tion, such as “positive energy,”<sup>9</sup> which will be more prevalent among the public. With regard to the content, more government affairs–relevant content should be posted, such as “dry goods,”<sup>10</sup> especially for political news, while irrelevant content should be published less. Attractive and exquisite language in the content with more positive emotional words and adjectives should be considered when posts are being written and edited, especially for living information posts. When users try to follow a government microblog account, they are more likely to prefer to receive original government content rather than other common information that can be easily obtained from many other channels. Thus, another implication is that government microblogs can increase competitiveness by publishing more dissimilar information to better design their social media strategies. The dissimilar contents are more likely to grab the public’s attention and promote the public’s intention to share them.

(3) For other microblog users. Although our study is focused on the mechanism of information diffusion in the context of government microblogs, an understanding of such an effect can also benefit other microblog users as well as government agencies. For business and marketing managers who seek to optimize social media strategies and increase their engagement with consumers on social media, publishing online posts with high argument quality and rich content with more positive emotional words and other subjective stimulus words would make sense. Moreover, information that can be highly different from that of competitors is an ideal choice. Another important implication is that managers should consider citizen/customer psychology and be cautious when using negative words in their published posts, as negative words play different roles in the persuasiveness of information in different contexts. Beyond providing managerial insights, our findings will also be useful for common users (citizens or consumers) who want to promote themselves on social media.

## 7. Conclusion

With the Internet and social media, more people are becoming involved in public affairs and interacting with government agencies when they are sharing or writing comments on online government posts. Aiming at deeper insight into the factors influencing diffusion of gov ernment microblogs, we initiated this study to investigate the effect of various semantic features from the content. Data from government microblogs of China’s municipal-level government accounts on the Sina Weibo platform from 2016 to 2018 were collected to achieve our research goal.

We found a positive impact of positive emotional words, adjectives and adverbs, and city names contained in the content on the diffusion of government microblog posts. Also, content similarity and negative words have a negative impact on the diffusion outcomes. Furthermore, different effects of posts of “living information” and “political news” were found. The empirical analysis suggests that the diffusion of living information is more likely to be affected by the positive emotional words in the content, while the effect of city names for political news is greater than that for living information. As well as the contribution to previous studies in a theoretical way, our findings provide several practical im plications for microblog service providers and government agencies.

Our study is not without limitations. Firstly, as we find from the regression results, for the POS of nouns and verbs, the total frequency would not be an important factor of information diffusion. Thus, further study will investigate in depth how to extract features concerning the richness of the content, such as counting the number of different types of nouns, or the distribution over different types. Secondly, we only studied the government-related content of a post by measuring how many city names it contains, and the effects of more specific topics could be further studied. Also, we tested our theoretical model only in the context of Chinese government microblogs, and the impact of semantic features on government microblog diffusion may differ greatly among different languages and cultures. Thus, future work could attempt a comparative study of different languages of government microblogs to improve the generalizability of the findings.
