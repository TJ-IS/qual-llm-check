# Generativity and the paradox of stability and flexibility in a platform architecture: A case of the Oracle Cloud Platform

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Platform Generativity Architecture Paradox Stability Flexibility Oracle Cloud

## A B S T R A C T

Generativity is a technology’s capability of producing new outputs without input from the originator. Platforms are important technologies that embrace generativity. While the literature generally assumes that generativity arises from platform governance and high-level platform design, we propose that generativity also arises from a platform’s three architectural components: the base, the interface, and the add-ons. Drawing on a case study of the Oracle Cloud Platform, we reveal how generativity emerges through the paradox of stability and flexibility in a platform’s architectural components. Further, standardization navigates this paradox by coordinating the dependencies between stability and flexibility across heterogeneous stakeholders

## 1. Introduction

“For tech to be a real driver of innovation and growth, IT needs to reorganize itself around flexible and independent platforms” ([1], p. 1). Leading players have been reshaping their business and core technology through platform transformations, ranging from enterprise systems, to mobile operating systems, to game consoles [2–5]. In general. a platform is a set of shared digital components (e.g., a database and the operating environment) that enables the creation of a wide range of services (e.g., applications) through boundary resources (e.g., application program ming interfaces [APIs] and software development kits [SDKs]) [6–10]. A platform exhibits a layered architecture [6], where “lower-level layers” (e.g., a database) enable and support functions at “higher-level layers” (e.g., applications). A platform centralizes network effects created by complementary inputs from heterogeneous, but loosely interconnected stakeholders (e.g., platform owners, service providers, and end users) in an ecosystem [6,11–13].

Why is a platform different? Although platforms exist as varied systems, they share a common characteristic: generativity [9,14–18]. Generativity refers to a platform’s “overall capacity to produce un prompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences” ([16], p. 1980). For example, although Apple only creates limited apps, it incorporates the iOS platform to facilitate an extensive variety of app development activities from third parties [19]. Scholars describe generativity as “the fruit of an urge to harness the possibilities enabled by the flexibility of digitizing” ([20], p. 753), simultaneously requiring “the necessary stability for a system in an evolutionary environment” ([17], p. 228). Here, stability refers to a platform’s ability to restrict negative change, whereas flexibility refers to a platform’s ability to embrace productive change. Thus, to realize generativity, a platform should navigate a paradoxical relationship between stability and flexi bility [2,17,21].

The paradox of stability and flexibility has drawn significant atten tion from research commentaries [2,20,22,23]. For example, Tiwana et al. regard the paradox as “[a] central governance challenge,” holding that “a platform owner must retain sufficient control to ensure the integrity of the platform while relinquishing enough control to encourage innovation” ([2], p. 679). Similarly, Tilson et al. suggest that a platform “need[s] to be organized in ways that are simultaneously stable and flexible” ([20], p. 753). Empirical studies find that the effects of the paradox of stability and flexibility depend on (1) management practices that can stimulate development activities from the third party and (2) strategic decisions that can bundle third-party-developed ser vices with the rest of the platform [11,24–27]. These findings rely on a common assumption that a platform architecture will functionally support the implementation of management practices and strategic de cisions about how to navigate the paradox and, therefore, realize generativity.

However, such an assumption is not necessarily warranted, partic ularly when we consider that a platform architecture is never fully complete because it is designed to accommodate uncertainties and diverse demands [12,16]. For example, if the owner of an enterprise platform decided to introduce stronger data processing power to respond to emerging requirements of the internet of things, the owner would expect extensive architectural variations to accommodate new applications, which, in turn, might overwhelm the platform’s existing capacity. Accordingly, a platform architecture should be “selfreferential” to support management practices and strategic decisions [17,21,28]. However, the “materiality” of a platform architecture re mains a “black box” and requires a more precise and nuanced under standing [17,21]. In this case, focusing only on platform governance underplays the role of platform architecture in realizing generativity [2, 29,30]. These considerations lead to our research question: how does a platform architecture realize generativity?

To answer the research question, we first identified three primary architectural components (i.e., the base, the interface, and the add-ons) of a platform through content analysis [31]. Identifying these architec tural components is important because “platforms are competing on multiple levels of the technical architecture,” and “specifying the appropriate unit of analysis is becoming increasingly difficult” ([21], p. 125). Using the primary components as themes, we then followed an interpretive literature review approach [32] to show that generativity can arise from the paradox of stability and flexibility in each component as interactive loops. To enhance validity, we conducted an in-depth case study [33] of the Oracle Cloud Platform (OCP) and highlighted the important role of standardization in navigating the paradox to realize generativity. Our study both theoretically and empirically extends the concept of generativity from a platform governance perspective to a platform architecture perspective. Further, we show implications for applying the concept of generativity to platform design in the context of cloud computing. The overarching endeavor is to put technology ar chitectures closer to the center of information systems (IS) research [2, $^ { 1 7 , 2 0 , 2 2 , 3 4 ] }$

## 2. Theoretical foundation

This section develops the theoretical foundation for our study. We first address the main views and concepts related to platforms, and then extract three key architectural components from definitions of platforms in the literature. Next, we use the three components as themes to develop a preliminary conceptual model using the concept of the paradox of stability and flexibility as a lens.

## 2.1. Platforms and relevant concepts

Scholars from different disciplines have adopted the platform concept with varying understandings. In economics, a platform repre sents a two-sided market in which two distinct user groups (e.g., buyers and sellers) benefit from network effects [35]. In management, a plat form describes a collection of assets that are shared by a set of products [6]. In computer science, a platform could be a hardware configuration, an operating system, or a software framework on which various asso ciated components run [27].

Two broad streams of the IS literature have extended the concept of platform to understand a range of phenomena. The first stream focuses on platform governance [3,11,14,26,36,37]. This stream generally argues that a platform would benefit from network effects if it could federate and coordinate third-party service providers to innovate and compete in the platform ecosystem [23,38–40]. For example, Parker et al. [26] found that a platform owner would use open external contracts in preference to closed vertical integration, through which the locus of value creation moved from inside production efficiency to outside innovation and competition. The second stream focuses on platform design [20,29,41,42]. This stream generally suggests that a platform should be designed as a modular system that decomposes a complex system into discrete modules—a set of core components with low variety and a set of peripheral components with high variety [20,29,43]. In this view, a platform should embrace multiple components $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ modules) that exhibit different configurations of variety and reusability to support its “evolvability” over time [22]. Despite the longstanding conceptualization of a platform as a com plex modular system [2,20–23], a review of the literature raises the concern that, while many studies define platforms in the context of their specific research objective (see Table A1 in the Appendices), few provide a well-supported view of a platform’s architectural components. Our literature review yielded only one article that explicitly indicated a platform’s multiple architectural components [43]. However, this study was limited by focusing on designing platforms to support online com munities and, in turn, left theoretical robustness to be further developed. The consequence of the limited treatment of a platform’s architectural components is that the literature focuses almost entirely on the value of platform governance, while treating the platform architecture as a black box [17,21]. Rich insights into platform governance are desirable, but focusing on platform governance alone is problematical and may have lured scholars into believing that there is no problem with the archi tecture that supports platform governance and overall functionality [50]. The problem stemming from lack of understanding of a platform architecture is now becoming critical, evident in the increasing chal lenge for platform transformations [6,46].

## 2.2. Defining a platform’s architectural components

We describe a platform architecture as the conceptual structure of different components and their relationships that are associated with a platform [53]. A platform exhibits a layered architecture, where lower-level layers enable and support functions at higher levels [6,23]. A clear identification of a platform’s architectural components is important because it permits a more accurate understanding of the focal technology (i.e., platforms) [21]. Further, defining components (i.e., a platform’s architectural components) that constitute a concept (i.e., a platform) is critical for theory development [33]. For example, there has been value perceived in defining the concept of system use as an inte gration of three basic components: user, system, and task [54]. To provide a comprehensive understanding of a platform architecture and its primary components, we conducted a qualitative content analysis of the definitions of platforms. Content analysis is a suitable approach because it can be used to reveal common ground among definitions by separating each definition into basic and meaningful conceptual struc tures—in our case, platform architectural components [31.55]. Specif ically, we searched articles published by leading IS outlets between 2000 and 2019, using keywords “platforms" and synonyms thereof. As a result, we identified 24 articles that define a platform as a complex modular system after excluding irrelevant meanings $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . ,$ multisided platform markets, account platforms, and platform economies). We then extracted definitions of platforms from the identified articles. Based on the definitions, we inductively formulated categories regarding the textural material that describes similar objects. Table A1 offers more details of the results. The results show that the IS literature has used the term “platform” to describe a variety of systems (e.g., enterprise sys tems, mobile operating systems, web browsers, and game consoles), yet three common architectural components of a platform can be detected: the base, the interface, and the add-ons. Table 2 summarizes definitions of the three components with examples.

The base refers to an extensible set of IT infrastructures that acts as a foundation for and is shared by the focal platform ecosystem. The base includes a platform’s main computing resources, such as central pro cessing units, servers, databases, and mainframe operating systems. The literature has used terms such as “an extensible technological founda tion” [56], “an extensible digital core” [57] and “extensible codebases” [2,58] to describe the base. Many definitions in our review expressed the idea that a platform’s base should be “extensible”—that is, be reusable and reprogrammable to support the requirement of evolution [17]. The base’s extensibility is rooted in an IT infrastructure’s three characteris tics, which are (1) programmability—as long as users agree on the meaning of digital data, the infrastructure allows for different ways to manipulate the data, (2) homogenization—any digital content (e.g., audios, videos, texts, and images) can be accessed by multiple digital devices and (3) self-reference—other platform parts require the use of the IT infrastructure and, in turn, accelerate the evolution of the infra structure [22]. Other complementary parts (e.g., APIs, protocols, and apps) are built on the base.

The interface refers to boundary resources that coordinate the con nectivity between a platform’s base and other complementary parts. Terms, such as “connectivity” [14], “network layer” [26], and “tech nology toolkit” [59] are used to describe the platform interface in the literature. The interface is an important component because it controls the access to a platform’s base and supports heterogeneous value-adding activities (e.g., app development) [59]. Typical examples of an interface are APIs and SDKs that facilitate the arms’ length relationships between platform owners and providers [60]. The literature suggests that the interface could be a focal unit of analysis when “used in the context of highly distributed arrangements where independent actors pragmati cally engage in innovations utilizing the opportunities and limitations of [a platform’s] digital or layered-modular arrangements” ([21], p. 131). For example, Apple provides the Xcode development environment with specific SDKs to coordinate the traffic between its iOS core and third-party apps. Thus, Apple can monitor all stages of value creation activities (i.e., design, development, debugging, testing, and publishing) on the iOS platform [19].

Add-ons refer to the products and services that add functionality to a platform ecosystem. The literature describes add-ons using terms, such as “extension” [56], “complementary products” [53] and “peripheral component” [51]. Add-ons can be in the form of apps (e.g., smartphone apps) or content (e.g., texts, videos, and audios on social media) made available by third-party providers (sometimes by end users such as content on Facebook) to satisfy broad user requirements [60]. Add-ons define the nature of a platform. For example, iOS add-ons (mobile apps) define iOS as a mobile operating platform, and Facebook add-ons (user-generated content) define Facebook as a social media platform [21]. The quality and quantity of add-ons determine a platform’s market performance by creating stronger network effects [61]. For example, for video game platforms (e.g., Nintendo, PlayStation, and Xbox), end users appreciate a large number of high-quality games, while game providers benefit from a large base of end users and, in turn, develop more games with higher quality to attract more end users [53].

## 2.3. Generativity and the paradox of stability and flexibility

Generativity refers to “a technology’s overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audi ences” ([16], p. 1980). Generativity in a platform is accomplished through a modular structure—interdependencies between platform components whereby change can spring up independently from one component without extensively affecting other components $[ 9 , 1 7 , 2 2 ,$ 62]. For example, on the iOS platform, change in adding new features for an app will not affect the iOS core or Xcode development environment. Generativity enables a platform to self-organize resource generating, assembling, and distributing, which allows the platform ecosystem to rapidly respond to diverse demands [20]. Platform gen erativity can arise from a paradoxical relationship between stability and flexibility [20,63]. That is, to realize generativity, a platform needs to be “organized in ways that are simultaneously stable and flexible as well as offering both control and autonomy” ([20], p. 753). From a governance perspective, strategically granting a greater level of access to a plat form’s resources can accelerate new service development, yet it is still necessary to retain some control over core resources to ensure quality [24]. From a design perspective, platform design should follow a “layered modular architecture” whereby heterogeneous layers follow multiple design hierarchies and are able to integrate heterogeneous re sources [22]. We both build on and depart from these insights to explore how generativity is enacted by a paradox of stability and flexibility in a platform’s three architectural components.

A paradox denotes the status of “contradictory yet interrelated ele ments that exist simultaneously and persist over time” ([64], p. 382), and is characterized by two aspects: (1) elements that seem logically opposite, even absurd, when juxtaposed and (2) responses that embrace the opposite elements simultaneously [65]. We define stability as a sys tem’s status of being resistant to change, and flexibility as a system’s status of being responsive to change. For example, an aircraft piloting system is stable because it resists unconventional operations, whereas an open-source system is flexible because it allows anyone to modify the source codes. When viewed as a paradox, stability and flexibility are conceptually opposite, but are also interdependent and potentially compatible [66]. Commentary by Tilson et al. [20] offers an insightful basis to understand the paradox of stability and flexibility in platforms, and encourages more research to make sense of the complex in terrelationships between contradictory elements in modern IS design. Specifically, they propose two perspectives on paradox: (1) the paradox of control from a governance perspective and (2) the paradox of change from a design perspective. These two perspectives echo the two streams (i.e., governance and design) of the IS literature we reviewed.

The paradox of control considers the governance practices of het erogeneous platform stakeholders and their preferences on modes of control related to change [20]. The literature focuses on how to “deli cately balance” control points between centralization and decentral ization of certain rights, and access to value creation activities $[ 2 , 6 7 ]$ These activities involve various aspects related to self-organizing [68], intellectual property (IP) sharing [69], control of main resources [50] and appropriation of value [45]. For example, Apple encourages mil lions of third-party developers to contribute to app development for its iOS platform, while, in the meantime, exercising strict control over app review, payment terms, resource access rules and many other aspects of app operations [19].

The paradox of change follows directly from platform design because it considers “how generativity emerges” and is defined by “the opposing logics of stability and flexibility that operate across infrastructural layers and components” ([20], p. 753). For example, to facilitate innovative content generation, an online community platform should be designed with tailored and extendable functions (e.g., open data and social network APIs) that can be applied to multiple contexts reflecting specific needs, while retaining a set of core functions (e.g., authentication and authorization) with low variety [43]. In this case, the set of core func tions allows new parts to be created without sacrificing the platform’s overall integrity, while the extensible functions refine how the stable functions are structured through feedback and reflection [20].

Although a growing body of the IS literature has tried to explicate the paradox of stability and flexibility in a platform, we observed that the literature to date has been built on two assumptions. First, the platform governance literature generally assumes that a platform’s architecture will naturally and functionally support the governance practices based on it. Second, the platform design literature generally assumes that the paradox of stability and flexibility is an outcome of the design decisions at a high level of the platform as a whole, focusing on the focal plat form’s degree of openness towards value creation activities from third parties. However, these assumptions overlook the question of the unit of analysis (i.e., what are the appropriate architectural components for studying platforms? $[ 1 7 , 2 0 , 2 1 ] )$ to ensure that the governance practices will be effective and that the high-level design will yield value in real izing generativity.

To illustrate the importance of gaining more insights into a plat form’s architectural components, consider the case of the iOS platform. Although the iOS operating environment is often studied as the focal unit of analysis [19,60,70], new phenomena are emerging from both apps and the iOS core. For example, despite the strict control over resource access, third-party apps could still “jailbreak” to remove re strictions on app development and downloads. In this case, the iOS operating environment needs to respond by either accommodating or further restricting the jailbreaking apps’ access to its core resources. Accordingly, the iOS core would need to readjust which parts of the core resources could be exposed to third parties. The Xcode would then need to coordinate the accessibility of the core resources, and the apps would need to reconsider their compatibility with the iOS core and Xcode accordingly. Thus, shifting units of analysis across different architec tural components complements the literature based on the governance and design “as-a-whole” perspectives in understanding platform gen erativity [21].

## 2.4. A model for generativity in a platform’s architectural components

To elaborate on the paradox of stability and flexibility in platform components and how the paradox relates to platform generativity, we conducted an interpretive literature review [32]. An interpretive liter ature review is appropriate because we seek to “develop theory” that is “unarticulated in published review” ([32], pp. 182–183). Specifically, we conducted the review in three steps: (1) we used our initial literature review (see Table A1) as a base, extracting papers that offers insights into platform architectures and components; (2) we then went back wards and forwards to identify relevant papers citing the key papers in the previous step [71]; and (3) finally, we analyzed the identified arti cles with the three architectural components $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the base. the inter face, and the add-ons) as themes, and developed preliminary propositions for further examination. Table 3 shows the 12 core articles identified in our review, with their views of the concepts of stability and flexibility in the design of the platform architecture and resultant outcomes.

Generativity in the base is associated with the resources’ availability to adjust the distribution of a platform’s core resources. Flexibility in the base is reflected in placing fewer restrictions on the use of a platform’s core resources to acquire inputs from a broader pool of contributors. For example, the base should share some IP with third parties to accom modate diverse use cases [24,69]. However, flexibility in the base leads to distributed use of a platform’s core resources, which, in turn, creates a challenge of “accumulating and consolidating diverse contributions to a single object [the base]” ([24], pp. 1853–1854). Thus, the base requires some mechanisms to tackle the challenge. Stability in the base is, therefore, reflected in placing restrictions on using a platform’s core resources by retaining a set of inalterable common features $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . ,$ , data files, controlfiles, and redo logs). For example, a platform should restrict changes to its resources that are directly related to the platform’s core competency [50,53] and apply design rules to regulate altering activities [24]. Given the dual characteristics of stability and flexibility, gen erativity in the base transforms “a singular universal material that as sumes the most diverse meanings” ([20], p. 752). The singular universal material represents stability. That is, the base contains prescribed com mon features for designing, developing, and managing other compo nents to protect its core artefact from misuse. Conversely, the diverse meanings represent flexibility, illustrating possibilities that build on the base. These possibilities reflect the heterogeneous interpretations that third parties hold about how to use a platform’s core resources. Thus, generativity is empowered by the mutual understanding that a plat form’s core resources must be properly utilized to meet diverse needs. Stability consolidates different uses of a platform’s core resources, whereas flexibility accommodates the broad uses of a platform’s core resources. Accordingly, we present our first proposition.

Proposition 1. In the platform base, generativity arises from the paradox of retaining unalterable core resources (i.e., stability) and allowing some core resources for sharing (i.e., flexibility).

Generativity in the interface is associated with the digital accessi bility to expand the breadth of value-adding activities without disrupt ing the platform’s core competency. Flexibility is naturally embedded in the interface because the basic premise of a platform’s interface is to enact complementary value creation activities (e.g., app development) [21,22]. For example, in a mobile operating platform, granting access to a platform’s core resources (e.g., code libraries and development tem plates) through boundary resources (e.g., APIs and SDKs) can exten sively spur app development activities [57,60]. However, with increasing third-party access to a platform’s core resources, a natural consideration is how a platform retains its functional integration. Sta bility defines the specifications of the digital connections and ensures compatibility between complementary value-adding activities and the platform’s core resources. Specifically, “control points” embedded in boundary resources can secure a platform’s meta resources (e.g., source code) by preventing risky app development [60,72]. Ghazawneh and Henfridsson [60] suggested that the joint effect of resourcing (i.e., enhancing the scope and diversity of a platform) and securing (i.e., increasing control over a platform) can generate a balance between the quantity and quality of third-party-developed services. Similarly, Kazan et al. [42] proposed two approaches to platform interface design: (1) designing access intentionally to co-innovate with third parties and (2) arranging standards in ways that culminate in vertically integrated systems. They found that both approaches should be applied inter changeably to respond to dynamic market demands. Stability prevents risky value-adding activities from damaging a platform’s functional integration, whereas flexibility enables a wide range of value-adding activities to contribute to the platform. Accordingly, we develop ou second proposition.

Proposition 2. In the platform interface, generativity arises from the paradox of restricting access to core resources (i.e., stability) and granting access to core resources (i.e., flexibility).

Generativity in add-ons is associated with the adaptability to respond to diverse demands at the direct-use end. Flexibility reflects addons’ loosely coupled structure in which change in one part does not affect other parts’ operation. Platform-based apps generally contain some common structural parts such as a front-end user interface, a backend service, and a database. Loosely coupled apps dissociate these parts, as each part could have its own separate development cycle with min imal dependency [73]. In this case, loosely coupled add-ons encourage third parties to continually contribute to the focal platform because it reduces the effort in managing dependencies within the add-on and between the add-on and the platform [49,56]. However, some parts should be tightly coupled because a loosely coupled structure may reduce add-ons’ overall performance due to inefficient data transmission [56]. Thus, stability reflects such a tightly coupled structure in which parts of an add-on are indivisible. Tiwana used the Blackberry OS apps as an example to illustrate tightly coupled apps as “a single directory hierarchy of NodeJS code or a single Java web application resource … file” ([61], p. 843). They found that tightly coupled add-ons can better leverage a platform’s evolving capabilities. Thus, generativity in add-ons determines the platform’s direct-use value and market perfor mance by engaging both stability and flexibility. Flexibility augments a platform’s market reach by increasing quantity of services, whereas stability ensures quality of these services, making them consistent with the platform’s gene. Thus, the platform could better adapt to the dual requirement of diversity and quality from the ever-changing business environment. We summarize our third proposition as such.

Proposition 3. In platform add-ons, generativity arises from the paradox of embracing tightly coupled structures $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ stability) and accommodating loosely coupled structures (i.e., flexibility).

In sum, Fig. 2 shows a preliminary model and highlights that the paradox of stability and flexibility in each of the three architectural components empowers platform generativity. As such, this model adds more specific insights to the literature that merely focuses on high-level platform design.

## 3. Method

Using an in-depth case study to refine a preliminary theoretical model and propositions is a suitable approach for theory building [74, 75], because “the process of developing a scientific theory employs hypothetico-deductive logic, which allows a scientist to craft the theo ry’s propositions so as to be not only logical, … but also empirical” ([76], p. 229). Such a research approach for proposition development with an empirical case study as proof of concept has had its rigor his torically demonstrated by the literature [77–80].

We adopted an embedded single-case study to examine the pre liminary model [81,82] for two reasons. First, there is a scarcity of theory explaining generativity in a platform’s architectural components.

Although Oracle has strong data management power to support large volumes of data transition in different cloud business cases, the OCP requires the existing architecture to better embrace the variety and velocity of data transition from the ever-changing use cases.

We selected the OCP as the locus for our case study for three reasons. First, cloud computing’s three main service models match a platform’s three basic architectural components: infrastructure as a service $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the base), platform as a service $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the interface) and software as a service $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ add-ons). In the initial focus group, Marco’s comment added to how the Cloud transformation and platform architecture design may affect Oracle and the whole technology service industry:

This Cloud transformation is probably the most massive change I have ever seen from the inside of Oracle, much as you work for companies in a similar situation. ## 4.1. The base: the multitenant database architecture

Oracle hosted core database resources in standalone data silos before transforming to the Cloud. However, client-side and third-party de velopers could not effectively use the core database resources because each use case required different pieces of resources from isolated data silos. Jennie explained that:

in a single-user database, a user can modify data without concern for other users modifying the same data at the same time. However, in a multiuser database, statements with multiple simultaneous trans actions may update the same data. Transactions executing simulta neously must produce meaningful and consistent results.

In this case, Oracle needed to redesign the OCP’s foundation data base architecture to better consolidate and distribute core computing resources. In July 2013, Oracle released a newly designed multitenant database architecture (i.e., Oracle 12c). Jennie and Marco described it as the “generalized technology base underneath” the OCP. The multitenant architecture design was the biggest architectural change in the history of the Oracle database by structuring a central data root domain and a pluggable domain in Oracle’s underpinning database management system.

Stability was encapsulated in the root domain. The root was a collection of Oracle-supplied metadata and common data schemas (e.g., controlfiles, datafiles, tempfiles, and redo logs) that most data reading and writing activities would use. The root did not store user data. Only Oracle licensed database administrators who had been granted the “create session” privilege could modify system-supplied schemes in the root. In this case, Oracle maintained complete control over the root to ensure that (1) every developer could access the core database resources at the same time (i.e., data concurrency) and that (2) every developer saw a consistent view of the data (i.e., data consistency). Jennie described the root as:

a unified infrastructure with a unified data model. Because we have this unified base, users can drop in whatever they want and when ever they want it. It will work the same way and it is the part that we control.

Flexibility was encapsulated in the pluggable domain. The pluggable domain contained user-created data schemas (i.e., pluggable databases) required for a specific set of features. A client-side or third-party developer could add a pluggable database to support a specific appli cation (e.g., a human resource module) for a specific use case (e.g., the higher education industry). The developer who created the pluggable database following a predefined template (i.e., the seed) could own the IP of the pluggable database. TechTarget reporters described the plug gable database design as “the most talked-about features of Oracle 12c” because it extensively expanded Oracle’s service range from industryspecific to diverse use cases. Marco further explained the importance of the pluggable databases:

We built a core infrastructure that is interoperable. People can expose the data in different contexts by plugging and playing in a sense out of their own interests. This is not particularly industry specific or functional specific. It is open. I think this is the key point.

In sum, traditional standalone data silos were insufficient to support diverse demands in the Cloud context. Designing a multitenant database architecture offered a solid ground for Oracle to consolidate multiple cases around its centralized data processing core competency. Specif ically, the root domain was a meta-database containing the core data resources controlled by Oracle and shared by the entire OCP, whereas the pluggable domain was a functional database tailored for multiple use cases and developed by third parties. The linkage between the root and the pluggable domains was an Oracle-supplied data access template. Sai summarized the design logic:

When Oracle encourages third-party or internally developed new capabilities, we try to integrate the capabilities to the DNA of Oracle. The new capabilities will be injected into the core data structure. At the end of the day, the Oracle Cloud still has the integrated service experience but in many specific uses. That is how Oracle has been trying to build the base underneath and even the whole Cloud architecture.

## 4.2. The interface: the fusion middleware architecture

Oracle encountered a critical challenge after introducing the multi. tenant database architecture, which is how to seamlessly integrate, but not restrict, third-party-developed contents with the rest of the OCP. Such a challenge required Oracle to add a “middle layer” to coordinate the accessibility between the OCP’s underpinning databases and useside services. Jennie described the challenge as how to “[integrate] all those pieces of multitenant structure and all applications together. So, the challenge is about interoperability.” Sai further explained the concept of “interoperability” that “Oracle is going to both horizontally and vertically manage the connection between the Cloud components. Interoperability defines the connection.” Oracle created a generic computing level called the Fusion middleware to respond to the chal lenge of interoperability. The Fusion middleware was a collection of standard-based software products that spanned a range of services from

Java EE developer tools to integration services, identity management, and business intelligence. Bill added:

Oracle understands that most of our customers operate in a very heterogeneous IT environment. So, they may have pieces of Oracle, pieces of SAP, pieces of Salesforce, and they likely have legacy sys tems with custom-built applications. Fusion ensures that any piece of services that users add to the Cloud can interoperate with the rest of the Cloud but in a manageable fashion.

Stability was embedded in the “control nodes” of the Fusion mid dleware. A control node upheld software development policy (e.g., identity, API management catalog and security control) to coordinate end-to-end connections from the OCP’s back-end databases to front-end applications. Control nodes were packaged in APIs and SDKs to support common services for performing various general-purpose functions and avoiding duplicating efforts. Third-party developers used APIs and SDKs to access the OCP’s core database resources in developing applications that served users’ needs. A TechTarget article analyzed how control nodes in Oracle’s Fusion middleware coordinated the traffic within the OCP and reported that:

[Oracle] needs to make sure that the application is able to connect to the database and that the connectivity provider is not hampering performance between the database and the application. Oracle sets the fetch buffer size to control how much data is sent across the network in each batch for each connection. … To leverage the ca pabilities of the core, Oracle develops the APIs for managing the various data types and then packages these APIs into SDKs for dis tribution to developers. All of the data types attempting to connect to Oracle’s core are assessed through predefined sets of control nodes and Oracle manages these nodes.

Flexibility was naturally reflected in the design logic of APIs and SDKs because both were designed in response to external opportunities and benefited from third-party heterogeneity. The Fusion middleware empowered third-party developers with APIs and other visual tools for tailoring customer experiences with and across software development environments. Specifically, the Fusion middleware provided multiple APIs for seamlessly synchronizing the OCP resources with desktop ap plications, enterprise systems, telephony integration, cloud app inte gration, and web services. In this case, the OCP offered customizable options to let users deploy any application on the Oracle Cloud with predictable performance. Further, Fusion APIs followed open standards (e.g., ISO SQL, JDBC, and Python PEP) to extend the OCP’s overall accessibility, as such third-party developers could create custom solu tions that range from sample data validation to complex telephone integration. Open standards were publicly available system develop ment norms that were maintained by a collaborative consensus among developer communities.

In sum, Oracle set up the Fusion middleware architecture to provi sion and manage interoperability from heterogeneous third-party de velopers. Specifically, the Fusion middleware allowed case-specific system development practices to access the OCP’s core database without interrupting the OCP’s overall integration. In this case, Oracle has attracted an increasing number of third-party developers to contribute to its OCP development. Aryan indicated Oracle’s design strategy for its Fusion middleware architecture that:

Oracle is trying to take the concept of Fusion and interoperability to the next level. Oracle is not going to just focus on supporting different functional verticals, but we are also going to ensure all verticals are interconnected with the whole Cloud.

Stability in the hybrid application architecture was twofold. First, Oracle designed, developed and owned the on-demand apps. Thus, they were tightly coupled with the OCP. Specifically, the on-demand apps followed a single security and integration strategy. Flexibility emerged in the on-premise apps. Clients could personalize on-premise apps based on their specific business contexts. The onpremise apps are loosely coupled because Oracle would not intervene in changes within an on-premise app as long as the app followed the standardized connection protocols. Specifically, in the application development framework, Oracle explained the design logic for the on premise apps:

Rather, according to Bill, the OCP allowed “dif ferentiation by reconfiguring client-side app development,” whereby “the clients can still use the platform to realize the reconfiguration.” Thus, the OCP kept expanding its service range by acquiring new functions from the on-premise apps. By July 2018, there were more than 2850 licensed third-party developers, and over 4500 on-premise apps complemented the OCP.

In sum, the on-demand apps were built from the ground up and tightly coupled with the OCP architecture. The on-premise apps were developed by third parties and loosely coupled with the OCP architec ture. With this hybrid application architecture, Oracle has been through what Aryan called a “big period of transition and coexistence of clients’ onpremise apps and Oracle’s on-demand solutions, and this transition period might go for decades.”

## 4.4. Synthesizing the findings

Our analysis of the OCP architecture shows that realizing platform generativity is not only about effectively implementing governance practices, but also about exercising architectural design in a platform’s three primary components. In attempting to build a foundation for un derstanding how a platform architecture can realize generativity, we discerned a preliminary base, interface, add-ons (BIA) model (see Fig. 2) from our interpretive literature review. In Fig. 4, we instantiate the BIA model by showing that the paradox of stability and flexibility exist within each of the OCP’s three important architectural parts: the mul titenant database, the Fusion middleware, and the hybrid apps. The paradox is not only intrinsic to platform design choices, but also, if harnessed, can be the driving force of generativity.

To harness the paradox of stability and flexibility, platform designers need to ensure that stability and flexibility coexist as “juxtaposition” [64]. That is, platform designers should implement mechanisms to arrange the dependencies between stability and flexibility in each component. Standardization can be the key to coordinate the de pendencies between stability and flexibility, as the Oracle practitioners explained:

One thing Oracle always tries to do is [be] standard based. Being standard based means that whatever technique a client uses at whichever layer, the client can leverage it with the rest of the Cloud. (Marco)

To understand Oracle’s Cloud priority, standardization is a key part because Oracle wants to have more developers working on our Cloud platform with a shared view. (Bill)

Oracle started from the ground up. So, we have built that infra structure underlying computing and storage, and then we have the capability to interoperate varieties on the top. Now, the challenge is how to give a really neat, encapsulated view of what we have and what we acquire from clients. Standardization is the answer. (Jennie)

All our customers are sitting in different business cases. So, [how are] they going to get to the Oracle Cloud? Standards define a framework to extend practices for generating value from them with various propositions. Standardization is mediating between all these com ponents [i.e., BIA] and providing integration. … Now, customers can use simple standardized data exchanges to frame pluggable databases, APIs and applications. It is all integrated and under con trol. (Aryan)

The empirical evidence highlights that standardization navigates the paradox of stability and flexibility to realize generativity. Specifically, in the base, Oracle set standardized data connection protocols in a plug gable unit named “Seed” to normalize the connection between the root and pluggable database domains. In the interface, Oracle adopted open standards to reconcile APIs with control nodes in boundary resources. In the add-on, Oracle applied standardized data exchange protocols to synchronize data transmissions between on-demand and on-premise applications. Thus, in addition to the three propositions, we also find that in a platform architecture, the paradox of stability and flexibility can be navigated through standardized processes (i.e., database connection in the base, software development in the interface, and data transmission in add-ons) to coordinate service variety in each of a platform’s three basic components, with the aim of realizing generativity.

## 5. Discussion

Prior literature emphasizes platform governance as the key to real izing generativity [2,9,27,63]. However, the platform architecture, which is often deeply embedded in a complex modular structure, has received comparatively little attention. [17,20]. Yet, the platform governance and high-level platform design would fail if the platform architecture did not functionally support the governance practices. Our interpretive literature review shows that platform generativity is rooted in a platform’s three primary architectural components: the base, the interface, and the add-ons. The base holds a platform’s main computing resources. Add-ons deliver a platform’s direct-use value. The interface manages interoperability between the add-ons and the base. Further, we developed a BIA model to illustrate how platform generativity emerges from the paradox of stability and flexibility in each component. Our case analysis of the OCP supports the propositions by showing that (1) the OCP includes three basic architectural parts (the multitenant database, the Fusion middleware, and the hybrid applications) and (2) the OCP is designed to “balance” stability and flexibility in each architectural part to achieve generativity. Further, the OCP data show that standardization through the system development processes navigates the paradox of stability and flexibility to achieve integration of service variety.

## 5.1. Implications for theory

The findings allow us to make several important implications for the emerging literature on platforms [2,21,34]. First, our study acknowl edges the contingent and dynamic nature of platform-based phenomena [2,21]. It complements prior research that has focused on platform governance and high-level design, and contributes to the discussion of placing digital architecture in a more central position in IS research [17, 20]. The premise of prior research was that the dynamics in platform governance are based in an evolving relationship between platform owners and service providers [19.27.50]. Drawing on the OCP case. we acknowledge that platform governance (i.e., Oracle’s strategic decision on transforming to a platform-based firm to respond to increasingly diverse demands) guides platform architecture design (i.e., redesigning Oracle’s digital assets). However, our model highlights that the platform governance’s effect must be actualized in the architectural design. Thus, our findings complement prior research by showing that generativity arises from a platform’s three key architectural components: the base, the interface, and the add-ons. It is insightful to not only consider platform-level governance, but also component design at the architecture-level in practicing generativity—a research direction that was suggested by prior work [20–22], yet has not received enough attention in the context of platform architecture. Exploring architectural components of a platform is important because it allows for accommodation of the dynamic nature and specific characteristics of generativity in a complex modular system without treating the opera tion logic of platform as a black box [17,83].

Second, our study offers a distinct treatment of generativity in the literature by untangling how generativity emerges from a platform’s architectural components by navigating the paradox of stability and flexibility. The IS literature has extensively adopted Zittrain’s definition and described generativity as “a technology’s overall capacity to pro duce unprompted changes driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences” ([16], p. 1980, emphasis added). However, unprompted changes may damage a platform’s functional integration because of inconsistent service quality and digital attacks [9]. The BIA model is reminiscent of the literature on the paradox that logically opposite el ements (e.g., stability and flexibility) can persist within complex modular systems (e.g., platforms) [64–66]. To realize generativity, platform design should follow an interactive looping process to navigate the paradox of stability and flexibility in different architectural com ponents. The BIA model answers the call for attention to a more precise and nuanced understanding of the nature of modern digital architecture that enables and contains generativity [20,21,29,34]. A question then can be, what coordinates the paradox of stability and flexibility and, in turn, realizes generativity? Our case study shows that standardization provides a general direction. Standardization refers to specifications that promote coordination among platform development activities from heterogeneous stakeholders [27,84,85]. Drawing on our case study, standardization ensures that the same assumption is shared among multiple stakeholders when practicing platform development activitie across the architectural components. In this case, to prevent a platform architecture from being episodic, designers should install standardized development processes to coordinate the iterations between stability and flexibility. Thus, reconciling a platform architecture with gen erativity is more than just a design issue. It is a complex and dynamic interplay between designing and practicing architecture-wide standardization.

Third, by shedding light on the paradox of stability and flexibility in creating generativity for the OCP, we contribute to research on platform design in the specific context of cloud computing [86–88]. The findings extend the literature by exploring how a platform architecture may empower cloud computing’s ability to adapt to changing and diverse demands in a flexible manner without losing control, noted as the “golden nugget of cloud computing and what makes the entire [cloud computing] concept extraordinarily evolutionary” ([89], p. 46). Spe cifically, the findings suggest that navigating the paradox of stability and flexibility in cloud computing appears to be accomplished through self-reinforcing loops at cloud computing’s three key layers (infra structure, interoperation, and service, corresponding to a platform’s three architectural components) [90]. That is, exposing parts of the computing resources allows third parties to recombine the cloud infra structure with their specific needs and, in turn, fundamentally expand the cloud infrastructure’s service range. In this case, the cloud infra structure gradually forms an ecosystem by accommodating on-premise services developed by the client-side providers. Further, generativity is only actualized when on-premise services are compatible with each other and with other cloud components. Such connection requires interoperation in terms of data types, programming languages, and computing algorithms. APIs and SDKs embrace the patterns of assem bling on-premise services into the cloud’s overall architecture. Thus, to fully explain how cloud computing successfully responds to diverse demands, it is insightful to go beyond considering how cloud computing as a whole enables and constrains heterogeneous development activ ities, and to acknowledge the role of the paradox of stability and flexi bility in different architectural components as an alternative perspective.

## 5.2. Practical implications

Our study has important implications for practice. First, we provide insights into platform architecture design, with the aim of enabling generativity. Although a platform’s value is measured by network effects from heterogeneous service development activities [6,45,91], a plat form owner should not separate platform value from its architecture design. This is because network effects do not naturally occur but are enabled by a platform’s generativity [17,62]. As our BIA model dem onstrates, generativity arises from a platform’s distinct architectural components. In this case, a holistic view of platform generativity only exists when considering the platform’s architectural components and addressing the paradox of stability and flexibility in each component. This insight suggests that platform owners should shift their managerial attention away from the sole focus of the platform as a whole and focus more on architecture design. Second, our model visualizes the complex concept of generativity in a platform context. In practice, there has long been a lack of effective metrics to boost decision-making in complex system design and management [92]. Platform practitioners may use our model as an analytical matrix to make decisions, test lay theories, and monitor resources. For example, based on the information collected from our follow-up focus group, Oracle has tailored the BIA model and used it on various occasions, such as drafting cloud structure blueprints, facilitating brainstorming sessions, and educating clients’ cloud transformation.

## 5.3. Limitations

A comprehensive examination of the effect of architecture design on platform generativity requires large-scale field studies incorporating a longitudinal design. This study is limited in that it focused on one multinational technology firm (Oracle) that has the design and devel opment power to credibly engage in platform design for all three architectural components. Although single-case studies are recognized as a “typical and legitimate endeavor” for theory development in the literature, we recognize that generalization can be a common criticism of a single-case study approach [93]. However, we contend that our study is generalizable beyond its singular case because our model is grounded in empirical observation of facts based on an interpretive literature review. As noted in Section 3, this study adheres to the idea of “generalizing from description to theory” ([76], p. 235). We encourage more research to further examine our model in other platform-related contexts, such as mobile operating systems, social media, and game consoles.

The role of end users has been mostly hidden due to privacy pro tocols in our case analysis. Our study has explained Oracle’s platform design strategy that focuses on how to better engage third-party service providers at different layers of the OCP architecture. Third-party pro viders enrich Oracle’s Cloud ecosystem by offering on-premise services that are compatible with Oracle’s on-demand solutions. Although end user engagement is not central to the arguments of this study, end user created content can be a worthwhile topic for research in a platform context. For example, many game consoles not only allow game de velopers to contribute to their platforms, but also encourage game players to redesign existing game settings and share the new design with the whole gaming community. In this case, the boundary between ser vice producers and end users becomes blurred. Thus, we encourage future research to explore and examine how end users may contribute to platform architecture design.

## 5.4. Future research

We propose three avenues for future studies. The first avenue con cerns the threshold of the paradox of stability and flexibility. In this paper, we assume that a platform is stable in the sense of restricting changes, and flexible in the sense of accommodating changes. This assumption leads to the question of where the threshold between restricting and accommodating changes lies when generativity emerges in different contexts. Henfridsson and Bygstad [94] indicated that gen erativity must constantly adjust and readjust its degree of stability and flexibility through self-reinforcement. Self-reinforcing mechanisms are associated with positive and negative self-reinforcing loops spanning a platform’s architectural components to respond to diverse demands [27]. From this perspective, opportunities remain for future studies to explore and examine thresholds that trigger (or pause) positive and negative self-reinforcing loops and, in turn, offer a more holistic view of the paradox of stability and flexibility in shaping platform generativity.

The second avenue concerns the interdependencies between gen erativity across multiple platform components (if any). In terdependencies between components of a complex modular system have received scant attention. For example, although some literature has explicitly or implicitly indicated that the interface coordinates the connection between the base and the add-ons, how generativity in fluences or is influenced by such coordination remains underresearched. Examining the potential interdependencies between gen erativity across platform components can be an important step towards a more comprehensive understanding of platforms’ inner structures.

Third, there is the question of how we understand and measure generativity outcomes. De Reuver et al. [21] suggested that people might account for the outcomes of platform generativity in myriad ways, including price, reliability, ease of use, and user support. However, these aspects often vary between distinct stakeholders. For example, end users may view quantity and quality of applications as the primary matric to measure platform generativity. Developers may view technical superi ority as a more important indicator. Owners may prioritize business performance. Thus, identifying the focal stakeholder can be critical for precisely identifying generativity outcomes. We encourage more studies that systematically examine outcomes of platform generativity in mul tiple contexts and offer a more comprehensive view.

## 6. Conclusion

Although the concepts of platforms and generativity have drawn increasing attention from the literature, scant comprehensive effort has been undertaken to theorize how a platform architecture can actualize generativity. In this study, we contend that generativity emerges from the paradox of stability and flexibility in a platform’s three basic architectural components: the base, the interface, and the add-ons. We also suggest that standardization can navigate the paradox by coordi nating the dependencies between stability and flexibility across het erogeneous stakeholders. Our study is among the first attempts towards a more comprehensive understanding of platform generativity by placing digital architectures at a more central position of IS research. We expect that this paper will provide an inspiring basis for sounder theorie of platforms and generativity in the future.
