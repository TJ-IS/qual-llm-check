# A regulatory control framework for decentrally governed DLT systems: Action design research

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Blockchain DLT Decentralized governance Regulation Risk

## A B S T R A C T

Although distributed ledger technology (DLT) systems seem to address conventional regulatory governance concerns, they also present systemic risks. This paper aims to address the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems. It achieves this by identifying regulatory controls that address the altered governance dynamics of decentralized systems. To address the research gap in DLT regulation, we co-designed regulatory control treatments with Australian regulatory DLT experts using a participatory action design research approach. This paper provides a theory-ingrained, practitioner-vetted framework to inform regulators, regulated entities and researchers of appropriate control responses to systemic and participant risks presented by decentrally governed DLT systems.

## 1. Introduction

Distributed ledger technologies’ underpinning systems such as Bit coin are noted for their ability to facilitate trust among otherwise un known transacting parties without recourse to central governing authorities. This shift to decentralized governance has been driven by diminishing societal trust in institutional intermediaries and govern ment authorities, and is more exacerbated by socioeconomic events such as the 2008 Global Financial Crisis and ongoing public debate over so cial media manipulation [3]. Distributed ledger technology (DLT) sys tems facilitate the move to decentralized governance through their novel application of cryptography and incentives, allowing participants to conduct trusted transactions between unknown parties without recourse to intermediaries or authorities [39]. This DLT-enabled tran sition away from reliance on trusted central parties, however, presents significant challenges to both regulators and regulated entities that rely on centralized governance mechanisms to ensure the stability and preservation of value in regulated contexts such as the financial system.

Governance as a concept has evolved from a state-centric model for command-and-control of society’s functions to a more decentred one that accommodates increasingly complex interactions among state in stitutions and societal participants [43, 44]. In the context of organi zational IT systems, governance is defined as the decision rights and accountabilities established to encourage behavior [51]. [4] extends this definition of IT governance to the context of pan organizational DLT systems, placing additional emphasis on aligning incentives between distributed stakeholders. Such decentrally governed DLT systems are seen to enable a scalability of governance that extends beyond con ventional governance structures that are constrained by their reliance on central authorities such as institutional intermediaries (MacDonald, Allen & Potts 2016). Although scalable decentralized governance por tends new possibilities for democratised decision-making, it also pre sents challenges to regulators that have relied on regulatory controls concentrated on centralized intermediaries and their accountable deci sion makers [7]. A key emerging research question is: ‘How should regulatory control regimes adapt to the emergence of decentrally gov erned DLT systems?

Due to the limited research and practical knowledge in addressing the problem of regulating DLT systems, the industry research partner (IRP – coded) was interested in addressing the research question in collaboration with the academic research partner (UTX – coded). A re view of the literature identified the limited existing research on the topic of regulatory risks and control responses to the emergence of decentrally governed DLT systems. To address this research gap, we adopted a participatory action design research (ADR) approach to incorporate DLT regulatory policy expertise into early problem formulation and the subsequent co-design of a DLT Regulatory Control (DRC) Framework for the Australian financial system. The early and ongoing involvement of regulatory experts in the research activity strengthened the validity and usefulness of the developed ADR artefact in a field with limited existing research [17, 23].

This paper is organized as follows. Firstly, it discusses the emergence of decentralized governance as a response to growing societal concerns with the reliance of conventional regulatory governance on centralized intermediaries. Secondly, it explores the challenges presented by emerging decentrally governed DLT systems in regulated contexts such as the financial system. Thirdly, it describes a participatory ADR approach to develop a regulatory response to these challenges. Finally, it presents a regulatory control framework developed to address the emerging regulatory challenges of decentrally governed DLT systems.

## 2. Related literature

Historically, the development of states and institutions heralded the emergence of centralized governance regimes as early societies shifted from reliance on trust between known counter-parties to reliance on central authorities to assure dealings between parties [24]. Centrally held ledgers of holdings, such as registries, and the centralised author ities that oversee their operation, have become a cornerstone of the modern state. Such centrally dependent governance mechanisms have enabled parties that are otherwise unknown to each other to trust the integrity of transactions within governed systems on an industrial scale [12, 35].

The centrally assured facilitation of trust among parties, however, has come at a cost. When introduced centrally administered governance may have been efficient, though when applied to the scale and complexity of today’s societies, such centralized governance is increas ingly seen as expensive and unwieldy [12, 43, 46]. In response to effi ciency challenges faced by centralized state authorities, institutional intermediaries such as banks evolved to enable the specialization and resource distribution needed for activity on a larger scale [2]. As with centralized authorities however, the rise of institutional intermediaries to orchestrate complex economic activity also comes with costs. These include rent-seeking opportunism, inefficient organizational bureau cracies, and the tendency of powerful institutions to concentrate wealth and power within societies [2, 12, 42]. The limitations of institutionally dependent governance have been exacerbated by public perceptions of declining public ethics and the emergence of technological alternatives that improve transparency [42, 46].

This decline of trust in institutions has driven the pursuit for alter native decentralized means of establishing trust among otherwise un known parties [7]. This shift towards decentralized governance among distributed parties is characterized by a reliance on ‘game-like in teractions, rooted in trust and regulated by rules of the game negotiated and agreed by network participants’ [43]. Technology has played a key role in this move beyond institutionally dependent governance. The ability for DLT systems to displace the role of trusted central authorities in assuring trust among parties is rooted in their underlying design. A distributed ledger is a decentralized record of information that is shared and agreed among various parties [39, 40]. As a technology of decen tralization, DLTs enable resource transfers among unknown participants in a highly scalable manner through a combination of distributed technology architecture, cryptography and incentives [4, 13]. Bitcoin is perhaps the best-known early application of DLT, maintaining a commonly agreed record of electronic cash balances among participants without reliance on a registry maintained by a central bank or financial institution. This displacement of central assuring authorities with cryptographic assurances and incentives addresses many of the earlier criticisms leveled at conventional institutionally oriented governance [7, 13, 52]. Significantly, the popularity of Bitcoin and other DLT-based systems such as Ethereum shows a growing societal willingness to arrive at consensus on agreed economic states without a reliance on central authorities or institutional intermediaries [1]. See Fig. 1.

Decentralized DLT governance is not without its own challenges. However, a growing body of academic research has now identified challenges associated with decentralized DLT governance, including the emergence of unaccountable, oligarchic governance structures [3]; the potential resistance of DLT-based smart contracts and decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) to regulatory sanctions [12]; and the displacement of institutionally dependent control points, such as banks, that have been conventionally relied on by regulators to manage sys temic risks in regulated contexts [3, 6, 7].

Regulation has been characterized as intentional interventions into defined fields with the aim of influencing the behavior of parties to achieve identified objectives [8, 27]. The relationship between gover nance and regulation has been explored in research. For the regulated global financial system, regulatory bodies such as the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO) define their regulatory objectives to include the reduction of systemic risk and the ongoing improvement of regulatory frameworks through the setting of standards [31]. In the context of growing acceptance of decentrally governed DLT systems, such financial regulators are increasingly raising concerns that decentrally governed DLT systems may impede core regulatory objec tives such as the reduction of systemic risk [33]. To date, DLT gover nance and regulation has only been lightly explored in academic research [4]. The scarcity of existing research on DLT regulation is compounded by limited industry experience in implementing DLT sys tems. This gap raises an important research question: ‘How should regulators respond to the emergence of decentrally governed DLT sys tems?’ This research aims to address this important research question and develops a DLT regulatory control framework using the well-known ADR method. ## 3. Case context

This paper examines the regulatory response to decentrally governed DLT systems in the regulatory context of the Australian financial system. The Australian financial system is considered a good case for DLT reg ulatory intervention as it incorporates business and technology organi zations that leverage strategic technologies for operational efficiency and growth, as well as capitalised financial institutions capable of investing in innovative DLT systems to defend and grow businesses while enhancing citizen and society trust [50]. The financial system record of adopting emerging technologies reflects a long history of seeding technology trends into other industries and broader society [26]. The regulatory response to decentrally governed DLT systems in the financial system is therefore an insightful indicator for future reg ulatory responses to DLT adoption in broader sectors.

Australia’s financial system is considered robustly regulated and a systemically important financial system [28, 29]. Importantly, Austra lian financial system regulators are active participants in the direction setting of global financial system regulation. ## 4. Research method

The study was initiated in 2017 as a joint academia-industry research project to address the gap in research in DLT regulation. Researchers from UTX partnered with an Australian financial system regulator and a cross-section of Australian financial system regulatory DLT experts to co-design a regulatory control framework that identifies and treats the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems.

The study contributes to research on DLT regulation by implement ing a participatory ADR design to co-develop a DLT Regulatory Controls Framework (DRC Framework) with regulatory policy experts. Partici patory ADR varies from conventional ADR by incorporating participants into the co-design of research artefacts from early stages of problem definition and artefact creation [38]. A key benefit of this participatory extension to ADR is its suitability for research problems with limited existing research [23]. The participatory ADR research approach is further supplemented by additional theory generating qualitative research techniques to increase the theoretical contribution of the design research artefact [5].

The research commenced with a Problem Formulation research stage to define and clarify the research problem to be addressed. Expert reg ulatory practitioners were involved in this early stage of research to inform the definition and refinement of the research problem. The study then progressed to a Build, Intervene and Evaluation (BIE) research stage to build and refine the developed DRC Framework research arte fact. Additional theory generating steps, including the development of regulatory control design principles, were incorporated into the BIE research stage to augment the theoretical contribution of the design artefact [5, 21]. The BIE research stage incorporated DLT regulatory expert participation throughout the co-design and evaluation of the developed DRC Framework. The final formalisation of Learnings research stage further generalised the developed artefact through refinement of the framework’s library of risks and regulatory controls and the development of guidelines to support its regulatory imple mentation. See Fig. 2.

To clarify the research problem during the Problem Formulation research stage, a review of literature was conducted, and interviews held with Australian financial system regulatory policy experts. This early exploration highlighted the lack of extant research on DLT regulation and the potential for disruption to conventional institutionally depen dent regulatory regimes presented by decentrally governed DLT systems.

To inform the formulation of the research problem in the face of limited existing research, Australian regulatory policy experts were recruited from the DLT Working Group of the Australian Council of Financial Regulators (CFR). The CFR is the peak coordinating body for Australia’s main financial regulatory agencies. Its responsibilities are to promote the stability of the Australian financial system and support effective and efficient regulation by Australia’s financial regulatory agencies [9]. The CFR’s DLT Working Group (DLT WG) was established to explore the potential implications of DLT and how it fits within the existing Australian regulatory framework. Members of the DLT WG comprise DLT policy representatives of the constituent regulatory agencies and are considered the leading DLT policy experts from each agency. As a collective, the DLT WG membership presents a balance of technical and regulatory expertise, with participants recognised for their expertise in DLT and its implications for their respective agency’s reg ulatory accountabilities. By involving a diverse range of research par ticipants from several leading Australian financial system regulators, the research aimed to mitigate the potential for a specific agency’s

Framework research artefact was proposed. As it is common in design research, the development of this initial theory ingrained research artefact was abductive in nature [21]. This initial framework captured how risks, control treatments and participants should be presented for regulatory consideration. The artefact was informed by the review of literature on governance and risks relating to regulation of distributed ledger technologies and initial regulatory expert interviews.

At the conclusion of the Problem Formulation research stage, we conducted a research workshop with the expert research participants to establish a reciprocal space for reflection and collaboration to review the initial research problem and initial DRC Framework artefact. The workshop ensured that key concepts and topics were understood and agreed by the research participants. Improvements identified for the initial research artefact included the incorporation of additional risks and potential regulatory control responses that had not been identified in the review of literature. This early incorporation of practitioner feedback is a feature of participatory ADR studies and supported the refinement of the research problem and the initial conceptualization of the design artefact [23]. A more resolved view of the research problem and solution emerged through this research stage.

The outcomes of the Problem Formulation research stage informed the subsequent BIE research stage. The BIE stage involved building on initial reflection and learning outcomes from the prior research stage and emphasised co-designing the DRC Framework artefact with the participating regulatory experts. This participatory research activity was supplemented with the additional qualitative research technique of developing regulatory control design principles. The inclusion of design principles for regulatory controls extended the theoretical contribution of the resulting DRC Framework research artefact by expanding it generalisability to a broader range of regulatory cases [5, 21].

During the BIE research stage, we conducted an initial co-design workshop using the Nominal Group Technique (NGT) to facilitate the participation of regulatory experts. NGT workshop techniques are useful for eliciting feedback from expert groups with variation in seniority and levels of extroversion [15, 25]. The NGT approach ensured participant expertise was equally represented by ensuring each expert practitioner was given ample opportunity to contribute to and challenge emerging group outcomes. Implementing the NGT approach involved first capturing the diverse views of participants on risks and control re sponses where individuals silently documented and then elaborated on their individual perspectives. Participants then individually voted to prioritize and categorize identified DLT risks and regulatory control responses before collectively discussing and prioritizing outcomes. The co-design workshop approach allowed participants to review and refine the risks and control treatments incorporated into the initial DRC Framework artefact proposed in the study’s preceding Problem Formulation research stage.

Intervention and Evaluation activities occur concurrently through the implementation of the BIE Research Stage and involved the ongoing synthesis of learning outcomes from participative research workshops, additional theoretical sampling and practitioner feedback elicited through structured questionnaires [18, 23, 47]. Throughout the BIE research stage, participants were encouraged to incorporate the emerging outcomes of the research activity to inform the regulatory policy direction setting to DLTs within their respective regulatory agencies. The emerging outcomes from participating agency regulatory responses to DLT systems were also incorporated into the research study through ongoing contributions into BIE research stage activities by the research participants.

A further step incorporated into the BIE research stage involved expanding expert participation to include international regulator and DLT platform providers. This expanded participant input was incorpo rated using unstructured interviews and structured questionnaires. This stage of the research activity proved helpful in identifying emerging international financial system risk standards for DLT risk identification and assessment. The interviews also identified further risk control treatments such as the inclusion of regulatory stop mechanisms to halt transaction of DLT system activity in the event of a developing risk contagion event. The knowledge gained during this BIE research step further refined the risk categories of the developed DRC Framework research artefact and expanded its global generalisability.

Evaluation in ADR research is conducted throughout the BIE research stage, contrasting with design research where evaluation oc curs at the completion of development of the designed artefact [18]. In evaluating an ADR artefact, both theoretical and practical evaluation is undertaken to evaluate and improve its quality [47]. Evaluation of the practical value, comprehensiveness, and quality of the developed DRC Framework was achieved through participant workshop activity and survey responses from the participating regulatory experts. Several improvement opportunities in the overall design and specific compo nents of the research artefact were identified in this research activity and incorporated into revisions of the developed DRC Framework. One research participant in noting they ‘strongly agreed’ with the statement that components of the developed framework was important to the regulation of DLT systems, noted that the ‘components become more important the more decentralized the governance of the financial network(s), and the more principles-based the regulatory environment is (as opposed to prescriptive-based frameworks, which are more certain and limiting)’. Another research participant in noting they ‘agreed’ with the statement that the framework was relevant for regulators seeking to address the risks of decentralized DLT governance, noted the ‘risk treatments are useful lists of ideas of tangible controls to apply. The principles are useful, even on their own without risk treatments’. All research participating ‘agreed’ or ‘strongly agreed’ with the statement that the ‘developed framework was relevant to regulators seeking to address the risks of decentralized governance in DLT systems.

The theoretical quality of the developed artefact was assessed using the criteria for evaluating and developing IS theories by [49]. To eval uate the theoretical contribution of the research as a whole, its level, importance, novelty and parsimony was evaluated and refined. Risks, control treatments and classes of regulated entities that were deemed by research participants as contributing insufficient value and usefulness to the framework were discarded. The elimination of framework elements deemed inconsequential to regulatory experts contributed to parsimony of the developed artefact.

To evaluate and improve the theoretical quality of the components of the DRC Framework we ensured its research constructs and their asso ciations were specified [49]. Specifications for key constructs were informed by a combination of industry standards (risks and control treatments), research (design principles), and input from expert regu latory participants (regulated entities). The framework presents the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems as associated to the regulatory controls that treat them, the classes of regulated entities or systemic risks they impact, and residual risk outcomes that result from their treatment. Regulatory control treatments are associated with the specific risks they treat, the residual risk outcomes they produce and the regulatory control design principles they apply.

The evaluation conducted throughout the BIE research stage resulted in several refinements to the developed DRC Framework:

1) Simplifying the framework by eliminating constructs such as participant types, risks and regulatory control treatments deemed detrimental to the framework’s parsimony.

2) Improving the validity and usefulness of the framework by adopting industry terms and definitions for risks and control treatments con structs [30–32].

3) Improving the theoretical quality of the framework’s constructs by specifying the associations between the DLT participants, risks, regulatory control treatments and residual risk outcomes.

4) Expanding the theoretical generalisability of the developed DRC Framework artefact to a broader range of regulatory cases by incorporating regulatory control design principles to facilitate the adaption of regulatory control treatments to evolving DLT risks.

Additional theory-generating techniques were incorporated throughout the BIE research stage of this ADR study. These further in crease the theoretical contribution of the developed design artefact by addressing the criticism that design research can emphasize artefact development for specific contexts at the expense of theoretical gen eralisability [5]. One additional theory-generating research technique adopted was the conducting of additional theoretical sampling throughout the BIE research stage in response to DLT risk and regulatory control themes that emerged from regulatory expert input. This tech nique resulted in additional literature sampling on the enforceability of regulatory compliance and resulted in its incorporation into refined versions of the DRC Framework.

External observer analysis was also conducted with IS research methodology experts to enhance the study’s research design. This resulted in a structured approach to developing regulatory control design principles that were incorporated into the DRC Framework to expand its suitability to a broader range of regulatory cases [21]. The theoretical validity of the developed design artefact was enhanced by the maintenance of a design log that recorded artefact design iterations and tracked unsuccessful and successful design components as they were co-developed and validated by research participants [11]. The logging of design iterations supported the iterative synthesis of participant feedback and artefact design throughout the BIE research stage.

The formalisation of Learnings research stage involved the formal presentation of the developed DRC Framework to the DLT working groups of both the IDP and the Australian CFR. Feedback from partici pants identified the benefit to regulators of including implementation guidelines and a case example to inform regulatory implementations of the DRC Framework. In response to this feedback, implementation guidelines and a hypothetical case for regulating DLT-enabled crossborder payments were developed to facilitate regulatory adoption. The formalisation of Learnings research stage concluded with the identifi cation of future research opportunities to extend the DRC Framework. These are elaborated in the future research section of this paper.

## 5. DLT regulatory control framework

A high-level version of the DRC Framework artefact is presented in Fig. 3. The framework is comprised of five key constructs: (1) regulated DLT entities; (2) risk categories and their constituent risks; (3) regula tory control treatments; (4) residual risk outcomes; and (5) design principles for developing, implementing and adapting DLT regulatory controls. The framework structure is informed by the general framework for risk management contained in the ISO31000 Standard for Risk Management Guidelines and Principles [34].

The DRC Framework identifies participants to the operation of DLT systems in regulatory contexts such as the financial system. DLT par ticipants were identified from the research literature and input from participating DLT regulatory experts. Risks are categorized according to their application to either specific types of DLT participants or their contribution to broader systemic risk. Each risk category is comprised of more atomic risks, each of which is mapped to risk dimensions informed by IOSCO financial system risk categories [30, 32]. Risk control treat ments were identified from research literature and augmented with regulatory controls identified by research participants during the BIE research stage workshops. Residual risk outcomes were identified ac cording to the planned effect of regulatory controls against risks and evaluated and refined using research participant feedback from research workshops and structured questionnaire responses undertaken during the BIE research stage. Regulatory control design principles support the adaptivity of the framework’s regulatory control library to an evolving risk landscape and support the generalisability of the framework to expanded regulatory contexts [21]. A key characteristic of the adopted schematic approach to representing design principles is the incorpora tion into each principle its rationale and the specification of actors required for its application. In the context of regulated markets such as the financial system, the inclusion of regulatory control design princi ples ensures the developed DRC Framework remains responsive to the rapid innovation of DLT systems. ## 5.1. Regulated entities

The review of literature, interviews and initial research workshop conducted during the Problem Formulation research stage identified key regulated entities affected by the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems. These entities were further reviewed and validated by the DLT regulatory experts during the BIE research stage. These regulated en tities and markets incorporated into the DRC Framework include:

1) DLT users that use or are otherwise impacted by the services pro vided by a DLT system. In regulated contexts such as the financial system, DLT participants are impacted by the realization of the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems and the regulatory controls that treat these risks.

2) DLT providers supply the DLT applications, platforms or underlying infrastructure that underpin and comprise DLT systems. DLT appli cations are DLT systems that either independently, or by leveraging underlying DLT platforms, provide services or products to DLT par ticipants. DLT platforms such as Ethereum provide functionality that enables the development of specific DLT applications through use of common components. DLT platforms and applications rely on DLT infrastructure such as telecommunication networks and internet infrastructure to provide DLT system functionality to DLT partici pants. DLT providers are impacted by the realization of the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems and the regulatory controls that treat these risks.

Although developed for the Australian financial regulatory context, the DRC Framework is designed to be adapted to a broader range of regulatory contexts.

## 5.2. Risks

Research reviewed in the Problem Formulation research stage identified a diverse range of risks associated with the emergence of decentrally governed DLT systems in the financial system. Risks repre sent the probabilistic occurrence of specified events or outcomes, and are mitigated by control treatments that are designed to reduce their probability or impact on occurrence [34]. In the context of the financial system, risks can occur systematically through the market, or to specific participants or classes of entities [30–32]. In the context of the DRC Framework, the risks of relevance are those associated with the decen trally governed DLT systems. Key risks associated with the emergence of decentrally governed DLT systems were identified in the research literature and validated by DLT regulatory experts during the BIE research stage:

1) The emergence of unaccountable, oligarchic DLT-enabled gover nance structures [3].

2) Potential resistance of DLT-based smart contracts and DAOs to reg ulatory sanctions [12].

3) Displacement of institutionally dependent control points conven tionally relied on by regulators to manage systemic risks in regulated environments [7].

Research participants also identified further DLT-enabled decen tralized governance risks. Key risks include:

## 5.3. Regulatory control design principles

Regulatory control design principles are incorporated into the DRC Framework to guide the design, implementation, operation and adap tion of regulatory control treatments. In the research context, these refer to the regulatory controls applied to reduce the residual outcomes of risks relating to decentrally governed DLT systems. The incorporation of regulatory control design principles into the developed framework al lows it to be adapted to a broader range of regulatory contexts. The adaptivity of regulatory controls is particularly important to the regulatory response to DLT systems as the speed enabled by the low cost and ease of DLT development has resulted in a Cambrian explosion' of DLT governance permutations [16].

To develop DLT regulatory control design principles we imple mented the schema for constructing design principles for design research developed by Gregor et al. [21]. This approach to design principle development incorporates into each principle the aims, actors, rationale and the mechanisms required to apply them into a diversity of implementation contexts. The developed regulatory control design principles were evaluated and refined with research participants in the BIE co-design research workshop and through use of structured ques tionnaires. ## 5.4. Regulatory control treatments and residual risk outcomes

Risk Control treatments are measures and actions implemented to reduce the probability or consequence of risks crystallizing. Residual risk improvements are the reductions in risk probabilities or conse quences through the application of risk control treatments [34]. In the context of the DRC Framework, improved residual risk outcomes result from regulatory control treatments reducing the probability or impact of a risk of decentrally governed DLT systems crystallizing.

Appropriate regulatory controls to treat the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems were identified using both research literature and input from regulatory policy experts using research workshops and questionnaires during the BIE research stage. In response to the disin termediation of conventional regulatory control points, the developed portfolio of regulatory control responses emphasised shifting regulatory controls towards the users and providers of decentrally governed DLT systems. 3) Provision to regulators of transaction visibility by specific classes of DLT providers to enable effective supervision of DLT activity and the ongoing identification and assessment of emergent risks.

4) Provision to regulators of DLT transaction stop authority to enable the termination of transactions deemed unacceptably risky to classes of DLT participants, or otherwise contributing to unacceptable sys temic risks such as the precipitation of contagion in financial markets.

5) Harmonization of regulatory control regimes across both domestic and international regulatory regimes to accommodate for the distributed operation of borderless DLT systems. Such harmonization minimises the cost of regulatory compliance to DLT participants and providers and reduces the likelihood of conflicting regulatory requirements.

6) Ongoing maintenance of regulatory expertise in fast-evolving DLT systems to ensure ongoing regulatory effectiveness and avoid the accumulation of unmitigated systemic risks in regulated markets.

Each of the regulatory controls identified during the research and incorporated into the DRC Framework presents a specific response to the disintermediation of conventional centrally administered regulatory control points. For regulators adapting control regimes to address the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems, planned portfolios of control responses should be reviewed against both existing controls and com plementary DLT control responses introduced by other regulatory agencies. A comprehensive list of identified regulatory controls mapped to relevant risks and underling design control principles is presented in Appendix 3.

## 6. Discussion

This paper presents a regulatory control framework to guide the regulatory response to the emerging risks of decentrally governed DLT systems in contexts such as the financial system. The limited DLT reg ulatory practices developed to date lack the support of an underlying body of academic research and do not substantively address the disruptive potential of decentralized governance to current regulatory regimes. This research combined theory, practice and regulatory expertise using a participatory ADR approach to involve regulatory practitioners from early-stage problem formulation through to the for malisation of research learnings. The research developed a practically relevant and theory ingrained design artefact – the DLT Regulatory Control Framework. It achieved this by identifying how decentrally governed DLT systems contributed to systemic and participant risk and producing a library of practitioner-vetted regulatory controls that ac commodates a regulatory shift from centrally to decentrally levered controls. With this framework, regulators and regulated entities can implement regulatory controls better suited to the decentralized governance characteristics of many DLT systems. The adaptation of regulatory control regimes to the decentralised governance character istics of many DLT systems will ensure regulator controls remains effective in managing systemic risks and minimizing harm to regulated entities.

The incorporation of DLT regulatory policy experts into the co-design of the DRC Framework enables this research to fill the gap in research on the regulatory response to DLT systems. The participatory nature of the study shows how regulator expertise can inform both the early formu lation of problem and co-design stages of research artefact development and evaluation. The inclusion of regulatory policy experts in the research study was beneficial to identifying a range of emerging DLTrelated risks and regulatory control responses relevant to the regulated context of the financial system. Based on the study’s learnings, regula tors could consider the following approach to adapting existing regu latory control regimes to accommodate for the emergence of decentrally governed DLT systems:

1) Identify regulated entities, including DLT participants and providers, affected by the operation of DLT systems in regulated contexts. Such parties should not only be identified based on the likelihood of their being impacted by decentrally governed DLT systems but also their potential to serve as alternative regulatory control points.

2) Identify and assess the risks related to decentrally governed DLT systems in regulated contexts. Such risks may manifest as systemic or within specific classes of regulated entities.

3) Identify and assess appropriate regulatory risk control treatments to mitigate identified systemic risks and risks to regulated entities. The DRC Framework provides a library of regulatory control treatments that can be selected and adapted to specific regulatory contexts.

4) Regularly assess DLT risks and evaluate the efficacy of existing reg ulatory control regimes in treating these risks.

5) Harmonize regulatory control regimes across domestic and interna tional boundaries to address the risks and regulatory controls asso ciated with DLT systems that operate across jurisdictional boundaries.

6) Seek to develop and maintain appropriate DLT expertise within regulatory agencies to ensure regulators remain adaptive to the fast pace of DLT innovation and the evolving DLT risk and controls landscape.

The research confirmed that regulators are appropriately concerned about the potential for decentralized DLT-enabled governance to disin termediate and displace conventional regulatory control points. The emergence of decentrally governed DLT systems means this reliance on centralized control points such as financial intermediaries may no longer be counted on to ensure market stability and the effective miti gation of systemic risk. The research suggests that researchers and reg ulators should work to better understand the risks associated with the DLT-enabled shift to decentralized governance and ensure regulatory control regimes are adaptive to these evolving risks.

## 7. Research implications, limitations and future research

The DRC Framework is a timely and addresses an emerging class of research problem in the area of ‘DLT Regulation’. This research has several implications for industry, research and society. From industry perspective, this study provided a concrete DRC Framework for DLT governed systems, which can be adapted to diverse and evolving regu latory contexts. From research perspective, this study contributed to the scarce literature and research gaps through the identification of regu latory control treatments to address the emerging risks introduced by the shift to decentrally governed DLT systems. From society perspective, this study provided regulatory controls for decentralized governance to ensure trust and stability in financial system. As decentrally governed DLT systems continue to displace conventional control levers such as institutional boards and intermediaries, regulators will likely face increasing pressure to adjust their control regimes to focus on the ac countabilities, decisions rights, trust, and incentives vested in distrib uted DLT participants and providers. This research implications need to be considered with its limitations that could be addressed by future research.

1) The research was developed using regulatory experts in DLT systems principally from the Australian financial system. Although later stage research participation was expanded to include limited international regulator and non-financial system DLT experts, care should be taken when applying the developed DRC Framework to broader regulatory contexts. To address this limitation, future research could expand regulator input from a range of regulatory jurisdictions and sectors. Future research should consider the interaction effects of over lapping domestic and international regulatory jurisdictions to iden tify opportunities to harmonize global DLT regulation.

2) The developed DRC Framework adopts a risk-based approach to regulatory oversight of the financial system and is best suited to regulatory regimes focused on systemic risk management and not necessarily other social benefit objectives. Future research could explore the impacts of decentrally governed DLT systems on regu latory regimes targeting the achievement of broader social benefit outcomes such as citizen enfranchisement, delivery of social services, and environmental protections.

3) Due to the emerging nature of DLT systems and limited global experience in observing the risks of their introduction and regula tion, the research did not incorporate use of longitudinal regulatory risk and control treatment data. Future research should evaluate the efficacy of implemented regulatory controls using quantitative and case study data from DLT regulatory control implementations.

## 8. Conclusion

In this paper, we discuss how decentrally governed DLT systems introduce risks to regulated markets that warrant a rethinking of con ventional regulatory control regimes. The recent emergence of DLT systems into regulated markets presents challenges to regulators with limited practical experience in identifying and treating the risks these systems introduce. This lack of industry experience is exacerbated by a limited body of academic research on DLT regulation. This presented us with a compelling research question – how should regulators respond to the risks of decentrally governed DLT systems? We addressed this question by supplementing the limited existing research on DLT gover nance and regulation with the expertise of DLT policy experts to inform both problem formulation and the co-design of a DLT regulatory control framework. We hope this collaborative approach of integrating aca demic research with the expertise of regulatory practitioners will inform future regulation research on DLT and other emerging technologies.

What is clear from this research collaboration is that regulation must adapt to the risks presented by decentrally governed DLT systems. These risks encompass the destabilization of regulated systems and potential harms to those participating in them. Conventional regulatory controls stand to be bypassed by decentralised governance and may no longer provide the leverage they once did to manage systemic risks. Regulators must assess this evolving risk landscape, adapt their regulatory control regimes, and update their knowledge of the emerging technologies that are transforming regulated markets. This study presents regulators, regulated entities and researchers with a framework of risks, regulatory control treatments and design principles to inform the response to the emergence of decentrally governed DLT systems.
