# Calculus interdependency, personality contingency, and causal asymmetry: Toward a configurational privacy calculus model of information disclosure

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Privacy calculus model Configurational perspective Social networking sites Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) Behavioral activation / inhibition system

## A B S T R A C T

This study proposes a configurational privacy calculus model (CPCM) to understand calculus interdependency, personality contingency, and causal asymmetry in the information disclosure behavior in social networking sites (SNS). The results based on qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) showed that high disclosure can be caused by the configurations with the presence of at least one perceived benefit among the three types of benefits (i.e., selfexpression, life documentation, and social rewards) while the absence of privacy risks, or the configurations with the presence of both perceived benefits and privacy risks meanwhile with the presence of behavioral activation system (BAS) but the absence of behavioral inhibition system (BIS). In contrast, low disclosure can be caused by the configurations with the absence of all the three types of perceived benefits, or the configurations with the presence of both perceived benefits and privacy risks meanwhile with the presence of BIS, but the absence of BAS.

## 1. Introduction

Privacy calculus model (PCM) has been widely used by prior studies as the theoretical foundation to understand individuals’ information disclosure behavior in social networking sites (SNS) [1, 2]. PCM con tends that users make decisions on whether to disclose their personal information based on a tradeoff between the benefits and the costs involved in the disclosure behavior. Despite its effectiveness, prior studies based on PCM have revealed inconsistent research findings [3]. One possible explanation for the mixed results can be attributed to the inappropriate assumption which states that benefits and costs function independently. That is, the impacts of privacy risks on behaviors do not rely on the levels of benefits. However, users’ disclosure behaviors may be not solely determined by benefits or costs, but the comparison be tween benefits and costs, such that users will commit to disclosure be haviors only when benefits outweigh costs. This theorization asks for taking the interdependency between benefits and costs during the cal culus process into account. However, although prior studies have highlighted the importance of benefit–cost tradeoff [e.g., 4, 5], the tradeoff has not been explicitly reflected in the research model.

Besides the tradeoff between benefits and costs, another issue related to calculus interdependency is about the tradeoff between different kinds of benefits. Extant literature on information disclosure in SNS has identified a variety of benefits derived from information disclosure, including self-presentation or self-expression [1, 6–8], relationship building and enhancement [1, 6] or social rewards [9, 10], information storage or life documentation [11, 12], and enjoyment [6, 13]. How ever, it is not necessary to fulfill all these benefits for users to engage in information disclosure. For instance, some users may stress on self-expression, while other users may emphasize on social rewards. Thus, theorizing the calculus interdependence should provide insight about the tradeoff between different benefits too.

Further, regarding the importance of benefit–cost tradeoff, it is necessary to accurately figure out how individuals calculate benefits and costs before making final decisions. One critical issue here is about how to set the weights of benefits and costs in the calculus process. The weight setting may vary across individuals because some individuals may pay more attention to the benefits, while others may care more about the costs. This is consistent with the dual motivational system theory which argue that there are two parallel motivational systems namely behavioral activation system (BAS) and behavioral inhibition system (BIS) [14]. Relying on different motivation systems, individuals will have different preferences for benefits and costs. Therefore, to precisely understand the privacy calculus process calls for considering the individual differences and viewing it from a personality-contingency perspective.

Finally, prior studies on information disclosure in SNS assume that users disclose or do not disclose personal information following the same logic [e.g., 1, 15, 16]. In particular, if the increase of a factor leads to the increase of the dependent variable, the decrease of the factor will lead to the decrease of the dependent variable too. However, anecdotal evi dences suggest that users may disclose or do not disclose personal in formation for different reasons [e.g., 17–19], calling for understanding the causal asymmetry.

To address these issues, not only the joint effect of benefits and risks, but also the joint effect of benefits/risks and dual motivational systems (i.e., BAS/BIS) should be considered. This causal complexity calls for a paradigm shift from a variance perspective to a configurational perspective [20]. Unlike the variance perspective which stresses on the independent effects of different factors, the configurational perspective sheds light on the holistic configurations of a set of causal elements which are highly connected. Although the variance perspective may be able to address the interdependence issue through interaction or moderation effects, the interaction or moderation effects can only handle the interdependencies between a limited number of variables (no more than three) [20]. Beyond addressing the issue of multiple in terdependencies, the configurational perspective is also able to generate a series of solutions which can reach the same final outcome (e.g., equifinality) [20]. Further, the configurational perspective also has the advantages in identifying the different mechanisms to explain why users disclose versus do not disclose their personal information in terms of causal asymmetry [20]. For these reasons, this study attempts to advance the traditional variance-based PCM (VPCM) by proposing a configurational PCM (CPCM) to shed light on the issues of calculus interdependency, personality contingency, and causal asymmetry dur ing the privacy calculus process.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Privacy calculus model

Privacy has become a major concern for users of a variety of infor mation services including location-based services [21-23]. social networking services [24–26], sharing economy platforms [27–29], and big data analytics [30, 31]. PCM has been widely used as the theoretical underpinning to explain individuals’ privacy disclosure behaviors based on the balance between benefits and costs derived from the disclosure behavior [22, 24, 32–35], such that perceived benefits and privacy risks or concerns are. respectively. positively and negatively associated with privacy disclosure behaviors. Previous studies drawing upon PCM tend to analyze the independent effects of privacy-related benefits and costs. When analyzing the effects of one of the two factors, the other factor is treated as given or the effect of the other factor is controlled. However, this approach cannot capture the exact benefit–cost calculus process which is related to the comparison between benefits and costs. Ac cording to justice theory [36] which is regarded as the theoretical foundation of PCM [4], it is the justice or the comparison between benefits and costs rather than benefits or costs only that determine individual behaviors. In this sense, individuals may disclose their personal information when privacy risks are high given that perceived benefits are much higher, or not disclose their personal information when perceived benefits are high given that privacy risks are much higher. Hence, privacy-related benefits and costs should be considered inter dependently other than independently.

Past research on the information disclosure in SNS has identified various factors that reflect the benefits associated with information disclosure. Specifically, following the theory of perceived value, these benefits can be classified into four categories, namely, utility value, social value for others, social value for self, and emotional value<sup>1</sup>[37]. Utility value refers to the extent to which SNS use can help users to improve communication efficiency and effectiveness, and has been captured by perceived usefulness in a broad term based on technology acceptance model [38–40]. However, perceived usefulness may reflect the benefits related to SNS use in general rather than the benefits specific to information disclosure. Thus, in this study, we deploy another context-specific construct, life documentation, as the utility value derived from information disclosure. Life documentation refers to the extent to which individuals perceive that disclosing information can document or archive their personal experiences [12]. Taking SNS as a platform to store personal information [1], users may disclose their in formation in SNS as a way to record their lives.

Social value consists of two dimensions, namely, the social value for others and the social value for self [37]. The social value for others refers to the social consequences for other individuals around the focal indi vidual. It can be captured by relationship building which stresses on building new relationships with others [1, 6] and relationship mainte nance that emphasizes on maintaining extant relationships [1, 6]. Social rewards are another term used to cover all these benefits [9, 10]. In this study, to simplify the discussion, we use the overall concept social re wards to capture the social value for others. The social value for self can be reflected by self-presentation [1, 6] or self-expression [7, 8, 41] which refers to the extent to which individuals perceive that disclosing information can express their personal interests, ideas, and feelings. It is also regarded as an important predictor of information disclosure in SNS and is included in this study.

Based on technology adoption theories, prior studies have also considered perceived enjoyment as the factor reflecting the emotional value [6, 13]. However, when revisiting these literatures, we find that this benefit may be derived from SNS use in general (majorly informa tion consumption) rather than information disclosure. Further, perceived enjoyment may be a result of the obtainment of other benefits (e.g., social rewards [9]), leading to conceptual overlap between different dimensions of perceived benefits. Thus, we have not included this dimension in this study to ensure context-relevance and avoid conceptual overlaps.

Although prior studies have investigated the impacts of various benefits on information disclosure, these studies treat different benefits as independent rather than interdependent. However, when making decision about whether to disclose their personal information, different users may focus on different benefits. In this case, it is not necessary to provide all types of benefits to users because obtaining certain benefits is adequate for users to disclose their information. For instance, Krasnova et al. [6] found the significant effect of relationship building but the insignificant effect of self-presentation on information disclosure. This suggests that there may be substitutive effects between different types of benefits, another issue relevant to calculus interdependence.

Further, during the privacy calculus process, not only the values of benefits and risks, but also the weights for benefits and risks matter. Thus, a follow-up question is: will individuals enact same behaviors when facing the same levels of benefits and costs? It is relevant to whether individuals think in the same pattern when integrating the specific evaluations on benefits and costs to form an overall evaluation. According to theory of reasoned action (TRA), attitude as an overall evaluation on the behavioral consequences can be assessed as the sum of the product of each consequence (i.e., belief) and its desirability [42]. Hence, beyond the levels of benefits and costs, the desirability or pref erences used to weigh benefits and costs also matter. Regarding that there are significant individual differences in preferences for benefits and costs depending on the dominant motivational system in use [14], a theory-informed understanding about privacy calculus calls for consid ering these individual differences.

## 2.2. Behavioral activation / inhibition system (BAS/BIS)

A series of dual process models, despite the different terms in use, argue that there are two distinct motivational systems guiding in dividuals’ behaviors: one system relevant to the negative or dark side (e. g., costs) and the other system relevant to the positive or bright side (e. g., benefits). Dual motivational system theory specifies these two sys tems as BAS and BIS, respectively [14]. BAS is based on nurture-related needs (e.g., advancement) and captures an approach orientation, while BIS is based on safety and responsibilities and reflects an avoidance orientation. Therefore, when making decision, individuals with BAS will try to maximize their gains while individuals with BIS will engage in minimizing their losses [43].

The role of BAS/BIS has not been widely examined in prior privacy literatures. In the limited studies, they may focus on the main effects of the two motivational systems [44, 45]. Specifically, Jin [45] examined the impacts of BAS and BIS on information sensitivity and disclosure. However, the moderating effects of BAS/BIS is still understudied with the exception of some studies in other research contexts, such as infor mation security policy [46] and crowdfunding [47]. Therefore, this study will engage in revealing the fit between privacy calculus factors and BAS/BIS. Regarding multiple interdependencies existing in the in teractions between these two categories of factors, we propose to view it from a configurational perspective.

## 2.3. Configurational perspective

There are three key advantages of the configurational perspective over the traditional variance-based perspective: integrity, equifinality, and causal asymmetry (see Table 1).

First, unlike the variance-based perspective that focuses on the in dependent effects (or interdependent effects of limited factors) and provides a local view of the impacts of certain factors, the configura tional perspective considers the interdependence of all the causal ele ments or conditions and offers a holistic view [20, 48–51]. In other words, the configurational perspective assumes that any causal element cannot lead to the outcome separately and the existence of other causal conditions gives meanings to the focal causal condition [20]. Compared with the variance-based perspective, the configurational perspective is more powerful to explain the contingency between privacy risks and perceived benefits (e.g., calculus interdependency) and the contingency between privacy calculus and BAS/BIS (e.g., personality contingency).

Second, unlike the variance-based perspective which assumes that there is one single solution to capture the causal relationship between variables, the configurational perspective postulates that there are multiple solutions which can lead to the same outcome (e.g., equifin ality) [20, 48]. Specifically, the variance-based perspective may suggest that when privacy risks are high, privacy disclosure will be low. This argument cannot explain the situation in which individuals may disclose their personal information even when privacy risks are high [52]. In contrast, the configurational perspective can well address this problem by arguing that there are multiple solutions leading to disclosure and privacy risks can be high in certain solutions, but low in other solutions (e.g., when perceived benefits are high or when promotion focus is stronger than prevention focus).

Third, unlike the variance-based perspective that assumes the sym metric causality between independent variables and dependent vari ables, the configurational perspective contends that the causes leading to high or low outcomes are different (e.g., causal asymmetry) [48]. Specifically, the variance-based perspective argues that the higher the privacy risks, the lower the privacy disclosure. According to the sym metric principle, the lower the privacy risks, the higher the privacy disclosure. However, even though low privacy risk may be a causal condition of a solution leading to high privacy disclosure, it does not mean that high privacy risk is a necessary condition for the solutions leading to low privacy disclosure. For example, individuals may not disclose their personal information even though privacy risks are low, because perceived benefits may be low too. Thus, the configurational perspective can better untangle the asymmetric mechanisms of infor mation disclosure.

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. Theoretical framework

Following Lee et al. [53], we deployed an abductive theory building approach to identify the configurations of information disclosure. The abductive theory building approach could take the advantages of both typology (a top-down theorizing approach) and taxonomy (a bottom-up theorizing approach) and is regarded as more useful for the research topics with fragmented and inconsistent knowledge [53]. Specifically, three mechanisms were taken as the foundation for developing the configurational hypotheses (see Fig. 1).

The first mechanism is about the substitutive effects between different dimensions of perceived benefits. The key logic here is that individuals may not consider all the three dimensions of perceived benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) when making decision. For instance, some individuals may only focus on self-expression while pay less attention to other benefits. In this case, they will feel satisfied as long as the need for self-expression is met. That is, whether life documentation and social rewards are available do not affect their decision-making. Prior empirical studies have provided certain clues to this argument. For example, Krasnova et al. [6] found that relationship building had a significant effect on self-disclosure while self-presentation did not.

The second mechanism is about the tradeoff between perceived benefits and privacy risks. This mechanism is based on the PCM which postulates that the net value reflected by the difference between perceived benefits and privacy risks determines individuals’ information disclosure behaviors [4, 24]. Thus, when hypothesizing the configura tions of information disclosure, the comparison between perceived benefits and privacy risks should be taken into account. As shown later, this mechanism is treated as the primary mechanism for hypothesis development.

The third mechanism is about the fit between benefits/risks and personalities, which argues that for individuals relying on different behavioral motivational systems, the weights of benefits and risks used in the privacy calculus will be different [14, 43]. When BAS is the dominant motivational system, individuals will rely more on perceived benefits over privacy risks [46, 47]. In contrast, when BIS is the domi nant motivation system, privacy risks will play a more important role [46, 47, 54]. This mechanism is used to solve the uncertain situations when both perceived benefits and privacy risks are high, or when both perceived benefits and privacy risks are low.

Based on the benefit substitution mechanism, the benefit–risk tradeoff mechanism, and the personality contingency mechanism, we propose the configurational hypotheses shown in Table 2. We will pro vide more detailed explanations for these configurations below.

## 3.2. Configurations for differential benefits and risks

Traditional PCM argues that individuals balance perceived benefits and privacy risks and disclose their personal information when perceived benefits outweigh privacy risks [4, 24]. In contrast, when privacy risks are greater than perceived benefits, individuals will not disclose their privacy [4, 24]. Thus, two obvious configurations, respectively, for high disclosure and low disclosure can be derived from PCM. A configuration with the presence of perceived benefits but the absence of privacy risks will lead to high disclosure, while a configu ration with the presence of privacy risks but the absence of perceived benefits will result in low disclosure. Within the research context of SNS, when individuals consider the benefits brought by information disclo sure including self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards are higher than privacy risks, they will disclose their personal infor mation, vice versa.

Further, regarding that there are multiple benefits associated with information disclosure in SNS, individuals may not rely on all these benefits for decision-making [6]. That is, the presence of certain benefits may be adequate to motivate individuals to engage in information disclosure, indicating that the presence of certain benefits may crowd-out the effects of other benefits (i.e., substitutive effects). In this case, the meanings of presence or absence of perceived benefits should take the interdependence between different benefits into account. Spe cifically, the presence of perceived benefits means the presence of at least one type of perceived benefits, which can be self-expression, life documentation, or social rewards. In contrast, the absence of perceived benefits means that all the three kinds of benefits should be absent.

Integrating the benefit substitution mechanism and the benefit–risk tradeoff mechanism, high disclosure may be achieved when at least one of the three benefits are present while privacy risks are absent, while low disclosure can be reached when all the three benefits are absent and privacy risks are present. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H1a. High disclosure can be caused by the configuration with the pres ence of at least one type of perceived benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) and the absence of privacy risks. H2a. Low disclosure can be caused by the configuration with the absence of all the three types of perceived benefits and the presence of privacy risks.

## 3.3. Configurations with the co-absence / presence of benefits and risks

When both perceived benefits and privacy risks are low, individuals will not disclose their personal information for two major reasons. First, according to PCM, whether individuals would like to disclose informa tion depends on the net value which is calculated as the difference be tween perceived benefits and privacy risks [4, 24]. In this case, net value will be low when perceived benefits are low, leading to low disclosure. Second, according to the dual motivational system theory [45, 55, 56], whether benefits or risks are regarded as the primary determinant de pends on the nature of behaviors, such that benefits will be the primary determinant for the approach behavior while risks will be the primary determinant for the avoidance behavior. Regarding information disclo sure as an approach behavior, individuals will not disclose their personal information once the needs for benefits are not satisfied. Further, considering the benefit substitution mechanism, individuals will perceive low benefits when all the three types of benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) are low. Therefore, we propose that:

H2b. Low disclosure can be caused by the configuration with the absence of all the three types of perceived benefits and the absence of privacy risks.

When both perceived benefits and privacy risks are high, it will be difficult to evaluate the net value simply based on perceived benefits and privacy risks. In this situation, the weights of benefits and risks in the privacy calculus process also matter [42]. That is, when the weights of benefits are greater than the weights of risks, benefits will outweigh risks and high disclosure will be achieved. In contrast, when the weights of risks are greater than the weights of benefits, risks will be greater than benefits and low disclosure will be reached.

According to the dual motivational system theory [45, 55, 56], the weights of benefits and risks can be evaluated through referring to the relative importance of promotion focus (BAS) versus prevention focus (BIS). Specifically, the weights of benefits will be higher when BAS is taken as the dominant condition, while the weights of risks will be higher when BIS is taken as the dominant condition. BAS is regarded as a dominant condition in two situations: BAS is present while BIS is absent, or both BAS and BIS are present where BAS serves as the core condition while BIS acts as the peripheral condition. Similarly, BIS is regarded as the dominant condition when BIS is present while BAS is absent, or when BIS is present as a core condition while BAS is present as a peripheral condition.

Integrating the rationales to evaluate the dominance of BAS/BIS, the personality contingency mechanism, and the benefit substitution mechanism, we propose the possible configurations leading to high disclosure and low disclosure. Specifically, for SNS users with high BAS but low BIS, they will rely more on potential benefits rather than po tential risks caused by information disclosure [45]. In this case, when SNS users face at least one of the disclosure benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) and high risks (i.e., privacy risks), they will disclose their personal information because the benefits outweigh the risks leading to a positive net value. This can be evidenced by prior studies on information disclosure which found that social rewards exert stronger impacts on self-disclosure than pri vacy concerns [9]. Further, Ma-Kellams and Wu [57] claim BAS/BIS as the key mediators to explain the role of gender differences in behavioral responses, and perceived benefits are found to exert a stronger impact on information disclosure than privacy risks for males who are BAS-dominated than females who are BIS-dominated [15], providing some cues for our conclusion.

In contrast, for SNS users with low BAS but high BIS, they will make their decisions majorly based on potential risks rather than potential benefits caused by information disclosure [45]. In this case, despite SNS users recognize that disclosing personal information can bring certain benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards), they believe that these benefits cannot offset their privacy concerns leading to a negative net value. Prior studies have shown that privacy risks have stronger impacts on disclosure than perceived benefits for females who are BIS-dominated than for males who are BAS-dominated [15, 57], consistent with our arguments. Therefore, we propose that:

H1b. High disclosure can be caused by the configuration with the copresence of at least one type of perceived benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) and privacy risks, meanwhile with the presence of BAS but the absence of BIS.

H1c. High disclosure can be caused by the configuration with the copresence of at least one type of perceived benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) and privacy risks, meanwhile with the presence of BAS as a core condition while the presence of BIS as a peripheral condition.

H2c. Low disclosure can be caused by the configuration with the copresence of at least one type of perceived benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) and privacy risks, meanwhile with the presence of BIS but the absence of BAS.

H2d. Low disclosure can be caused by the configuration with the copresence of at least one type of perceived benefits (e.g., self-expression, life documentation, and social rewards) and privacy risks, meanwhile with the presence of BIS as a core condition while the presence of BAS as a peripheral condition.

A study about the personal information disclosure in the context of SNS will be conducted to test the proposed configurational hypotheses.

## 4. Research methods

## 4.1. Measures

The measures for all the constructs were adapted from prior studies (see Appendix A) and adjusted to fit with the research context (i.e., in formation disclosure in SNS). Specifically, three items to measure pri vacy risks were adapted from Zhou [58]. Both BAS and BIS were measured according to Carver & White [14]. The dependent variable intention to disclose was measured with three items adapted from Xu et al. [4]. As to the three types of benefits, self-expression was measured with four items adapted from Gao and Feng [7], life documentation was measured with three items adapted from Huang et al. [12], and social rewards were measured with three items adapted from Jiang et al. [9]. Seven-point Likert scales were used for all the items. The respondents were asked to answer the extent to which they agreed with the state ments, ranging from 1=strongly disagree to 7=strongly agree. As the survey was conducted in China, the back-translation approach was used to translate the original English items into Chinese.

## 4.2. Data collection

WeChat Moment was taken as the research setting, because it is regarded as the most influential SNS in China. According to the annual data report of WeChat, its monthly active accounts exceeded 1.15 billion in 2019. In WeChat Moment, users can post image, text, and videos to share their personal opinions, feelings, activities, photos, etc. Thus, it is fit with our research objective to understand information disclosure in SNS.

We used a reputed panel company, WJX.cn (www.wjx.cn) in particular, to collect the data to ensure the coverage of the population. There are over 2.6 million members who can be accessed by WJX.cn, and there are over 30,000 projects that take WJX.cn as the data collection channel. We paid 15 RMB for each questionnaire as the ser vice fee. To ensure the respondents were WeChat Moment users, we added one screening question (“Are you a WeChat Moment user?”) before the formal questionnaire and only those with prior WeChat Moment experience were eligible.

Finally, we obtained 529 valid responses. About 60% of the re spondents were female, over 60% of them were with the age above 25, about 80% of them were with a bachelor or master degree, over 60% of them are professionals rather than students, over 70% of them had used WeChat Moment for over five years.

## 5. Data analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM), SmartPLS in particular, was used to evaluated the measurement model [59], while fuzzy-set quali tative comparative analysis (fsQCA) [60] was used to test the hypotheses related to configurations of causal conditions.

## 5.1. Measurement model

Measurement model was evaluated by checking construct reliability and validity. Before the formal analysis, two items of BAS (e.g., APRO3 and APRO13) and two items of BIS (e.g., AVDO1 and ADVO7) were removed firstly due to low item loadings. After removing these items, the CRs and AVEs were above 0.7 and 0.5, respectively, for all the constructs (see Table 3), indicating that these constructs were with good reliabilities [61]. Convergent validities were evaluated by checking the item loadings on their corresponding constructs. The results showed that all the item loadings on their respective constructs were greater than 0.6, ensuring the convergent validities of these constructs. Finally, discriminant validities were evaluated by comparing the square roots of AVEs and the relevant correlations. Table 3 showed that the square roots of AVEs were higher than the related correlations for all the constructs, suggesting that the discriminant validities of these constructs were good [62].

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

After confirming the reliability and validity of the constructs, we packaged the items using mean values to form a single value for each construct, because QCA only can handle variables with single item [60]. As all the variables measured in this study were continuous variables, fsQCA other than crisp-set QCA (csQCA) was used to analyze the data. Unlike SEM that is based on variance or co-variance analysis, fsQCA is based on set theory, Boolean algebra, and counterfactual analysis [63]. Thus, the raw scores of the variables should be firstly transformed into membership scores ranging from 0 to 1 through the process of calibration. The calibration can be accomplished by specifying three qualitative breakpoints which indicate full membership, full non-membership, and the crossover point. Following the calibration procedure in prior studies [e.g., 48, 49], we firstly sorted the variable in ascending order, and then specified the full membership threshold as the point covering 75% of the data values, the full non-membership threshold as the point covering 25% of the data values, and the cross over threshold as the point covering 50% of the data values (i.e., median).

Before conducting the analysis of sufficient conditional combina tions. we first checked the necessity of each condition to identify whether some conditions were always present or absent in all cases. The necessity analysis results (see Table 4) showed that the consistency and coverage values of each condition were lower than the recommended threshold of 0.9 [64], suggesting to further consider the combination of conditions in the sufficiency analysis.

To conduct the sufficiency analysis, a truth table can be generated using the membership scores through the truth table algorithm in fsQCA software. Specifically, if there are k variables, there will be 2<sup>k</sup> rows in a truth table covering all possible permutations in terms of the high versus low levels of these variables. The truth table can be further trimmed by setting the minimum number of cases (i.e., frequency cutoff) and the minimum consistency level (i.e., consistency cutoff) [20]. The selection of frequency cutoff should ensure that at least 80% of the cases are included in the analysis [64]. In this study, following prior studies [e.g., 48. 491. we set the frequency cutoff as 2 and 96% of cases were left. A series of robustness check was conducted by setting the frequency cutoff as 3 (89% of cases left) and 4 (83% of cases left), and the results sug gested that the findings were almost consistent (see Appendix C). As to the consistency cutoff, we considered two measures namely raw con sistency and PRI (proportional reduction in inconsistency) consistency. Raw consistency should be above 0.75 and PRI consistency should be at least above 0.5, and the consistency cutoff can be set by capturing the break point in which the consistency significantly drops between two rows [48, 64]. In this study, raw consistency was set as 0.75 and PRI consistency as 0.6 which could be regarded as the break point (see the truth tables for high disclosure and low disclosure in Appendix B).

Based on the reduced truth table, Boolean algebra and counterfactual analysis were conducted to generate the configurations for high disclosure and low disclosure, respectively (more technical details can be found in Ragin [60]). Table 5 shows the results of configurational analysis. The analysis yields six solutions for high disclosure while three solutions for low disclosure.

As to high disclosure, the solutions 1, 2, 3, and 5 (i.e., SH1–3 and SH5) showed that at least one type of perceived benefits was present while privacy risks were absent (i.e., PB•\~PR), lending support to H1a. In solutions 4 and 6 (i.e., SH4 and SH6), at least one type of perceived benefits was present and privacy risks could be either present or absent.

These two solutions could be further decomposed into more solutions regarding the status of privacy risks. If privacy risks were absent, SH4 and SH6 could be regarded as the configuration with the presence of at least one benefit and the absence of privacy risks (i.e., PB•\~PR•BAS•\~BIS), consistent with the prediction of H1a. If privacy risks were present, SH4 and SH6 indicated that both perceived benefits (at least one dimension) and privacy risks were present. Meanwhile, to achieve high disclosure, BAS should be present while BIS should be absent (i.e., PB•PR•BAS•\~BIS), supporting H1b.

Further, these solutions also demonstrated the substitutive effects between different benefits. Specifically, social rewards were found to be present in SH3, SH4, SH5, and SH6 while be either present or absent in SH1 and SH2. Self-expression was found to be present in SH1, SH2, SH3, and SH6, but absent in SH4 and SH5. Life documentation was found to be present in SH1 and SH6. absent in SH4 and SH5, and either present or absent in SH2 and SH3. These solutions confirmed that it was not necessary to require all the benefits to be present, such that individuals might disclose their personal information in SNS when some of the benefits were obtained. Besides, these solutions also suggested that the three types of benefits may have different priorities. Regarding the key objective of information disclosure in SNS is for social values, the two types of social values (i.e.. social rewards and self-expression) should be more important than the utility value (i.e., life documentation). As shown in the results, either social rewards or self-expression can appear alone in some configurations (e.g., SH2 for social rewards, and SH4 and SH5 for self-expression), while life documentation must appear together with other benefits (e.g., SH1 and SH6), suggesting that utility value alone is not adequate to satisfy users’ needs for benefits. Moreover, as to the two types of social value, social rewards which are related to the dyadic interactions with others should be more important than selfexpression which only stresses on the unidirectional interaction (i.e., others’ responses are not so necessary). As illustrated in SH4, social rewards alone were adequate to compensate for privacy risks. In contrast, self-expression could compensate for privacy risks only when social rewards and life documentation were present too (e.g., SH6).

As to the configurations for low disclosure, the first solution (i.e., SL1) indicated that when all the three kinds of perceived benefits were absent and when privacy risks were present, low disclosure could be caused, supporting H2a. The second solution (SL2) showed that all the three types of perceived benefits were absent and privacy risks could be present or absent. Thus, SL2 could be further decomposed into two subsolutions one with the presence of privacy risks and the other with the absence of privacy risks. When privacy risks were present, this solution is consistent with the prediction of H2a. When privacy risks were absent, the solution provided support to H2b. The third solution (SL3) reflected that at least one dimension of perceived benefits (i.e., self-expression) was present and privacy risks were present too. In this situation, both BAS and BIS were present, while BIS served as a core condition while

BAS acts as a peripheral condition, consistent with the prediction of H2d. SL3 also provided evidences for the priority of different benefits. Specifically, users may not disclose personal information when selfexpression / life documentation and privacy risks co-exist, while social rewards must be absent, indicating that social rewards may be the most important benefit such that its presence may compensate for privacy risks and make users disclose their personal information.

However, two hypotheses H1c and H2c were not supported by any solutions. As to H1c, one possible explanation is that when both BAS and BIS are present, even though BIS is present as a peripheral condition, perceived benefits may not be able to outweigh privacy risks. As to H2c, we further checked the truth table for low disclosure and found that in all the configurations related to PB•PR•\~BAS•BIS, social rewards were present. Consistent with our argument about benefit priority, social re wards as the most important benefit might be adequate to compensate for privacy risks and lead to high information disclosure finally (see Table 6).

## 6. Discussions and implications

## 6.1. Discussion on the key findings

The data analysis results reveal different theoretical findings derived from the configurational perspective (CPCM) compared with the variance-based perspective (VPCM; see Table 7).

First, VPCM views the impacts of perceived benefits and privacy risks as well as the different dimensions of perceived benefits independently, while CPCM considers the calculus interdependency between perceived benefits and privacy risks. Specifically, based on the independent assumption, VPCM suggests that the higher (lower) the perceived ben efits (the privacy risks), the higher the disclosure behavior. In contrast, CPCM implies that high (low) perceived benefits (privacy risks) do not necessarily lead to high disclosure because information disclosure is determined by the comparison between benefits and risks, such that individuals may not disclose their information even when perceived benefits are high given that privacy risks are high too. Meanwhile, un like VPCM which suggests to enhance all the dimensions of perceived benefits. CPCM contends that the fulfillment of certain benefits may be adequate for individuals to engage in information disclosure behavior and it is not necessary to ask for all the dimensions of perceived benefits to be obtained.

Second, beyond VPCM, CPCM argues that the privacy calculus pro cess is contingent on individuals’ personality (i.e., BAS/BIS). Specif ically, VPCM assumes that perceived benefits (privacy risks) are always positively (negatively) associated with disclosure behavior. However, for different individuals, they may rely on benefits and risks in different fashions. Other than simply considering the values of benefits and costs, the weights used to integrate benefits and costs to form an overall evaluation also matter. CPCM proposes that when both perceived ben efits and privacy risks are high, whether the net value is positive or negative will be further determined by the dominant motivational sys tem (BAS versus BIS) in use. Individuals will disclose their personal in formation when BAS is dominant while not when BIS is dominant.

Third, compared with VPCM, CPCM sheds more light on the causal asymmetry existing between high and low disclosure behaviors. VPCM assumes that individuals disclose or do not disclose personal information following the symmetric mechanisms. For instance, individuals will disclose information when perceived benefits are high while will not disclose information when perceived benefits are low. However, CPCM argues that the configurations for high and low disclosure behaviors are different. Even if the presence of a condition $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ , perceived benefits) is regarded as a condition within a certain configuration for high disclo sure (e.g., SH6), it does not mean that the presence of this condition cannot appear in the configurations for low disclosure (e.g., SL3).

Specifically, SH1, SH2, and SH3 indicates that when users believe that information disclosure can fulfill their needs for social rewards, self expression, or life documentation, and privacy risks are low, they will disclose their personal information. This is consistent with prior studies based on VPCM which found the positive effects of perceived benefits (e. g., social rewards [9, 10], self-expression [1, 6–8], and life documen tation [11, 12]) and the negative effects of privacy risks. SH4 and SH6 show that when users believe that information disclosure can fulfill their needs for social rewards, self-expression, or life documentation and they rely on the BAS for decision-making, they will disclose their personal information regardless whether privacy risks are high or low. This is consistent with some prior studies which found the insignificant effects of privacy risks [e.g., 65]. In other words, it implies that users may disclose their personal information when perceived benefits are high while privacy risks are low consistent with VPCM, or when both perceived benefits and privacy risks are high, consistent with prior studies which found the positive relationship between privacy risks and disclosure [41]. In this study, we further figure out that these contro versial findings may be caused by the individual differences in BAS/BIS. SH4 and SH5 show that users may disclose their information even when self-expression and life documentation are low, reflecting the substitu tion effects between different disclosure benefits, such that social re wards may have stronger impacts than other benefits [6].

For low disclosure, SL1 shows that when perceived benefits are low while privacy risks are high, users will not disclose their personal in formation, consistent with VPCM [e.g., 9]. SL2 suggests that when perceived benefits are low, users will not disclose their information regardless whether privacy risks are high or low, consistent with prior studies which found the insignificant effects of privacy risks [e.g., 65]. SL3 indicates that users may not disclose their information even when they can obtain certain benefits (e.g., self-expression) because they rely on the BIS for decision-making and privacy risks are high. This can be evidenced by prior studies which found that a single benefit may be not able to offset the negative effect of privacy risks [1].

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

The key theoretical contribution of this study is to propose and empirically test a CPCM. This is the first study, to the best of our knowledge, which views the privacy calculus process through a configurational perspective. CPCM shifts the research focus from a local view which investigates the independent effects of a variety of privacy calculus factors to a holistic view that cares about the configurations of a set of causal conditions. Besides bringing in a new theoretical perspec tive, this study also introduces a new data analysis technique, fsQCA specifically, to the research on information disclosure, enriching the research methods which are mainly based on SEM. fsQCA is matched with the configurational perspective of theory building and has the advantages over SEM in providing better explanations for multiple so lutions, equifinality, and causal asymmetry. The configurational theo retical perspective together with the fsQCA analysis technique lay the foundation for future research.

Beyond the theoretical and methodological shift in general, this study also makes three specific theoretical contributions to prior research on privacy and disclosure behavior. First, this study extends PCM by figuring out the interdependency between perceived benefits and privacy risks and the interdependency between different dimensions of perceived benefits during the privacy calculus process. VPCM based on the independent assumption cannot well explain why individuals may still tend to disclose their personal information even though they are concerned about their privacy [52]. In contrast, CPCM contends that disclosure behavior is not solely determined by perceived benefits or privacy risks but by the comparison or difference between perceived benefits and privacy risks. This calls for embedding the interdependency between perceived benefits and privacy risks into the theorization of the privacy calculus process. Further, the interdependency between different dimensions of perceived benefits suggests that not all the benefits should be satisfied to motivate individuals to disclose personal information, and there are substitutive effects between different di mensions of perceived benefits, providing an explanation for the inconsistent findings about the impacts of different benefits in prior studies [1, 6].

Second, this study identifies the contextual factors contingent on privacy calculus through highlighting the individual differences in motivational systems. Prior studies based on VPCM only emphasize on the values of perceived benefits and privacy risks but neglect the important role of weights used in the privacy calculus process. CPCM proposes to consider both values and weights during privacy calculus. Specifically, this study introduces the dual motivational system theory into the model and theorizes the contingency between BAS/BIS and benefit–cost tradeoff. Unlike VPCM which is unable to predict in dividuals’ disclosure behaviors when both perceived benefits and pri vacy risks are high, CPCM claims that individuals will disclose personal information when BAS is greater than BIS while not disclose personal information when BAS is smaller than BIS.

Third, this study identifies the asymmetric mechanisms to explain high and low disclosure behavior. Although prior research has recog nized a variety of asymmetric concepts, such as satisfaction and dissat isfaction [66], trust and distrust [67], the differential mechanisms to explain these asymmetric concepts are not been well investigated. Based on the configurational perspective, CPCM specifies the asymmetric configurations leading to high and low disclosure. Specifically, high disclosure can be caused when perceived benefits are high while privacy risks are low, or when both perceived benefits and privacy risks are high in the condition of high-BAS–low-BIS. Low disclosure can be caused when perceived benefits are low regardless whether conditions are high or low, or when both perceived benefits and privacy risks are high in the condition of low-BAS–high-BIS. The asymmetric mechanisms suggest that individuals disclose or do not disclose personal information for different reasons.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Some practical implications can be derived from the findings. First, the calculus interdependency between perceived benefits and privacy risks indicates that individuals take the comparison between perceived benefits and privacy risks as the basis for decision-making. Thus, during the service design process, practitioners should simultaneously consider the comparison between the potential benefits and costs induced by the design rather than consider them separately. Second, the substitution between different dimensions of perceived benefits suggests that it is not necessary to provide all the types of perceived benefits. Instead, service providers can identify users’ preferences and focus on those benefits during service design and delivery. Second, the personality contingency between BAS/BIS and privacy calculus indicates that individuals with different motivational systems will rely on different decision factors. Therefore, service providers should capture individuals’ motivational systems through analyzing their past behaviors and provide personal ized services that are matched with their motivational systems. Third, the causal asymmetry reveals the different reasons for individuals to disclose or not disclose personal information. Thus, for service providers with different marketing orientations (e.g., promoting disclosure versus avoiding withholding) should carry out different design strategies ac cording to the configurations identified in this study.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

It should be acknowledged that there are several limitations of this study. First, this study is conducted in certain special research contexts (i.e., SNS) in China, so whether the findings can be applied in other research contexts (e.g., e-health [68], e-commerce [5]) and other countries (e.g., USA) still call for further investigation. Future research in other research contexts may confirm our research findings or further advance our proposed model by considering contextual differences. Second, as the key objective of this study is to propose a CPCM, we only cover the key factors relevant to the privacy calculus process. However, there may be other important factors which also influence disclosure intention (e.g., social influence [1]). Thus, the proposed CPCM can be further extended by considering these potential factors. Third, this study takes behavioral intention rather than actual behavior as the outcome variable. Regarding the intention–behavior gap, researchers can inves tigate the configurations for actual disclosure behavior in future research. Fourth, in this study, information disclosure is investigated at the general level rather than the message-specific level. Regarding that individuals may disclose different types of information in different ways, future research can be conducted at the message-specific level through the methods of experiment or scenario-based survey to explore the causes leading to the disclosure of a specific message.
