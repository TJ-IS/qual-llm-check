## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Conceptual model, IT assimilation Case study Qualitative data analysis Morphological analysis IT audit IT governance

## A B S T R A C T

This study develops a conceptual model of organizational IT assimilation maturity based on constructs in the IS literature, qualitative data analysis of three exploratory case studies, and general morphological analysis (GMA). We inductively extract four dimensions of IT assimilation – business processes, information architecture, IT processes, and end-users – and characterize different maturity levels for each dimension. Applying Zwicky’s GMA on the combinatorially large space of IT assimilation configurations, we prune the space to a more limited number of internally consistent and empirically feasible IT assimilation maturity profiles. We discuss how the model can support IT audit and governance activities.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) permeates most organizational pro cesses, and the interaction among people, process, and technology (PPT) characterizes activities that make an organization function [78]. The significant levels of investment in IT have naturally led organizations to seek ways to make efficient and effective use of this investment [31]. Two related functions that address this need are IT audit and IT gover nance. An IT audit assesses a company’s technological infrastructure to ensure processes and systems run accurately and efficiently remain secure and meet compliance regulations [30]. IT governance, on the other hand, is a framework to align IT and business strategy with the objective of ensuring IT investments enhance business value [44]. It is intended to be a forward-looking and goal-seeking architecture rather than a point-in-time assessment like an IT audit. The two are related in that an IT audit serves as a test of whether specific IT governance ob jectives are being met and it also provides input to continually refine and update the IT governance architecture. In their 2019 Global IT Audit Benchmarking Study, Information Systems Audit and Control Associa tion (ISACA), the premier information systems (IS) audit practitioners organization, found that processes for managing data, harnessing emerging technology appropriately, and handling human resources, continue to be top-of-the-mind concerns for IT auditors and CIOs [42], substantiating our observation about the pervasive presence of IT, the interaction among PPT, and the continuing challenge of managing this resource to meet organizational goals.

The IS literature has long recognized that it is this interaction among

PPT that generates business value, rather than the technology alone [53]. This leads us to introduce the term “IT assimilation maturity” to signify the extent and sophistication of technology pervasiveness within an organization that catalyzes this interaction. We use the acronym IAM (conveniently pronounced ‘I am’ to convey the pervasiveness of the technology) as a shorthand for this term and point out that organizations differ in their IAM profiles. For instance, if one compares United Parcel Service (UPS) and the US Postal Service, it is clear that they differ in the scope and sophistication of their IT infrastructure, their business pro cesses, and the extent to which their people are efficacious in the use of IT [16, 47]. On the other hand, while UPS and FedEx are dissimilar in their competitive positioning [14], their IAM profiles are quite similar based on a review of their IT infrastructure, digitalization of processes, and technology used by employees. As an example outside the USA, two large banks in India – the State Bank of India and HDFC Bank, both with a significant scale of operations and a large number of heterogeneous customers, vary in their IAM profiles [54]. The differences in IAM pro files across organizations are a manifestation of different levels of maturity in the assimilation of IT by these organizations. While there have been numerous studies on specific aspects of IT assimilation within organizations [1, 48], there is a lack of studies that characterize an or ganization’s IT assimilation maturity in a holistic manner. A holistic view of IT assimilation is needed to reflect the interaction among PPT in the assimilation process, and a characterization of maturity is needed in order to assess an organization’s current state of IT assimilation and guides its evolution in the future. The aim of this study is to take initial steps toward building a conceptual model of IAM, analogous to the

Capability Maturity Model (CMM) for software development, or the Business Process Management Maturity Model [5]. The conceptual challenge in constructing an IAM model lies in parsimoniously identi fying the different dimensions of IT assimilation and ordinal levels for each dimension. So, an IAM model contributes to our theoretical un derstanding of IT assimilation. Since the assessment and evolution of organizational IT assimilation lie at the heart of IT governance, an IAM model can also contribute to practice as a tool to support the IT gover nance function.

We use an imbricated approach of combining extant literature and data drawn from exploratory positivist case studies involving interviews with senior executives in multiple organizations, augmented by sec ondary data, to construct a parsimonious model of IAM. The conceptual model that emerges from this analysis consists of four dimensions – business process, information architecture, IT process, and end-users. We then develop a four-level ordinal scale for each of the four di mensions drawing from the literature and our case studies. In order to structure the possible set of dimensions and ordinal levels and analyze them for mutually consistent configurations, we draw upon Fritz Zwicky’s (1967) general morphological analysis (GMA) approach which has been widely used in structuring problem complexes which are multidimensional and not easily quantified [43, 67]. We use empirical feasibility and internal consistency rules to identify the combinations, generating a manageable set of IT assimilation profiles that are mean ingful from an IT audit and governance standpoint.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we highlight the multifaceted nature of IT assimilation which corroborates the need for a holistic view of the phenomenon. We then briefly discuss the IT governance function, how the concept of assimilation is foundational to carrying it out, and the potential benefit of a maturity model in per forming the function. This is followed in Section 3 by the specific research objectives for constructing the IAM and the methodology used in our study. Section 4 progressively builds out various components of our IAM model using the methodology described in the preceding sec tion, and in Section 5, we close the loop by returning to IT governance and showing how the conceptual model of IAM can be applied to support that function. We conclude in Section 6 with a discussion of limitations of the study and directions for refinement of the model.

## 2. Theoretical background

Recall our earlier observation that it is the interaction among PPT that generates business value from IT investments. It follows from the interaction that IT assimilation itself is multifaceted and we now pro ceed to review selected findings from the literature to support that deduction, which implies that our IAM model itself needs to be multi dimensional. Also, since a major practical application of the conceptual IAM model is as a tool in carrying out IT governance and audit, we re view relevant details of IT governance to convey a sense of how the model could support that function.

## 2.1. IT assimilation

The IS literature has had a continuing interest in IT assimilation and its central role in deriving business value from IT investments [1]. Conceptualizations of IT assimilation have ranged from simple two-dimensional views comprising of breadth and depth of technology use [29] to more sophisticated views that include depth, breadth, vol ume, and diversity [48, 60]. They have recognized the need to incor porate the extent of business processes supported by IT use [58, 60] in assessing IT assimilation. In addition to business process, IT assimilation conceptualizations have examined IT infrastructure [1, 58] and the ca pabilities of IT staff in providing technical solutions to support business needs [31] as factors influencing IT assimilation.

Staged models of IT assimilation [12] have also been developed, conceptualizing assimilation as a continuously evolving phenomenon rather than a ‘state.’ An evolving process of assimilation involves sup port, strong leadership, training as well as organizational learning and reflection [2]. However, conceptualizing IT assimilation as a staged model also suggests the existence of intermediate states and levels of assimilation during its evolution. Although IT assimilation has been studied extensively and specific dimensions, such as IT process (e.g., [22]) and enterprise architecture (e.g., [71]), have been examined in isolation, a conceptual model that characterizes IT assimilation holisti cally in its different dimensions is yet to be developed. As noted earlier, a holistic view is needed to capture the interaction among PPT in the assimilation process. Moreover, studies have acknowledged that some organizations choose to satisfice in their IT assimilation efforts, often reaching an assimilation plateau [2]. However, besides the lack of a holistic view, a model that captures different levels of IT assimilation maturity is yet unavailable. Awareness of different levels of maturity would help organizations recognize deficiencies in their IT assimilation efforts and develop plans to address it. In addition to contributing to our conceptual understanding of the assimilation phenomenon, an IAM model can serve as a useful tool for IT governance and IT auditing as discussed in the next section. Table 1 presents a representative list of papers that have studied specific aspects and dimensions of IT assimi lation along with those which have used stage models to analyze the change in one or more of these dimensions.

## 2.2. IT governance and audit

IT governance “provides firms with effective mechanisms, such as the allocation of IT decision rights and management of IT risks, to achieve firms’ business goals” [44]. IT audit, on the other hand, is pri marily concerned with the process of collecting and evaluating evidence to determine, assess, evaluate, and review internal controls in an orga nization’s IT environment that safeguard IT assets, help maintain data integrity, ensure IT and related resources are consumed efficiently, and organizational goals are achieved effectively. The IT governance liter ature has argued that there is a value from an IT audit on several di mensions, including strategic, operational, financial, and IS/IT quality management, making it more of an opportunity than a burdensome necessity [55]. In order to visualize the opportunities for such value-addition, IT auditors are increasingly facing the challenge of keeping pace with the rapidly changing technology landscape, espe cially in being able to assess risks in a more technology-agnostic manner [63].

The controls that an IT audit exercise assesses could be strategic, such as plans and policies, technical controls, including applications or technical procedures, and physical controls, including those related to IT-related infrastructure [30]. Typically, IT audit approaches have been risk-based where potential risk and control mechanisms are identified [55]. However, the scope of IT audit has expanded over time to include proactive IT management and governance responsibilities since senior management expect IT auditors to possess the ability to assess the fit of IT solutions to organizational process, analyze the effectiveness of IT resource management, and monitor IT process deliverables [63]. Thus, a framework to help IT auditors assess this fit and the extent to which the firm has assimilated IT into its functioning would be beneficial in car rying out their responsibilities. We see our IAM model providing a conceptual tool for assessment and planning associated with IT gover nance and audit. Of course, some degree of domain-specific refinement or customization may be appropriate to apply the model in practical settings.

## 3.1. Exploratory case studies

Theoretical frameworks are important to the study of IT in organi zations as they enable us, among other things, to synthesize and inte grate empirical findings [79]. In Gregor’s [35] classification, our IAM model is a Type I theory, where the theory aims to provide an analysis and description. Given the exploratory nature of Type I theories, we use a positivist case study approach [24] to develop the theory and demonstrate its applicability. The case study approach has been deemed appropriate in this stage, as basic constructs of the phenomenon, and relationships among them “are classificatory, compositional, or asso ciative, not explicitly causal” [36]. It is also necessitated by the embeddedness of the assimilation phenomenon within its real-life context.

We analyzed multiple case study sites as they would yield more compelling evidence and help develop a more robust theory [24]. Since we seek to better understand the phenomena of interest within contex tual settings, and the emphasis is on meaning rather than measurement, this approach is consistent with prior studies which examine IT-related phenomena in organizations [15]. We combine our analysis of the literature and the content of in-depth interviews with multiple senior executives from the study sites to identify a parsimonious set of dimensions that characterize IT assimilation, and a set of concepts to characterize increasing levels of magnitude along each dimension, which form an ordinal scale. The dimensions and scales together define our IAM model.

Our approach can, therefore, be classified as the use of grounded theory method (GTM) to develop a ‘model’ [85] since definitions of the relevant variables (dimensions) and the relationships between them (combining dimensions to generate feasible IT assimilation profiles) are specified. Typically, when using GTM, researchers free themselves of preconceived theoretical concepts and relationships prior to collecting empirical data. Authors have also suggested the contrary that experi enced researchers exhibit theoretical sensitivity, while using GTM, in a way that allows them to question whether a particular variable or relationship would hold under varying conditions [81]. Such models developed through a GTM can subsequently be reframed as hypotheses and validated in specific contexts.

## 3.2. Case sites

Three Indian organizations were selected as our case study sites, from the manufacturing, educational, and banking sectors (MNFG, EDUC, BANK, all pseudonyms). MNFG is an automobile manufacturing firm with a turnover of over US \$ 1.8 billion and has been a dominant player in the light and heavy commercial vehicles space in India. EDUC is a well-known postgraduate public institution and has been in exis tence since about 1960. It is supported by the central government and is known for its strong and innovative academic programs. The institution is small by design with a student enrollment of about 1200. BANK is an Indian private bank that was acquired by a multi-national financial services firm in the early 2000s. The new entity grew rapidly and by 2014 became a 600-branch strong bank with over 10,000 employees, assets of around US\$ 10 billion, and around 2 million customers. In dustry diversity and age were the two main reasons driving our choice of organizations, and an initial analysis of their information infrastructure through secondary data sources revealed appreciable variability in the deployment and use of IT throughout the organization. Industry di versity captures differences in information intensity inherent in their respective product-market domains, resulting in wider applicability of the derived IAM model, and choosing organizations that have been in existence for a reasonable period of time aided in understanding the different aspects of assimilation and its evolution. Detailed descriptions of the three organizations are provided in Appendix 1.

## 3.3. Data collection and analysis

We contacted the head of IT in each of the organizations and chose respondents through a snowballing process, matching their background and experience with the objectives for this study. We collected quali tative data primarily through multiple semi-structured interviews with nine senior executives (Table 2) for a total duration of 36 h. The profiles of the interviewees are presented in Appendix 2. Collectively, their re sponsibilities and experience can be seen to span PPT, and they had each been with their organizations for several years (details as shown in Appendix 2). Hence, they were uniquely qualified to respond to queries about technology assimilation, and its evolution, along with IT audit and governance practices within each organization.

The interview narratives and secondary textual data were coded using NVivo [65]. Unlike the pure grounded theory approach which suggests using open coding without a priori specification of codes and categories [80], we used a hybrid of template coding and axial coding procedures [7] (Table 4). While template coding allowed us to use findings from the literature to create a pre-defined set of codes, axial coding allowed us to relate codes, categories and constructs to each other, combining inductive and deductive approaches to analysis and abstraction [75]. An initial template was created based on extant liter ature, but codes in the template were not restricted. Codes were added as new insights and were found from the interviews and other empirical data. Such a hybrid procedure was necessary given the exploratory and descriptive nature of this study where the literature review, data collection, and analysis were conducted in an overlapping manner.

We also carried out a process of shifting of codes from one category or hierarchical level to another when ambiguities arose. For instance, one CIO referred to “IT people” as a type of user, while another CIO categorically argued that “IT people” are organizational resources in the context of technology and should be treated akin to infrastructure, ap plications, and data. The authors resolved these ambiguities through mutual discussion to achieve consensus in the coding hierarchy. To maintain consistency, each organization’s transcripts were coded by one of the authors, but the definitions of the codes were agreed upon a priori. Through multiple iterations of coding, we were able to cluster the cat egories in the template based on common themes that emerged. Since only one author coded the entire text, we followed Schreier’s (2012) suggestion and the author repeated the coding process for the data after a reasonable time period. The coder achieved 73% agreement in the coded text – calculated as a simple percentage of agreement. This in dicates that our coding frame has been applied to the text in a consistent manner, and the interpretation is reasonably systematic, thus, achieving stability and, therefore, reliability of the coding frame [76]. The tem plate used in the second round of coding included the complete set of codes used in the first round given that the descriptions of the codes were agreed upon after many rounds of detailed discussions among the three authors during the earlier coding and theory building process. Descriptions for all codes, like those presented in Table 5, were arrived at through discussion among the authors. Wherever possible, clarifications from the interviewees were sought to fill the gaps in data, if any, and refine the description of important themes.

## 3.4. Morphological analysis

In the next stage of this study, we draw on Fritz Zwicky’s [21] GMA approach [86] following the process recommended by Ritchey [67] and Johansen [43]. A typical GMA begins with the identification of the key dimensions of the problem complex and is followed by assigning rele vant alternative values for each dimension. Together, these form the morphological field of the problem setting [43]. Using the alternative configurations generated in the field, each ’field configuration’ (mor photype) is generated by selecting one value for each of the dimensions. Theoretically, this yields numerous possibilities, especially if each dimension can take many values. In reality though, not all combinations may be internally compatible or empirically feasible [43]. The next step, therefore, is to reduce the number of morphotypes through a process of cross-consistency assessment, which eliminates options that have mutually contradictory values across different dimensions [43, 51]. The final set of configurations so obtained can now be considered possible configurations that a given situation may take. GMA has been applied in a variety of contexts, such as defense planning [43]; generating IT product ideas [40]; analyzing business models of platform-as-a-service providers [33]; co-creation design and innovation [28]; strategic archetypes for digital transformation [27], and has also been presented as a viable scenario development and analysis tool for practitioners [56].

The IAM model was constructed based on these findings and we were able to identify and describe the dominant frames of IAM model based on these themes and their manifestations. The history of the evolution of IT within the case study sites, as described by the respondents, helps identify ordinal levels within the context of each frame. To maintain consistency with existing literature, we adopt similar terminology to refer to the different stages in each dimension.

## 4. Development of IAM model

In this section, we apply the methodology described in the previous section to progressively develop different components of the IAM model. Using the constructs obtained through qualitative data analysis in the preceding section and findings from the literature, the dimensions of assimilation are identified, followed by ordinal levels for each dimen sion. Finally, morphological analysis is applied to identify a manageable number of assimilation configurations. Taken together, the dimensions of assimilation, ordinal levels, and space of feasible assimilation con figurations constitute the IAM model.

## 4.1. Dimensions of IT assimilation

Coding and categorizing the qualitative data from the three case sites, imbricated with findings from extant literature suggests four di mensions of the assimilation framework, and we refer to them as busi ness process, information architecture, IT process, and end-users technology capability. The first dimension, business process, refers to the extent of technology support across organizational business processes. The second dimension, information architecture, includes aspects related to technology resources and their organization. The third dimension, IT process, covers activities related to managing IT in an organizational environment. The fourth dimension, end-users, covers the technologyrelated capability of individuals in the organization manifested in the nature and extent of their IT use. We now describe the four dimensions in detail, substantiated through related extant literature, and our anal ysis of the three case studies is mentioned above.

## 4.1.2. IT architecture

The term IT architecture has often been used to refer to a plan for IT infrastructure [71] while also being seen as a tool for aligning IT and business strategy. Ross, Weill, and Robertson [73] broadened the term to refer to an organizing logic for IT infrastructure, business applications, and data as captured in the firm’s technical choices. Some authors have also defined IT infrastructure more expansively to include IT organiza tion, processes, and integration strategies – aspects that are also some times abstracted as information architecture. Conceptualizations of IT infrastructure and resources have thus been disparate in both definition and the different elements that are included [31]. We draw on these conceptualizations to describe a firm’s information architecture as its IT-related resources, including infrastructure, applications, data, and IT staff. Thus, in profiling assimilation, we use these four components as elements of the information architecture dimension.

IT infrastructure typically includes computing and communications infrastructure covering both hardware and systems software. In our in terviews, one CIO classified IT infrastructure on the basis of purpose, such as data center-related infrastructure, including servers, storage, etc., and end-user computing infrastructure, such as PCs, laptops, printers, plotters, video conferencing equipment, etc. Another compo nent includes support infrastructure such as power supplies, tempera ture control, fire suppression and surveillance systems, and physical mechanisms required to implement the computing environment.

Applications refer to the portfolio of software used by the organiza tion to support business processes and individual activities. Enterprise applications include digitized operational backbone applications [72] and common firm-wide software such as email, workflow systems, and information security applications as well as business function-specific applications such as computer-aided manufacturing, process/dis tributed control, and automation systems for the operations function; product life cycle management tools for R&D; sentiment analysis tools for marketing and the like. The second type – applications supporting individual activities – is end-user applications such as office productivity tools and applications developed by end-users to cater to their individual task needs.

Data are one of the critical assets of an organization, equally signif icant, if not more, as financial, human, IP, and physical assets [84]. Typically, data in an organization can be structured data generated from business processes and unstructured data related to knowledge, opin ions, comments, communications, etc., or those related to social media interactions with different stakeholders. Similar to the digitized business process operational backbone and the digitized services backbone, Dallemule & Davenport [18] argue that data strategies can be offensive and defensive as well, where the business objectives fulfilled by the use of such data in the former case can help increase profitability, revenue, and customer engagement or, in the latter case, minimize downside risk. While either strategy may generate significant value from data for the firm, measuring the data and information footprint [74], or may by itself aid firms in thinking of the information they generate and provide managers a means of envisioning and assessing value from such infor mation assets, such as through the monetization of data [18].

We use the term IT staff to include developers, service providers, functional support personnel, end-user support personnel, etc., who create, maintain, and protect the technology ecosystem in the organi zation so as to allow end-users to perform their activities [31]. Tradi tionally, the IT workforce has been more technically proficient compared to end-users. Benbasat et al. [6] identified certain generic skill sets that IT staff should possess, including that of interacting with non-technical people, appreciating users’ concerns regarding data pri vacy, and awareness of the potential impact of technology on people’s roles and responsibilities. Given the steady increase in end-users technical prowess over time, the role of IT staff in providing support as well as ensuring appropriate controls has become more demanding.

## 4.1.3. IT processes

Feeny and Willcocks [26] identified three core IT capabilities that help organizations derive value from IT. These include creating and sustaining a vision for the business and IT, designing appropriate ar chitecture, and ensuring low-cost but high-quality delivery of IT ser vices. Such capabilities are also reflected in the sophistication of IT management. IT management in organizations relates to planning, building, maintaining, monitoring, and governing IT resources [8]. Practitioner frameworks are available to help guide CIOs in governing IT effectively. For example, ISACA’s Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology (COBIT) framework delineates five broad pro cesses such as Align, Plan and Organize (APO); Build, Acquire and Implement (BAI); Deliver, Service and Support (DSS); Monitor, Evaluate and Assess (MEA) in the context of management of enterprise IT; and Evaluate, Direct and Monitor (EDM) in the context of governance of enterprise IT [41]. APO includes planning and managing the IT strategy, the portfolio of IT applications, the manpower required, relationships with vendors, the service agreements, mitigating risks, and the tech nology security in the organization. BAI refers to identifying re quirements, managing and building the required solutions, managing the change resulting from technology, including acceptance and tran sitioning, configuration management, and IT asset management. DSS focuses on managing everyday operations, handling service requests and incidents, resolving problems, ensuring continuity of services, and managing process controls. MEA refers to assessment and monitoring of performance, audit, internal controls, and compliance with external requirements. EDM ensures the benefits of delivery, resource optimi zation, risk optimization, and stakeholder transparency. The COBIT 2019 processes can be broken down into finer-level processes which can then be directly executed. Although all processes are critical, some of the IT processes have been found to demand more attention from IT man agers given their importance for effective control and achieving reliable financial reporting [45].

## 4.1.4. End-Users

End-users are non-technical domain-centric individuals who perform business activities using the IT infrastructure [23]. End-user computing, a term popularized in the 70 s, reflects users’ ability to retrieve data using command level interfaces, partially program using user-friendly languages and tools, and develops applications that can then be used by other end-users [68]. Cotterman and Kumar [13] further refined the classification of end-users to include those who operate, develop, or control IS in the organization while also utilizing their output. In an organizational environment, CIOs are likely to encounter a wide variety of end-users and would therefore need to cater to their varying expec tations and diverse ways and means of using IT. Such diversity may be further accentuated in a digitally enabled business process environment where internal and external end-users may vary in their needs, capa bilities, and expectations. Acknowledging the unexpected problems, they may face and respond to such issues demands that their heteroge neity be acknowledged [60]. While, on the one hand, the diversity in end-users has the potential to increase productivity and give rise to so phisticated, innovative, and productive user-developed applications, it may also result in a shadow IT environment which creates significant risks and control problems for IT managers and IT auditors alike [63, 70].

A typology of end-user activities includes information creation (e.g., use of knowledge management system by an expert or even a simple activity of writing an email); information capture and storing (e.g., use of transaction processing systems); information search (e.g., seeking financial data on a company website); information retrieval (e.g., locating old records); information management (e.g., managing files and folders); information sharing (e.g., sending file attachments to a colleague); information analysis $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ conducting a what-if analysis in a spreadsheet package); and presenting information for others’ con sumption $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ creating a presentation or report for senior manage ment). An end-user may also initiate or generate transactions such as a customer who purchases a product from the firm’s website, a sales agent who closes a sale and records it in the billing system, a payroll clerk who processes the monthly payroll, or a manager who approves a leave request. Understanding the many facets of end-users allows the IT team to formulate policies regarding end-user support and control [34].

## 4.2. Ordinal scales for dimensions

The four dimensions identified above provide a framework that captures the multifaceted nature of IT assimilation. To apply the framework and to assess the IT assimilation profile of an organization, it is necessary to identify values that each of the different dimensions can take. Following the GMA approach, we identify different ordinal levels for each of the four dimensions, reflecting increases in the conceptual magnitude of that dimension, even if there are no widely agreed-upon units to measure the magnitude. Ordinal levels (sometimes termed ‘stages’ or ‘groups’) in maturity models are not uncommon in IS research and have previously been used in the context of benefits from cloud computing [61], business process management [69], and IT architecture [71]. It is also common to use such descriptive ‘levels’ in GMA as has been highlighted by Ritchey [67] and Johansen [43].

To keep the IAM model complexity manageable, we simplify the scale to four ordinal levels for each dimension. We draw from extant literature, where available and our analysis of the exploratory cases, i.e., intertwining thematic and axial coding to derive the ordinal levels. Fo example, for the dimension “information architecture,” we draw from Ross’ (2003) well-established stage model of IT architecture compe tency. However, for business process digitization, we did not directly find ordinal levels in the literature. Although there are many models that refer to business process maturity, none particularly with respect to digitization. We relied primarily on our analysis of the exploratory cases, where the gradual increase of digital footprints across core and support processes was evident. The nomenclature used to indicate the business process digitization maturity levels drew from the cases. Finally, since the dimensions are now being refined to include an ordinal scale for each, we add the term ‘maturity’ to the labels of each of the dimensions. We intersperse our discussion below, with qualitative data from the case studies to illustrate the variation in the ordinal ‘level’ within the different dimensions.

## 4.2.1. Business process digitization maturity levels

Successful design and implementation of business processes, espe cially core business processes, are imperative for an organization’s success, and IT is a key enabler. However, the extent of IT assimilation reflected in business process digitization varies across organizations. We identify four levels of IT assimilation with respect to digitizing business processes. First, firms use technology to monitor the performance of business processes. For example, in EDUC, the academic programs administration office used the academic administration software to monitor attendance records of students, the extent of completion of the grading process, etc. The course administration process, from course registration to posting of grades, is among the core processes at EDUC. Successful execution of this process is key to the performance of the organization. The process is, therefore, monitored closely, and the program administration offices are expected to be aware of the status of each course through such monitoring. Respondent CSC at EDUC stated as:

Course administration is key to our program success. The timely completion of activities in that process is essential to ensure smooth functioning. Any delays get accumulated and we may face a crisis at the end of the academic year. So, we monitor that process closely and information related to course administration is tracked on an every-day basis.

## 4.2.2. IT architecture maturity levels

Based on Ross’ (2003) stage model of IT architecture competency, four levels of maturity are proposed for this dimension, consisting of silo, standardized, rationalized, and modular. The first level, silo, con sists of disparate infrastructure, departmental, or function-oriented in dividual applications, fragmented data, and a technology support workforce who are more technical and lack significant business knowledge. The second level, standardized, is characterized by enterprise-wide applications, achieving a certain degree of standardi zation through centralization in planning, procurement, implementa tion, and governance. In EDUC, for instance, while procurement is partly centralized, there are frequent exceptions to standardization, leaving several applications unable to communicate with each other, and resulting in data pockets with a high degree of inconsistency. Respon dent SSA of EDUC expressed the need for more standardization and commented as:

This is an academic institution. While we want to give people the freedom to go with their preferences, especially at the program or department level, we do want to avoid compatibility issues across the institution. We therefore try to enforce a certain amount of standardization, but we are far from achieving that. And it often becomes a balancing act.

MNFG, on the other hand, migrated to their home-grown enterprisewide application, forcing parts of the organization on siloed applications to shift to the new platform. Then, CIO and his team had initially used cost savings and integration benefits to justify the move in internal discussions, but they were also supported by the top management through a directive to reduce costs through standardization.

The next level, rationalized, extends the standardized level to include standardization of data and processes along with applications. At this level, IT staff have a good understanding of the business imperatives. A significant role that IT staff play at the rationalized level, in addition to keeping the infrastructure appropriately updated, is to design IT-related processes that ensure standardization and rationalization. This is possible only when IT staff are aware of business imperatives for tech nology support. In BANK, business imperatives drive information infrastructure decisions. For example, the CIO of BANK commented as:

I have, let’s say, 500 branches. I can do an ABC analysis of a branch. If I can say that these are my most important branches who must have the best equipment possible, the best bandwidth possible. The SLA, say the up-time should be 99.9%, so on and so forth. On the other hand, I have a very unprofitable branch, … I actually do this kind of an analysis and I say, it is ok if they have a seven-year PC, it is ok if my up-time is 90% and not 99% because the business does not justify the up-time requirements … And therefore, if…those particular people crib saying that the PCs are down all the time, I will discount that. We are ruthlessly business-driven in that respect.

When MNFG transitioned to SAP, the IT team mandated process standardization. This resulted in increased predictability of processes, with system output being driven by data quality [71]. As MNFG pro gresses, rationalization is likely to incentivize greater ownership of data. The MNFG CIO commented as:

It calls for discipline and then ownership. The moment you have owner ship established for data within the organization…. They will take re sponsibility for the data. I’m telling you, quality of data and therefore the maturity of data will go up and that impacts overall IT maturity, …

The fourth level, modular, occurs when an organization’s applica tions, data, and infrastructure are loosely coupled, balancing standard ization but supporting local differences. While none of the three firms studied here were at the modular level, there are indications of plans in that direction. For instance, MNFG is attempting to modularize some of its applications in its joint ventures. EDUC, while currently in silo mode, is envisioning a modular architecture for its academic program admin istration application, which will be replicated across its multiple pro grams, allowing for local customization but maintaining standards in data. The head of IT at EDUC has identified that a significant barrier to moving to a higher level is a lack of qualified IT staff.

## 4.2.3. IT process maturity levels

Several models of IT process maturity exist in the IS literature [69], focusing, for example, on strategic alignment [49]; business process maturity [32]; software development [11]; and process capability [41]. There are similarities across these models in the definition of different maturity levels but the parameters and attributes which determine the levels differ. We chose to use four levels of IT process maturity drawing from the business process maturity model and COBIT’s maturity levels of IT processes. To maintain consistency with the other two dimensions of our model, we restrict the number of levels to four and name them as ad hoc, repeatable, managed, and optimized.

Ad hoc process maturity is characterized by an absence of definition or documentation for any of the IT processes. Organizations in this stage rely heavily on individual competency to perform IT processes. In EDUC, for example, there was a heavy reliance on the chairperson of the IT committee and the systems administrator to plan, procure, and deploy infrastructure and applications. The decision-making structure evolved over the years into an IT committee, although many processes continued to be ad hoc. The committee, though, relied heavily on precedence and thus had a semblance of level 2 – repeatable process maturity. Respondent CSC of EDUC’s comment substantiates this as follows:

We have many IT resource procurement processes which we have been following for many years now. These processes have worked us for us in the past, so we merely repeat the same taking them as having precedent. Since we are subject to professional audits, we find it easier to state that there is precedence for these procurement processes. We rarely revisit them except if there is an entirely new requirement from users.

Repeatability ensures consistency and is achieved only when there is sufficient monitoring and control of the process. Repeatability can be effected either through structure, as in EDUC, or through documentation as in the case of organizations following ISO or TQM standards. The third level of maturity of IT processes, managed, refers to standardiza tion across IT processes in the organization. The objective is to make use of the standardized processes to achieve predictable results within an acceptable range. A significant proportion of effort in a ‘managed’ or ganization is devoted to the centralization of many of the IT processes, including planning and procurement. The implementation may still be effected at the functional or department level except for shared services and enterprise applications. MNFG considered itself to be a managed organization with respect to IT processes. The previous CIO had initiated efforts to centralize IT processes which the current CIO continues to implement. Some task-specific applications can still be implemented by the business functions, but there are processes in place to ensure compatibility with the enterprise application platform. MNFG-CIO observed as:

Business folks don’t have sufficient time to invest in clearly describing their needs. And then we build or procure based on whatever they have said and all those disconnects which happens and then our own problems of not being able to integrate things properly leading to multiple chal lenges. Can you sort of get everything right in the first place itself? That calls for… maturity. That’s why you differentiate between an organiza tion which is very good in its planning and an organization which is very good in its execution. We are dominantly an execution organization. Once we learn to plan better and get the full cycle right, we may move to becoming more optimized.

The optimized level occurs in organizations that strive to keep technology in pace with business needs. This requires a balance between standardization, customization, and innovation. Continuing planning cycles, technology scan, fit, and forecasting activities are crucial in an optimized environment. Together, the four levels present a continuum along which organizations typically move in the evolution of IT processes.

## 4.2.4. End-Users maturity levels

An organization’s IT use maturity is manifested in the pattern of IT use by individuals [9]. Two dimensions of IT use, scale and sophisti cation, have been identified in the literature [83]. Scale refers to the magnitude of use, including frequency, regularity, and duration of use. Sophistication refers to the refinement or exhibition of higher level of knowledge in patterns of use. End-user maturity can have a conflicting impact in that self-reliant end-users and may contribute to organiza tional application portfolio by developing their own applications, but this may also lead to users making their own IT decisions without the involvement of IT departments leading to IT governance challenges [70].

A typology of users reflecting the combination of scale and sophis tication has been developed – amateurs, super novices, passive experts, and active experts [25]. Amateurs refer to end-users who use IT infre quently with minimal features to perform basic tasks. In the context of organizational systems, such as an enterprise resource planning appli cation, for example, amateurs may, a few times a week, seek data to support their managerial decision-making. Super novices, on the other hand, may do so more frequently, but may merely seek information from the system. Passive experts use advanced application features such as those which require more knowledge and competency, but they do so infrequently. A product-level marketing manager may be interested in knowing the quarterly sales of her product and may attempt to do a year-over-year comparison or may create visualizations of the geographic sales of her product vis-\`a-vis others in the same category.

Active experts rely heavily on the technology, use it to its fullest, and do so quite frequently. A production manager may need to use the ERP to perform his daily material planning or to schedule plant operations, allocate tasks to workers, or to identify problems in the assembly line flow. The MNFG-CIO’s way of articulating this was interesting and commented as:

the moment a person joins today, we have a well-lubricated system where automatically the moment the master record is created in the ERP system, there’s a mail which goes to help-desk and help-desk also creates the email ID. Done! So, the moment you’re given an ID that means you’ve start…you’re given provision for using the IT facilities. There is no concept today of saying who will use the IT facility. Everyone uses IT facility

## 4.3.1. Cross-consistency assessment

The matrix in Table 6 represents the entire morphological field of the IAM model, which yields a total of $4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 2 5 6$ possible assimilation profiles. The process of cross-consistency assessment [43, 67] required a systematic examination of each pair in the matrix [51].

Since only one level may be chosen from a dimension in creating a possible assimilation profile, pair-wise comparison results in an exami nation of 96 pairs $[ 4 \times 1 2 + 4 \times 8 + 4 \times 4 ]$ of levels. A set of rule identifying internally inconsistent pairs was created. Two examples are shown below.

Example 1. If IT processes are “Ad-hoc”, Information Architecture cannot be “Modular” [modularity in information architecture requires intensive planning, monitoring and assessment processes]

Example 2. If a vast majority of End-Users are “Amateurs” Business Process Digitization Maturity cannot be “Automate” [For automated business processes, end-users have to help structure these processes and it envisages high degree of technology support]

## 5.1. Transitioning to mature IAM profiles

IS maturity models are often used to assess as-is status, provide di rections for improvement initiatives, and help monitor and control the progress of such improvements [69]. Our prescriptions to organization planning to evolve their assimilation profile on one or more of the di mensions are as follows:

• Assess if a transition to a higher maturity level on the dimension is beneficial in comparison to the costs of doing so.

• Analyze if the transition aligns with the organization’s busines objectives.

• A firm may choose to first address the dimensions where they are at lower levels of maturity. However, evolving on all the dimensions simultaneously may also help since the synergies across dimensions can then be exploited.

• Should organizational complementarities, such as an IT-enlightened leadership, end-users’ readiness and willingness, enterprise risk appetite, etc., allow a firm that may also use an incremental and evolutionary approach and aims at continuous improvement toward a more mature IT assimilation profile.

Purposive proactive policies can be crafted to facilitate transition to more mature IT assimilation. ## 6. Conclusion

Recognizing that the interaction among PPT is what generates value from IT investments, the conceptual model of IT assimilation maturity that we have developed in this paper is multidimensional and takes a holistic view of assimilation. By representing an organization's assimi lation profile in a parsimonious manner and mapping out a set of empirically feasible profiles of different maturity configurations, our model contributes to the field’s conceptual understanding of the assimilation phenomenon. From the early studies on IT assimilation to the more recent ones, the notion of assimilation has often been left ambiguous and specific to the context in which the research is con ducted. Armstrong and Sambamurthy [1], in their investigation of the role of senior leadership and IT infrastructures on IT assimilation in an organization, measured assimilation by asking survey respondents about their perceptions of the extent of IT use in different aspects of the firm’s value chain. This operationalization of assimilation focuses primarily on its technological aspects while using value chain activities as the frame. A later study on the assimilation of collaborative information technol ogies [3] reveals a similar approach to operationalizing assimilation (extent of support to organizational tasks), although they define IT assimilation as being synonymous with IT diffusion. The current study directs the IS discipline’s attention to the multifaceted nature of assimilation. The IAM model built in the current study disaggregates the assimilation construct into a parsimonious set of dimensions that cover not only the technological aspects of assimilation but also the organi zational process and people dimensions. Further, it details the scales for each dimension. Collectively, the dimensions provide a conceptual lens to view IT assimilation in its full complexity, and the scales offer a way to exercise discipline on assessing the level of advancement on each dimension. Periodic assessments using the IAM, as highlighted, will allow a comparison of the as-is' with the to-be' IT assimilation profiles on the dimensions. A key contribution of this study has also been the identification of broad support and control mechanisms to facilitate the transition to higher levels of maturity (‘to-be’ state) on the individual dimensions. The different stage models in the extant literature have focused either on specific dimensions, such as IT architecture [71] or overall business value from IT [82]. Mechanisms suggested in this paper are, on the other hand, cognizant of the need to stitch together control and support mechanisms across different dimensions to facilitate the transition to a more mature state. The IAM along with the transition mechanisms have important implications for research in IT auditing, particularly in creating opportunities for value-addition through the auditing process, such as in analyzing the alternative transition path taken by firms and monitoring IT resource management and IT process deliverables [63].

Additionally, the IAM has the potential to contribute to practice, and the reactions of the CEOs at the three case study sites to our findings provide preliminary confirmation. As a recent CIO survey referenced earlier in the paper showed, managing IT assimilation continues to be a challenge, and the IT governance function has taken on increasing importance within organizations as a result. Three major outcomes of IT governance are benefits realization, risk optimization, and resource optimization [41]. Maturity models serve descriptive, prescriptive, and comparative purposes [69], all of which can support the IT governance function. When applied and used for as-is assessments, they serve a descriptive purpose to help understand the current state of the IT assimilation in the organization. Such an assessment of the as-is can also be used by an organization to compare and benchmark itself against other organizations in the same industry which may aid competitive benchmarking or other firms in ecosystem to exploit possible knowledge-sharing opportunities. The prescriptive purpose is served when the firm creates a future to-be planned state for IT assimilation maturity. In this case, the IAM model provides alternatives for target assimilation profiles and possible transition paths to reach profiles. The grounded theory approach through which the IAM model was devel oped, and the use of GMA, gives confidence that the maturity profiles generated are internally consistent and empirically feasible. Moreover, the parsimony of the IAM model does not preclude the possibility of drilling down to assess a firm’s IT assimilation at finer levels of granularity.

There are several directions in which this conceptual model can be improved through follow-up studies. One shortcoming of our conceptual model is the absence of measurable numerical scales for the different dimensions. We recognize the benefits of quantitative measures for the assessment of organizational IT assimilation. However, quantitative measures may force a reductionist approach and limit the ability to capture the innate complexity of the IT assimilation phenomenon. We surmise that an ordinal scale is an acceptable compromise between a purely nominal scale that captures contextual complexity but is unable to capture magnitude and a purely numerical scale that reflects magnitude but is unable to capture contextual complexity. That said, the ordinal scales presented in this paper can be refined further by analyzing additional technology use cases from organizations in a broader range of industries. For example, the ‘super novice’ level in end-users maturity level can be defined differently and more precisely for different industry sectors through analysis of use cases from that sector. By refinement, we mean further elaboration on contextual details that would determine a particular ordinal value. Such refinement will make it easier to apply the model in specific settings. Apart from refining the ordinal scales, another direction in which the model can be refined is in the area of maintaining currency of the set of feasible assimilation profiles. With the rapid pace of technological evolution and increasing sophistication of end-users, it will be necessary to periodically revisit the current set of feasible assimilation profiles. Also, it would be useful to match subsets of feasible assimilation profiles with specific industry sectors. This could be done through additional case studies from different industries and would provide industry-specific guidance in carrying out the IT governance function. Finally, through longitudinal case studies, one can provide more specific action plans for the evolution of an organization’s assimilation profile. This would be akin to developing more specific versions of Table 10 for different industry sectors. Such follow-up in vestigations would be consistent with guidelines offered by Becker et al. [5] who recommend strengthening the model through the use of multi-method procedures.

## Appendix 1. Organizational profile of exploratory cases

Case study 1: MNFG

MNFG is an automobile manufacturing firm with a turnover of over US \$ 1.8 billion and has been a dominant player in the light and heavy commercial vehicles space in India. It is part of a larger conglomerate but has been functioning with minimal interference from the larger group. The company experienced a slump in sales during the global economic crisis in 2008–2009, but also used this as an opportunity to revamp its IT infra structure given the lull in business activities. The company firmly believed that investing in IT during the difficult times was one way to leverage the benefits of IT when the economic cycle switched directions and moved upward

IT at MNFG started in 1970s with IBM mainframes. In the 1980s, COBOL-based materials planning applications and other home-grown solutions on DEC PDP infrastructure were used at the company’s prime factory location, while the other units were on IBM mainframes. IT applications were implemented as disparate systems. In 1989, MNFG migrated to the UNIX environment with Oracle 5, implementing applications to meet differen functional needs, such as the marketing function, upgrading as newer versions were available. In 2001, MNFG commenced implementation of a home grown ERP solution, successfully completing it in 2002. Applications catering to the needs of different functions, deployed at different points in time, were gradually integrated with the home-grown ERP. In 2008, an assessment of the home-grown solution revealed numerous shortcomings, and the company subsequently decided to implement SAP in all its units and offices, and this was completed in a phased manner across the company. The company has since then transitioned from a collection of disparate applications held together by middleware to a more integrated application platform.

The large internal IT team that existed prior to SAP implementation gradually changed its character to focus more on three activities – vendor management, customization of SAP, and building bridges with end-users. While the role of the functional support personnel was not new to MNFG, the intensity increased post-SAP implementation. The CIO has championed the use of various business applications. More recently, the company has extensively invested in garnering insights from the volumes of data generated by its portfolio of applications. Efforts are underway to create user friendly dashboards and business intelligence applications. Business leaders acknowledge that the ease of use and the speed in design, develop ment, implementation, and monitoring of such applications has helped the firm become more competitive.

A significant change was also seen among functional, business end-users in terms of system usage. Earlier, it would be an uphill task to convince users to capture transactions in real-time, but users now complain if the system is down for even a few minutes. This holds for end-to-end processes such as order to service, design to delivery, material planning to accounts payable, and so on. End-users such as field staff, who access customer information on-the-go, registering both vehicle orders and reviewing past orders when they meet the customers in the field, view the technology platform as the lifeline of the organization. Except for a few small pockets of manual processes, there is a substantial “digital footprint” across the processes in the organization. Users have also taken greater ownership of the data. The CIO visualizes this as “true democratization of data-driven decision-making.”

## Case study 2: EDUC

The technology journey at EDUC, an institute of higher education, began in the 70 s. The IT portfolio in the initial years was focused on administrative tasks, especially accounting and payroll. While the use of IT for academic research was encouraged, access was through third-party equipment residing in off-campus locations. In the mid-80 s, an IT research center was set up. Over time, this center transformed the computing culture of EDUC to span teaching, academic program management, and communication. EDUC’s hardware infrastructure included diverse platforms such as unit-record machines, mini-computers, distributed computing platforms, network file servers, PCs, and high-end rack servers. EDUC’s in vestment in PCs was initially cautious, rationing them to student labs and senior faculty. The widespread distribution of PCs took about 20 years. EDUC's initial IT staff was not formally qualified in IT management. having only had basic training to write computer programs. However, the team recruited for the IT center was qualified in computer science and IT, and center services ranged from research in emerging computing environments, software development to training for end-users. Over time, the profile of IT staff at EDUC has shifted toward a small set of IT managers with much of the technology infrastructure services being outsourced.

Networking started in the early 90 s with rudimentary services like file and print sharing and email. Internet access came in the mid-1990s with a 64 kbps radio link and email came to be used widely. Word processing and spreadsheet applications became more common. The late 90 s saw an expansion of the network to student residences and with that came Internet access for students in their dormitories in the year 2000. Considering the dependence of students on technology, some portion of the IT management process was delegated to them. This also led to a lot of innovation in IT applications from among the students. Students founded an Internet group and created a web-based extranet to facilitate interactions between stu dents, faculty, alumni, administration, and the external public. A few operational support applications exist, such as financial accounting software, a limited-capability academic administration software, a library system, and a course management system. Much of the functional processes are supported by email and spreadsheets rather than customized applications. There is poor integration among the existing applications. Data are stored in multiple places often leading to inconsistency.

IT planning was essentially centralized, where a Computer Services Committee constituted of administrative staff, faculty, and student repre sentatives, determined requirements, budgeting and procurement of equipment and software. Despite the 30-year evolution of IT and the presence of a committee, there has been no strategic IT plan. The absence of formal IT policy allows users to bypass the committee and implement applications in an ad hoc manner. Independent one-off budgets were also created for major expenditures like the financial accounting software. Occasionally, pro curement and implementation of both infrastructure and software were conducted with little consultation or approval process of the committee. IT related processes are largely driven by informal traditions rather than standard operating procedures.

The disparity between different types of end-users at EDUC is large. While students are extremely tech-savvy, a significant proportion of the faculty members use technology to a bare minimum. The administrative personnel lag even further behind in their IT use.

## Case study 3: BANK

BANK is an Indian private bank that was acquired by a multi-national financial services firm in the early 2000s. The new entity grew rapidly and by 2014 became a 600-branch strong bank with over 10,000 employees, assets of around US\$ 10 billion, and around 2 million customers. While the original bank invested in IT solutions, it did so at a slow pace till the late 90 s. When the new entity BANK was formed, the foreign partner emphasized IT support. In the early 2000s, BANK signed a \$20-million IT outsourcing contract with a large global plaver who began providing a whole range of IT services to the bank from hardware identification to procuring and implementation of the required technology. In addition, the vendor also managed the data center operations, systems and network management, apart from support at various locations. The bank also availed services such as process consulting, crafting an IT strategy, designing and managing operations, and back-office solutions from the vendor. BANK’s initiative in integrated IT applications began with the deployment of its core banking system in 2006. The focus of the bank’s IT efforts was targeted toward providing better, faster, and cheaper services for its retail and corporate banking customers in addition to managing market transactions in investments and derivatives.

By 2007, BANK formed the IT Service Management Group, a technology organization headed by the CIO, which managed BANK’s enterprise-wide technology needs. While most of the IT operations were managed using outsourced partners through service level agreements, an information security team and an internal IT audit department continued to be housed within BANK. This highlighted the emphasis that BANK placed on these two ac tivities. The CIO created a technology blueprint carefully aligned with their business strategy. This blueprint has been used to drive all technology decisions. From 2007–2009, BANK continued to invest in many applications, including centralized cash management, wealth management, mobile banking solutions, applications supporting the financial markets business, etc.

Despite the global recession, between 2009 and 2011, BANK invested significantly in enhancing its IT infrastructure, especially in applications that help customers transact remotely such as Internet banking and mobile banking. According to the CIO, the increase was 20–40 percent over their previous year’s spending. This also included their investment in enhancing security. By 2010, the bank began offering dedicated host-to-host inte gration to the ERP systems of their corporate and business banking customers. The present CIO has left no stone unturned in introducing virtualization and service-oriented architecture in BANK’s systems. Large-scale adoption of service-oriented architecture, he felt, has generated significant reuse, resulting in faster time to market. 2012 saw another round of IT innovation at BANK, including the Total Relationship Value (TRV) – the ability to capture in a consolidated manner, the customers’ relationship across products with the Bank, and therefore offer preferential features and pricing.

Given the high level of information intensity of banking as an industry, BANK has exploited IT support for a significant amount of its activities and processes. End-users in the bank are both comfortable and moderately conversant with IT. Among the plans for IT at BANK is refurbishing the data architecture which will help end-users to leverage data for decision-making, whether in real-time or for more contemplative analytical consumption.
