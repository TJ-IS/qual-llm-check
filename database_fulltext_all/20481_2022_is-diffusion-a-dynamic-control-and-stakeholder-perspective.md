## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Control balancing Stakeholder engagement Stakeholder sensitivity IS diffusion IS implementation ISD

## A B S T R A C T

Our research uses a novel perspective of control balancing and stakeholder orientation to explore an information system (IS) phenomenon called enterprise IS diffusion. Employing a case-based, grounded theory approach, we analyze four large-scale IS implementation projects in a Canadian government organization. Our findings offer a synergistic approach to IS diffusion by integrating a dynamic control configuration perspective with stakeholder engagement, stakeholder sensitivity, and the impact of shifting stakeholder orientations on IS diffusion stages.We draw from control, control balancing, and stakeholder theories. Moreover, we refer to the IS implementation and IS diffusion literature to develop our argument. In doing so, this research develops a new and vital con struct—stakeholder orientation—for IS implementation projects. We also identify the existence of four distinct stakeholder orientations, a combination of stakeholder engagement with stakeholder sensitivity in large ISD projects: (a) strategic, (b) responsibility, (c) best interest, and (d) economic cost.Through analysis and synthesis, we establish vital relationships among shifting control configurations, stakeholder engagement, stakeholder sensitivity, and IS diffusion stages, where maintaining an optimal stakeholder orientation, facilitated by appropriate control configuration, leads to a successful IS diffusion outcome. Furthermore, we propose an extension to the existing control balancing theory by identifying missing links and trigger factors.

## 1. Introduction

The deployment of packaged software as an enterprise information system (IS) has steadily increased over the past two decades, ranging from multi-million-dollar enterprise resource planning (ERP) imple mentation to “one-of-a-kind” software for individual users. This trend is apparent in both public and private sectors where the reduction of expenditure and the speed of deployment are two of the salient driving forces of IT procurement [18]. Despite being a part of an organization’s strategic IT initiatives that require a large investment of organizational resources, information systems development (ISD) projects continue to show a propensity to fail to achieve desired benefits, with under half being successful ([51]; Standish [90]). According to a recent report published by the Consortium for IT Software Quality (CISQ), the total cost of poor-quality software in the USA is \$2.84 trillion, with software failure the largest single category [62]. Some studies place the failure rate of IS development and implementation projects as high as 70% [28]. Although technical complexity, dynamic power structure, requirement volatility, and conflicting interests are often attributed to such failures, Dwivedi et al.’s [27] review of IS implementation failures and successes calls for a “multi-perspective” approach to the problem.

IS success and failure are a direct consequence of the effectiveness of collaboration, yet diverse stakeholder engagement resulting from effective collaboration still lacks sound understanding [27]. Further more, IS failure is often a direct consequence of strong user resistance inhibiting IS diffusion, something that can be examined through orga nizational controls. Yet an explicit investigation of the relationship be tween control and IS diffusion is largely missing in this domain [2]. Engaging diverse groups of stakeholders and effectively managing re lationships are critical activities that can be classified as “stakeholder orientation” and are within the control of a project team. Optimal stakeholder orientation can minimize conflicting interests [1, 66] and can facilitate overcoming various inertias that lead to IS resistance [96]. Thus, consideration, engagement, and management of stakeholders can pave the way for successful IS diffusion within an organization. Although the key tenet of stakeholder theory emphasizes the “consid eration” aspect, ensuring proper “engagement” solicits a different approach. Aside from being a complex task and embedded with numerous interdependencies, early stages of IS diffusion require elicit ing input from multiple stakeholders, often with non-overlapping knowledge [71]. Along with the benefits of complementary knowledge and skills offered by various stakeholders, engagement also entails the challenge of divergent priorities, goals, motivations, and power re lations, all of which require proper control and management to achieve a positive outcome [59].

Extant literature in IS has drawn on control and control balancing theories to establish the significance of control in managing ISD projects ([21, 56], 1997). The interplay between formal and informal controls [21], dynamics of control activities during different IS phases [43], and their enactment—i.e., how the controller interacts with the controllee (the target of control) to put the portfolio of controls into practice—have been examined in relation to ISD success and overcoming IS-resisting behavior. Although applications and effects of controls in an ISD context are quite mature, a sociocognitive perspective of control has received increased attention. Such a perspective probes into a previously overlooked phenomenon where the influence of control on controlee’s emotions leads to an IS accepting/resisting behavior [74], and control-related cognitive activities such as the “sense giving-sensemaking” cycle influence mutual understanding among stakeholders [53]. Hence, a sociocognitive perspective focusing on the facilitation of “social learning” [6], and maintenance of “shared conception” [53], is better suited to explain many of the existing IS diffusion challenges. Stakeholders’ conceptions of reality are different and evolving [24, 82, 95], and each stakeholder group’s perception of success is defined by the attainment of their own goals resulting from ISD activities [6]. Therefore, an understanding of the “shared concep tion” or “mutual understanding” development process through the dy namic application of controls is of utmost importance for successful IS diffusion.

Nonetheless, to date, no research has explicitly studied how dynamic control and stakeholder orientations can facilitate the successful diffu sion of complex IS. As such, the motivations and contributions of the current research are multifold. First, the examination of dynamic control configuration in conjunction with stakeholder orientation for large-scale IS implementation is nonexistent to date. We identified four major stakeholder orientations in public sector IS diffusion. This offers a richer understanding of Donaldson and Preston's (1995) multi-core view of stakeholder theory and provides guidance for IS researchers to move beyond the research on control dynamics. Second, capturing the dy namic nature of stakeholder orientation in a complex IS implementation project through a well-defined framework will facilitate understanding of the normative and instrumental application of stakeholder theory. This will also help us understand Jones and Wicks’ (1999) convergent stakeholder theory, according to which the instrumental usage of stakeholder theory does not require sacrificing the normative core. From this perspective, the current research can be considered a significant theory elaboration effort. Third, the current research analysed an en terprise IS implementation process through the control balancing theory and identified the necessity of control balancing for a multi-partner IS implementation. The validation of this relatively new theory using four distinct IS implementation cases significantly contributes towards the generalizability of this theory. Finally, Dwivedi et al. [27] called for a “multi-perspective” approach to study IS success and failure as a future research direction due to divergent views and collaborative needs among varied stakeholder groups. Our research answers this call by linking control configurations and stakeholder orientations to IS diffu sion stages. Although Abubakre et al. [2] examined the impact of formal control on three stages of an IS diffusion, the current study adopts an encompassing approach by examining both pre- and post-deployment stages of IS diffusion.

Therefore, we primarily seek to explore a complex relationship among dynamic control balancing, stakeholder orientation, and IS diffusion phases. The objectives of the current research can be summa rized by the following research questions:

1 How does the dynamic nature of control configuration ensure an effective stakeholder orientation during large-scale IS implementations?

2 How does an optimal stakeholder orientation relate to ISD project success and the diffusion of IS?

Inspired by the above research questions, Section 2 reviews the IS implementation literature concerning stakeholder engagement, control balancing, and IS diffusion. Section 3 outlines the current study’s casebased grounded theory approach. In Section 4, the case data and dis cussion leading to a new framework and theoretical codes are presented. Section 5 offers a critical discussion and theoretical integration of stakeholder engagement and control balancing. Finally, Section 6 offers concluding remarks that summarize our findings and their implications on theory and practice.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Stakeholder engagement and sensitivity

The concept of stakeholder engagement is becoming increasingly important among researchers and management practitioners of IS for explaining the diverse outcomes associated with the implementation of IS. Whereas earlier researchers (e.g., [3, 14]) attributed project failure to inappropriate social interactions among project stakeholders, several recent studies report stakeholder engagement as a vital ingredient for project success ([32, 63]; Wallbauma et al., 2010). A large-scale IS development project affects several stakeholder groups, such as project team, operational support, sponsor and project steering committee, downstream and upstream IS systems that integrate with the new IS, business owners, internal and external users, and vendors, thereby cutting across the intra-organizational hierarchical structure as well as inter-organizational relationships. This is a vital project characteristic as IS implementation success is often evaluated on the basis of stakeholdei “perceptions and expectations” [51], in addition to more traditional matrices such as process efficiency, product effectiveness, user satis faction, and degree of completion [53]. Whereas the mere existence of several stakeholder groups alone is not a matter of concern. different groups often possess diverging interests and beliefs and may pursue their own agendas [86] that can derail a project from its objectives. Situations like these can be appropriately labelled as pluralist when “stakeholder are perceived to have a basic compatibility of interests; not necessarily agree on ends and means, but able to achieve compromise” [36]. Pluralism can appear as a substantial threat for any IS project if all stakeholder groups are not optimally engaged and diverging goals and understandings are not effectively managed. Consequences of such inadequate engagement could range from missed system requirements or features to one group stakeholder influencing through artefacts and communications another group’s sensemaking process [39]. Therefore, different stakeholder groups play distinct roles during different phases of implementation to facilitate the IS diffusion process. Cooper and Zmud’s [23] frequently cited IS implementation model that indicates six stages through which an IS progresses to achieve diffusion within the organization: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routiniza tion, and infusion. Although the first three stages involve business case or opportunity proposals, securing project funding, and execution of the project, which are all within the control of a project team, success in the remaining three phases is largely determined by the stakeholder groups, who are often not optimally engaged during the execution of the project [84, 96]. Some authors have oscillated between involvement and engagement by employing the attribute of reciprocity or mutual benefit. MacLeod and Clarke [67] and others have pointed to care and commitment as the determinant of engagement levels. In an attempt to clarify the term engagement, Pushor [81] explained, “in comparison to involvement, engagement comes from en, meaning ‘make,’ and gage, meaning ‘pledge’—to make a pledge [45], to make a moral commitment.” Although stakeholder engagement can be depicted as an organizational effort to involve relevant stakeholders in a positive manner through exchange and cooperative relationships, and for the purpose of current research, we adopted the definition offered by Jenkin et al. [53], where stakeholder engagement comprises three dimensions: depth, scope, and timing. Depth of engagement refers to the level or amount of informa tion shared with certain groups via planning and the control mechanism. This can influence the sensemaking of the affected stakeholder group, thereby leading to a different understanding of the situation. Scope re lates to the aspect of who is engaged in an activity. Boland and Tenkasi [12] and Markus and Mao [69] previously highlighted the significance of this engagement dimension, arguing that we consider aspects such as “relative proportion” and “structural position” of stakeholders. Timing refers to the positioning of stakeholder engagement activities in relation to the project lifecycle. This has been reflected in earlier research where the adoption of different control mechanisms was driven by various stimuli such as uncertainty, project performance, and stakeholder in teractions [19].

While the significance of stakeholder engagement is well established in the literature, organizational stakeholder orientation is not [5]. Pre vious research on stakeholders acknowledged the significance of such orientation, characterizing it in terms of “how to integrate them into organizational decision-making processes” [64], thus, leaving the im plications for research and practice unclear [61]. Stakeholder theory is dominated by its normative core. As Jones and Wicks [54] wrote, “the interests of all stakeholders have intrinsic value, and no set of interests is assumed to dominate the others.” This is closely aligned with Freeman’s [37] original call for managerial attention to all stakeholder interests as a vital success factor and which bears responsibility for the implications of organizational actions. However, as organizational or managerial decisions have implications for the stakeholders of an organization, stakeholders likewise can affect the organization. For example, a managerial decision to reduce testing of the feature set of an IS could result in an inferior product for end-users, and a resistance by end-users to adopt a new IS could result in IS diffusion failure. This reciprocal relationship, which often forms the core notion of strategic stakeholder management, has been largely neglected in existing stakeholder theory literature [33]. In a public sector IS implementation, stakeholder tax onomy is often significantly different between private or profit-oriented organizations. In addition to primary stakeholder groups (e.g., em ployees, customers, partners, vendors, or government), there are also indirect stakeholders, including civil society and pressure groups who defend the interest of specific stakeholder groups, which thus gives rise to notions of reciprocity, loyalty, and fairness by stakeholders towards the organization. This disproves the self-interested maximizer of eco nomic theory, “who grabs what he can for himself” [78]. The notion of reciprocity argues that most people assess the fairness of others and reciprocate by, first, rewarding those whom they deem fair, and, second, incurring costs to punish those whom they deem unfair [13]. According to this argument, if stakeholders deem an organizational attitude towards them to be fair, they may reward the IS initiative by demon strating an “IS-adopting” attitude. Conversely, if the stakeholder groups deem organizational attitude towards them to be unfair, they may punish the organization by adopting an “IS-resisting” attitude. Besides the possibility of an instrumental approach adopted by organizations, the ethical dimension of stakeholder theory could very well be aban doned by pressure groups and stake watchers when the strategy formulation is guided by a political resource perspective [33]. There fore, the complexity of stakeholder taxonomy and reciprocity concerns clearly highlight the need for a systematic understanding of organization stakeholder orientation during IS implementations.

Earlier research on stakeholder orientations defines the concept in terms of allocated resources [10], such as time dedicated to certain ac tivities. During IS implementation, the allocated time for certain tasks clearly has implications for all stakeholders as project time is a scarce resource and cannot be equally distributed among all stakeholders. This brings forth the notion of preferential treatment of one group over the other or varied “stakeholder sensitivity” during the project. Time allo cation is only one aspect of stakeholder orientation, and other aspects of resource allocation—such as amount, basis, and target—should also be considered attributes of organizational stakeholder orientation, which together represent the notion of stakeholder sensitivity. Support for this construct is also established through Mitchell, Agle, and Wood [73] conceptualization of stakeholder salience in terms of power, urgency, and legitimacy. To further clarify this concept, we can focus on the organizational or project leadership’s motive. A project’s stakeholde engagement can be motivated by a desire to help its target (oth er-serving) versus the goal of benefitting the organization (self-serving). In other words, every stakeholder engagement can be classified as substantive or symbolic. Support for this distinction is also available through Greenwood’s (2007) “responsible treatment of stakeholders or stakeholder agency,” and Ahmed’s (2017) claim that “an organization’s attitude towards the definition of stakeholder scope.” For example, ac cording to Greenwood [42], mere engagement of stakeholders does not ensure responsible treatment of those stakeholders, and so could potentially be classified as “symbolic” engagement. It is only ensured when the organization acts in the interest of legitimate stakeholders. Ahmed [5] conceptualized this aspect in terms of the breadth of stake holders engaged at any given stage of the IS implementation process, and its perceived influence on the outcome of that stage. Hence, a nar row stakeholder focus would imply symbolic engagement. Therefore, complementary to stakeholder engagement is the concept of stakeholder

sensitivity, wherein an organization endorses the normative core of stakeholder theory. Drawing from corporate social responsibility (CSR) literature, we adopt the 3 C’s of substantive CSR—coherence, consis tency, and commitment [30]—to gage the level of stakeholder sensi tivity. ## 2.2. Control balancing and control configuration

Stakeholder engagement with project activities is generally attained through the deployment and enforcement of certain tools, techniques, artefacts, processes, and rituals. These are generally considered to be manifestations of different types of control mechanisms in an ISD project environment. Hence, a second theoretical perspective of our research revolves around the concept of control and control balancing.

The concept of control, often thought to be dyadic in nature, involves a controller (the person exercising control) and a controllee (the target of control). Numerous empirical studies have investigated control in an organizational or ISD context ([57], 2004). However, a majority of the studies adopting control theory have focused on different modes of control that can be classified into two broad categories: formal and informal control. Formal control is typically documented and initiated by management. Informal control is usually comprised of undocu mented measures initiated by the employee(s) themselves [52] and is commonly identified as self-control and clan control (Ouchi, 1978). Furthermore, a review of the control literature indicates a dominance of control antecedents ([56. 59]. 1997). the effects of control [46. 71. 75] and dynamics related to control choices [19, 58] in vertical or hierar chical relationship structures.

Kirsch [56] defined managerial control for IS projects as “attempts to ensure that individuals working on organizational projects act according to an agreed-upon strategy to achieve desired objectives.” The dyadic nature of control has inspired numerous empirical studies relating control to organizational or IS project contexts through diverse per spectives ([57], 2004). However, the targeted adjustment of the control configuration or control balancing is a lightly investigated and recent perspective within control literature [43]. Going beyond the dyadic nature of control, control balancing is a well-suited theoretical lens for examining the dynamic nature of a control configuration and measuring the control effectiveness in both vertical and horizontal relationship structure.

Enterprise IS implementation comprises numerous critical and interrelated activities within each implementation phase, which requires soliciting inputs from multiple stakeholders, often with nonoverlapping knowledge [71]. In the project initiation phase, for example, different groups perform requirement gathering, RFI, RFP, solution space analysis, bid/vendor evaluations, proposed solution evaluations, contract negotiation, and vendor selection. In the delivery and transition phase, for example, different groups of stakeholders are engaged with training and knowledge transfer, unit testing, integration and system testing, service level agreement (SLA) and warranty nego tiation, data and meta-data migrations, and handling go-live and par allel operations.

Along with the benefits of complementary knowledge and skills offered by various stakeholders, engagement brings the challenge of divergent priorities, goals, motivations, and power relations, which requires proper control and management to ensure a positive outcome [59]. Strong support for the presence of such non-technical challenges is offered by several earlier studies where IS project derailment had little relationship with technical challenges or technology [8, 68]. Further more, these stakeholder groups are often dynamic in nature and are related to each other in a horizontal relationship structure. As a result, aligning individual behaviours with organizational objectives at different phases of the IS implementation project requires adjustment to the control configuration [56]. Whereas antecedents are a critical factor in determining the choice of control mechanism [47], the circumstances leading to a change of an existing control are equally important for maintaining the control’s effectiveness.

The concept of control effectiveness is further significant as an applied control does not always generate the expected behavior change. This is evident in the paradoxical pattern identified by Tiwana and Keil [91], which leads to the distinction between attempted control and realized control. An analogous distinction has also been reported by Heumann et al. [47], suggesting a gradual weakening of control effec tiveness as it propagates multiple hierarchies. This has brought forth the notion of “controls recognized” by the controllee, which enables the examination of control effectiveness in a horizontal relationship or a relationship where the controllee has higher power than the controller. Furthermore, recent contributions in this area highlight the critical role of “control enactment” (i.e., how the controller interacts with the con trollee to put the portfolio of controls into practice) and controlee re actions [74, 101]. Although such organizational context and structural hierarchy of stakeholders pose particular challenges in adopting and applying controls for ISD projects, structure significance, and the ne cessity of controlling interaction in such contexts have been highlighted by Boland and Tenkasi [12] through concepts such as perspective making and perspective taking, and their implications on project outcome.

Acknowledging this challenge in the context of a large offshore IS implementation project, Gregory et al. [43] adopted a different conceptualization of the control type by defining a “control configura tion.” They also conceptualized control balancing as the act of making targeted adjustments to the control configuration. Gregory et al. [43] defined control configuration utilizing three distinct dimensions related to control: control types, control degree, and control styles. Control types are typically related to attaining certain goals and can be classified into three different categories: procedural, social, and hybrid. The sec ond dimension, control degree, indicates the measurement or intensity of the applied control, ranging from tight or frequent application to a relaxed or infrequent control application. The final dimension, control style, is related to the concept of controller versus controllee roles and directions of the control application, which can be either unilateral or bilateral in nature. Synthesizing these three control characteristics, Gregory et al. [43] proposed three distinct control configurations: authoritative, coordinated, and trust-based. ## 2.3. IS diffusion

Although not a primary objective of our current research, stake holder engagement facilitated through formal control mechanisms has been found to impact different stages of IS diffusion lifecycle, yet this is a largely overlooked aspect in IS literature [2]. IS diffusion is an encom passing view towards the lifecycle of an enterprise IS that consists of a series of stages [85] and is much larger than ISD project duration. Even with a successful ISD project, many organizations fail to attain the ex pected return on IS investment due to post-project user resistance resulting from superficial stakeholder engagement [9, 50]. Interestingly, optimal stakeholder engagement appears to not only affect immediate ISD phases, but also the IS diffusion phases in the long term.

Several theoretical and conceptual models have been developed to understand and explain diffusion patterns of IS implementation and define a successful IS diffusion. For example, Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, and Davis [94] developed the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), identifying critical determinants of user accep tance and attitude towards new IS. Seddon and Kiew [87] argued for a focus on “usefulness.” DeLone and McLean [25] suggested retaining the use aspect of IS by considering the variability of IS usage, and, more recently, Petter and McLean [79] investigated aspects of use and user satisfaction. Although these models have provided valuable guidance in understanding ISD success and diffusion, they are not suitable for capturing an intervention perspective. This is primarily due to their focus on the latter part of the IS diffusion stages. For example, DeLone and McLean’s (1992) model of IS diffusion “systems quality” and “in formation quality” misses pre-project initiation, requirement analysis, and much of the development phases of ISD lifecycle. Petter and McLean [79] initiated at the same point as DeLone and McLean [25], but their updated model [26] included “service quality.” This new construct also captures a post-deployment aspect of IS diffusion. UTAUT focuses on critical determinants of user acceptance and usage behavior in relation to IS diffusion. Venkatesh et al. [94] reported four key determi nants—performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions—which mainly examine the user’s cognitive aspects and attitude towards the IS.

An intervention perspective is suitable for examining the early ISD phases where the project team retains significant control over the ac tivities and can influence post-deployment state of the IS, as discussed in other IS diffusion models. For example, a development phase interven tion may include targeted adjustment of control configuration and how deliberate enactment of certain stakeholder orientation leads to a desired outcome. However, to integrate such perspectives with IS diffusion outcomes, we need an encompassing IS diffusion model that represents early ISD phases. One of the well-known and frequently cited models in this domain is Cooper and Zmud’s (1990) IS implementation model, which captures the effects of actor-intervention on IS diffusion. According to this model, IS implementation consists of six stages that lead to IS diffusion: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routinization, and infusion (see Fig. 1). Initiation is the very early stage of IS implementation project where the organization proposes a tech nical innovation to solve a business problem or exploit an opportunity. Adoption involves an organization’s strategic initiative and alignment review process that attempts to ensure top IS and business executives agree to invest in an IT system and the required resources to accom modate the new system. Adaptation corresponds to a project’s execution phase. This refers to the development, installation, and maintenance of the adopted IS. Acceptance is a critical stage for all IS implementation initiatives and begins immediately after the transition of a newly developed IS from the project environment to operations. At this point, employees are expected to employ the IT artefact in their work. This early operational use of an IS often determines whether a system is likely to be accepted or rejected. Routinization refers to the stage when the new IS becomes a system of record and parallel operations of “old” and “new” IS are no longer required. This is when the use of the IS becomes a routine activity in the organization. Finally, infusion relates to the “business benefit realization” aspect of an IS project where increased organizational effectiveness is obtained from utilizing the implemented IS to its full potential in an integrated manner.

For the current research, we adhere to Cooper and Zmud’s (1990) model of IS diffusion to demonstrate the impact of certain stakeholder orientation on IS diffusion stages.

## 3. Research methodology

Seeking to investigate the nature of control configuration and stakeholder orientation and their relationship during an IS imple mentation and to generate a descriptive and explanatory theory in this regard, we employ a case-based grounded theory method [41, 70]. Appendix A outlines the details of the process followed while analysing data and mapping them into core categories.

Four cases are analysed in the current research. These include two successful and two failed implementations of an enterprise IS by a Ca nadian government organization. A failed or “not so successful” project is defined by looking at multiple criteria including a lower return on investment (ROI), higher support cost, low user satisfaction, and consideration for possible replacement. Table 3 presents a summary of all four cases analysed through current research.

We interviewed a total of 41 project participants, with a minimum of 9 participants from each selected case. Each interview lasted approxi mately 80 min. Our selection process considered people from different roles so as to construct a representative sample. Adhering to the advice of Walsham [98] regarding the interpretive case study, we present a summary of interview participants along with their roles on Table 4.

In addition to primary interviews, we also collected secondary project data. This was used to triangulate our findings and enhance validity [102]. To facilitate a rigorous, systematic, and comprehensive analysis process, we transcribed all interview recordings. The tran scribed interviews and field notes taken during the interview have been analysed inductively [16]. The reliability of this research was based on the use of a well-documented methodology and case study protocol [102]. A case database was created, wherein all case interviews and field notes were stored. This was to support a clear and documented chain of evidence [102]. This database supported the derivation of the conclu sions from the initial research questions. We also adopted several stra tegies to ensure the validity of the findings.

Themes, patterns, and categories were validated using secondary data that primarily comprised project documentation, meeting minutes, social event records, email communications, and project office records. Adhering to the “principle of the hermeneutic circle” [60], a member check was conducted after primary interview and analysis to validate interpretive codes as “individual” and “collective whole.” This was a foundational principle for conducting interpretive case study research. A detailed description of the quality control process for this research is presented in Exhibit B-1 of Appendix B.

Such a strategic stakeholder orientation appeared very effective in launching an IS initiative to solve business efficiency problems facilitating initiation and adoption (Cases A and C), as well as successful utilization of new IS systems after project delivery phase, leading to routinization and infusion (Cases A and B). ## 6. Best interest orientation and control shifts impact on IS diffusion stages

Another visible shift in stakeholder orientation was identified during the mid-project lifecycle for all four cases where primary tasks involved conceptual design and architectural design of the IS. As shown in Table 5's first column, “stakeholder orientation." best interest orientation was one of the four dominant stakeholder orientations that emerged during the mid-project lifecycle. Best interest stakeholder orientation aimed to balance technical constraints and stakeholder interests through more focused stakeholder engagement (column four) and encompassing consideration or stakeholder sensitivity (column five). Although re quirements were generally well understood at this point, due to tech nical and architectural conflicts, open discussion forums in a social setting introduced various perspectives and technical approaches. This indicated a positive expectation by each group about others’ future task performance and commitments, facilitated by a high level of shared understanding of future obligations.

A trust-based control portfolio (column two of Table 5) was observed in all four cases, although not in a harmonized fashion. The adoption decision of control styles was influenced by several different factors, both generalizable and idiosyncratic to projects. Such controls were reflected through a project’s reduced communications, status meetings, progress updates, managerial reviews, audits, and other administrative controls. A trust-based control was adopted in Cases A and B due to higher levels of shared understanding, positive performance by the controlee (i.e., vendor and IT support teams), and a mature level of self and clan controls (column three). A trust-based control is particularly important in an agile development environment with diffuse stake holder groups [48], where clan formation is possible, and empowerment of the team is necessary for effective task execution.

In Cases A and B, trust-based control demonstrated a positive influ ence by motivating project participants and reducing information asymmetry (column three of Table 5). This was reflected through behavioural changes such as the vendor assuming a higher level of re sponsibility than required by contract and proactive communications on the client side. It was also found that improper application of a trustbased control can have a detrimental effect on IS implementation pro cesses and on overall project objectives. Projects under Cases C and D often applied an authoritative or coordinated control, whereas Cases A and B opted for a trust-based control for the corresponding phases (column two). Adhering to rituals by an inexperienced project manager and a lack of technical aptitude within the core project team were some of the identifiable traits responsible for such control discrepancies in the former pair. An authoritative control at that point of the project’s life cycle had a negative impact on shared understanding. Furthermore, this encouraged some groups to adopt a more defensive approach to work place behavior, resulting in delayed delivery of work packages, incon sistent and incomplete requirements, poor functional quality, and increased costs due to unexpected changes.

Consequently, both adaptation and acceptance aspects of IS diffusion were found to negatively impact for Cases C and D, where development work halted awaiting client clarifications and a large volume of enhancement requests were generated during client signoff of the project (column six of Table 5).

## 7. Economic-cost orientation and control shifts impact on IS diffusion stages

As shown in Table 5’s first column, “stakeholder orientation,” economic-cost orientation was one of the four dominant stakeholder orientations that developed at mid-project (development). In economiccost orientation, the key project focus was “aligning stakeholder needs with organizational and project capabilities from a physical and logical resources perspective and removing roadblocks for the core project team.” During the development phase, all four projects had to manage a distributed development team containing offshore vendor teams. Therefore, such orientation was necessary not only for the effective conversion of intangible requirements into tangible deliverables but also for maintaining project commitments on time, scope, and money.

Although the level of unfulfilled expectation was low, some negative anticipation was observed due to the distributed development envi ronment and uncertainties related to the integration of different technical components (column three of Table 5). Hence, increased information sharing and collaboration led to a coordinated-control portfolio, where bilateral, hybrid controls were applied (column two). Although Henderson and Lee [46] indicated that joint application of both formal and informal control in IS implementation results in higher performance, Kirsch [57] advocated a portfolio of controls to address the challenge of diffused stakeholder groups with diverse knowledge and skills. Complementing Kirsch’s (1997) argument for a control portfolio, Soh, Kien, and Tay-Yap [89] introduced the concept of controllee-initiated controls, as well as called for separate controls tar geted at each stakeholder group to address distinctive attributes of each element in a multi-stakeholder IS implementation. The coordinated-control configuration (column two) observed in cases A, B, $\mathrm { C } ,$ and D closely supports these earlier arguments. Furthermore, a co ordinated control was also found to facilitate the concept of value co-production by stakeholders [11, 77, 106], where poor performance by one stakeholder group has negative consequences for others. While contingent upon a strong mutual commitment (Gregory et al., 2014), a coordinated-control style significantly improved the efficiency of in formation flow and resource deployment among small groups of stakeholders.

This bilateral- and hybrid-control configuration was also tethered with a strict change control mechanism to prevent any unplanned scope alterations. Besides informal meetings, Cases A, B, and C arranged site visits by offshore vendors that helped clarify shared understanding of technical issues and client work culture and established a vendorproposed coordination process to overcome negative impacts of time zone differences between Canada and Europe. A bidirectional control is observed in Cases A and B, where the client-initiated regular discussion sessions with the project’s technical team and the vendor to redesign business processes, validate proposed requirements, and understand the technical capabilities of the new IS solution. This eventually resulted in intense cooperation among the parties, as well as enhanced sharing of knowledge among stakeholder groups.

The findings here indicate an instrumental approach towards stakeholder engagement through an economic-cost orientation to be highly effective in the adaptation stage of a new IS (column six of Table 5). Besides the obvious impact of better understanding and effective work coordination among stakeholders, a coordinated-control configuration significantly reduced both requirement risks and user risks, thus leading to efficient development cycles and lower resistance in accepting deliverables by establishing perceptual congruence among distributed development teams (column six).

## 8.3. Best interest dimension (normative orientation)

The upper left or second quadrant (Q2) of Fig. 3 comprises the best interest dimension of the organization’s stakeholder orientation. This is manifested in the project’s strong sensitivity and low-to-moderate engagement of stakeholders (as shown in Fig. 3). Best interest can be interpreted as the organization working in the best interests of stake holders (moderate-to-high sensitivity) without necessarily engaging them. This notion is supported by the information orientation versus communication orientation aspects found in general stakeholder theory, indicating deliberate choices a manager can make. Although an orga nization’s ability to maintain stakeholder sensitivity without engage ment may be questionable, this orientation still appears to be well aligned with the normative aspect of stakeholder theory [29], resem bling a traditional version of CSR. Furthermore, stakeholder agency theory [48], a modification of agency theory, attempts to explain the suppression of strategic behaviours of a firm through incentive align ment mechanisms and various structural forms that police the implicit and explicit contracts between managers and stakeholders. Moving to a micro-level, each project’s stakeholders are a part of the nexus of im plicit or explicit contract with the project team, where the project management team assumes a central position in the nexus of such contracts. Such conceptualization also displays a striking resemblance to agency theory, where principals appoint or hire agents to carry out ac tivities on their behalf.

At this stage of the project’s lifecycle, all four cases experienced some level of unfulfilled expectations, such as delay in provisioning of infra structure components, missing procedure manuals for the legacy sys tems, and unanswered questions for requirement clarifications. Furthermore, redundant and conflicting requirements also signaled an absence of shared understanding. With the accepted MOU, approved project charter, and legally enforceable contract, all four projects introduced some degree of unidirectional, procedural control through status review, change advisory board (CAB), and issue-tracking mech anism. Because these control mechanisms can only target a selective subset of stakeholder groups, the scope aspect of stakeholder engage ment was significantly reduced compared to the responsibility orienta tion. Bounded by a fixed project budget, an integrative approach to any stakeholder request was applied by the CAB, which also reduced the sensitivity level of stakeholder orientation at this point. For Cases A, B, $\mathrm { C } ,$ and D, the conceptual design and planning and design and architec ture phases demonstrated a strong best interest orientation, as processes and tools like DeepDive™-II, design authority, capacity planning, balanced representation, and RTM dominated the implementation flow. Aspects of best interest were demonstrated through various processes. One highly visible process of this stage was the design authority. Comprising two key elements—design authority group and design approval cycle—the design authority process was introduced early in the life cycle of the project. In addition to minimizing discrepancies between client and vendor expectations, instrumental use of this process determined what got implemented and how.

The best interest was the prevalent stakeholder orientation for the analysis and conceptual design, and design and architecture phases (rows 4 and 5 of Appendix C Table C1). ## 8.4. Economic-cost dimension (instrumental orientation)

As project implementation moved towards design and development phases (rows 4, and 5 of Appendix C Table C1), each project team adjusted processes, tools, and control objectives to promote harmony among different stakeholder groups and other internal processes, and yet kept the core project team highly focused on implementation ob jectives and free from any interruptions or diversions.

The bottom left or third quadrant (Q3) of Fig. 3 comprises the economic-cost dimension of an organization’s stakeholder orientation. This orientation was enacted through a project’s low sensitivity and low level engagement of its stakeholder. The location of a project's devel opment lifecycle in Fig. 3 indicates a significantly lower intensity of both the engagement and sensitivity of stakeholders (Q3 of Fig. 3). Main taining high stakeholder engagement and sensitivity does not come without a cost during an IS implementation project. High stakeholder engagement often requires valuable project time and resources dedi cated to addressing various concerns that could significantly reduce the project’s effectiveness, as well negatively impact the scope of the de liverables. To avoid disastrous consequences, projects may adopt an economic-cost orientation, which is an economically based view. Under this view, a firm treats its stakeholders as instrumental, minimizing any interest in building relationships with them. Although an economicefficiency perspective aligns well with Friedman’s [38] theorem limiting a firm’s responsibility to maximize profits, such a self-interested position has been critiqued on ethical grounds ([20], pp. 231–33; [100], pp. 76–77; [72], pp. 205, 214–15; [49], pp. 716, 718–19). To put Friedman’s view on business ethics into perspective and minimize such diverging views, Wagner-Tsukamoto [97] introduced an economic revision of Friedman’s theorem by introducing concepts of ethical cap ital and active moral agency. Wagner-Tsukamoto’s revision allowed integration between a project’s economic-cost orientation, driven by performance or efficiency gains and predominantly in a utilitarian, consequentialist tradition with moral judgment.

An economic-cost stakeholder orientation was attained and enforced through the adoption of a distinct-control configuration that appeared as coordinated control among a small group of stakeholders, coupled with reduced sensitivity towards any new requests. This salient theme of coordinate control is aimed at “aligning stakeholder needs with orga nizational and project capabilities from a physical and logical resources perspective and removing roadblocks for the core project team.” At this point, control utilization indicated very little effort at relationshipbuilding or engaging stakeholders in an encompassing manner. Low stakeholder engagement at this point not only enabled projects to attain much-needed cost efficiency gains but also helped avoid design dis ruptions, often observed in high stakeholder engagement situations. Both Cases A and B demonstrated very low engagement and sensitivity combination during the development cycle (Q3 in Fig. 3), which helped manage several challenges, such as balancing out the early cost over runs, scope creep resulting from unchecked and on-the-fly customer requirements, and high utilization of project resources, thus minimizing any negative effects of shared resources. This also supported a vital success factor for an agile development process that involved empow erment of the project team in the decision-making process.

Three projects—Cases A, B, and D—demonstrated a bilateral-control style over the vendor, replacing the unilateral control observed during earlier phases. Such a shift was driven by the project team’s reduced negative anticipation (i.e., low unfulfilled expectations and higher level of shared understanding) and project context. All three cases had an offshore component in their development cycles. To maintain the shared understanding and overcome data conversion and integration chal lenges, projects adopted a less authoritative approach by introducing site visits and joint decision making. Frequent open communications and team empowerment significantly improved individual motivations and sense of belonging. The immediate effects of this control shift were categorized by high team dynamics, team cohesion, and, in Cases A and B, the vendor taking on more responsibilities than anticipated by several informants.

While the control type between vendors and project teams was largely social in nature, the dominant-control style with internal IT support teams appeared to be more authoritative in nature. The desire to secure scarce organizational resources compelled project teams to take such a proactive attitude. An absence of such authoritative control also resulted in the unavailability of internal resources for Case C.

## 9. Discussion and conclusion

This study set out to explore the existing phenomenon of enterprise IS implementation through a novel perspective of control balancing, stakeholder engagement, and sensitivity. Stakeholder engagement and sensitivity jointly describe four types of stakeholder orientations: stra tegic, responsibility, best interest, and economic cost.

The objective was to identify relationships among control configu ration, stakeholder engagement, and stakeholder sensitivity, and how they influence different phases of IS diffusion. This study drew from control theory, control balancing theory, stakeholder theory, stake holder agency theory, and IS diffusion literature to develop the argu ment. In doing so, current research developed and validated a new and vital construct for IS diffusion—stakeholder orientation. Control theory and control balancing was employed to capture and validate the dy namic nature of control configurations in IS projects. The study also leveraged stakeholder theory and stakeholder agency theory to capture the dynamic stakeholder orientation in IS implementation projects. Finally, through analysis and synthesis, it established vital relationships among control configurations, stakeholder orientations, and IS diffusion stages, where maintaining an optimal stakeholder orientation, enacted by appropriate control configuration, leads to a successful IS imple mentation and diffusion outcome.

Existing empirical research has identified a large number of factors contributing to the success or failure of an enterprise systems imple mentation. These factors often relate to politics, motivation, and conflict resulting from multi-stakeholder interactions and relationships [8, 68]. This study proposed at the onset of research the dire necessity of an optimal stakeholder engagement, tethered with a mechanism to regulate stakeholder’s influence on project activities. This is primarily driven by the fact that stakeholders’ goals and priorities are often divergent [8, 68]. This investigation on stakeholder engagement and sensitivity found a dynamic pattern where stakeholder engagement and sensitivity varied at different phases of an ISD project. This pattern indicates fluctuating stakeholder engagement and sensitivity throughout the lifecycle of an ISD project. Combining the nature of stakeholder engagement with stakeholder sensitivity, this research validated a new construct, stake holder orientation, to categorize engagement variations. This allowed for the identification of four distinct stakeholder orientations: strategic, responsibility, best interest, and economic cost.

Stakeholder engagement and sensitivity are essential for an ISD project’s survival and long-term success. At the same time, the possi bility of opportunistic stakeholder behavior is a realistic threat to a project’s objectives. Additionally, traditional control mechanisms are often ineffective in a complex and outsourced IS implementation. Therefore, a stakeholder orientation is necessary to manage both the engagement and sensitivity of stakeholders. Appropriate stakeholder orientations have also been found to support the existing notion of stakeholder integration [80] in the present analysed cases via integra tion of stakeholders’ demands, knowledge absorption, and improved performance. This, in turn, helped mitigate several other risk di mensions for projects, such as complexity risk and planning and control risks. Therefore, the current research found a positive correlation be tween optimal stakeholder orientations and favourable IS diffusion outcome.

## 9.1. Implication for theory

This research on IS diffusion from a multi-perspective approach of fers two key contributions on theory and provides guidance for IS re searchers to move beyond the research on control or diffusion alone. First, findings by Ballejos and Montagna [7] clearly indicate the signif icance of the stakeholder perspective when considering a multi-party joint IS implementation effort, which is even more salient for govern ment organizations due to a larger stakeholder group, as well as different ethical considerations and value-maximization objectives [35]. Ballejos and Montagna [7] also found that engagement alone is not adequate for ensuring sufficient stakeholder consideration in an IS design process. Rather, a level of consistency in stakeholder representation is necessary to avoid underachievement of IS project goals due to poorly conceived procedures. Yet, a majority of stakeholder-related research in the IS domain focuses on the concept of organizational re sponsibility towards the stakeholder, which directly aligns with the normative core of stakeholder theory. The current study complements this conventional approach of stakeholder research in a substantial way, both by considering the reality that stakeholders can impact the orga nization or IS diffusion, and by focusing on the balancing aspect of stakeholder orientation. Furthermore, capturing the dynamic nature of stakeholder orientation in IS diffusion through a well-defined frame work facilitates our understanding of normative and instrumental as pects of stakeholder theory. This also helps us understand Jones and Wicks’ (1999) convergent stakeholder theory, according to which the instrumental usage of stakeholder theory does not require sacrificing the normative core. The new construct, stakeholder orientation, allows the identification of four distinct stakeholder orientations: strategic, re sponsibility, best interest, and economic cost. This further enriches the understanding of stakeholder theory in the context of IS diffusion. Ac cording to Fisher and Aguinis [34], this contribution can be classified as theory elaboration through new construct specification. Second, thi research analysed IS diffusion stages through the control balancing theory and identified the necessity of control balancing for a multi-partner IS implementation. The current application of control balancing theory is much more complex compared to that of Gregory et al. (2014). Validation of this relatively new theory using four distinct IS implementation cases offers significant support towards the general izability of this theory. In all four cases of IS diffusion, it was found that the existence of control balancing or deliberate control-configuration shifts were driven by certain trigger factors, as originally identified by Gregory et al. (2014). Furthermore, this study also found positive or negative IS diffusion impact of such control configuration, justifying the necessity of control balancing in IS diffusion.

## 9.2. Implication for practice

The findings of this research also have important practical implica tions for IT implementation. The study has provided some preliminary evidence concerning the criteria that potential adopters utilize to evaluate IT innovations. Stakeholder engagement is a well-touted critical success factor in most large-scale IS diffusions; yet, a systematic approach to control this aspect is almost non-existent. Change agents assuming the role of a “controller” can utilize the systematic conceptu alization of stakeholder orientation and its optimal configurations pre sented in this study to influence all six phases of an IS diffusion process. This, in turn, should increase the likely effectiveness and efficiency of most managerial interventions. For example, to avoid stakeholder resistance during adoption, adaptation and acceptance stages of IS diffusion, the controller should adopt a comprehensive approach to re quirements (the depth of engagement), keep all direct and indirect stakeholders informed (the scope of engagement), secure all out of project human resources early (the timing of engagement), and use a relaxed exclusion filter for requirements (the sensitivity of engagement) during the early lifecycle of an ISD or IS implementation. In a similar fashion, manifestations of the remaining three stakeholder orientations can be achieved through similar policy adjustments or managerial in terventions leading to desired IS diffusion outcomes.

## Appendix D

Interview Guide

The following questions were used as a guide during the interview. Each interview was emergent; the interviewer adapted questions and asked additional probing questions as needed.

Client organization follows a seven-step IS project implementation model (initiation, requirement and planning, analysis and conceptual design, design and architecture, development, delivery and transition, close-out) and four-stage investment gate screening for strategic investment approval (provided by the PMO of the organization). Some questions are based on this organizational context.

1. What is the name of the project?

2. What was your role on the project and within the organization?

3. How long have you been with the company and the project?

4. When did the project start and end?

5. Can you describe the project organization chart? Is this typical for the organization?

6. Who else was directly or indirectly involved with the project that are not captured by the org chart?

7. Did this project need to be justified formally before it was approved? If so, what justifications were provided?

8. What were the purpose, goals, and objectives of the project? (Probe: Discuss linkages or dependencies on other systems or projects.)

9. Indicate major activities during each phase of the project?

10. At any time, was this project’s priority changed?

11. At any time were their resource allocation changes on the project? If so, why were those changes made?

12. Were there any changes to requirements during the project? How were changes in requirements handled?

13. Were you given adequate ownership and decision-making ability in running this project? (for Project Manager)

14. Please describe any tracking and monitoring tools, process used the project for different group during each stage of the project?

15. What was the meeting frequency during each phase and roughly size of attendees?

16. Did meeting and reporting frequency change at any point of the project?

17. Please indicate the direction of control and frequency for each phase of the project

18. How did you handled roadblocks (problems, unexpected issues, surprises)? Did you have predetermined escalation paths? (Probe: Discuss risk and risk management approach; also discuss any major issues and why they arose.)

19. How did the business (for example, product managers, marketing, sales) communicate with the project team?

20. How did the project team communicate with the business?

21. How did the software vendor Dev team communicate with the business?

22. Was the project implemented on time? Budget? And Scope?

23. Was this project considered successful? Why or why not?

24. What was the number of incidents at post-deployment?

25. Are you currently considering any alternative solution or still using old legacy processes?

26. Were all of the objectives and goals of the project met?

27. Were some objectives for this project not met? Was this a conscious decision (for example, ran out of time or money)? If some objectives were not met, what was done as a result?

28. Was the business (and users) satisfied with the results of the project? why?

29. Was the development team satisfied with the results of the project? why?

30. How would you rate each project phase on information shared or received from different groups directly or indirectly related to the project? Use a scale of 0 to 5 where: 0 [none], 1 [very low], 2 [low], 3 [average/about 50%], 4 [significant], 5 [very detailed documents]

31. How would you rate each project phase on number of different groups directly or indirectly involved with the project activities?

Use a scale of 0 to 5 where: 0 [none], 1 [very few], 2 [few], 3 [about half of the different groups], 4 [most], 5 [at most all]

32. How would vou rate project team's willingness to listen to the concerns of different groups and address any of those concerns raised?Use a scale of 0 to 5 where: 0 [none], 1 [very little], 2 [little], 3 [], 4 [eager], 5 [very eager]

33. How would you rate project team’s ability to reach out to multiple groups during each stage?Use a scale of 0 to 5 where: 0 [missing], 1 [very poor], 2 [poor], 3 [average], 4 [efficient], 5 [very efficient]

34. Please indicate the major challenges/roadblocks you can recall on each of the following phases and reasons for those?

35. Please indicate any incorrect decisions or something that you would differently if you had the opportunity to redo the same project?

36. Please indicate any beneficial process, tools, artefacts, decision/approach the project utilized during each phase that can benefit other projects if adopted?
