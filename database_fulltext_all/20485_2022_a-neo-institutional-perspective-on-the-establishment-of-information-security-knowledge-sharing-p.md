# A neo-institutional perspective on the establishment of information security knowledge sharing practices

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Information security knowledge sharing establishment Neoinstitutional theory Security compliance Security culture

## A B S T R A C T

Information security knowledge sharing (ISKS) among an organization’s employees is vital to the organization’s ability to protect itself from any number of prevalent threats, yet for many organizations, their ability to establish ISKS practices is hampered by a lack of understanding of where and how the key drivers of these practices will emerge. Based on neoinstitutional theory and a multi-study field survey of 834 professional managers in the USA, we develop and test a model that explains the establishment of ISKS practices in an organization as a product of the institutional forces abut to the organization providing normative, mimetic, and coercive influences on top management beliefs and participations in ISKS. Our findings also emphasize the importance of establishing ISKS practices for ensuring employee compliance with information security policies and an effective culture of se curity. Prior research has shown the importance of institutional forces on organizational processes as well as the importance of ISKS to organizational security efforts. However, this study is one of the early studies to provide insight into the manner, in which institutional forces hold sway over the people responsible for establishing the ISKS practices of a firm; insight that it is essential for firms that have yet to establish such practices or have struggled in their attempts to do so.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade or so, information security (InfoSec) programs have slowly evolved to encompass both the technical and human aspects of security threat protection, detection, mitigation, and recovery [1]. For many InfoSec programs, their focus has shifted away from a purely technical one to a socio-technical one. These programs continue to build solutions around the technical infrastructures of an organization, but now also attend to the potential of employees as targets of an attack [2]. For years, employee targeted attacks, such as phishing and evil twin, have plagued organizations, but only recently have organizations star ted to integrate more human-centric solutions such as user behavior analytics and other forms of employee monitoring and surveillance into their InfoSec programs [3, 4, 5]. However, even for firms that have evolved their security programs to a socio-technical level, the lack of InfoSec knowledge among their employees remains their greatest threat [6], and the reported \$11.45 million USD average cost to organizations in 2020 due to employee-related security incidents speaks to this threat [7].

InfoSec knowledge refers to information and knowledge about organizational practices and strategies which can safeguard an organi zation’s information assets, such as customer data, product information, and sales information [2, 8]. There are a number of reasons why em ployees may lack InfoSec knowledge [2]. It might be due to the low-level engagement of employees when developing InfoSec policies [9] or the way rules and regulations are communicated to them in terms of their responsibility for the protection of information assets [10, 11]. Recent research points also to ineffective techniques for motivating, engaging, and training employees to act securely when working with organiza tional systems and their data; issues that can thwart an organization’s compliance efforts [12] and diminish or negate their culture of security [13]. As long as employees are ill-equipped with adequate InfoSec knowledge, organizations will continue to struggle to defend themselves.

One way in which organizations have approached this problem is through InfoSec knowledge sharing (ISKS) [14, 15]. For organizations to improve their employees’ InfoSec knowledge and subsequent ability to help defend themselves, there is a critical need to establish effective ISKS practices. The establishment of ISKS practices refers to the development of a set of techniques and procedures for sharing information and knowledge about organizational policies, standards, and processes that can safeguard an organization’s information assets [2, 8]. Information systems (IS) have created opportunities for computer crimes in which hackers and unauthorised users can gain access to organizational data. Therefore, it is important that organizations give their employees the knowledge they need to be assets rather than liabilities to their InfoSec security programs. Firms that have established ISKS practices are in a much better position than others to distribute such knowledge.

Researchers have emphasized the importance of top management for the effective implementation of any organizational innovation [16]. Top management is the primary human agency that translates institutional forces into managerial actions, such as changing organizational struc tures and establishing policies based on their beliefs of institutional needs [17]. Therefore, the key to establishing ISKS practices is the support and involvement by the top management of a firm. This is because when top managers establish ISKS practices, they tend to allo cate the organizational resources required to ensure an appropriate level of efficacy for them [2]. The InfoSec literature tells us that the effective establishment of ISKS practices entails organizational processes through which to conduct both formal (e.g., education programs) and informal (e.g., informal advisory services) InfoSec programs [15, 18]. These include training employees on general InfoSec threats and providing guidance on reactions to InfoSec incidents [2, 15, 19]. Like most orga nizational processes, in order for them to become institutionalized, the top management of the organization must be engaged, both in the way they think about the processes as well as in the way they participate [13, 20]. The establishment of ISKS practices also improves the overall InfoSec compliance in organizations by promoting the effective imple mentation of InfoSec standards and policies that are able to minimize InfoSec risks in organizations [21]. However, studies which have focused on the need to establish a foundation that can cultivate all these activities in one place [22, 23] are scarce.

The InfoSec literature also suggests another key ingredient for effective ISKS in an organization and its ability to leverage institutional forces, such as professional affiliations and government regulatory bodies, as effective knowledge resources [2]. Institutional forces are the forces exerted by relevant institutes on a firm [24], including profes sional associations, regulations, and technical standards, among others. For the establishment of ISKS practices, it is both common and desirable that organizations follow the best practices of other organizations, i.e., being isomorphic to the other organizations [25]. Prior research has established that in contexts, such as InfoSec, where legitimacy and survival within highly turbulent environments are of paramount importance, and isomorphism is highly desirable [26, 27] and serves to help validate managerial decision making [28].

Prior research has shown that coercive, normative, and mimetic institutional forces help form the isomorphic pressure applied to orga nizations as they struggle to protect themselves from InfoSec threats [27]. Coercive forces, such as government rules and regulations, moti vate top managers to mandate security policies and procedures in thei organizations, while normative and mimetic forces provide an under standing of security policies and procedures which are highly adopted by organizations [27]. For organizational survival, it is important that managers implement security policies and procedures, which are construed as appropriate by social norms and values [29]. These isomorphic institutional forces have been shown to influence the secu rity knowledge sharing within an organization, including the support its top management has for InfoSec programs, in general [30, 31]. These institutional forces also influence decisions about the development of integrated frameworks for capturing and sharing security knowledge to mitigate InfoSec risks [2]. However, what the extant literature doesn’t clearly explain is how these institutional forces provide such influence. How do isomorphic institutional forces influence the establishment of ISKS practices? Without such an understanding, organizations may under value, misidentify, or misappropriate potential external knowledge re sources, weakening their ability to establish effective ISKS practices and, ultimately, deteriorating their security compliance and security cul tures. For this reason, the question of how is vitally important and is what drives the current study.

Toward answering this question, we apply neoinstitutional theory to develop a model to explain how institutional forces influence an orga nization’s establishment of ISKS practices, particularly through its top management beliefs and actions. We then test the model using a field survey of professional managers from the USA, the results of which highlight the value of institutional forces to a firm’s ISKS establishment as well as the pivotal roles the firm’s capacity to recognize, assimilate, and apply knowledge, and top management involvement play in shaping this influence. For a body of literature that has primarily focused on individual-level phenomena, these results provide InfoSec scholars and practitioners with a much-needed organizational-level perspective [32].

Our research is particularly noteworthy for the following reasons: First, this research focuses on investigating the effects of institutional forces on the establishment of ISKS practices. Although previous research has explained the importance of InfoSec mechanisms, such as organizational processes and InfoSec awareness programs [15, 33], research that explains the impetus for establishing ISKS practices and the source and manner of influence for their establishment is missing. Second, previous research has mainly focused on the individual behavioral side of InfoSec, with an aim to understand individual’s InfoSec compliance and misuse behaviors [10, 34]. This research pro vides a much-needed organizational perspective in the literature. Although previous research has explained the importance of top man agement involvement in organizational initiatives [17, 30], there is a lack of research that specifically focuses on the impact of top manage ment beliefs and participation on the establishment of ISKS practices in organizations, and the manner in which those beliefs are influenced by institutional forces outside their firms.

## 2. Theorizing with neo-institutional theory

The neoinstitutional theory emerged from the old institutional the ory because the old institutional theory was lacking explanations for how institutionalization processes generate organizational value [27]. Based on neoinstitutional theory, rationalized institutional elements and networks of social organizations influence formal organizational struc tures [35]. The persistence of cultural understandings varies as per the level of institutionalization [36]. The neoinstitutional theory was enhanced by the discussions of DiMaggio and Powell [26], where they explained that organizations change as per the influences of the actors within the organizational context. Previous research on neoinstitutional theory [37–40] explained how cultural processes and actors affect the institutional arrangements (e.g., organizational practices and structures) within which they operate. Organizations adjust their behaviors as per the social pressures that originate from other organizations in the same environment [41]. The neoinstitutional theory has been used to under stand InfoSec implementations in various contexts such as higher edu cation [29, 42] and small and medium enterprises (SMEs) [30].

There are two main elements in neoinstitutional theory: (1) institu tionalization – the process where the organizational formal structure is established [43] and (2) isomorphism – the force on an organizational unit to resemble other units in the same environment [26]. Further, neoinstitutional theory explains three forms of institutional isomorphic influence: normative, mimetic, and coercive [26].

Normative influence occurs mainly from social values, social norms, and professionalization. While social values provide desirable methods of comparing and assessing existing behaviors, social norms define how things should be done [44]. Professionalization is the members of an occupation who attempt to specify work conditions and procedures to create an occupational autonomy [26]. Formal education and profes sional networks create an environment that supports shared social values and norms [45], leading to professionalization. Normative in fluence may have positive impacts (e.g., value creation and opportunities to gain reputation) as well as negative impacts $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . ,$ de viations from goals and standards) on organizations [46].

Mimetic influence occurs when organizations mimic the actions of other organizations due to uncertainly of the organizational environ ment, lack of understanding of organizational technologies, or ambig uous organizational goals [26, 31]. There is a high probability that organizations mimic other organizations when they have boundary-spanning ties [47]. Mimetic influence minimizes the cost of finding viable solutions when organizations encounter similar issues [27].

Coercive influence is generated by the formal and informal pressures of external organizations, such as government and other powerful or ganizations upon which they are dependent [26, 45]. The cultural ex pectations of the environment within which the organization functions also play a role in generating coercive influence. The formal and informal pressures and cultural expectations can range from establishing rules, monitoring other organizations to identify whether they adhere to rules, and implementing rewards and punishments accordingly [44].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Applying neoinstitutional theory, we hypothesize that top manage ment beliefs regarding ISKS and their participation in it are directly related to the establishment of ISKS practices in a firm (see Fig. 1) and that these beliefs and participation are directly influenced by the normative, mimetic, and coercive influence of external institutional forces.

## 3.1. Institutional forces

The top management has often expressed a lack of commitment to ISKS, but has also shown that they can be motivated by institutional forces, such as government regulations [48]. According to Hu and Hart [27], institutional forces are more powerful than internal factors in influencing top managers with regard to InfoSec issues. The members of an industry, such as suppliers, customers, and other stakeholders, collectively evaluate and promote ISKS practices within their industry. The normative influence of the members of an industry through their social values, social norms, and professionalization have the ability to influence top management beliefs [30] or directly influence top man agement participation without affecting beliefs.

Our research model suggests that normative influence can be considered as an important determinant to the establishment of ISKS practices within organizations. A person’s beliefs are shaped by the subjective culture of their social networks and the interpersonal agree ments the person has developed with social networks in specific social situations [49]. For example, top managers’ decisions to implement InfoSec policies and procedures or their participation in ISKS are often persuaded by the normative influence of suppliers, customers, and other stakeholders [50]. This is mainly because the firm can learn about the benefits and costs associated with the InfoSec practices from its business partners [51]. When organizations share InfoSec practices through their organizational networks, those practices become stronger and better able to influence the beliefs or actual participation of the managers in them [51]. Since universities, companies, and professional associations, such as ISACA, highlight the importance of normative influence in the InfoSec industry. This strong sense of professionalism is a source of normative influence on managers’ beliefs about the importance of ISKS, and when ISKS is a socially accepted norm, the managers have a higher tendency to participate in it. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: The normative influence of institutional forces is positively associated with top management belief in ISKS.

H2: The normative influence of institutional forces is positively associated with top management participation in ISKS.

Mimetic influence occurs when organizations attempt to mimic the InfoSec policies and procedures of competitors without proper consid eration. Our research model suggests that memetic influence can be considered as the determinant for the establishment of ISKS practices. Organizations can mimic other organization’s security policies in two ways: following the security controls, which are successfully imple mented by the other organizations in the industry, or following the general practice of the industry [51]. With a compliance perspective, the managers may adopt ISKS in a manner aligned with industry norms [52]. The strength of mimetic influence can be highlighted by the fact that the organizations follow the security policies and procedures of leading companies with the intention to gain a reputation. Managers mimic other organizations to minimize search costs of security solutions and to reduce the risk by not being the first to invent security policies. When the organization operates in an environment with unpredictable security threats, managers may attempt to mimic other organizations InfoSec practices [53]. Moreover, since ISKS practices require innova tion, the organizations tend to mimic the practices of other organiza tions [54]. Formal benchmarking and the availability of security policies and procedures may contribute to these types of mimicking activities. Thus, we hypothesize:

Coercive influence occurs when formal and informal pressures are exerted on organizations by other organizations [26]. InfoSec rules, policies, and procedures established by the government and other powerful organizations [55] can be considered as potential sources of coercive influence. Pressures that arise from regulations may include legal requirements, health, and safety requirements, as well as contractual agreements with other organizations [56]. Since coercive influence may be felt directly or indirectly on top managers’ beliefs and participation in ISKS, it can be considered as a determinant for estab lishing ISKS practices within organizations. For example, regulatory agencies such as international organization for standardization (e.g., ISO) have a direct influence on top managers’ participation in ISKS, as they are expected to follow the rules established by the regulatory agencies (e.g., ISO270001 standard). Even without a direct impact, the ISKS strategies followed by dominant organizations in an industry can originate an indirect pressure on the top managers to believe and participate in ISKS [50]. For example, when the dominant organizations in an industry are equipped with integrated ISKS practices, it motivates top managers in other organizations to follow similar ISKS practices in order to achieve competitive advantages. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5: The coercive influence of institutional forces is positively associated with top management belief in ISKS.

H6: The coercive influence of institutional forces is positively associated with top management participation in ISKS.

## 3.2. The establishment of information security knowledge sharing (ISKS) practices

Top management beliefs guide their actions, decisions, and behav iors [31]. When top managers believe that ISKS is not necessary for the organization, they do not invest their time and energy to explore it. In contrast, when they believe that ISKS can provide benefits, they are likely to support and participate in it [30]. Our research model indicates that top managers’ beliefs and participation in ISKS are essential com ponents of the ISKS practices establishment process. Top managers understanding of IS security threats, the level of vulnerability of their organizations, and the ability of ISKS to minimize security threats in fluences how they participate in the planning and development of ISKS practices [57]. When senior managers believe that the ISKS has the potential to provide benefits to the organizations, they participate in articulating a vision and strategy for them. Thus, higher levels of belief among top managers in ISKS influence higher levels of commitment to formulate goals and standards to monitor ISKS practices [30]. We hypothesize:

H7: Top management belief in ISKS is positively associated with their participation in it.

As per our research model, there is a relationship between top management belief, participation, and the establishment of ISKS prac tices. Top management identifies and prioritizes their organizations strategic issues, which in turn receive organizational commitment and resources [48]. When top management believes that establishing ISKS practices is necessary for organizational success, they tend to identify and prioritize ISKS through policies, regulations, trainings, and aware ness programs. Since the implementation of information technologies to support ISKS depends on budget allocations, it is important that the top managers allocate sufficient budgets for ISKS practices. When top managers believe that ISKS creates significant competitive advantages for their organizations, they tend to encourage informal security consulting and adversary services within the organizations. Top man agers’ beliefs and participation in ISKS can signal to other managers and employees the importance of establishing InfoSec practices [58]. When the top managers formulate a strategy for the establishment of ISKS practices, it becomes easier to implement security-related programs within the organization. We therefore hypothesize:

H8: Top management belief in ISKS is positively associated with their firms’ establishment of ISKS practices.

H9: Top management participation in ISKS is positively associated with their firms’ establishment of ISKS practices.

## 3.3. ISKS outcomes

Organizations invest in ensuring the effective implementation of InfoSec policies, standards, and regulations to protect their information assets [59] and to make certain that their employees follow their orga nizations’ security rules and procedures [60–62]. Thus, security compliance can be considered as an output of ISKS establishments. We define InfoSec policy compliance as the extent to which employees adhere and recommend others to adhere to the InfoSec policies of their organizations [22]. Ensuring employee compliance toward InfoSec policies has been a major issue faced by organizations [63, 64]. When the employees do not comply with security policies, organizations cannot gain the intended results from their security solutions [65]. Or ganizations can conduct training and awareness programs to improve employee understanding of the InfoSec threats and the importance of complying with the security policies and procedures established by the organizations [66]. When employees are aware of the danger of InfoSec threats, they tend to comply with the InfoSec policies and procedures. Moreover, organizations can shape employees’ attitudes to comply with security policies by establishing technology platforms that support InfoSec knowledge sharing. Therefore. it is important that organizations establish security policies and procedures to support ISKS practices so that their security compliance can be strengthened [67]. We hypothesize:

H10: A firm’s establishment of ISKS practices is positively associated with its security compliance.

Cultivating an InfoSec-aware culture minimizes the security and privacy risks to information assets within organizations [68, 69]. A se curity culture, as an organizational sub-culture with a specific goal of InfoSec, requires an understanding and awareness of InfoSec issues and policies [70]. An InfoSec culture is a collection of implicit and explicit forces that shape employees’ security attitudes and behaviors over time, playing a critical role in the success of an organization’s InfoSec man agement function [70]. Organizations are mostly equipped with tech nical countermeasures and controls in place, but to minimize InfoSec risks, they must focus on creating and growing a security-aware culture that accounts for the diverse range of possible InfoSec threats [69, 71]. Organizations should provide employees with security awareness and training programs to ensure they are well equipped to follow InfoSec policy regulations [72, 73]. InfoSec protection should be a natural part of employees’ daily activities in the workplace; that is, InfoSec should be integrated into the corporate culture and employees’ InfoSec behaviors [74].

Conducting InfoSec awareness programs can be considered an initial step toward shaping security culture, and it is important that organi zations’ incorporate security behaviors into the routines of their em ployees, so that the employees can follow security practices on a daily basis [75]. This can be achieved by educating employees about the se curity policies and procedures of the organization as well as by ensuring that the employees accurately interpret and understand the policies [70]. An InfoSec culture emerges from the awareness emplovees have of what constitutes acceptable security behaviors. When employees are aware of the importance of InfoSec policies, they tend to promote good security and privacy practices within their organizations [76]. Imple menting technologies for creating an integrated platform to provide necessary InfoSec-related knowledge and assist InfoSec knowledge sharing leads to an organizational environment that supports security-minded thinking. We argue that establishing security awareness programs can help to form a security culture, where InfoSec is consid ered as an important organizational value. Thus, we hypothesize:

H11: A firm’s establishment of ISKS practices is positively associated with its security culture

## 4. Research design

To empirically test the research model presented in Figure 3, we conducted an independent field survey of professional managers from the USA. We used filtering questions to identify managers who are aware of the InfoSec procedures, policies, and regulations in their respective organizations, such as “Are you in charge or aware of InfoSec-related policies and procedures in your organization?” The survey was distrib uted via the Qualtrics platform and resulted in 834 complete responses.

To conduct this study, we developed the survey using previously validated measurement items. The measurement items for the three forms of neoinstitutional were influenced, and top management beliefs and participation in ISKS were adopted from Liang and Saraf [31]. For ISKS practices establishment, we adopted items from Flores and col leagues [2]. The six items measuring security culture were adopted from Chen and Ramamurthy [70], and for security compliance, we adopted items from Siponen and Pahnila [60]. A five-point Likert scale (strongly disagree, disagree, neither agree nor disagree, agree, and strongly agree) was used to measure all of these key constructs. All the constructs of the measurement model are first-order reflective constructs except ISKS establishment, which is treated as a formative second-order construct with two reflective first-order factors, formal knowledge sharing, and support for knowledge transfer. The construct definitions and mea surement items are presented in Tables S1 and S2 of the supplementary document, respectively.

## 4.1. Pilot study

Prior to conducting the main study of US professional managers, we conducted a pilot study involving 403 Australia and New Zealand pro fessional managers with responsibilities across a range of roles and company sizes. The goal of this pilot study is to not only test the survey mechanism, but also provide a preliminary empirical gage of the instrumentation used in this research.

The demographic description of the sample is provided in Table S3 of the supplementary document. We used partial least squares-structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) SmartPLS 3.0 software to evaluate the measurement model. PLS has been adopted as the most common approach in quantitative studies to evaluate the relationships between variables in human InfoSec behaviors [2, 73, 77] and is recommended for exploratory research [78] as well as for testing models that contain formative constructs [79]. Because this research is exploratory and uses a model with the formative construct (e.g., the establishment of ISKS practices), we believe PLS is a suitable tool for this analysis.

To reduce the potential for common method bias (CMB), we followed procedural guidelines established in the literature [80]. These CMB avoidance procedures included providing participants with definitions of all terms in the survey and using Likert scales with varied scale an chors. We also provided attention check questions and included reverse coded items that were validated in previous studies. We also conducted Lindell and Whitney’s [81] marker variable test, which tests the rela tionship of a theoretically unrelated construct with a dependent vari able. If the theoretically unrelated construct significantly increases the explained variance of the dependent variable, there is evidence of CME (see Table S4 of the supplementary document for the implemented procedural and statistical approaches). Using the brand image construct as a marker variable. we found the differences of variance between the marker variable and security compliance and culture to be marginal (0.2%). In addition, to test for CMB, we followed an approach proposed by Kock [82] to conduct a full collinearity assessment. When a variance inflation factor (VIF) reaches a value greater than 3.3, it is an indication of pathological collinearity and also an indication that the model may be contaminated by CMB [82]. We used the approach suggested by Petter and Straub [79] to assess the formative construct validity, which entails testing multicollinearity among the indicators of the formative construct. All of the multicollinearity VIF values were less than 3.3, with values ranging from 1.10 to the highest value of 2.95, thus inferring no cause for concern with respect to CMB. Overall, the results from these techniques support that CMB is not a significant issue for this study.

## 4.1.1. Measurement model assessment of pilot study

According to Hair and colleagues [83], the validity and reliability of the measurement model are tested through the evaluation of loadings or correlation weights, internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. In the first stage, we assessed the adapted mea surement items to provide a preliminary empirical gage of the instru mentation. Therefore, in the research model presented in this study, we assessed the normative and mimetic influence of institutional forces on top management belief in ISKS. We also tested the impact of the coercive influence of institutional forces and top management belief in ISKS on the establishment of ISKS practices. We then evaluated the influence of the establishment of ISKS practices on security culture and InfoSec policy compliance in organizations.

To check if a construct explains more than 50 percent of the item’s variance, the loading should be greater than 0.708. Therefore, noncontributing items need to be removed from the measurement model [83]. All the items reported a loading greater than 0.7 except ABSC1 and ABSC2 (see Table 1), which were subsequently removed from the measurement model. For internal consistency, the values of Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability (CR) should be between 0.7 and 0.95. The evaluation of these estimates indicated that all of the constructs were within acceptable thresholds. Convergent validity can be assessed through the evaluation of average variance extracted (AVE) values that should be above 0.5 for each composite [83]. The assessment of AVE values showed that all were above the cut-off value of 0.5, as shown in Table 1.

For a formative higher-order construct, the weights of the lowerorder constructs are especially important as they represent actionable drivers of the higher-order construct [84, 85]. The weights of the first-order constructs (formal knowledge sharing and support for knowledge transfer) on the second-order construct (ISKS practices) and their significance are presented in the supplementary document (Table S5).

The discriminant validity of the constructs was tested by evaluating the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) criterion [86]. For conceptually similar constructs, HTMT values greater than 0.9 suggest the lack of discriminant validity between the constructs. The HTMT value should be lower than the thresholds of 0.9 [87, 88]. For distinct constructs, HTMT values lower than 0.85 indicate discriminant validity, and HTMT yields specificity rates of 80% or higher in terms of inter-construct correlations as high as 0.95 [89]. In our study, the results show an acceptable level of discriminant validity for all the constructs based on the $\mathrm { H T M T _ { i n f e r e n c e } }$ and $\mathrm { H T M T } _ { 0 . 8 5 }$ criterion with the values lower than 0.85. The values of the HTMT criterion for the discriminant validity of the constructs are presented in Table S6, as a supplementary document. The results of the survey of Australia and New Zealand professional managers validate the measurement items of the research model.

## 5. Measurement model assessment

We also checked the validity and reliability of the measurement model. All the items reported a loading greater than 0.7 except TOPB3-R (as shown in Table 3). which was subsequently removed from the research model. For internal consistency, the values of Cronbach’s alpha and CR should be between 0.7 and 0.95. The evaluation of internal consistency indicated that all of the constructs were within acceptable thresholds. The assessment of convergent validity through AVE values showed that all were above the cut-off value of 0.5.

For a formative higher-order construct, the weights of the lowerorder constructs are especially important as they represent actionable drivers of the higher-order construct [84, 85]. The weights of the first-order constructs (formal knowledge sharing, support for knowledge transfer) on the second-order constructs (ISKS practices) and their sig nificance are examined (Table S7, as a supplementary document).

The discriminant validity of the constructs was tested by evaluating the HTMT criterion [86]. For conceptually similar constructs, HTMT values greater than 0.9 suggest the lack of discriminant validity between the constructs [87, 88]. $\mathrm { H T M T _ { i n f e r e n c e } }$ yields specificity rates of 80% or higher in terms of inter-construct correlations as high as 0.95. In general, $\mathrm { H T M T } _ { . 9 0 }$ and $\mathrm { H T M T _ { i n f e r e n c e } }$ approaches detect discriminant validity is sues reliability [89]. In our study, based on the HTMT and HTMT criterion, the results show an acceptable level of discriminant validity for each pair of constructs except for support for knowledge transfer and top management participation, which was 0.905 and vio lates the 0.9 threshold. Eventually, we dropped two items of support for the knowledge transfer construct (SUKT1 and SKUT5) to meet the HTMT criterion. The values of the HTMT criterion for the discriminant validity of the constructs are exhibited in Table S8, as a supplementary document.

## 5.1. Structural model assessment and hypothesis testing

While VIF values should not be greater than 5.0, values less than 3.0 are seen as an ideal [83]. ## 6. Post-Hoc analysis

We conducted multigroup analysis (MGA) on the role of professional managers to detect any significant differences in path coefficients of hypothesized relationships. We followed the approaches suggested by Henseler and Ringle [93] to test the variances in population parameters from the differences in path coefficients and t-values. The parametric test results indicate that there are significant differences in moderation impact of absorptive capacity on the relationships between ISKS estab lishment, security compliance, and security culture among CEO and non-CEO groups (Δβ = 0.107, 0.132, Δt-value = 1.99, 2.19, Δp = 0.049, 0.028, respectively). Findings reveal that CEOs have a greater appreci ation for ISKS since they have a broader view of the firm and its potential relevant strategic vulnerabilities. CEOs appreciate the organization’s prior knowledge of IS security mechanisms that supports the assimila tion of external knowledge, which in turn improves the positive impact of an integrated ISKS practices on organizational security culture and compliance. However, they may also be less inclined to participate in given actions or goals they may see as more pressing.

## 7. Discussion

With organizations taking a more techno-social approach to their InfoSec programs, the pressure to elevate their ISKS practices is greater than ever. For many organizations, however, the structures and pro cedures needed to effectively transform the knowledge available to them from institutional forces are limited or underdeveloped. Much of this underdevelopment may be associated with a lack of understanding of how institutional InfoSec knowledge resources influence organizations as well as the value placed on them by top management. Moreover, it is important to study the isomorphic institutional forces as most organi zations mimic other organization’s security policies and procedures rather than implementing the security policies and procedures based on critical analysis of their own organization’s goals and strategies. Espe cially, the organizations tend to follow other organizations’ security practices when facing dynamic security threats. Unfortunately, there is a lack of research that provides insights into this organizational phe nomenon, with the majority of InfoSec research focused on the indi vidual level. Little attention has been given to the issue of why organizations continue to struggle with developing ISKS practices that can help them improve both employee InfoSec policy compliance as well as their InfoSec culture.

Toward addressing this research gap, we apply neoinstitutional theory to develop a model to explain how institutional forces influence an organization’s establishment of ISKS practices. Tests of the model show that institutional forces inform the establishment of ISKS practices indirectly by their coercive, normative, and mimetic influence on top management’s ISKS beliefs and participation. Top management’s participation in ISKS is vital to their establishment. Our findings reveal that top management’s participation is most influenced by the memetic influence of external InfoSec knowledge resources. This suggests that the firms do not establish ISKS practices in a vacuum, but rather tend to simply mirror the practices of their peers.

Our study also suggests that the key to a well-formed collective se curity compliance efficacy is ISKS, wherein effective security responses provided by institutional sources are codified and distributed among workforce groups. Similarly, security cultures are reinforced through consistent successful patterns of outcomes. ISKS is paramount to this success.

Organizational-level per spectives, such as those provided in this study, are critically important to the InfoSec academic community's ability to expand and accumulate InfoSec knowledge, where organizational-level research has been described as an aspirational goal for behavioral InfoSec research [94]. Many previous studies have investigated the behavioral side of InfoSec with two main focuses: (1) to understand individuals' risky behaviors through protection-motivation, emotion-focused coping, neutralization techniques, and among others [e.g., 34, 49, 95,96,97,98,99]; (2) to evaluate which external, organizational, and managerial factors influ ence effective InfoSec management [e.g., 2, 9, 15, 100, 101]. While individual-level studies have increased the understanding of employee InfoSec compliance and misuse behaviors, they have not paid as much attention to investigating the effects of institutional forces on security outcomes.

Second, the impact of institutional forces on top managers’ beliefs and decisions is a relatively well-researched phenomenon in the litera ture (e.g. [17, 39],). However, there is a lack of research that explains how institutional forces influence their beliefs and decisions, especially when it comes to their thinking on the subject of security-related phe nomena. This study contributes to this space by explaining how insti tutional forces find their way into the organization and shape top management perspectives on the development of ISKS practices, and ultimately, enhance security compliance and security culture-related outcomes. Moreover, extant research has evaluated InfoSec best prac tice frameworks and their organizational mechanisms, such as organi zational processes and InfoSec awareness programs [e.g., 15, 33, 100]. These studies suggest that top management beliefs and commitment to InfoSec programs are essential for achieving effective InfoSec proced ures and driving organizational changes related to InfoSec programs in their organizations [27, 30, 73]. However, research which investigates the organizational structures and procedures for transferring externally sourced security-related knowledge to employees in organizations is scarce. Our study contributes toward addressing this scarcity by explaining the mediating role of top management beliefs in terms of how institutional forces are translated through coercive, normative, and mimetic influence into ISKS practices. This has tremendous implications to both academia and practice because (a) the current range of organi zational behavior models do not explicitly account for this mediating effect and (b) it is behooved upon organizational insiders to understand, and perhaps inventory, the institutional knowledge sources to which their top management subscribes. Further research designed to explore the transformational nature of institutional resources into organiza tional security outcomes and the strategies and mechanisms by which this transformation occurs is needed.

Third, from a theoretical perspective, this study contributes to the neoinstitutional theory by applying it in the context of ISKS establish ment. The neoinstitutional theory has been applied to explain how cultural processes and actors affect the institutional arrangements [37–39]; however, research which applies neoinstitutional theory in the context of ISKS is scarce. Future applications of neoinstitutional theory to the study of ISKS could extend this insight, perhaps focusing on the theory’s ability to explain the direct effect of institutional forces on the establishment of ISKS practices.

Finally, this study provides initial forays into some suggested future work presented in prior studies. For example, Flores and colleagues [2] suggested testing characteristics of a firm (e.g., size and industry type) which may yield differences in the theoretical models of ISKS estab lishment. We found that organization size plays a role in the establishment of ISKS practices, where larger organizations show a greater level of attention in establishing ISKS practices in their organizations than smaller organizations. Therefore, managers of SMEs should pay more attention to the establishment of ISKS practices to improve their em ployees’ compliance level and enhance the security culture in their or ganizations. Moreover, it has been suggested to investigate the outcomes of the establishment of ISKS practices with regards to employees behaviors/misbehaviors (e.g., compliance) in a firm [e.g., 2]. This study reveals that InfoSec compliance in organizations can be improved as a result of establishing ISKS practices through promoting the effective implementation of InfoSec standards and policies that are able to minimize employees’ risky behaviors in organizations.

## 9. Limitations

As is the case with all research, our study has its share of limitations. First, the study is limited by its sampling frame. While organizational managers across a range of roles and companies in the USA are appro priate for testing our research model, it’s important to note that the memetic, normative, and coercive pressures felt by these managers are most likely unique to their region and should not be assumed to map cleanly to other global regions. An extension of this sampling frame to include European and Asian managers, along with a multi-group anal ysis of the survey, results could help improve the generalizability of this study’s findings. Further, this study also suffers from the same limita tions as all cross-sectional surveys in that the results are time-sensitive and may over-inflate the influence of regional InfoSec events that were prevalent at the time of data collection. Data collection over multiple points in time could help to offset this limitation.

Also, our measure of coercive influence, adopted from Liang, Saraf [17] and Barton, Tejay [30], is limited in its ability to provide explicit forms of government (e.g. federal, state, or municipal governments), industry, or competitive conditions that could provide coercive pressure on organizational managers as they consider ISKS practices in their firm. Future research would benefit from a more granular inspection of co ercive sources of influence in this context. Finally, we used several techniques to minimize the occurrence of CMB. However, future research may benefit from capturing dependent and independent vari ables at different points in time in order to better rule out CMB through the research design.

## 10. Conclusion

Given the evolving critical importance of establishing ISKS practices to an organization’s InfoSec outcomes, organizations’ ability to harness the InfoSec knowledge and expertise available to them from outside sources is critical. Yet, how these resources are tapped into and influence ISKS practices is not well understood. Our study provides some needed insight into this important question and suggests that these institutional resources do find their way into the organization by normative, mimetic, and coercive means, but much of their influence on the establishment of ISKS practices is mediated by the ISKS beliefs and participation of top management. Based on our findings, it also appears as though an orga nization’s security compliance is dependent on the organization’s ca pacity to absorb external security knowledge resources into their InfoSec practices.
