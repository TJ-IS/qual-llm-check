# The influences of three task characteristics on innovative use of malleable IT: An extension of adaptive structuration theory for individuals

## A B S T R A C T

Malleable information technology (IT) stands for contemporary mobile computing technologies, which are fluid and flexible and provide much room for users to engage in innovative use, We draw upon adaptive structuration theory for individuals (ASTI) to examine the influences of three task characteristics, namely two task environ ment factors of transformational leadership and innovation climate and the task process factor of work autonomy, on employees’ innovative use of malleable IT. We collected survey data from 315 frontline employees of 20 different companies across 5 industries in China that had implemented a mobile computing system for more than one year. The results indicate that transformational leadership exerts a positive influence on innovative use of malleable IT. and innovation climate mediates this positive influence. Work autonomy also has a positive influence on innovative use. A multiple group analysis based on gender further reveals that the two task envi ronment factors of transformational leadership and innovation climate both exert stronger influences on female employees, while work autonomy, as a task process factor, has a stronger influence on male employees. Our study provides an extended interpretation of ASTI in the use context of malleable IT and offers practical im plications for managers who seek to promote innovative use of malleable IT.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, organizations have struggled to survive and sustain themselves in the global market’s new wave of cross-boundary competition while also learning to embrace the trend of digital trans formation [1]. As a result, organizations are expected to continue making huge investments in digital technologies in the coming years [67]. Total digital technology spending is expected to increase by 13% from 2016 to 2020, reaching USD \$2.4 trillion in 2020 [114]. Malleable information technology (IT) refers to the new generation of mobile computing technologies that feature interactivity, openness, and reprogrammability [49, 86]. Due to these powerful and flexible char acteristics, malleable IT can potentially empower users who do not have the related professional technical knowledge and skills to realize inno vation during usage processes, which can ultimately help organizations unleash the embedded business value of malleable IT [45, 60, 91, 97, 110]. Nevertheless, how to stimulate employee creativity in general and motivate innovative use of malleable IT in particular are enduring challenges facing managers.

Innovative use stands for novel ways of using technology features and functionalities by users to support task performance [5, 56]. Infor mation systems (IS) scholars have approached the phenomenon of innovative use through different theoretical perspectives. One school of thought emphasizes the deterministic role of technology functionalities or the timing of technology implementation in enabling users’ innova tive use. For instance, compared with the implementation stage, the post-implementation stage, which entails user inertia and sophistication toward system use, allows for more unexpected extensions or novelties (Liang et al., 2007; [45, 84, 85, 95]). Another branch of study focuses on the significance of users in realizing innovation during usage processes. Specifically, users who are innately motivated [56, 90] or possess enough technology-related knowledge and skills [86, 110] are likely to exhibit innovation in use. A similar view underscores the importance of interactions among users in initiating innovation during usage pro cesses, such as the leadership style of users’ immediate supervisors [97] or the level of tolerance for change and innovation among teams or departments [57, 64].

We identify several knowledge gaps in the extant literature regarding innovative use. First, most prior studies examined innovative use of the previous generation of technologies, such as enterprise IS for the optimization of business processes or intelligence tools for supporting business decisions. Understanding user innovation with the new gen eration of malleable IT, which is editable, pliable, and transfigurable [49, 86], is yet to be advanced in the IS field. Second, prior research adopted the theoretical stances of either technology determinism or user constructivism in understanding innovative use. The influence of task factors on innovative use in the organizational context is yet to be captured and examined. Adaptive structuration theory for individuals (ASTI), which evolved from adaptive structuration theory (AST) [30, 77], posits that three categories of factors influence user adaptation when using technologies within organizations: technology, individuals, and task characteristics [86]. ASTI further distinguishes between task environment factors and task process factors [86]. To the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have so far investigated the influence of task characteristics on innovative use, which considers task either as a contingency [5] or as a structural factor [45] arising from the general work environment.

Toward this end, our study aims to investigate users’ innovative use of malleable IT within organizations and, at the same time, extend the theoretical framework of ASTI. We expect to contribute to ASTI in the following ways. First, we identify and conceptualize three representa tive task characteristics, including transformational leadership, inno vation climate, and work autonomy. Following ASTI, we classify transformational leadership and innovation climate as task environment factors, while work autonomy can be classified as a task process factor. On the one hand, transformational leadership and innovation climate have been considered as belonging to the user or group system category in AST [77, 86]. We argue that, as the level of theory shifts from the group level in AST to the individual level in ASTI, the influential factors rooted in user interactions need theoretical repositioning and reinter pretation. On the other hand, work autonomy represents the most salient factor during task performance processes and is thus a task process factor. Second, we provide a nuanced theory on the differential impacts of the three task characteristics on innovative use of malleable IT. We not only hypothesize the mediation effect of innovation climate on the relationship between transformational leadership and innovative use of malleable IT but also theorize the relative influences of the two task environment factors versus the process factor in influencing inno vative use based on gender.

The structure of the paper proceeds as follows. We first review the prior literature on innovative use of technologies and conceptualize innovative use of malleable IT as it is to be investigated in our study. We also elaborate on ASTI and identify three task characteristics that require further examination. We then propose the research model and hypotheses, followed by the data collection and analysis procedures and, finally, discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

## 2. Innovative use of malleable IT

## 2.1. Prior literature on innovative use

Innovation occurs when organizations and individuals generate novel and useful ideas with the purpose of challenging the status quo [3, 68]. Innovative use of technology refers to users’ novel ways of using technology to support task performance [5, 56]. Employees as well as organizations effectively realize the potential business value embedded in implemented technologies through innovative use [25, 41, 110]. Two points need further clarification regarding the prior literature on inno vative use. First, since individuals refer to their own cognitive schema to make sense of their behaviors (Weick et al., 2005), whether a particular usage pattern is innovative or not is in the eye of the beholder [56]. The same usage pattern can be non-innovative to one user but innovative to another simply because the used functionalities are known to the former user but unknown to the latter. Thus, innovative use of technology in: volves not only the generation of new ideas by users when employing technology (e.g., [5, 85]) but also their exploration of the technology’s functionalities that were unknown to them before (e.g., [71]). Second, the role of task element in conceptualizing innovative use is ambiguous. Previous studies either conceptualize innovative use by integrating innovation requirements into tasks [15, 85] or assume task character istics to be stable in both theory development and empirical examination [56, 86, 93].

IS scholars approach innovative use mainly through two alternative theoretical lenses. One school of thought posits that user innovation with technology takes place depending on a particular timing during the technology implementation processes. For example, users of enterprise systems, such as enterprise resource planning systems or customer relationship management systems, tend to exhibit innovative use during the post-implementation stage ([5, 45, 57]; Shao, 2017a; [91]). In contrast, users of intelligence tools are likely to display innovative use in unexpected scenarios during the initial phase of implementation [71, 90]. In general, business intelligence tools, built with various flexible features, possibly provide more potential for innovation, compared with enterprise IS that are oriented toward operational efficiency and stan dardization [51]. The other school suggests that innovative use may be attributable to factors arising from individual users. Researchers have found that users’ motivational states [56, 90], innovation tendencies toward IT [23, 90, 110], efficacious perceptions relating to technology use [86, 110], and technical knowledge and skills [56, 101, 110] all possibly influence innovative use of technology within organizations. In addition, innovative use may also be subject to influences from in teractions among users. The fundamental logic is rooted in the ante cedent of social influence in the technology acceptance literature [107]. In particular, users’ perceptions of their immediate supervisors’ lead ership behaviors [97, 115], as well as their team or department’s climate for promoting creativity and innovation [57, 64], influence innovative usage behaviors within organizations. To summarize, most studies consider either technology or user as the key factor constituting user adaptation behaviors in using technology. Very few studies have ever responded to the call to understand the influence of task characteristics on users’ innovative usage behaviors (e.g., [5, 45]).

## 2.2. Conceptualizing innovative use of malleable IT

As we have noted, malleable IT refers to the new generation of mo bile computing technologies, which are open and transfigurable to various usage scenarios [86] and provide more opportunities for inno vation when in use, as compared with the previous generation of tech nologies, such as enterprise IS and intelligence tools. We follow the prior literature on innovative use and define innovative use of malleable IT as users’ application of mobile computing technologies in novel ways to support task performance. We specify that the frame of reference for innovative use of malleable IT is specific to each user and assume that their task requirements remain relatively stable within a given time period [56].

Consider the example of field engineers from a utility company travelling to different local customer sites to fix or perform regular maintenance services on a vast amount of equipment. The engineers need different tools that vary in weight, volume, and value to execute such planned or emergent visits. The utility company needs to coordi nate and deliver the corresponding tools ahead of each visit so that the field engineers can start working upon arrival on site. The coordination and scheduling of field engineers, as well as their support tools for both planned and emergent visits, are time- and effort consuming. The utility company then implements a large-scale mobile computing system to enable better customer services of their frontline personnel, that is, to facilitate the flexibility and efficiency of the field engineers’ on-site work. Each field engineer can choose to have a peripheral mobile de vice that allows for inputs, edits, and information queries about cus tomers, equipment, and tools, as well as scheduling and routing, or can install a mobile application with these functionalities on their own smartphones. The mobile system is malleable in nature because it enables instant access to cloud-based information storage by engineers and has pliable functionalities, such as text, picture, and video editing and routing.

Field engineers may engage in, but are not limited to, the following novel ways of using the malleable mobile system. For example, Dave, a field engineer, detected a new issue with an equipment that was beyond his expertise during a regular maintenance appointment. Before the mobile system was in use, Dave would either ask the customer to schedule a new appointment with another specialist, which complicated the tedious repairing process, or call upon a few colleagues he knew for instant advice, most of whom refused to answer due to their limited experience or unavailability. Dave performed the following actions through the use of the mobile system. First, Dave downloaded the comprehensive instruction manuals from the mobile system, which were not available at the local site, and quickly searched a few keywords in the manuals for further information. He then took pictures and videos, edited them with highlights and simple explanations with add-on edit ing features downloaded from the cloud, and uploaded them to the mobile system with an instant open call for help among co-located colleagues. Dave’s nonconventional use of the mobile system improved communication effectiveness and information sharing among the colleagues. Andy, one of Dave’s colleagues, encountered a similar situation and used the mobile system in alternative ways. Andy searched through the cloud-archived historical task records and identified and browsed the documented solutions for similar task problems. He also explored the mobile system and downloaded new add-on editing fea tures from the cloud, with which he sketched questions and solutions. Once he resolved the problem, Andy used the new add-on editing fea tures to create a new document, outlining this unexpected issue along with the procedures and solutions that he implemented and uploaded this document to the mobile system.

Similar examples of innovative use of malleable IT are observable in other industries, for example, the financial industry. Consider that a banking corporation has installed a similar version of a mobile computing system as the one implemented in the utility company mentioned above, to facilitate customer services for frontline em ployees. Sam, a financial advisor, was to meet a few potential customers with particular borrowing needs for starting a small business. Before the mobile computing system was in use, Sam would contact a few senior colleagues (usually via e-mail or telephone) to see if they had handled similar cases and had related experiences to share. More often than not he would receive merely a training handbook. This time, Sam turned to the implemented mobile computing system and performed a few actions to help with his situation: (1) he searched through the content relating to customer scheduling and management on the mobile system to deter mine whether any informal discussions of similar cases were docu mented, (2) he initiated an open call to colleagues through the contact lists available on the mobile system for instant solutions and advice, (3) he downloaded a few editing add-ons from the mobile system to take notes of key ideas and draft solution details, and (4) after finishing his meetings with customers, he created two different posts on the social media platform of the mobile system, synthesizing and sharing the ex periences he gained for handling these special customer groups. To summarize, these real-life examples across different industrial contexts demonstrate what innovative ways of using mobile technologies and applications are and how such nonstandard ways of use improve communication and task performance among frontline service personnel.

Given our research focus on what and how task characteristics contribute to users’ innovative behaviors in using malleable IT, we proceed to refer to ASTI and identify three task characteristics that potentially exert significant influences on innovative use of malleable IT.

## 3. Adaptive structuration theory for individuals and three task characteristics

AST, which originated from structuration theory, delineates the deep structures interwoven between technologies and users [30, 77]. Struc turation processes integrate different sources of structures with social interactions and ultimately archive mutual adaptation among different structures. Through such structuration processes, technologies deliver process outcomes, gain new meanings, and even generate emergent structural resources that are not only transferable beyond the physical constraints imposed by time and space but also perpetuate through future usage processes [72, 86]. AST has mainly served to explain technology usage behaviors at the group level, such as in the context of group decision support systems (GDSS). According to AST, the factors that influence the structuration processes associated with technology use fall into three categories: the structure of technologies, group in ternal systems, and the structure of the task and the task environment [30, 31].

ASTI extends AST from the group level to the individual level and tailors it to the context of malleable IT [86]. Similar to AST, ASTI also identifies three categories of factors that potentially influence individual adaptation behaviors in technology use, namely technology, task, and individual characteristics. Specifically, technology characteristics entail both functional features and symbolic spirits of technology [65]. Indi vidual characteristics encompass individual personalities, abilities, knowledge, and experiences [86]. Task characteristics include struc turation sources arising from task processes as well as the task envi ronment where technology use takes place [86].<sup>1</sup> Notably, AST has positioned constructs such as leadership styles and group climates in the group internal system category, while ASTI includes these factors in the category of task characteristics. Indeed, when the level of theory de scends along the organizational hierarchy from the group level in AST to the individual level in ASTI, factors that concern user interactions warrant theoretical reinterpretation.

From the viewpoint of individual users, the perceived leadership styles of immediate supervisors can be specific to the ability of the employees to perform and complete particular tasks [32, 36, 63]; the collective climate is also more situation specific than the general work environment and potentially interferes with the task design and affects employees’ task perceptions and performances [22, 37, 58]. Therefore, leadership styles and collective climate are suitably considered as task environment characteristics in the task category. Consistent across the AST and ASTI frameworks, work autonomy, denoting employees’ levels of discretion over how particular tasks are scheduled, coordinated, and completed, has been recognized as a salient task process factor in the task category. As such, our study particularly focuses on how employees varying perceptions toward task characteristics, including two task environment factors of leadership styles and collective climate, together with the task process factor of work autonomy affect their innovative use of malleable IT. Meanwhile, our study assumes that the physical task content (e.g., level of task complexity, task varieties, task interdepen dence, etc.) remains relatively stable within a period of time [56]. Overall, a supportive, tolerant, and autonomous task structure em powers employees to participate in creative performance in general [99, 103] and motivates them to engage in innovation when using technol ogies to support work performance [16, 73]. In addition, insights into gender stereotypes from organizational science help us further interpret the differential influences of the three identified task characteristics on innovative use of malleable IT within organizations. Specifically, male employees tend to prefer freedom and independence at work, whereas female employees are inclined to seek connections in their surrounding task environment, such as connections with leaders and co-workers [52, 113]. Based on the above literature, we build our research model and propose five research hypotheses regarding the influences of the three task characteristics on innovative use of malleable IT within organiza tions, namely transformational leadership, innovation climate, and work autonomy.

## 4. Research model and hypotheses

According to the ASTI [86], task characteristics that potentially in fluence user innovation and adaptation behaviors during technology use involve both task environment and process factors. We identify trans formational leadership and innovation climate as two important task environment factors [57, 64, 95], and work autonomy as the most salient task process factor [5, 57] that influences innovative use of malleable IT. We further propose the differential influence of the envi ronment factors in relation to the process factor based on gender ste reotypes within organizations [52, 113]. We also control for individual characteristics (i.e., IT knowledge and personal innovativeness with IT), technology characteristics (i.e., perceived usefulness), and user de mographic characteristics (i.e., age, education, and work experience) [7, 86, 92, 96]. ## 4.1. Two task environment factors: transformational leadership and innovation climate

Transformational leadership has been identified as one of the most significant antecedents of employee creativity within organizations [33, 46, 48]. Transformational leaders usually possess charismatic person alities, challenge and inspire followers with creative thinking, provide followers with emotional support and personalized care, and encourage followers to seek novel solutions in face of difficulties or problems at work [8–10]. Researchers have extensively demonstrated the influence of transformational leadership on employee creativity in organizations. Specifically, transformational leaders tend to be creative and risk-tolerant, serving as exemplary role models for followers [8–10]. When followers perceive that their direct leaders favor a diversity of ideas and options and are tolerant of experimental failures, they are more likely to exhibit innovative behaviors within organizations [36, 43].

As we have mentioned, malleable IT is characterized by flexibility, interactivity, and reprogrammability, which offer much room for po tential user innovation [49, 86]. With the encouragement of trans formational leaders, users are motivated to adopt innovative ways of thinking in the face of unusual problems and challenges [4, 79], and explore or even innovate with malleable IT [95]. Meanwhile, trans formational leaders tend to inspire users by articulating organizational values and missions with a strong emphasis on the potential value embedded in malleable IT. Transformational leaders also offer personal coaching and encourage users to strive for innovation in technology usage that is in alignment with organizational strategies. As such, users would be innately motivated and enthusiastic about exploring new ways of using malleable IT to realize its potential business value [95]. These arguments lead to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Transformational leadership is positively associated with innovative use of malleable IT.

Organizational climate refers to a contextual situation that shapes employees’ thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. In contrast to organiza tional culture, which is complex and collectively held, climate is sub jective, temporal, and subject to direct manipulation through social power and influence [29]. We define innovation climate as the extent to which employees perceive their teams or departments to be open to innovation and creativity and have a relatively high tolerance for risk-taking behaviors [57, 87]. The close relationship between trans formational leadership and innovation climate has been thoroughly discussed and verified in the previous literature [44, 47].

We propose that a similar relationship between transformational leadership and innovation climate also exists in the context of malleable IT. Specifically, transformational leaders can define and shape how users perceive and understand work contexts, for example, challenging task requirements, setting superordinate goals, and calling for novel solu tions, all of which cultivate an innovation climate among users [47]. Transformational leaders also tend to emphasize the importance of learning in collaboration and job performance [95], so that users are encouraged to explore and experiment with the malleable technologies and become willing to exchange knowledge with others. As such, users are likely to perceive a learning environment that favors novel ideas and creativity, which subsequently fosters an innovation climate. In addi tion, transformational leaders, through charismatic behaviors and vision articulation, often maintain a system of rewards and values, which helps direct users’ attention, efforts, and shared perceptions toward innova tion [42, 44]. These arguments project the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Transformational leadership is positively associated with innovation climate.

Researchers have found innovation climate to be a salient factor affecting employees’ innovative behaviors [12]. On one hand, innova tion climate positively reinforces employees’ perceptions that their teams or departments encourage critical thinking and risk-taking be haviors [4, 87]. On the other hand, innovation climate facilitates inno vative behaviors by protection from bureaucratic constraints and providing supportive resources [6]. As such, employees are motivated to explore new ideas and put their novel ideas into practice. When teams or departments are afforded with an innovation climate, colleagues are willing to support each other and share their skills and knowledge regarding technology use [13]. Openness to diverse opinions also en ables employees to generate new ideas during their adaptation to using technology and helps them examine features and functionalities from new angles, which cultivates innovative behaviors in using technology [94, 95].

In the context of malleable IT, an innovation climate within teams or departments legitimizes and reinforces users’ exploration of the imple mented technologies [18]. Conversely, if teams or departments do not advocate for an innovation climate, users tend to feel that the work environment does not support their innovative use of the malleable IT’s features and functionalities, and that colleagues do not appreciate their novel ideas regarding the use of technology [44]. In this situation, users tend to feel discouraged and uncomfortable about executing exploration using malleable IT due to the potential conflict between innovative behaviors and a risk-avoidant work environment [5, 87]. Based on the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: Innovation climate is positively associated with inno vative use of malleable IT.

## 4.2. The task process factor of work autonomy

Work autonomy refers to the extent to which tasks provide em ployees with freedom, independence, and discretion regarding sched ules and procedures throughout the execution processes [39]. Work autonomy represents a significant task-related factor in understanding employees’ work motivation, commitment, and creativity within orga nizations [14, 104, 111]. Work autonomy aligns with employees self-determination and supports their control over task schedules and procedures [28, 74]. When performing tasks that allow for a high level of autonomy, employees are likely to engage in creative thinking as well as trial-and-error experimentation [2, 105]. Work autonomy also pro vides employees with opportunities to strive for nonroutine perfor mance and yields optimal solutions during task performance [89]. In particular, when employees display a high need for personal develop ment, work autonomy fosters creative performance within organizations [24].

Work autonomy also plays a significant role in affecting user inno vation in using malleable IT. A number of studies have found that work autonomy can effectively nurture innovative use of technology (e.g., [5, 57]). Finding new ways of using features and functionalities to support work performance usually requires users to devote time and effort in exploration and experimentation [81]. Correspondingly, work auton omy provides users with the freedom and authority to decide which task domains should be represented by malleable IT [16], as well as the opportunity for self-discretion and determination in trying out new al ternatives using malleable IT [57]. In contrast, a low level of work au tonomy tends to impede users from adapting features and functionalities of malleable IT in their daily work $[ 1 6 , 5 7 ]$ . The above argument leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4: Work autonomy is positively associated with innova tive use of malleable IT.

## 4.3. Boundary conditions set by gender

Studies in organizational science propose that gender stereotypes set important boundary conditions for the interpretation of how task characteristics influence emplovee attitudes and behaviors within organizations. Gender stereotypes provide effective guidance for em ployees to follow when reacting to new, ambiguous, or complex situations [17]. Previous organizational studies report that gender ste reotypes correlate with task-related attitudes and preferences [52, 113]. Specifically, male employees tend to prefer autonomy and dominance, such that they tend to seek freedom, independence, and power among their peers in their organizations [113]. In contrast, female employees favor affiliation and camaraderie and therefore are inclined to devote resources to forming friendships and soliciting emotional bonding with co-workers and supervisors within their organizations [52]. IS scholars have found empirical evidence that is consistent with such gender ste reotypes in the context of technology use $( \mathrm { e } . \mathrm { g } . , [ 5 , 6 4 , 6 9 , 1 0 6 - 1 0 9 ] )$ The association between performance expectancy and technology use intention is stronger for men, while the relationship between social in fluence and use intention is stronger for women [107].

In the context of malleable $\Gamma \Gamma ,$ male and female users also display differential preferences over the three representative task characteris tics: transformational leadership, innovation climate, and work auton omy. Compared with female users, male users tend to be more assertive, independent, and in favor of autonomy and achievement [52, 61]. When provided with a similar level of discretion and freedom in task-related schedules and procedures, male users are more likely to engage in exploration and experimentation using malleable IT [5]. Compared with male users, female users tend to exhibit a more communal orientation in their social activities and place more emphasis on personal relationships [61, 106]. As such, female users are more open to sharing knowledge and experience with colleagues [53, 61, 100]. In the face of a trans formational leader and an innovative climate, female users are more sensitive to the social and emotional support for innovation and are thus more likely to engage in innovation and risk taking using malleable IT [64]. The above rationale leads to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 5: Gender moderates the influence of transformational leadership, innovation climate, and work autonomy on innovative use of malleable IT.

Hypothesis 5a: The influence of transformational leadership on innovative use is stronger for women than for men.

Hypothesis 5b: The influence of innovation climate on innovative use is stronger for women than for men.

Hypothesis 5c: The influence of work autonomy on innovative use is stronger for men than for women.

## 5. Research methodology

## 5.1. Data collection

We contacted a third-party IT service provider and selected 20 of their 35 client companies for survey administration in China. The 20 selected companies were all privately owned enterprises, encompassing industries including retail (6 organizations), finance (5 organizations), manufacturing (4 organizations), real estate (3 organizations), and healthcare (2 organizations). All selected companies were similar in size in terms of employee numbers (i.e., with an average number of 745, a minimum of ${ \bf 5 2 6 } ,$ and a maximum of 987) and annual revenue $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ with an average revenue of 335.43 million RMB, a minimum revenue of 116.78 million RMB, and a maximum revenue of 481.21 million RMB) The selected companies all purchased and implemented a mobile computing system from the third-party firm for more than one year. The target mobile computing system (hereafter, the mobile system) con tained three major modules: customer management, task management, and enterprise social media. Specifically, customer management archived customer portfolios as well as transaction details for the pur pose of customer loyalty management. Task management documented information on task requirements, routine and emergent schedules, execution details, and post-service follow-ups, as well as quite a few editing tools downloadable from the cloud. Enterprise social media served as a platform for work- and non-work-related discussions and information exchanges among colleagues. The mobile system aims to facilitate the mobility and flexibility of frontline service employees during customer service processes and can accommodate customer and task information content and formats across quite a few industries. Note that the mobile system could be adaptable across different industries because it supported customer services delivered by frontline service personnel, whose task variety, complexity, and interdependence were more or less the same across different industries and were usually un changed within a certain period of time. The only variance with task characteristics existed in the concrete perceptive factors such as super visory leadership, climate, and level of autonomy. Similar to the ex amples we introduced in the theory section, the malleable mobile system is pliable given its cloud-based information storage and access, and is reprogrammable with versatile editing and routing functionalities.

We conducted informal interviews with the top managers from the 20 selected companies prior to the formal data collection and summa rized three main points as follows. First, most of the interviewed top managers indicated that the mobile system was usually purchased as a comprehensive package with adaptable parameters and modules based on employees’ task assignments and contents. Thus, the organization wide implementation of the mobile system required a minimum amount of contextualization efforts. Second, the top managers shared that frontline service employees as end users can either install a mobile application on their personal smartphones or use on-site tablets installed with the mobile application, which could be easily configured with their work processes. The top managers also confirmed that the mobile system was pliable, flexible, and easy to use for frontline service employees, thereby providing them with numerous opportunities to use the mobile system in novel ways to handle business activities and problems. Third, almost all top managers indicated during the interviews that innovative use of the mobile system by frontline employees was usually difficult to observe and assess by a third party. In particular, they mentioned that employees’ innovative behaviors in using the mobile system were highly subject to the influences of their supervisors, who coached them, instilled organizational and personal values in them, and influenced their learning and performance processes.

Our target sample comprises frontline service employees who used the malleable mobile system provided by the third-party firm on a daily basis. Although mobile computing devices and applications are more adaptable and easier to use compared with traditional enterprise IS [86], we still ensured that our participants used the mobile system for more than one year, so as to ensure adequate assimilation of the target malleable IT in their work processes [76, 95, 97]. Our research team randomly selected 381 frontline employees from the full employee lists of the 20 contacted companies. The research team then used university email accounts to directly send the questionnaire link to the official email addresses of the selected employees. As such, the top management did not have direct access to the information of the selected employees, although they helped introduce our proiect briefly before data collection and reminded the emplovees in general to complete the questionnaires throughout the data collection process. We informed the participants in the email that (a) their participation in our survey was voluntary and they could withdraw at any time during the process and (b) their per: sonal information, such as gender, age, education, and work experience, would be kept confidential and would not be accessed by the top man agement or their leaders. We did not ask participants for their specific position in their organizations, since all invited participants were frontline employees. All the above procedures implemented in the sur vey administration process helped mitigate social desirability bias [38, 54]. The online survey instrument was composed of two sections. In the first section, we asked the participants to provide their demographic information. In the second section, we asked the participants questions regarding (a) leadership behaviors of their direct supervisors, perceived innovation climate, and degree of autonomy within their teams and (b) technology-related knowledge, tendency for innovation with technology in general, and innovative use of malleable IT. The employees were invited to complete the questionnaires at their convenience within two weeks. We provided each participant with a reward of RMB࿥5 upon completion of the online questionnaire.

We sent online questionnaires to the 381 selected employees and received 352 responses. We screened out the problematic question naires, which included redundant IP addresses, the same answer to all questions, and an archived response time of less than 1.5 min [26]. Ultimately, we retained 315 valid responses, with a response rate of 82.68%. Table 1 shows the sample demographics, most of which were young individuals with a bachelor’s degree or higher. This is consistent with the demographic characteristics of the frontline workforce across different industries as well as those of digital natives in the millennium age. We conducted two types of variance analysis (ANOVA) tests to check for nonresponse bias [82]. First, we performed ANOVA tests with the four demographic variables in Table 1 including age (F(1379) = 0.144, p = 0.705), gender (F(1379) = 1.221, p = 0.270), education level (F(1379) =1.842, p = 0.175), and work experience (F(1379) = 2.228, p = 0.136) between the 315 valid responses and the 66 missing responses, which were accessible from the full employee lists, and found no sig nificant differences. Second, we performed ANOVA tests on the seven latent variables listed in Table 2, namely transformational leadership (F (1, 98) = 0.068, p = 0.795), innovation climate (F(1, 98) = 1.066, p = 0.304), work autonomy (F(1, 98) = 2.454, p = 0.120), innovative use of malleable IT (F(1, 98) = 1.326, p = 0.252), IT knowledge (F(1, 98) = 0.348, p = 0.557), personal innovativeness of IT (F(1, 98) = 1.415, p = 0.237), and perceived usefulness (F(1, 98) = 0.109, p = 0.742) between the first 50 and the last 50 valid responses according to the data collection timeline and identified no statistical differences.

## 5.2. Survey instrument

We measured all constructs using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” and developed the survey instrument based on the extant literature. In particular, transformational leadership was operationalized as a second-order reflective construct and the measures for the five dimensions were adapted from Bass & Avolio’s [11] Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire. Innovation climate was operationalized by following the studies of Bock et al. (2015) [13] and Jaiswal & Dhar [44]. Work autonomy and innovative use of malleable IT were measured according to the method described by Ahuja and Thatcher [5] and Li et al. [56], respectively. Note that we modified the last item from Li et al. [56] due to different technological characteristics between malleable IT and traditional enterprise IS. We also controlled for IT knowledge [88], personal innovativeness with IT [7], perceived usefulness [27], and demographic information of users, including gender, age, education, and work experience.

We applied the back-translation technique to translate the instru ment from English to Chinese [19]. Specifically, a senior IS professor translated the instrument from English into Chinese initially, then another senior IS professor translated the Chinese version back into English: the Chinese-version instrument was then refined based on careful comparison and examination between the two versions of the English instrument. We conducted a pre-test to examine the content validity of the Chinese measurement scales. Another two IS professors and three MBA students from the Internet industry were invited to examine whether there was any ambiguity or inaccuracy in the trans lated measures. Prior to formal data collection, we performed a pilot study to examine the construct validity of the measures. A total of 60 out of the 80 invited respondents from another client company (not included in the 20 selected companies for formal data collection) completed the questionnaires in the pilot study. Psychometric analysis in SmartPLS 3.0 [40] suggested acceptable psychometric properties of all constructs in our research model. We refined the wording of the survey instrument based on the analysis results and informal interviews with the pilot sample. The informal interviews also confirmed with the pilot sample that our modification of the last item on innovative use was appropriate when we adapted this item from the traditional enterprise system context to the malleable IT context. ## 5.3. Structural equation modelling analysis

We employed the partial least square (PLS) method, a componentbased structural equation modeling technique, for data analysis. PLS is suitable for theory development purposes, especially when second-order constructs are included in the research model and hypotheses [34, 75]. The final sample of 315 responses well surpasses the sample requirement of PLS – ten times the total number of items in the construct with the most measures (i.e., 15 items in transformational leadership) or the total number of constructs affecting the same dependent variable (i.e., 10 independent variables including the controlled ones) [21]. We used SmartPLS 3.0 [40] and performed the data analysis in three steps: the measurement model, the structural model, and multiple group analysis.

## 5.3.1. The measurement model

We assessed construct reliability and validity with reference to Cronbach’s alpha, factor loadings, and the average variance extracted (AVE) of the constructs. As noted in Table 3, the Cronbach’s alpha of all constructs exceeded 0.75, and the item loadings of each construct were greater than 0.7. In addition, the AVE of each construct was larger than 0.7, which is well above the threshold of 0.5. The outcome of the analysis demonstrated good reliability and validity of the measurement model [21].

We evaluated construct discriminant validity according to the cri terion that the square root of the AVE for each construct was higher than the construct’s correlation with other constructs (Chin et al., 2003). As shown in Table 4, the square root of the AVE for each construct in our research model was higher than that construct’s correlation with the rest of the constructs. As such, we proved adequate discriminant validity with regard to the measurement model [21].

## 5.3.4. Common method bias tests

We acknowledge that a cross-sectional survey method and self reported data are possibly subject to the influences of the consistency motif and social desirability, and our findings could be vulnerable to common method bias [78]. Thus, we performed two statistical tests to rule out potential alternative explanations from the common method bias. First, we conducted the Harman’s one-factor (or single-factor) test to examine whether a substantial amount of common method variance was present in our data. The results of an exploratory factor analysis in SPSS 24.0 showed that there were eight factors with eigenvalues above 1, and the greatest variance explained by a single factor was 22.57%. Second, we used the PLS marker variable method by Ronkk¨ o ¨ and Yitalo [83] to further diagnose the common method bias in our data [62, 70, 112]. We selected disposition to trust as a marker variable, which refers to an individual’s tendency to depend on others [35] (see detailed measures in Appendix A), since it was a trait that stays invariant across contexts and theoretically does not relate to any variables in the research model ([59, 66]; [83]).

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Theoretical implications and future research directions

We make three major theoretical contributions to the extant literature on ASTI (Table 7). First, this study applies ASTI to the context of malleable IT and extends the ASTI framework to examine the effects of three representative task characteristics on innovative use of malleable IT within organizations, namely transformational leadership, innovation climate, and work autonomy. Previous IS studies have focused on the deterministic role of either technology ([5, 45]; Shao, 2018; [57, 91]) or its users [23, 56, 86, 90, 110] but have yet to pay attention to the influences of concrete task characteristics that manifest in both the task environment and the task process. Accordingly, we classify trans formational leadership and innovation climate as two important task environment factors, and work autonomy as the most salient task pro cess factor within the category of task characteristics in the ASTI framework. We find that transformational leadership had a positive impact on innovative use of malleable IT, both directly and indirectly, through fostering an innovation climate. Work autonomy provided users with freedom and discretion in using malleable IT and thus had a pos itive impact on innovative use. These empirical research findings enrich the theoretical framework of ASTI not only by identifying and concep tualizing specific exemplary task-related factors in user adaptation and innovation but also by determining how different aspects of task char acteristics affect innovative use of malleable IT—a topic that has yet to be covered in the literature on ASTI and AST.

Second, we reinterpret transformational leadership and innovation climate as two salient task environment factors in ASTI, which was originally conceived as group system factors in AST. We notice the ambiguity regarding the theoretical positioning of leadership styles and collective climates across the two alternative frameworks of AST and ASTI. While AST classifies interactions among users, such as immediate supervisors’ leadership behaviors and innovation climate, as constituted through interactions among peer users, ASTI does not account for these interactions when AST is extended from the group level to the individual level [86]. Therefore, our study contributes to the development of ASTI by reinterpreting transformational leadership and innovation climate as two representative environment factors in the task category. In addition, we identify the influential mechanism of transformational leadership on innovative use of malleable IT within organizations. Although supervi sory leadership has been recognized as a significant task environment factor affecting IS success at the organizational level [94, 95], re searchers have called for greater attention to the mechanisms through which direct supervisors can promote innovative use at the individual level [60, 64, 96]. Our study responds to this open research question and uncovers the mediating mechanism of innovation climate in the rela tionship between transformational leadership and innovative use.

Third, we identify gender stereotypes when delineating the influence of the three representative task characteristics on innovative use of malleable IT, including transformational leadership, innovation climate, and work autonomy. Organizational studies set gender as a significant contingency factor affecting employee perceptions, attitudes, and pref erences over job attributes [5, 52, 80, 113]. Still, how gender creates boundary conditions for the influence of task characteristics on tech nology use, in general, and its innovative use, in particular, is worth further exploration [64, 69, 106–109]. Our findings suggest that male users’ innovative use of malleable IT was more vulnerable to the task process factor of work autonomy, while female users’ innovative use was more attributable to the task environment factors of transformational leadership and innovation climate. As such, our study augments the knowledge regarding how gender stereotypes affect innovative use in the context of malleable IT.

Finally, we discuss the limitations of this study that present oppor tunities for future research directions. First, we tested the research hy potheses with data from frontline users of one particular mobile computing system in China, which potentially limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies can examine innovative use of other forms of malleable IT and compare how innovative use of malleable IT differs across empirical settings, including employees in different hierarchical positions or from different industries. Alternatively, researchers can replicate our findings in other cultural contexts and examine whether different cultural dimensions, such as individualism and power distance, are important contextual factors that need to be incorporated in our proposed research model. Second, this study conducted a cross-sectional survey to test the theoretical model and unpacked the theorized re lationships among the constructs. Future research can design a longi tudinal study or experiment to examine the causal relationships or even draw insights from qualitative data on innovative use of malleable IT. Third, we used self-reported data for hypothesis tests. Although we adopted quite a few of procedural remedies in survey administration to mitigate potential social desirability bias and the marker variable test results indicated minimum common method bias, future studies can collect data from multiple sources (e.g., objective measures of innova tive use or supervisor-rated innovative use) to examine our research model and hypotheses. Fourth, we controlled IT knowledge and personal innovativeness with IT as significant individual characteristics in the research model. Future studies can explore the potential influences from other individual characteristics, such as absorptive capacity and creative self-efficacy, to obtain interesting findings.

## 6.2. Practical implications

This study provides guidance for managers on how to motivate and cultivate their employees’ innovative use of malleable IT, according to task-related factors within organizations. Given the ubiquity of the new generation of digital technologies within organizations, employees exploration and innovative usage behaviors serve as the key for orga nizations to actualize functional affordances and maximize the eco nomic benefits of the implemented malleable IT. Our theoretical framework calls for managers to pay attention to employees’ adaptive and innovative usage behaviors, rather than focusing on standard or routine use of malleable IT. In addition, managers should not only focus on the influence of technical installation processes and/or user training programs on adaptive usage behaviors but also on how various facets of task characteristics facilitate and nurture user innovation and explora tion. While managers value frontline service personnel as important catalysts for products and services innovation [20, 55], our findings offer new insights regarding how to alter frontline personnel’s percep tions toward the task environment and task process, thereby cultivating their creativity through the use of malleable IT.

First, our findings underscore the importance of frontline managers leadership behaviors in stimulating employees’ innovative use of malleable IT. While top management usually makes decisions regarding the adoption of enterprise IS. it is the supervisory leaders who effectively fire up the frontline employees throughout the technology usage pro cess, and their transformational leadership behaviors serve as a catalyst for employees’ innovation. Our findings especially highlight the signif icance of transformational frontline leaders in the context of flexible and transfigurable malleable IT. Since frontline leaders cannot directly monitor or control the depth and breadth of employees’ technology use behaviors, leadership behaviors, such as management by exception and monetary rewards, may not be effective in stimulating employees innovative use of malleable IT [64]. Our findings suggest that frontline leaders can promote an innovation-oriented climate within teams or departments by exercising transformational leadership behaviors, in terms of charismatic attributes and behaviors, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and interpersonal consideration. Task envi ronment factors that manifest in transformational leadership and inno vation climate are beneficial in motivating employees’ creative performance and encouraging them to engage in innovative use of in formation technologies.

Second, the empirical results also indicate that work autonomy is a vital process-based task factor, which leads to employees’ innovative use of malleable IT. For example, managers can provide employees with more freedom and independence regarding their work schedules and execution processes. Such empowerment practices can enhance the creative thinking of employees and encourage them to devote time and energy to try out new functionalities of malleable IT. Empowered by the autonomous work processes, employees are also motivated to engage in improvisation when using malleable IT, customizing and extending the pliable functionalities to support new scenarios at work.

Last but not the least, managers need to recognize the differences between male and female employees’ innovation processes in using malleable IT. In particular, male employees are more likely to be influenced by work autonomy. Thus, managers can allocate and rotate male employees in job positions with more flexibility and empower ment. Compared with men, female employees are more likely to be affected by task environment factors such as transformational leadership and innovation climate. Hence, managers can motivate female em ployees’ innovative use of malleable IT by providing them with inspi rational articulation of the organizational mission and vision, intellectual stimulation that challenges the status quo, and interpersonal consideration that accounts for not only their career development but also their personal lives.

## 7. Conclusion

This study refers to and extends the theoretical framework of ASTI to investigate the influence of three task characteristics on users’ innova tive use of malleable IT within organizations. We identify trans formational leadership and innovation climate as two important task environment factors and work autonomy as the most salient task process factor. Specifically, we find that transformational leadership has a sig nificant and positive influence on innovative use of malleable IT, both directly and indirectly through innovation climate. Work autonomy also significantly influences innovative use of malleable IT. Furthermore, the environment factors of transformational leadership and innovation climate have stronger influences on female employees rather than on male employees, and the process factor of work autonomy has a stronger influence on male employees rather than on female employees. Our findings significantly advance the understanding of the important task characteristics in the context of malleable IT and the ways in which such task characteristics influence innovative use of malleable IT.
