Keywords: Digital piracy BitTorrent Internet buzz TV viewership Mediation Restricted maximum likelihood estimation

## A B S T R A C T

Notwithstanding the biggest victim of digital piracy, we have a limited understanding of how TV drama piracy affects its legal consumption. Unlike movies or music, TV dramas are typically a series of episodes, where pirated past episodes may not necessarily carry a negative impact on the legal consumption of current episodes. The consumption boosted by piracy can promote Internet buzz, which may draw the attention of potential consumers and may result in broader legal consumption afterward. Based on an empirical analysis regarding the re lationships among the viewership of 20 TV dramas broadcast in the US, social media activities on Twitter, Google and YouTube search trends, and the number of BitTorrent users who pirated these TV dramas, we show that although the direct effect of media consumption through BitTorrent to TV viewership is negative, the indirect effect that mediates Internet buzz is positive, which dilutes the negative effect of digital piracy. Such positive effects of digital piracy are more substantial for the second half of dramas, less popular dramas, and serial dramas. We discuss the theoretical contributions and managerial implications of our study.

## 1. Introduction

Digital piracy has been regarded as the most critical issue to address in the media industry. The characteristics of information goods that are easily copied and transmitted through the Internet make them vulner able to digital piracy. Several efforts have been devoted to preventing or discouraging digital piracy by using technological solutions such as Digital Rights Management (DRM), pricing strategies such as versioning, and anti-Internet piracy laws on copyright enforcement and manage ment [19,41]. Statistics show, however, that digital piracy is still prev alent: users made 174 billion visits to Internet piracy sites in 2019 [37]. TV shows and dramas are reported as the biggest victims, taking up 49.4% of all digital piracy activities [4].

As a critical threat to digital content businesses, many studies have empirically investigated the effects of movie piracy (e.g., [7,15,26,28, 30,32,38,43]) or music piracy (e.g., [1,3,22,24,35]). Notwithstanding the biggest victims of digital piracy, we have paid little attention to its effects on TV shows or dramas. Unlike movies or music, TV dramas are typically a series of episodes, where pirated past episodes may not necessarily carry a negative impact on the legal consumption of current episodes. Specifically, when media consumption is subject to word-of-mouth, the consumption boosted by digital piracy can promote Internet buzz, which may draw the attention of potential consumers, thereby resulting in increased legal consumption of subsequent epi sodes. Thus, the overall effect of digital piracy on TV dramas would depend on the relative size of the substitution effect, where consumers in a piracy channel would choose a legal channel if the piracy channel were not available, and the attention effect, where the Internet buzz promoted by piracy users elicits the attention of potential consumers, leading them to legal consumption.

Previous business cases also indicate the possible coexistence of substitution and attention effects of digital piracy. Black Widow suffered a sharp decline in theatrical revenues, with its high-quality pirate version released in its second week [31]. However, in the Pirate Bay shutdown in Sweden, we noted a drop in word-of-mouth and a contemporaneous decline in ticket sales [33]. Furthermore, ShotX could achieve a noticeable boost in sales as its free torrent release garnered wide attention from potential game users [46]. David Petrarca, the di rector of Game of Thrones, also mentioned that he was not bothered about Game of Thrones’ rampant piracy because it might boost a cultural buzz [45]

As such, we investigate the effects of digital piracy on TV dramas, with a particular focus on the mediating role of Internet buzz in the relationship between media consumption in a piracy channel and a legal channel. The mediation effect may be heterogeneous, depending on various factors, as well. For example, the effect could be larger for a less popular drama because it may require more public awareness. Under standing the heterogeneous effects is critical for developing appropriate counter-piracy strategies. Thus, we also consider such moderating fac tors in our analysis.

To examine the mediating role of Internet buzz, we collect data, including the viewership history of 20 TV dramas broadcast in the US, social media activities on Twitter regarding those TV dramas, Google and YouTube search trends of those dramas, and the number of Bit Torrent users who pirated those dramas during their broadcast periods. Our empirical analysis shows that although the direct effect of media consumption through BitTorrent on TV viewership was negative, the indirect effect mediating Internet buzz was positive, which diluted the negative effect of digital piracy. Consequently, the overall piracy effect on TV viewership was not significant.

This attention effect can be strong enough to neutralize the substi tution effect, especially when Internet buzz drives the media consump tion of potential legal consumers. The attention effect would prevail as Internet users continue to generate additional buzz. We confirm our conjecture with further analysis, which replicates the main analysis for the first and second halves of the broadcasting period of these TV dramas, considering that there is more buzz concerning the dramas as subsequent episodes are progressively broadcast. The analytical results from the first half reveal minimal mediation effects across the TV dramas, whereas the results from the second half show significant mediation effects that could even result in an overall positive effect of digital piracy.

Also, the attention effect can be more substantial for less popular dramas because they need greater public awareness. We replicate our analysis for dramas with high and low viewership and find that the attention effect was significant only for low viewership dramas. Partic ularly, the positive effect was greater for their second half. In high viewership dramas, the attention effect was not significant both in their first and second halves. This finding is consistent with prior movie pi racy studies, revealing promotional effects of piracy for movies in need of public awareness (e.g., [21,38]). Lastly, we compare the model esti mates between procedural dramas (that do not need prior viewing of any other episode to understand, e.g., ‘CSI’) and serial dramas (that require viewing previous episodes, e.g., ‘Lost’). In serial dramas, the story is interwoven across episodes; thus, consumers would be highly motivated to watch the next episode by the Internet buzz about past episodes. The positive word-of-mouth effect was significant only for such serial dramas.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Next, we review the prior literature on the effects of digital piracy and Internet buzz. Then, we describe our data and empirical approach. Subsequently, we present the results of our analysis. Lastly, we conclude with a discussion of the results and implications of the study.

## 2. Related Literature

This study builds on and contributes to two streams of research. The first stream empirically examines the effect of digital piracy on legal consumption across different contexts. The second stream focuses on the role of Internet buzz in generating consumer demand.

## 2.1. The effect of digital piracy

The effects of digital piracy on the legal consumption of information goods have drawn a great deal of attention from the Information Systems community during the last decade. A plethora of empirical studies have examined whether illegal movie or music consumption through a file sharing platform or an application (e.g., BitTorrent) indeed affects legitimate consumption. Despite the negative public stance against digital piracy, prior studies have yielded mixed empirical findings regarding how digital content consumption in a piracy channel affect consumption in a legal channel. Several studies provide empirical evidence revealing a negative ef fect of digital piracy on content sales. For example, based on a longi tudinal panel survey, Hennig-Thurau et al. [15] argue that illegal media consumption through file-sharing platforms decreased revenues of the movie industry by 9.4%. Likewise, Liebowitz [24] shows that illegal music consumption through file-sharing platforms resulted in a large decline in record sales of the US music industry. Similarly, Ma et al. [28] show that pre-released piracy copies of a new film reduced box office revenue by 19%. Danaher and Smith [7] further investigate the causal impact of the shutdown of piracy sites and find that the Antipiracy Act increased the revenue of movie rentals by 6.5%–8.5%. Lastly, Godinho de Matos et al. [12] show that a free subscription to video-on-demand (SVoD) services for a certain period can increase households’ TV con sumption and decrease their illegal uploads and downloads in the piracy channel after the complimentary subscription period, suggesting a negative association between legal and illegal media consumption.

Contrary to the abovementioned empirical findings, another large body of literature suggests an insignificant effect of digital piracy. For example, Oberholzer-Gee and Strumpf [35] examine the relationship between illegal consumption on file-sharing platforms and music album sales, revealing an insignificant effect. Similarly, Smith and Telang [43] show that the availability of pirated movie copies has no impact on subsequent DVD sales. In addition, based on microeconomic survey data in Canada, Andersen and Frenz [3] find an insignificant relationship between the number of P2P file downloads and CD album sales. Marti kainen [30] focuses on the relationship between movie sharing through BitTorrent and DVD sales, and shows that the ’file-sharing elasticity of sales’ is not statistically different from zero.

There have been attempts to explain the above insignificant effects. For example, the instrument variable used in Oberholzer-Gee and Strumpf [35] has been criticized regarding its criteria [25]. Smith and Telang [43] also raise concerns about the data collection period related to an insignificant effect. However, a systematic explanation as to why the piracy effect could be negligible was not provided.

Recent empirical studies provide evidence of positive or conditional effects of digital piracy, where they focus on its promotional effects. In this regard, Lu et al. [27] explain why digital piracy could increase sales with four different mechanisms: trial, network effects, advertising, and word-of-mouth. As such, efforts to reduce piracy may bring unexpected results. For example, Zhang [49] shows that removing DRM could in crease digital music sales by approximately 10%. Kretschmer and Peu kert [22] also show that the blockage of YouTube music videos may decrease music sales. Furthermore, piracy activities can work as an advertiser particularly when the digital content is unnoticed by the public. For example, Peukert et al. [38] reveal that piracy site shutdown may increase box office revenues for movies in the top decile of the number of opening screens but decrease for movies in the bottom decile. Koschmann and Qian [21] also find that high-quality illegal copies may increase box office revenues at launch, then decrease the revenues af terward. Lastly, based on the correlation between the presence of digital piracy and the volume of IMDb reviews, Lu et al. [27] argue that movie piracy may create word-of-mouth, thereby counteracting the substitu tion effect of digital piracy.

Although we have rich empirical evidence regarding the effects of movie or music piracy, we still have a limited empirical understanding of the effects of TV drama piracy. TV dramas are different from music or movies in that a series of episodes are broadcast over time. Regardless of piracy or legal channels, those who consume early episodes may generate Internet buzz, which may draw the attention of potential consumers and may result in broader legal consumption afterward. Such a word-of-mouth effect could prevail for late episodes, given that the Internet buzz would snowball and make the drama more visible on the Internet over time. Further, different drama characteristics may play a role in determining the piracy effects, which we identify as moderators for the piracy effect.

## 2.2. Internet buzz and consumer demand

The effect of Internet buzz on consumer demand has been empiri cally examined across different contexts, including online communities, blogs, Twitter, and Google trends. Prior studies have consistently revealed that Internet buzz can work as a sales indicator or positively affect future sales.

For example, based on an analysis of conversations on a popular online community site, Godes and Mayzlin [11] provide evidence that online buzz about TV shows significantly increases their TV ratings. Dhar and Chang [9] also show that the volume of blog posts and future sales of music albums are positively correlated. Asur and Huberman [2] used Twitter data to forecast the box-office revenues of movies. They argue that a prediction model based on tweets provides better accuracy than the Hollywood Stock Exchange, a well-established wisdom-ofcrowds prediction platform. Echoing this notion, Rui et al. [40] examine how word-of-mouth on Twitter affects movie sales based on a dynamic panel model. They show that Twitter users with more followers affect sales to a greater extent than users with fewer followers, thus implying the positive effect of social media buzz on sales. Correspondingly, Hennig-Thurau et al. [16] analyze the impact of word-of-mouth on Twitter regarding consumers’ adoption of new movies; they find a sig nificant effect of word-of-mouth, especially for tweets with negative sentiment. Dewan and Ramaprasad [8] focus on the interplay among blog buzz, radio play, and music sales, and find a stronger buzz effect on sales for niche music relative to mainstream music. Lastly, a recent empirical study focuses on Google search trends and finds that they positively affect market response [10].

Given that Internet buzz involving TV dramas (such as social media postings or search trends) is promoted by users who consume or will consume these media contents, the piracy users who would not watch the drama if the piracy channel were not available could contribute to the Internet buzz and, subsequently, the TV viewership as well. Recent statistics illustrate that 90% of piracy activities are conducted outside the US, mostly in developing countries [4]. That is because of lenient anti-piracy laws or actions in such countries, which lessen piracy users psychological burden of punishment [36]. Further, such users might perceive their piracy as a victimless crime for their own country, as popular content is generated from developed countries [14].

TV viewership is estimated from the US residents only, whereas non-US residents can also make Internet buzz. As such, those piracy users outside the US would not bring a significant substitution effect as they are (although they may promote piracy activities in the US.) Thus, pi racy users on BitTorrent might not necessarily impose a negative effect but could positively affect TV viewership in the US.

Although we provide our rough predictions, how media consumption in the piracy channel affects media consumption in the legitimate channel remains an empirical question, which would depend on the relative sizes of the substitution effect and the attention effect. We empirically examine these effects in the next section.

## 3. Empirical Approach

## 3.1. Data

We obtained data from multiple sources for our analysis (Table 2). First, we gathered data concerning the viewership of 20 TV dramas broadcast in the US from early March 2018 to early June 2018 (Table 3). Specifically, we collected the data of their TV ratings and the estimated number of viewers over time from the TV by the Numbers website,<sup>1</sup> which are used as our main dependent variables (the variables are named ’TV\_rating’ and ’Viewers,’ respectively).

Second, we crawled µTorrent, the most popular BitTorrent client owned by BitTorrent itself, throughout the broadcasting periods of those TV dramas to collect the number of piracy users for each episode. Spe cifically, we collected the number of seeders who distribute each episode of those dramas on the platform twice a day at 12 AM and 12 PM PST/ PDT, and we use the daily average number of those for our analysis (the variable is named ’Seeds’). Similarly, we collected the number of leechers who own only a part of the file so that they upload that part but download the other part at the same time (the variable is named ’Leeches’). For our main analysis, we use the total number of BitTorrent users who were involved in the piracy of those dramas, i.e., the sum of the numbers of seeders and leechers (the variable is named ’Piracy’). We also investigate the separate piracy effects of seeders and leechers.

Third, we observed the Google and YouTube trends of those dramas during the same periods, which involve the number of titles of those dramas that are used as the search terms on those platforms. As such, those trends reflect the current attention of Internet users. Both the Google and YouTube search trends are exposed to general Internet users, which may entice people’s attention to popular keywords, including TV drama titles. We use both Google and YouTube search trends to inves tigate their mediating role in the relationship between media con sumption through BitTorrent and TV viewership (the variables are named ’Google’ and ’YouTube,’ respectively).

Fourth, we obtained data from AC Nielsen with respect to social media users’ activities on Twitter regarding those dramas during the same periods. The data include the number of tweets that mentioned the title, actors’ names/handles, or character names, or that had fangenerated hashtags or phrases of those dramas. The data also include the number of activities related to those tweets, such as replies, retweets, likes, and quotes. We adopt three different measures of social media activity for our analysis: the number of tweets, engagements, and in teractions regarding those dramas (the variables are named ’Tweets, ’Engagements,’ and ’Interactions,’ respectively). The number of engage ments is defined as being equal to the sum of the numbers of replies, retweets, likes, and quotes; the number of interactions is defined as being equal to the sum of the numbers of engagements and tweets. Both measures for engagement and interaction are defined by Twitter and are widely used in academia (e.g., [18,47]) or in practice (e.g., [6,34]). Those social media activities are expected to play a similar role as Internet search trends, given that they can work as advertisements for Twitter users, such as followers.

Lastly, we collected the IMDb star ratings of each episode and whether to receive the Primetime Emmy Award as a quality measure for the episode.<sup>2</sup> We must control for the quality of the episode to obtain correct estimates because the higher quality would simultaneously attract greater digital piracy, more Internet searches and social media activities, and higher viewership, all of which could generate spurious relationships among our variables of interest.

## 3.2. Empirical model

Following our predictions, the digital piracy of TV dramas, measured by the number of piracy users on BitTorrent, could not only adversely affect TV viewership (substitution effect), but could also promote Internet buzz on those TV dramas, which may attract the attention of potential viewers and may enhance TV viewership (attention effect). To tease out the substitution and attention effects, we use a mediation model adapted from Krull and MacKinnon [23]. Given our multilevel data structure (in our study, each drama has more than or equal to five observations), the ordinary least squares estimator would underestimate standard errors of our parameters due to the common errors shared by episodes within the same drama. Their multilevel mediation technique allows us to estimate error terms for both episodes (lower-level units) and dramas (higher-level units) by explicitly modeling the within-group homogeneity of errors.

Our empirical model consists of three equations: 1) TV viewership such as TV ratings or the estimated number of viewers (the DV) on the number of piracy users (the IV); 2) the Google (or YouTube) search trends or social media activities (the MV) on the number of piracy users (the IV); and 3) TV viewership measures (the DV) on the Google (or YouTube) search trends or social media activities (the MV) and the number of piracy users (the IV). The direct, indirect, and total effects are calculated by the estimation results of the three equations. We also include dummies indicating each drama (e.g., Modern Family), each broadcaster (e.g., NBC), each week (e.g., the first week of the observa tion), each day of the week (e.g., Monday), and the broadcast day of the week for each drama (e.g., every Thursday for Grey’s Anatomy) in our models to control for their fixed effects. We also control for the quality of each episode with IMDb star ratings and dummies indicating whether to receive the award, and the length of each episode in minutes. Our model specifications are:

$$

$$

$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} T V \_ V i e w e r s h i p _ {i, t} & = \alpha_ {3} + \beta_ {D 3} D i g i t a l \_ P i r a c y _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {B 3} B u z z _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \gamma_ {3} C o n t r o l _ {i, t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{Eq. 3}
$$

where $T V _ { - } V i w e r s h i p _ { i , t }$ is the TV rating or the estimated number of viewers of drama i at t, Digital $P i r a c y _ { i , \mathrm { t - 1 } }$ is the number of BitTorrent users that pirate drama i at t-1, Buzz is the Google or YouTube trend measure of drama i at t, Control is a vector of the control variables of drama i at $t , \alpha _ { 1 }$ (α and α ) is the intercept in the first (second and third) equation, β $( \beta _ { D 2 } \mathrm { a n d } \beta _ { D 3 } )$ is the coefficient of Digital $P i r a c y _ { i , \mathrm { t - 1 } }$ in the first (second and third) equation, $\beta _ { \mathbf { B 3 } }$ is the coefficient of $B u z z _ { i , \mathrm { t - 1 } }$ in the third equation, $\gamma _ { I }$ $( \gamma _ { 2 }$ and $\gamma _ { 3 } )$ is a vector of the coefficients for Control in the first (second and third) equation, and $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is the disturbance term.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

In this study, we empirically investigate the effects of TV drama pi racy on its legitimate viewership, which was silent in the research stream of digital piracy effects. The availability of the piracy channel might seduce potential consumers who would consume the contents of the legitimate channel if the piracy channel were not available. How ever, such substitution effect could be counteracted by the attention effect, where the Internet buzz promoted by piracy users (who would not consume the contents if the piracy channel were not available) may draw the attention of potential consumers and may lead them to legal consumption. Particularly, TV dramas are different from music or film in that those who consume early episodes may generate Internet buzz for later episodes. Furthermore, consumers who watched early episodes would gain greater utility from watching late episodes than those who did not, leading them to the legitimate channel for their on-time con sumption. This study is one of the first that empirically investigates the effect of TV drama piracy. Unlike movies or music, TV drama is a series of episodes, where piracy of early episodes may promote the legal consumption of later episodes. We show that such effects are stronger for later episodes, as the Internet buzz snowballs over time. Further, such effects are stronger for less popular and serial dramas.

Comparing procedural dramas and serial dramas may cast an inter esting research question for other piracy contexts, such as movie piracy. Recent empirical studies regarding movie piracy identify conditional promotional effects of pirated content. Along with this research stream, our finding may indicate that, in the case of movie series, piracy activ ities of a prior version may help the success of a newer version when their stories are interwoven.

A paper in the Marketing discipline introduces a similar discussion but in an offline setting [39]. They found that the sales of counterfeit shoes created larger sales of authentic shoes. This is because those counterfeits can increase brand awareness for authentic products with mass advertising. Given that there are consumers who do not want to or have no idea of how to purchase counterfeits, consumers who bought counterfeits may play a role in advertising for those who stick to pur chase authentic products. Our findings are consistent with their argu ment in that there are consumers who only use legitimate channels to watch TV dramas. These consumers may be exposed to Internet buzz regarding the dramas, which will entice them to watch such dramas.

From a practical point of view, the negative effect of digital piracy might be limited, particularly for developed countries such as the US. Under strict anti-Internet piracy laws. US residents are not allowed to consume pirated content on BitTorrent clients. Statistics show that most digital piracy activities occur outside the US, which attests to the pos sibility of a limited substitution effect. Those piracy users outside the US, however, could contribute to the Internet buzz of TV dramas, which may entice the attention of US residents. The indirect effect could produce an overall positive effect of digital piracy, such as the second-half episodes of the TV dramas in our analysis. Further, copyright holders may release previous episodes for free if their serial drama suffers from eliciting public attention. By doing so, they could exploit the attention effects for their later episodes.

We conclude by articulating a limitation and providing suggestions for future research. Although we focus on BitTorrent to investigate pi racy effects, there are other piracy channels. As such, our finding does not reflect all piracy effects. Furthermore, there are other revenue sources for broadcasters, such as video-on-demand services or paid subscriptions, for replays on their websites. The piracy effects on those services could be another meaningful topic for the media industry. Also, we may underestimate the potential benefit of TV drama piracy because we do not consider other positive mediation effects driven by piracy, such as offline buzz. Lastly, the attention effect could depend on cultural orientation, where collectivism might enforce the effect, considering that people from collectivistic cultures are more likely to follow others opinions. Further study may be conducted to explore the role of cultural orientation in the piracy effect.<sup>8</sup>
