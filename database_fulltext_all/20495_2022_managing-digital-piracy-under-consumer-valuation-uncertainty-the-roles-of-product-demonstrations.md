## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Copyright protection Digital good Digital piracy Antipiracy Product demonstration Quality uncertainty Fit uncertainty

## A B S T R A C T

Product demonstrations (e.g., software or video game trials) are commonly used in digital-good markets to resolve prospective consumers’ valuation uncertainty. We theoretically investigate how a monopolistic digitalgood firm may utilize the tool of product demonstration, together with technical and legal antipiracy mea sures which increase the difficulty or cost of pirating, to optimally manage the impact of piracy on its new product’s profitability. We unexpectedly find that, in equilibrium, the firm’s profit may strictly decrease with the difficulty of pirating when the true product quality is not especially high, such that the firm chooses not to offer product demonstrations. This is because, with higher piracy costs, the firm has more incentive to offer product demonstrations and, as a result, rational customers’ quality belief conditional on non-demonstration is lower, which leads to a lower profit for the firm. We extend our model to incorporate consumers’ fit uncertainty about the product due to idiosyncratic tastes. Related managerial implications for public policy and legislation regarding piracy and copyright are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

When progress in information technology facilitates the creation and consumption of more diverse digital content, it also makes unauthorized copying of copyrighted digital content (i.e., digital piracy) immensely convenient and rampant in many information goods markets (e.g., software, recorded music, books, movies, games, etc.). In response to the serious challenges posed by digital piracy, copyright owners are widely known for pursuing various actions to minimize infringement. The antipiracy measures can be broadly categorized into preventive, deter rent, and economic strategies [22]. Preventive measures refer to prac tices of building into products technical protection, such as digital rights management (DRM) systems, to increase piracy cost. Deterrent mea sures entail educational, investigative, and legal campaigns to discourage users from pirating by increasing both the likelihood of detection and the severity of punishment upon prosecution. Economic measures include a wide range of product (e.g., sampling, versioning, and bundling) and pricing strategies.

Meanwhile, the high pace of new product rollout in markets for in formation goods often confronts consumers with salient valuation un certainty about a particular new digital product. Valuation uncertainty concerns not only product (vertical) quality but also product fit [11]. Although information about some product attributes can be easily communicated to consumers before purchase with little cost (i.e., so-called search attributes), valuation uncertainty about a product’s experience attributes can usually only be resolved via consumption and real experience [61,62]. A digital product typically entails crucial experience attributes, which affect how consumers perceive a product’s quality and fit with individual tastes [15,18,56]. For example, con sumers may need to use a piece of software to ascertain its general ease of use and whether it fits individual habits. Likewise, consumers may need to play a video game on a certain gaming device to know its quality on control and graphics (e.g., the same video game may differ sub stantially in control and graphics when it is played on a personal com puter (PC) vs. console gaming platforms) and whether it meets individual gaming tastes.

Recognizing consumer uncertainty about experience attributes im pedes new product acceptance. Firms often facilitate various formats of product demonstrations to allow consumers to learn the level of a product’s experience attributes before committing to their purchases [11,15,18,33,35–37]. Here, we use “product demonstrations” to refer to a wide range of firm activities that allow consumers to obtain real consumption experiences with digital products before purchasing them. For example, demonstration kiosks in an electronics store allow con sumers to play a video game, public events and shows enable consumers to hear a new music album, and software and game developers offer trial versions or trial periods for their new products.

Although various mechanisms have been discussed in prior literature to resolve consumer valuation uncertainty, we focus on product demonstration as the main mechanism to address consumer valuation uncertainty of digital experience goods for two main reasons. First, product return, another common mechanism to resolve consumer valuation uncertainty in physical durable goods markets, is ill defined for digital goods, due to their infinite duplicability. Product return policies, such as money-back guarantees, that are common in physical durable products are indeed limited in information goods industries. For example, according to the product return rules on the PlayStation Store, except in the case of faulty content, consumers are not eligible for a refund once they have started to download or stream the purchased content [15].<sup>1</sup> Second, third-party product reviews, an emerging infor mation source to alleviate product uncertainty, require considerable time after a product is launched to accumulate a sufficient number of reviews to serve as a good and unbiased indicator of product quality.<sup>2</sup> Moreover, third-party reviews might be inherently limited in resolving uncertainty about experience attributes, especially about consumers own individual fit with the product [24].

Clearly, the presence of digital piracy and consumer uncertainty about product value are both significant issues to an information-good producer during new product releases. For example, when Nintendo launched the first mobile game of its classic Super Mario series—Super Mario Run—on Apple iOS on December 2016, the game required a constant internet connection to play, due to piracy concerns (i.e., an always-on DRM).<sup>3</sup> In addition, Nintendo offered the first few levels for free to allow users to try the game before purchasing it.<sup>4</sup> Moreover, anecdotal evidence suggests that a firm may strategically and jointly use antipiracy technology and product demonstration when releasing new products. A case in point is that Electronic Arts, one of the largest game developers and publishers worldwide, often shows markedly varied approaches to its new game titles on piracy prevention and product demonstration. For example, the firm has always embedded the latest effective DRM systems—e.g., Denuvo Anti-Tamper—in its EA Sports FIFA franchise but has never done so for its EA Sports UFC franchise. Specifically, representing a new type of DRM technology, Denuvo Anti-Tamper made its debut in September 2014 and has proven to be the most effective solution in protecting video games from being cracked on PC platforms, which are much more prone to piracy than console platforms (e.g., Xbox and PlayStation). In most cases, Denuvo Anti-Tamper can ensure that the crucial first several weeks of a game’s release are piracy free, which is unprecedented because PC games used to be cracked shortly after release, and even often on the same day of launch [50,68].<sup>5</sup> At the same time, Electronic Arts invests much more in providing con sumers with information about the value of each new release of FIFA through, e.g., in-store demo kiosks, public events, and contests. Inter estingly, for the latest releases of the two series, the firm developed a comprehensive demo version for FIFA but only allowed for very limited trial opportunity for UFC.<sup>6</sup>

In this paper, we build a game-theoretical model to examine how a monopolistic information-good firm may strategically leverage antipi racy measures and invest in product demonstrations to optimally manage the impact of digital piracy on the profitability of its new product, whose true value is not completely known to prospective consumers. In developing our model, we inherit much of the setup from prior literature to reveal the essential effects of product demonstrations and (preventive and deterrent) antipiracy measures on consumer decision-making. Although previous research has devoted considerable attention to studying how information-good manufacturers can opti mally cope with digital piracy (see the next section for a summary), the issue we focus on here has been largely neglected. We believe that this is not a trivial issue to be ignored, given the importance of both piracy prevention and product demonstration for new digital products. We contribute to the literature on digital piracy by first systematically examining the interplay between product demonstration, as an eco nomic antipiracy instrument, and other preventive and deterrent anti piracy measures in strategically managing piracy threats to new digital products.

Our analysis yields several novel findings to the extant literature. First, when the true quality of its product is not especially high, such that the firm chooses not to offer product demonstrations, the firm may be surprisingly better off when pirating becomes less costly. This is due to a strategic effect of the publicly observable piracy cost: When the piracy cost decreases, the adverse impact of piracy on the firm’s profit in tensifies accordingly, which may lead the consumers to expect that a firm is more likely to conceal quality information since product dem onstrations are more costly. Consequently, the consumers’ posterior quality belief when the firm indeed does not offer product demonstra tions increases, which benefits the firm’s profit. To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel mechanism that has not been identified in previous research. Second, when we incorporate consumers’ fit uncer tainty about the product into our model, we find that fit uncertainty further weakens the firm’s incentive to offer product demonstrations and motivates the firm to leverage the strategic impact of piracy cost on its profit. Third, our findings have a clear and important implication for public policy and legislation on copyright and piracy: It is wise to tailor antipiracy enforcement according to product quality type and infor mation transparency.

## 2. Related literature

Our work is at the intersection of two literature streams: digital pi racy and product information provision or disclosure. Early works on digital piracy, or on unauthorized copying in general (e.g., photo copying of physical textbooks before the digital age), center on the net impact of unauthorized copying on social welfare [see, e.g., [5,44,64, 82]]: While the availability of a pirated good can increase social welfare through greater consumption (i.e., a decrease in “underutilization loss”), piracy can also result in smaller profits for the producers, which in turn reduces the producers’ incentive to invest in quality (i.e., an increase in “underproduction loss”). So, in general, the optimal copyright protec tion hinges on the relative magnitudes of underproduction loss versus underutilization loss. Relatedly, Chen and Png [16] compare the welfare implications of three government instruments of copyright enforcement: setting fines for copying, levying taxes on copying media, and giving subsidies for legitimate purchases. Dey et al. [20] contrast innovation and welfare implications for supply-side copyright enforcement (i.e., restricting the supply of pirated goods) versus demand-side copyright enforcement (i.e., penalizing illegal consumption). Within a two-sided market, Ishihara and Muller [39] investigate a digital platform pro vider’s decisions on how much software to produce in-house and how much to outsource from a third-party software provider.

Contrary to the common belief that piracy always undermines the legal firm’s profit, there is a strand of literature arguing that piracy may have more nuanced and even beneficial impacts on the legal firm’s profit. Liebowitz [58] shows that if copyright owners can indirectly extract revenue from users who are not the original purchasers by charging higher prices to the original purchasers, unauthorized copying might actually help copyright owners. In the presence of a positive network effect where consumers’ willingness to pay increases with the total number of users of either legal or pirated versions, firm profits might increase, due to more active unauthorized sharing among con sumers [19,71,75]. Moreover, the shadow diffusion of pirated products can indirectly contribute to legal products’ diffusion via, e.g., word-of-mouth effects [34,67]. In contexts where negative network ef fects are salient (e.g., by denying pirates security patches that could make legal users more vulnerable to attacks), a firm might be better off by tolerating piracy (e.g., by allowing pirates to patch) [4].

Previous research has identified reasons other than demand-side externalities for the fact that piracy or weak copyright protection does not need to be detrimental to copyright owners. Takeyama [76] shows that unauthorized reproduction may increase the firm’s profit when the availability of copies offers commitment value to the firm and thus al leviates the durable-good pricing problem. Ben-Shahar and Danny [8] argue that a firm may strategically promote infringement of its own copyrights for the sake of engaging in predatory pricing and thus raising competitors’ barriers to entry. Jain [40] shows that when piracy enables competing firms to soften price competition by driving out price-sensitive consumers, it can increase firms’ profits and lead to higher-quality products. Vernik et al. [78] argue that firms may also be reluctant to adopt copyright protection technologies such as DRM so lutions simply because these technologies may hurt legal users’ valua tion and willingness to pay. Guo and Meng [30] identify situations where weak copyright protection benefits a firm’s profit by encouraging more consumers to expend costs to search for price information when they ex ante are unaware of the price of the legal product. Kim et al. [48] show that the existence of piracy may benefit a monopolistic manufac turer and retailer by alleviating the double marginalization hazard.

This study is more closely related to the literature that studies a manufacturer’s strategies to manage digital piracy. Sundararajan [74] analyzes a firm’s optimal choice of nonlinear pricing schedules (in term of a quantity–price schedule) and technological deterrence levels (e.g., by implementing DRM systems) to cope with digital piracy. Chellappa and Shivendu [15] examine a firm’s pricing and sampling strategies to optimally manage piracy when a proportion of consumers may use a pirated product first to ascertain the fit with their individual tastes before they consider purchasing a legal version. Wu and Chen [79] explore the possibility of versioning (i.e., offering multiple versions with different quality levels and prices) as a profitable instrument to fight digital piracy. Gopal and Gupta [22] study the implications of product bundling on firm profits as a potential manner to combat piracy. August and Tunca [4] and Kannan et al. [46] examine a firm’s strategic use of restricted patch distribution in the presence of piracy. Johar et al. [42] show how a content provider can design its content quality and content delivery speed to respond to the threat of piracy. Tunca and Wu [77] discuss the impact of copyright protection targeted at individual piracy on firm payoffs in the presence of commercial piracy. Geng and Lee [21] compare the impact of reducing piracy quality, increasing piracy search costs, and reducing the number of piracy services in controlling piracy in a setting where legal retailers compete with each other as well as with piracy services. We aim to contribute to this stream by studying product demonstration (and, more generally, information provision) as another possible economic lever for a digital-good seller, coupled with technical and legal deterrence, to optimally manage digital piracy.

As noted above, our work also builds on prior literature in eco nomics, marketing and information systems on sellers’ information provision strategies when consumers are confronted with valuation uncertainty due to information asymmetry [see 24, for a comprehensive review]. More precisely, our paper draws on previous theoretical studies on a firm’s incentive to provide information to alleviate consumer valuation uncertainty. Here, consumer valuation uncertainty refers to a situation wherein customer knowledge of their own valuation, depending on product quality, product fit, and other factors, is unknown and can be affected by a firm’s information strategies [e.g., [10,17,31, 35,60]]. Previous research has examined a firm’s incentive to disclose information about vertical product quality in a monopolistic market [e. g., 23,45], competitive market [e.g., [32,51,57,83]], distribution channel [e.g., 27,29], and multi-period market [e.g., 26]. In addition, there is a stream of research on a firm’s incentive to disclose information about product fit in a monopolistic market [e.g., 43,72], competitive market [e.g., 2,25], and distribution channel [e.g., 33,73]. Product demonstration, as an effective disclosure instrument, has also received special attention [e.g., [11,36,37,69]]. Moving to information goods, developing product trials has been one focal format of information disclosure or product demonstration in prior literature [e.g., [15,18,56, 63,80]]. Following this stream of literature, the goal of information disclosure is to facilitate consumer learning of real product value, and thus information provision in our model does not play a signaling role.<sup>7</sup>

The basic model in this work borrows from Lahiri and Dey [55], who argue that weak copyright enforcement can actually increase the legal firms’ incentive to develop higher-quality products to more significantly differentiate them from pirated versions. The critical difference lies in the information asymmetry between the seller and the consumers in our model, which however does not play a role in Lahiri and Dey [55]. Essentially, Lahiri and Dey [55] investigate a developer’s quality deci sion problem in the presence of piracy without any source of uncer tainty; however, we study the developer’s product demonstration issue given the existence of quality and fit uncertainty among consumers.

Our study is also closely related to Chellappa and Shivendu [15]. However, our work significantly differs from their work in the following aspects. First, we allow experience attributes of a digital good to affect both product quality and product fit in our model [see similar settings in studies such as [11,18,52]], but experience attributes only affect prod uct fit in Chellappa and Shivendu [15]. Second, and more substantially, with the possibility for consumers to fully resolve their fit uncertainty by pirating first, Chellappa and Shivendu [15] investigate how a firm should set the optimal portion to offer in a product sample (or trial version) to allow consumers to fully or partially resolve fit uncertainty in a legitimate way. Instead, our focus is on the interplay of (technical and legal) antipiracy measures and product demonstrations (including product samples or trials) that aim to fully resolve consumers’ product quality and fit uncertainty. Third, we draw conclusions and insights from very different perspectives. Chellappa and Shivendu [15] provide pricing and sampling strategies for a digital-product developer, while we uncover the impact of piracy on the developer’s product demonstration policy and overall profitability.

## 3. Main model: quality uncertainty

We start with a simple setup without product fit uncertainty, such that consumer valuation uncertainty exclusively concerns product (vertical) quality. We introduce product fit uncertainty into our model later. The setup with only product quality uncertainty is relevant as long as consumers know whether products from a certain category fit with their tastes upfront. For example, before the release of Super Mario Run, as mentioned above, prospective consumers arguably faced much less uncertainty about whether the game would fit their individual tastes, since most, if not all, consumers already know what kind of leisure value would be delivered by the Super Mario series. What remains uncertain instead is how Nintendo, in its first attempt, would adapt this classic console gaming concept to the mobile platform in various aspects, including gameplay, controls, graphics, and character customization. These attributes can be largely viewed as vertical quality attributes in the sense that consumers all derive higher value from a game scoring higher on those attributes. Moreover, these attributes can also be largely viewed as experience attributes, in that consumers need to try the game to ascertain its value.

We consider a monopolistic firm selling a newly developed infor mation good of quality $q \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ at a price $p \geq 0$ to consumers. We as sume that the basic quality of any products produced by the firm is constant and normalized to zero, so q indicates the incremental quality level. The monopolistic setting constitutes an abstraction of the fact that digital products within a given category are often viewed as highly differentiated by consumers, so it is not uncommon to see a producer with at least some pricing power within a certain category [7,44,64]. Throughout this paper, we adopt the term “quality” to refer to all of the relevant aspects regarding a digital product, such as functionalities, performance, reliability, and features that affect the product’s perceived desirability [9,15,28]. In practice, information goods often vary considerably in quality. A low-quality product could be produced either intentionally (e.g., out of budget constraints) or accidentally (e.g., due to randomness in the production process). We assume that the product development cost is sunk and that the firm maximizes its profit based on a realized product quality.<sup>8</sup> The marginal production cost of the infor mation good is assumed to be negligible [e.g., 55,70].

After quality realization, consumers do not know the true q and hold a prior belief that q is uniformly distributed between 0 and 1, $q ^ { o } \sim U [ 0$ 1]. So, ex ante, consumers have the largest uncertainty about the true quality of a newly developed product. As noted above, we assume that quality uncertainty is largely driven by experience attributes, so the firm can invest in product demonstrations to resolve consumer uncertainty. For simplicity, we assume that, through product demonstrations, con sumers can learn the actual level of these experience attributes and thus the true quality of the product. Designing appropriate product demon strations to inform consumers of the true quality is not a trivial task, so we assume that the firm needs to incur a fixed demonstration cost of d > 0. The exogenous parameter d can also be interpreted as the difficulty of offering fully informative product demonstrations.

The product demonstration cost is modeled as a one-time fixed cost because many common product demonstration activities, e.g., in-store showrooms and public events, may only cause a fixed cost to the firm [51]. Developing a limited-functionality trial version of software or video games also necessitates some appropriate planning and discretion in abridging the full version; for trial versions with full functionalities but with a limited trial period, extra and effective security measures need to be embedded into the products to prevent piracy. These are all more properly viewed as a one-time fixed cost to the firm, and once a proper trial version has been developed, the marginal production cost is also negligible.

To focus on our main insights (i.e., the interplay between antipiracy efforts and product demonstration activities), we only consider two discrete choices of the firm: either a firm offers product demonstrations (to fully resolve consumer uncertainty) or it does not. As observed in previous research, such a discrete setup is usually sufficient to obtain useful insights [e.g., [15,53,79]]. This is also a common setup in prior literature on product information disclosure [e.g., [27,29,32,33,51]]. In essence, we consider a setting wherein the firm can only offer fully informative product demonstrations or nothing, and it necessitates non-negligible costs to design fully informative product demonstrations. Hence, we exclude the possibility that the firm can (strategically) decide the level of informativeness in product demonstrations.<sup>9</sup> This setup again (like the monopolistic market setting) constitutes an abstraction of a situation wherein a firm has at least some power, especially during new product releases, over controlling the opportunities through which consumers can experience the products to reduce their valuation uncertainty.

When the firm offers product demonstrations, consumers update their quality belief to the true product quality by participating in product demonstrations. When the firm does not offer product demon strations, consumers also update their quality belief by taking into ac count the firm’s decision (i.e., the absence of a demonstration also conveys information). We denote consumers’ posterior quality belief (after the firm’s product demonstration decision) by ̃q. The value of ̃q becomes common knowledge when the legal version’ price is set and consumers make their purchase decisions.

Other than buying the legal product, consumers may opt for a pirated version as a substitute. However, a pirated copy is often seen as a lowerquality substitute for the original product by consumers [e.g., [20,55, 74]]. Indeed, pirated software and video games may miss key func tionalities and even contain malicious code [41]. Pirated music and movies might be incomplete or poorly compressed [47,65]. Illegal software users may not have access to product updates and patches [4, 54]. A series of consumer surveys by Business Software Alliance revealed that software users generally recognize that a licensed software is more secure and reliable than its pirated version [13] and the main reason why consumers avoid unlicensed software is the security threat from malware [14]. Following a common approach in prior literature, we assume that a pirated version’ quality is proportional to the original product quality as captured by a degradation parameter [e.g., [5,48,55, 81]]. Specifically, given a posterior quality belief about the legal version ̃q, consumers’ quality belief about the pirated version is β̃q with $0 < \beta < 1$ , where $\beta$ measures the quality degradation for the pirated version.

Although piracy is “free” in the sense that it usually requires no monetary payment, a consumer incurs other costs in practicing piracy. For example, a consumer usually has to spend significantly more search costs (e.g., time and effort) in obtaining a pirated copy than simply purchasing a legal copy [65,74]. Pirating users also suffer disutility (e.g., fines, imprisonment, etc.) when detected by a government authority [4, 16,55]. Accordingly, we assume that consumers incur a piracy cost of r > 0 if they go for the pirated product. This includes both search and downloading costs and potential legal punishment costs.10Moreover, in line with prior literature, we associate a firm’s technical (preventive) and legal (deterrent) antipiracy measures with the piracy cost, r, to capture the essence of the firm’s efforts to reduce the attractiveness of a pirated product [e.g., [5,20,55]].

Consumers are heterogeneous in terms of their willingness to pay (preference) for quality, denoted as $\theta ,$ and $\theta \sim U [ 0 , 1 ]$ [e.g., 55]. So, given a posterior quality belief $\widetilde { \boldsymbol { q } }$ and the price p of the legal product, the utility of the legal version to a consumer with quality preference θ is θ̃q − $p ,$ and the utility of the pirated version is θβ̃q − r. ## 3.2.3. Choice of copyright protection

In reality, a firm might be able to exert some efforts (by incurring a non-negligible cost) to influence the level of piracy costs through various technical and legal measures. But due to various limitations and costs, it is unrealistic to assume that a firm can freely impose any desired level of piracy costs. To examine the firm’s incentive to affect copyright pro tection, we assume, more realistically, that the current level of piracy cost in the market is $r _ { L } > 0$ and that the firm decides whether or not to increase the piracy cost to r<sub>H</sub> $( r _ { H } > r _ { L } )$ with a cost of $C \geq 0 .$ . We focus on the firm’s optimal choice between r and $r _ { H } .$ . From the analysis in Sec tion 3.2.2, we know that increasing piracy costs does not necessarily benefit a firm’s profit; even if it does, the revenue gain from increasing piracy costs may not necessarily justify the cost the firm needs to expend to do so (i.e., C). So, straightforwardly, the firm’s optimal choice on copyright protection can be succinctly summarized in the following corollary.

Corollary 2. The firm will expend a cost of C to raise the piracy cost from r to r $i f$ and only $i f \pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r _ { H } ) - \pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r _ { L } ) > C ;$ , where $\pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r )$ is specified in Eq. (4) and Lemma 2 describes how $\pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r )$ changes with r.

That is, the firm will choose to increase the piracy cost if and only if the revenue gain from selling the digital product is sufficiently large to cover the cost C. Note that a necessary condition for $\pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r _ { H } ) - \pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r _ { L } )$ $> C \ ( \geq 0 )$ to hold is that $\pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r )$ increases with $r , \mathrm { i . e . , } \partial \pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r ) \ / \partial r > 0 .$ From Lemma 2, we can immediately see that this condition is more likely to be satisfied when the true quality q is sufficiently large; in other words, when q is small, it could be that $\partial \pi ^ { \sharp } ( q , r ) / \partial r < 0$ such that the firm will definitely prefer a lower piracy cost, even if C equals zero.

To explain this, for the surprising strategic effect highlighted in Proposition 2 to operate, the firm’s product quality needs to be below the demonstration threshold such that the firm does not offer product demonstrations. In other words, for a sufficiently high-quality seller who always optimally chooses to offer demonstrations, consumers would always know its products true quality; so the focal strategic effect would not apply. The implication is that high-quality products would always prefer more stringent copyright protection, whereas mediocre- or lowquality products might be averse to stringent copyright protection. This is because a higher piracy cost necessities an additional antipiracy cost. Moreover, a higher piracy cost may hurt the net profit even if it is free to strengthen copyright protection (i.e., $C = 0 )$ due to the focal strategic effect.

## 4. Extensions

We now explore two further extensions to the main model in Section 3.

## 4.1. Introducing fit uncertainty

So far, we have considered the case where consumers are uncertain about the (vertical) quality of the new digital product. In reality, con sumers often face uncertainty about whether the product fits their idiosyncratic tastes. In other words, different consumers may derive quite different values from the same product due to heterogeneous tastes. As noted in the Introduction, consumers are confronted with fit uncertainty in their decision-making processes when the digital goods entail some crucial experience attributes whose value to individual consumers can be ascertained only after they have experienced the products. Here, following the framework of Chellappa and Shivendu [15], we introduce a new parameter k to denote consumers’ perceptions, which indicate whether the product fits their tastes, and k is multipli cative to the product value consumers derive from the quality attributes. For example, setting aside product uncertainty, given product quality q, the value of the legal version to a consumer with quality preference θ and fit perception k is kθq and the value of the pirated version to the same consumer is kθβq.

We further assume, ex ante, that k follows a publicly known twopoint distribution and it can take only two possible values: $k = 0$ and k = 2 with equal probability (i.e., k may be 0 or 2 with $1 / 2$ probability). This is the case for each consumer regardless of her quality preference and quality uncertainty. Specifically, $k = 0$ means that the product does not fit a certain consumer’s taste and brings zero value to the consumer, and k = 2 means that the product fully fits the consumer’s taste. This specific parameterization of k is chosen to ensure that, ex ante (i.e., before the firm makes the demonstration decision), the current extended model (i.

$\mathrm { e } _ { \cdot , }$ when both quality and fit uncertainties are introduced) is comparable to the main model in Section $3 . 2 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . }$ ., when only quality uncertainty is considered). The main model with only quality uncertainty is equivalent to a case where each consumer has a fit expectation of 1. In the extended model, k does not take the value of 1, but it has an average value of 1, so that consumers, ex ante, have a fit expectation of 1.

The firm decides whether or not to offer a product demonstration to disclose the vertical quality q and resolve the fit uncertainty k at a fixed cost d. With demonstrations, consumers will know the true value of q and k; without demonstrations, consumers remain uncertain about q and k but will update their beliefs about q and k conditional on the firm’s decision. The updating mechanism regarding q is the same as in the main model, and the updating mechanism regarding k is as follows: if the firm offers product demonstrations, each consumer knows her true fit status; if the firm does not offer product demonstrations, consumers only take the expected value of the product fit (i.e., a value of 1). In the end, consumers still choose from three options: (i) buying a legal version, (ii) obtaining a pirated version of the product, or (iii) forgoing use completely.

This new setup represents a case where, on top of quality uncer tainty, consumers have additional fit uncertainty about the new product. Note that the trade-off on product quality q inherent in the firm’s demonstration decision remains the same as before, while the trade-off on the product fit k inherent in the firm’s demonstration decision is as follows: If the firm chooses to offer product demonstrations, half of the consumers will know that the product does not fit their tastes and thus exit the market, and the other half will know that the product perfectly fits their tastes and value the product more compared to when no product demonstrations were offered. By contrast, if the firm chooses not to offer product demonstrations, all consumers have the average (mediocre) fit perception.

We are thus primarily interested in the following two questions: (i) Would the focal strategic effect of piracy costs highlighted in the main model still be relevant in this extended model? And (ii) how would the additional fit uncertainty affect the firm’s incentive to offer product demonstrations and its equilibrium profit compared to when only quality uncertainty is present?

As in the main model, we still focus on the case when $0 < \beta \leq 2 ( 3 ~ -$ ${ \sqrt { 2 } } ) / 7 .$ . We also impose a similar condition on $d , \mathrm { i . e . , } 0 < d < 2 ( 1 $ $\beta ) ( 2 - 3 \beta ) / ( \beta ^ { 2 } - 3 2 \beta + 3 2 )$ , to rule out the trivial case where the firm never offers product demonstrations.

For ease of exposition, we label the main model in Section 3.2 as Case 1 and the current extended model as Case 2. The following proposition describes the firm’s demonstration incentive in Case 2 relative to Case 1.

Proposition 3. In Case 2, when $0 < \beta \leq 2 ( 3 - \sqrt { 2 } ) / 7$ and $0 ~ < d$ $< 2 ( 1 - \beta ) ( 2 - 3 \beta ) / ( \beta ^ { 2 } - 3 2 \beta + 3 2 ) ,$ , there is an unique threshold $\widehat { \boldsymbol { q } } ^ { \natural } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ such that the firm offers product demonstrations if and only if its true product quality $q > { \widehat { q } } ^ { \natural }$ . Moreover, as the piracy cost r increases, the demonstration threshold $\widehat { q } ^ { \sharp }$ first increases (when $0 < r < d \beta ( 4 - \beta ) / ( 2 - 3 \beta ) ) ,$ , then de creases (when $d \beta ( 4 - \beta ) / ( 2 - 3 \beta ) \le r < 8 d \beta ) ,$ , and eventually remains constant (when $r \geq 8 d \beta )$ . Proposition 3 shows that, facing additional consumer fit uncertainty, the firm’s demonstration incentive varies with the piracy cost r in a similar pattern as in the main model. However, the interesting difference is that in this extended model, the firm always (weakly) has less incen tive to offer product demonstrations. This is counter-intuitive at first sight, because in the current setting product demonstrations can “kill two birds with one stone” (i.e., they resolve both quality and fit un certainties), which would seem to result in more demonstration incen tive from the firm. This somewhat surprising result needs to be understood by recalling that resolving fit uncertainty has a segmentation effect: low-valuation consumers (due to a taste mismatch) are excluded from the market and the firm is then forced to focus on the highvaluation consumer segment; however, if the firm offers no product demonstrations, it effectively adopts a “mass market” approach by selling to both consumer segments. Thus, if the “mass market” strategy is relatively more profitable, the firm will have less incentive to offer product demonstrations (which renders the firm unable to adopt the “mass market” approach) when consumer fit uncertainty is present. The following lemma confirms that, when consumers have no uncertainty about product quality and fit, the genuine threat of piracy makes the “mass market” approach always more profitable than the “high pre mium” strategy.

Lemma 3. When there is no consumer uncertainty about product quality and fit, a firm with a given product quality q is always (weakly) worse off in Case 2 than in Case 1. The firm obtains equal payoffs in both cases if and only if the piracy cost is sufficiently large such that piracy poses no real threat in both cases.

Note that the effect of product demonstrations is to resolve product quality and fit uncertainty. Lemma 3 shows that, ceteris paribus, the firm always gains less from offering product demonstrations in Case 2 than in Case 1, if the piracy cost is not so large as to eliminate the threat of piracy. When the piracy cost is indeed sufficiently large to eliminate the threat of piracy, the firm makes the same profits in both cases. This explains why a firm always (weakly) has less incentive to offer product demonstrations, as highlighted in Proposition 3.

In essence, what Proposition 3 and Lemma 3 together show is that the segmentation effect of product demonstrations and the genuine threat of piracy interact with each other to reduce the firm’s incentive to offer product demonstrations to disclose its vertical quality.

The next lemma confirms that our key result obtained in the main model, as highlighted in Proposition 2, continues to hold in this extended model. That is, the surprising negative strategic effect of piracy costs on the firm’s profit is still relevant when both quality and fit un certainties are salient.

Lemma 4. In Case 2, given that the firm’s true product quality q is not especially high, such that it chooses not to offer product demonstrations, the firm’s profit may decrease with the piracy cost r when r falls into an inter mediary range.

Similar to the base model without fit uncertainty, given that the product quality is not high and, consequently, product demonstrations are not provided, increasing the piracy cost would lead to two coun teracting effects on the firm’s profit. On one hand, regardless of the fit uncertainty, as long as piracy is more costly for consumers, there is a weaker threat and an improvement in the firm’s profit. This is the deterrence effect.

On the other hand, if consumers hold that an increase in the piracy cost significantly benefits the firm’s profit such that it is more likely to offer product demonstrations, consumers will then anticipate lower product quality if the firm still chooses to conceal product information under a higher piracy cost. This effect still holds under consumer fit uncertainty. A lower quality belief held by consumers means a lower price that the firm can charge, which in turn hurts its profit. This is the strategic effect. Like with the base model without fit uncertainty, combining the two effects can lead to the counter-intuitive finding in Lemma 4 because the strategic effect can dominate the deterrence effect

Next we investigate how the introduction of fit uncertainty affects the firm’s equilibrium profit. Proposition 4 summarizes the key insights.

Proposition 4. (i) If the firm’s product quality q is sufficiently small such that the firm chooses not to offer product demonstrations in both Cases 1 and 2, the firm always obtains a larger profit in Case 2 than in Case 1.

(ii) If the firm’s product quality q is sufficiently large such that the firm chooses to offer product demonstrations in both Cases 1 and 2, the firm al ways obtains a larger profit in Case 1 than in Case 2.

This again boils down to the focal strategic effect of piracy costs on the firm’s profit. Therefore, a firm facing idiosyncratic consumer tastes is more likely to be a beneficiary of the focal strategic effect of piracy costs.

## 5. Implications

The focal insight of this economic analysis is that the publicly observable cost or difficulty of pirating digital goods may exert a stra tegic influence on a legal firm’s profit, in addition to the deterrence effect it has on users of unauthorized copies of the digital product. The strategic impact exists because piracy costs alter the firm’s product demonstration strategy and thus consumer expectations about product quality. Consequently, the firm may obtain a larger profit when copy right protection is less tight. This strategic impact only applies to a firm whose (vertical) quality is relatively low (i.e., a less favorable mix of attributes that consumers generally all desire). Therefore, for a firm’s star and signature products (e.g., blockbuster films, triple-A video games, and killer apps), in which the firm has invested a lot to ensure high levels of quality, the strategic influence of copyright protection is irrelevant. The profits from star products always benefit from tightened copyright protection.

Our analysis thus provides a new perspective to understand why different firms or even the same firm may hold noticeably varied atti tudes toward the impact of piracy on their products. For example, in the video-on-demand streaming industry, large established companies are always eager to strengthen copyright protection to fight piracy [59]. In the movie industry, small films seem to benefit from piracy while blockbusters do not [66]. Our mechanism centers on the role of product demonstrations in influencing consumers’ expectations about product quality. We showed that a firm may strategically coordinate its product demonstration strategy and the level of piracy cost or difficulty (as demonstrated by the practices of Nintendo and EA discussed in the Introduction) based on its true product quality to influence consumers expectations for the purpose of maximizing profits. In doing so, the firm may find it more profitable not to offer product demonstrations and to maintain a low level of piracy cost (or not to further strengthen copy right protection by adopting additional antipiracy instruments, even if those antipiracy instruments are costless).

Interestingly, when consumers are uncertain about both product quality and product fit (Section 4.1), we found that fit uncertainty can put a firm in a better position to benefit from the focal strategic effect of piracy costs on consumers’ quality expectations. This result has an interesting implication: Niche products are likely to be hurt more by a very strict copyright protection policy than mainstream and hit prod ucts. Two reasons might explain this in light of our model. First, de velopers of niche products (e.g., small indie studios or small development teams in large firms) often face more frugal budgets so that niche products generally encompass a less desirable mix of vertical quality attributes than mainstream products. Second, niche products, as their characteristic feature, often bring substantial value to fans (as it fits their taste) and practically no value to those antagonistic to the product (due to a taste mismatch), so sellers of niche products are even more likely to avail themselves of the focal strategic effect of piracy costs due to the segmentation effect inherent in offering product demonstrations (see Section 4.1). Hence, the fact that EA takes very different actions toward its FIFA franchise compared to its UFC franchise, as discussed in the Introduction, can be fairly interpreted as good anecdotal evidence consistent with our model’s conclusions. FIFA is certainly a more mainstream product than UFC among all EA’s sports game franchises, and EA invests much more in product demonstrations (for the purpose of disclosing quality) and antipiracy efforts in FIFA than in UFC releases.

It is widely acknowledged that increased product variety in digital goods industries (e.g., eBooks, music, movies, software, video games, etc.) has been a critical factor for societal welfare gain in the last two decades or so [1,12]. Maintaining the profitability of niche-product developers is therefore crucial in the interest of social welfare. Unlike prevailing viewpoints, our analysis suggests that the existence of piracy or slack copyright protection is not necessarily devastating to the prof itability of a niche product, although it is indeed detrimental to the profitability of a hit product. Social planners (e.g., public policymakers) should carefully evaluate the net impact of strengthening copyright protection before rolling out new legislation on piracy and copyright. Importantly, strengthening copyright protection can hurt social welfare directly because it renders a niche-product firm less profitable. More over, it can even hurt social welfare indirectly in the long run if it stifles product variety by forcing more niche-product developers to exit the market.

Our results thus have clear implications for government and public agencies, who can presumably largely affect the enforcement of copy right protection and thus piracy costs [16,55]. Instead of a universal antipiracy enforcement for all firms and products, our results suggest that it may be wise to tailor the efforts in cracking down on piracy ac cording to product types. More antipiracy efforts can be geared toward mainstream and hit products, while a more lenient approach can be adopted toward niche products. This is also practically feasible because in many digital-goods industries, the production budget can serve as a good indicator to distinguish niche products from mainstream products. So, for example, the government can invest more efforts to detect or set a higher penalty for unauthorized downloads of Game of Thrones than The Flash episodes.

In a more subtle and indirect manner, the government could provide subsidies or other incentives to encourage digital-product developers to adopt effective third-party antipiracy technologies. The cost of adopting an effective third-party antipiracy technology is not a trivial issue. For example, although Denuvo Anti-Tamper has proven to be the most effective antipiracy technology, as mentioned in the Introduction, the price of embedding Denuvo Anti-Tamper in a video game is intimidat ingly high, even for many large firms. In essence, our results suggest that when considering whether government should subsidize digital-product providers to adopt effective but expensive antipiracy technologies, it would be wise to design a subsidy scheme such that big-budget main stream product developers are subsidized more than niche-product developers.

## 6. Conclusion

Digital piracy has always been one of the most charged, complex, and divisive debates in digital-goods industries. It is generally believed that unlicensed illicit copying is detrimental to legal firms’ profitability. However, this belief has been challenged by academic researchers (see Section 2 for a summary). In practice, the past decade has witnessed increasingly divisive attitudes toward piracy in the industry. For example, EMI Music has completely abandoned DRM in all its tracks sold on the iTunes Store [6]. We aimed to contribute to this debate through a perspective that is largely neglected by previous research, namely, that a firm may strategically coordinate its product demonstrations and anti piracy efforts to manage consumers’ quality expectations and thus product profitability. As a result, we identified an important boundary condition for the common belief that tightening copyright enforcement benefits a legal firm, i.e., whether the common belief holds is critically contingent on the quality of the firm’s product.

We extended our model to incorporate consumers’ fit uncertainty about the product due to their idiosyncratic tastes. We showed that fit uncertainty further lowers a firm’s incentive to offer product demon strations and motivates the firm to leverage the strategic impact of piracy costs on its profit. More importantly, the key result that tight ening copyright enforcement may or may not benefit a legal firm re mains valid. We further extended our model to the case where quality degradation from piracy is less significant. We found that our main in sights are not dependent on the upper bound imposed on this quality degradation.

The economic mechanism we identified in this study does not rely on either demand- or supply-side externalities (e.g., network effects, indi rect appropriability, and softened competition) proposed in previous research. In this regard, we provided an alternative perspective on the benefits of piracy, particularly in markets where the network effect or competition is weak. Nevertheless, it remains as an important and challenging empirical issue to disentangle and compare all the possible mechanisms.

The current study can be extended in several meaningful directions. First, it is reasonable to assume that consumers may not know the demonstration cost accurately. So, formally incorporating consumers uncertainty about the true demonstration cost might be a good direction to extend the model, because consumers’ beliefs about the true demonstration cost will affect how they consider the firm’s decision not offer a demonstration. Second, one may extend the model to take into account the government’s decisions on copyright enforcement. The government may achieve this through various instruments, like fines upon detection, taxes on copying media, and subsidies for legal pur chases. Third, one can consider incorporating the way consumers learn about product quality over time—through word of mouth, for instan ce—in a dynamic setting. Lastly, it would be interesting to investigate the role of channel decentralization and horizontal competition in copyright enforcement, since legitimate consumer purchases can be differentially influenced by different channel members and competitors who may have different incentives for copyright enforcement.
