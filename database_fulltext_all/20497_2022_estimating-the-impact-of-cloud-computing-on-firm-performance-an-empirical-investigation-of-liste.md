# Estimating the impact of cloud computing on firm performance: An empirical investigation of listed firms

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cloud computing Cloud service Cloud adoption Firm performance ROA Tobin’s Q

## A B S T R A C T

This study empirically investigates the economic impact of cloud service adoption on firm performance. We exploit the difference-in-differences models coupled with propensity score-based matching to estimate the per formance effect of cloud computing on worldwide listed firms that adopted cloud service during 2010–2016. The results indicate that cloud computing has a significant and positive impact on the profitability and market value of listed firms with varying degrees in both short and long time periods. In addition, the magnitude of the performance effect varies with firm size and industry type. This research contributes to empirical examination on the economic values of IT investments.

## 1. Introduction

Cloud computing has evolved as a widely recognized information technology (IT) paradigm substantially transforming the delivery of IT services. As an innovative IT business model, cloud computing broadly refers to IT services over the Internet, where hardware and software computing services are delivered on-demand to end-users in a self service manner [1]. An increasing number of companies have devoted efforts to undertaking cloud computing to enhance their IT capabilities and transform operations and businesses. For example, General Electric Company began its digital transformation in 2014 and adopted Amazon Web Services to host more than 2000 cloud-based applications, which has helped the company improve operational efficiency and perfor mance [2]. According to a recent report from Gartner, the worldwide public cloud service spending will increase by 18.4% in 2021 with the total market reaching \$304.9 billion [3].

While many firms have proactively adopted cloud computing, there are other firms that hesitate to implement this novel IT service model as it remains unclear whether such cloud services can improve firm per formance. Some researchers posit that in comparison with traditional on-premise IT services, cloud computing is equipped with various stra tegic and operational benefits, such as improved organizational flexi bility and agility, higher IT capabilities, and shared resources and collaboration environment, which enable firms to achieve better per formance [4–6]. However, others argue that the adoption of cloud computing requires considerable efforts, such as high purchase expenses and investments in employee training [4], which entail additional costs and thus may impair firm performance. Moreover, Jede and Teuteberg [7] contend that the implementation of cloud computing exerts a negative influence on IT professionals’ perceived job outcomes and process performance, which might harm firm performance as well.

Given this continuing controversy and the growing importance of cloud computing, it is essential to empirically examine the performance implications of cloud computing in order to guide theory and practice development regarding IT investment [8]. Although the craze of cloud computing has attracted enormous academic interests, extant research on cloud computing is still in its infancy with extensive conceptual studies on adoption intention [9], followed by a few studies on the pricing and technical issues [10–12]. There is a paucity of empirical research that utilizes secondary data to examine the effects of cloud computing on actual firm performance [13,14]. Moreover, prior studies are mainly confined to small and medium-sized companies [15–18]. It is still unclear whether cloud computing can add economic values to publicly listed firms that are more mature and developed [19].

To bridge the above research gaps, this study aims to empirically examine the effects of cloud computing on firm performance. Following previous literature [20], we focus specifically on two complementary indicators of firm performance: firm profitability (measured by return on assets (ROA)) and market value (measured by Tobin’s Q). Further more, cloud computing may have heterogeneous performance effects on firms with different characteristics, and thus, contingency conditions deserve attention in better understanding the link between cloud computing and firm performance. Overall, this study intends to answer the following research questions: (1) Can cloud computing, as an inno vative IT delivery model, affect firm performance? (2) What is the magnitude of the short- and long-term impacts of cloud service adoption on firm performance? And (3) Can cloud computing have heterogeneous performance impacts on firms with different characteristics?

To address these research questions, we focus on worldwide listed firms that adopted cloud computing service during the period from 2010 to 2016. We propose econometric models and employ the difference-indifferences (DID) approach with propensity score-based matching (PSM) to estimate the effects of cloud service adoption on firm performance. Akin to a quasi-experiment design, the “treatment” group contains firms adopting cloud service during the 2010–2016 period and the “control” group is a matched counterpart without adopting any cloud service during that period. Then, the effect of cloud computing on firm per formance is revealed by the DID-matching estimator representing the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT). To investigate both the short- and long-term performance effects, we estimate a series of ATTs in the current adoption year and up to five years after adoption.

This study provides several interesting findings. Our results indicate that firms adopting cloud computing experience significantly improved profitability and market value, measured by ROA and Tobin’s Q, respectively. Specifically, the baseline results suggest that the profit ability of adopters can significantly increase by 0.0686 (in the current adoption year), 0.0700 (in one year after adoption), 0.0727 (in two years after adoption), 0.0877 (in three years after adoption), 0.1100 (in four years after adoption), and 0.1420 (in five years after adoption) on average as compared with non-adopters in the control group. Mean while, the average Tobin’s Q of cloud adopters exceeds non-adopters by 2.455 (in the current year), 2.047 (in one year after the adoption), and 1.804 (in two years after the adoption), respectively. We also extend the baseline models by accounting for various determinants of firm perfor mance and obtain consistent results. Beside, our findings are robust even after controlling for serial correlation and using alternative model specifications and subsample regressions.

In addition, we reveal the critical role of industry type and firm size in altering the performance effects of cloud service adoption. Interest ingly, we find that manufacturing firms have significantly higher level of profitability after adopting cloud computing than service firms, whereas the effect of cloud computing on market value is significantly stronger for firms in service industries as compared with manufacturing in dustries. Moreover, after adopting cloud computing, small firms accomplish higher improvement in profitability than big firms, whereas big firms achieve significantly higher market value than small firms.

This study has several important theoretical and managerial impli cations. First, we add to the scant literature on the empirical examina tion of IT business value by demonstrating both short- and long-term causal impacts of cloud computing on firm performance. Second, our results highlight the importance of contextual factors in altering the performance effects of IT investment, which advances knowledge on the understanding of contingency factors for the success of IT investment. Third, different from prior studies largely focusing on small and young firms, we provide strong empirical evidence that listed firms that are more mature and developed can also attain significant economic bene fits from cloud computing. These results can offer incentives and guidelines to managers in their search for effective and successful cloud computing strategies.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the relevant literature and develop our hypotheses. Then, we introduce the data collection process, measures and variables, and provide a descriptive analysis in Section 3. Research models are proposed in Section 4, followed by the results in Section 5. We discuss the theoretical contributions and managerial implications of this research in Section 6 and reach the conclusion in Section 7.

## 2. Literature review and hypotheses development

## 2.1. Related research on cloud computing

Both academia and industry have defined cloud computing from different perspectives (see Madhavaiah et al. [21] for a comprehensive review). These definitions have either implicitly or explicitly acknowl edged that cloud computing is a novel IT service delivery model equipped with a “3–4–5” framework. Namely, there are three types of services (i.e., IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS), four deployment models (i.e., public, private, community, and hybrid cloud), and five fundamental characteristics (i.e., on-demand service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, and measured service; [1,4,22,23]). These critical elements distinguish cloud computing from traditional IT service model.

Extant research mainly draws upon the resource-based view (RBV; [24]) to document the advantages and benefits of cloud computing from both strategic and operational perspectives [1,4,8,13]. For example, a large body of literature has suggested that cloud computing can bring important operational benefits for firms, such as cost reduction [1,25] and improvement in operational efficiency [26]. It is also argued that the adoption of cloud computing can transform the role of IT department from regular maintenance to business assistance, and improve both intra- and inter-firm collaborations, therefore facilitating innovations [27–29]. In addition, cloud computing enables firms to achieve a higher level of strategic agility and flexibility. Berman et al. [27] maintain that cloud computing has the ability to shift the competitive landscape of companies through business value creation. Similarly, Luo et al. [28] pinpoint that cloud computing can provide great strategic value that helps companies acquire competitive advantages.

Recent studies have begun to focus on the performance effects of cloud computing on operations and businesses. However, this stream of research is still in its nascent stage with the majority of work dominated by theoretical conceptualization or analytical modelling of cloud computing. A large body of literature suggests a positive impact of cloud computing on business, operations, and strategy. For example, Luo et al. [28] propose an integrated theoretical framework that draws upon theories on technological frame of reference, diffusion on innovation, and dynamic capabilities to conceptualize how organizations can utilize cloud computing to achieve sustainable competitive advantage. This work provides a theoretical connection between cloud computing adoption and its strategic impact on organizations, which responds to Merali et al. [30] who posit that strategic information system (IS), such as cloud computing has the potential to add value to businesses. Chou et al. [31] examine cloud service adopters from a few representative industries (e.g., IT, travel and tourism, finance, and banking sectors) in Taiwan and find that the adoption of cloud services is positively asso ciated with service innovation. Liu et al. [19] survey 184 users of Ali baba Cloud in China and evidence that cloud computing flexibility and integration are positively related to organizational agility. Similarly, based on a survey of 147 Indian firms, Kathuria et al. [14] investigate how firms can capitalize on cloud computing to enhance performance and propose a strategic value appropriation path for adopters to improve their business performance. Loukis et al. [32] conduct a survey of 102 Dutch firms and conclude that both operational and innovational ben: efits of SaaS can have positive impacts on firm performance. Other studies also reveal a positive association between cloud computing success and performance but yield different findings on the influence mechanism. For instance, drawing upon the theory of transaction cost economics, Schniederjans and Hales [33] collect survey data in the U.S. and observe that cloud computing is positively associated with the economic and environmental performance and this positive relationship is partially mediated by supply chain collaboration. Garrison et al. [34] analyze survey data of 302 organizations and find that managerial, technical, and relational IT capabilities can significantly influence the success of cloud computing implementation, and the effect of cloud computing on performance is moderated by different cloud delivery structures. Apart from internal IT capabilities, Khayer et al. [35] survey 300 Chinese firms and uncover that external factors, such as end-user satisfaction can also affect cloud success, which contributes to firm performance as well. Different from above conceptual research that unexceptionally analyze survey data, Son et al. [8] utilize secondary data to investigate the economic value of cloud computing from the perspective of investors’ reaction to the stock market. They observe that the stock market tends to react positively to firms’ announcements of cloud computing initiatives. This indicates that the financial market can recognize the value of cloud computing immediately, which justifies the positive effect of cloud computing on firm performance.

The aforementioned studies predominantly reveal a positive rela tionship between cloud service adoption and outcomes of organizations. However, several studies corroborate that cloud computing can also induce a negative influence on outcomes. For example, Jede and Teu teberg [7] scrutinize the impact of SaaS applications on the perception of job outcomes and process performance for employees from IT depart ment. Their survey results showcase that SaaS adoptions can exert a higher negative influence on IT professionals’ perceived job outcomes and process performance when the perceived organizational or technical changes due to SaaS adoption are more radical. This result is also consistent with previous literature on IT adoption suggesting that IT professionals may hold a negative attitude toward applying a system to complex core businesses which require enormous specialty and domain knowledge [36,37].

In addition, some studies develop analytical models or use simula tions to examine the pricing [10] and usage [11,12] of cloud computing and the associated economic consequences [38]. For instance, Choudhary [10] compares the SaaS-based licensing model with the traditional perpetual model and reveals that SaaS can lead to increased investments in product development and improved quality of IT services as compared with perpetual licensing model. Choudhary and Vithayathil [11] develop a stylized analytical model to investigate the circum stances under which the adoption of SaaS can positively affect the organizational structure of a firm’s IT department as a cost center or a profit center. Beside, Fazli et al. [12] employ analytical models to examine how the autoscaling feature of cloud computing affects a firm’s decision-making of market entry and the resultant equilibrium prices, profitability, and consumer surplus. Based on a multi-agent simulation, Yu et al. [38] investigate the role of cloud computing in enhancing collaboration and further simulate the impact of collaboration on service levels in supply chains.

To summarize, the literature review identifies several significant research gaps. First, existing literature is dominated by conceptual or analytical work investigating the antecedents of cloud service success and technical issues of cloud computing. More importantly, the extant studies have mixed arguments regarding whether the implementation of cloud computing can add economic values to adopters. Most empirical research is based on survey analysis which focuses on the role of cloud computing in perceived operational or financial performance. There is a paucity of empirical research that utilizes secondary datasets to examine both short- and long-term causal effects of cloud computing on actual firm performance that considers both firm profitability and market value. Second, it is interesting and meaningful to investigate whether contingency factors, such as industry type and firm size can moderate the effect of cloud computing on firm performance, which has not been addressed in previous literature. Third, the majority of studies focus on the impact of cloud computing on small and medium-sized firms or restrict the analysis to a narrow scope of industries. There is a dearth of research providing empirical evidence on publicly listed firms that are more mature and developed. Finally, prior literature substantially doc uments the operational or strategic impact of cloud computing but has paid very limited attention to the financial outcomes, such as firm profitability and market value. One exception is Son et al. [8] who investigate the stock market reaction to firms’ announcements of cloud computing initiatives. However, this study considers announced rather than actual adoption. Beside, Son et al. [8] only consider short-term stock returns, which is unable to uncover the long-term impact of cloud computing on firm profitability and market value. Therefore, it is important and necessary to unravel the impact of actual cloud service adoption on long-term performance outcomes to more accurately assess its economic values [39].

## 2.2. The effects of cloud computing on firm performance

We adopt RBV of the firm as our theoretical lens. RBV suggests that firms acquire competitive advantage and superior performance through resources and capabilities that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable [24,40]. RBV describes firm as a unique collection of re sources [24]. A firm’s resources include tangible (e.g., raw materials and equipment) and intangible assets (e.g., firm image, information, and human capital), which are either owned or controlled by the firm [40, 41]. Previous studies have widely employed RBV to explain how IT re sources contribute to a firm’s competitive edge and how a firm uses its IT resources to achieve superior performance [20,42]. Recently, building on RBV, Son et al. [8] explore how cloud computing influences firms anticipated performance and thus stock market reaction. Liu et al. [43] also utilize RBV to discuss how cloud computing affects external inte gration, internal operations, and firm innovation. Therefore, in this study, we apply RBV as a useful theoretical lens to develop insights into the effects of cloud computing on firm performance.

Particularly, we posit that as a novel IT service delivery model. cloud computing can help firms enhance IT capabilities, improve organiza tional flexibility and agility, and facilitate better intra- and interorganizational collaborations, which can serve as valuable and rare re sources [4,27]. Such resources derived from cloud computing can create business value and competitive edge for firms [43]. Furthermore, pre vious research suggests that organizations are confronted with various challenges and uncertainties when developing strategies for the execu tion of migrating business to the cloud [34]. This leads to considerable investments in firm-specific resources (e.g., time, financial capital, human resources, domain knowledge). Moreover, an emerging and crucial trend is to enhance the integration of firms' core businesses into the cloud and utilize customized cloud services and products, thus increasing the degree of difficulty for competitors to imitate in the short term [8]. In addition, the implementation of cloud computing enables firms to deploy their IT resources more effectively and flexibly. This contributes to the integration of IT capacities into firm-specific value chain activities, which could lead to unique competitive advantage that is difficult to imitate by competitors [43]. Beside, researchers have maintained that cloud computing can empower organizations by allowing them to achieve seamless coordination among supply chain partners and establish more collaborative relationships [33,38], which could promote business innovations [8,29]. Also, the adoption of cloud computing can provide novel channels for firms to innovate processes, products, and customer services that are particular to specific firms [31]. Such innovative and unique products, process routines, or services could not be easily substituted by rivals. thus contributing to the achievement of competitive advantage [43]. In short, from the RBV perspective, we contend that cloud computing is beneficial for firms to gain strategic resources and capabilities, which enable them to attain competitive advantage and better performance.

Specifically, we conjecture that the adoption of cloud computing can help improve firm profitability. First, cloud computing is an on-demand service model with a “pay-as-you-use” pricing scheme [1]. Companies can pay for the usage (e.g., hardware, platform, and application) without the hassle of investing in physical IT infrastructure. This can significantly lower the IT deployment time, avoid the upfront IT in vestments, and allow for the conversion from fixed costs of capitals to alterable costs of operations [5]. Hence, compared with traditional IT services, cloud computing can reduce the complexity of IT operations and is more cost-effective, which can yield higher operational benefits for firms [6,8].

Second, IT resources can be easily accessed via cloud service plat forms without the restriction of device, time, and location [8]. These solutions enable firms to scale the resources as needed and utilize them flexibly, which can enhance their IT capabilities and organizational agility in operations and strategy $[ 1 , 4 , 6 , 1 3 , 1 9 ]$ . Consequently, cloud computing can help improve firms’ IT capabilities and facilitate business value creation, thereby boosting their competitive advantage and per formance [27,28,30].

Third, the adoption of cloud computing is beneficial for firms to enlarge their revenues. Because cloud computing adoption can lead to improved management flexibility and organizational agility, companies can shift more attention from regular IT maintenance and imple mentation to dedication in business innovations and better customer services [27–29]. This helps firms maintain a high level of customer service that can result in increased revenues. Moreover, cloud computing can facilitate information sharing between firms and external partners and strengthen favorable relationships and collaborations [8, 33,38]. Thus, firms can respond to customers’ demands more rapidly and effectively, which, as a result, enhances customer satisfaction and revenues.

In short, the implementation of cloud computing can help firms reduce the complexity and costs of IT operations, improve organiza tional flexibility and agility, and promote business innovations. This contributes to cost reduction and revenue growth, thereby enhancing firm profitability. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H1a. Cloud computing has a positive effect on firm profitability.

Previous studies suggest that firm performance is a multidimensional concept [20] and measure it with both accounting-based (i.e., firm profitability) and financial market-based metrics (i.e., mar ket value). Different from firm profitability that is based on historical data, market value is a forward-looking performance measure because it can reflect investors’ evaluation of firms’ future growth potential [44]. Beside, market value is risk-adjusted and more robust to fluctuations in accounting practice [45]. Prior studies on IT business value have high lighted the importance of measuring firm performance with market value due to its ability to capture firms’ long-term growth potential and intangible value [46–52]. Thus, to provide a more comprehensive view of the performance effects of cloud computing, we further explore the linkage between cloud computing and market value.

Specifically, we posit that the implementation of cloud computing is helpful for firms to improve their market value. It is widely acknowl edged that cloud computing can yield various strategic and operational benefits for adopters $[ 2 5 , 2 6 , 2 8 , 3 2 ]$ ]. Therefore, the adoption of cloud computing conveys a signal to investors that firms are likely to achieve better future performance, which leads to higher market value. More over, prior research shows that the stock market reacts positively to the announcements of cloud computing initiatives since such announce ments signal to investors that firms can obtain better future cash flows [8]. Furthermore, it is argued that firms implementing cloud computing can generate market-based intangible assets, such as customer satisfac tion [31], which in turn could reduce uncertainty about firms’ future earnings. This can also lead to investors’ positive assessments of firms future growth potential, thereby enabling firms to attain higher market value. For these reasons, we propose the following hypothesis:

## H1b. Cloud computing has a positive effect on market value.

## 2.3. Heterogeneous performance effects between manufacturing and service industries

Previous studies have shown that cloud computing has been applied to different sectors, such as manufacturing and service industries, which may lead to different performance effects [43]. Therefore, in this study, we further examine whether the effect of cloud computing on firm performance is heterogeneous across manufacturing and service in dustries. Specifically, we posit that the effect of cloud computing on firm profitability is stronger for firms in manufacturing industries than those in service ones. This is because manufacturing firms mainly engage in the production of tangible products, which is more capital intensive and tends to have higher operational complexity than service firms that are centered on intangible services [53,54]. As a result, the implementation of cloud computing can be more effective for manufacturing firms to develop IT capabilities to reduce the operational complexity induced by the characteristics of products or production processes [53]. As such, cloud computing can bring more operational benefits (e.g., cost reduc tion, and flexibility and agility improvement) for manufacturing firms, thereby leading to higher profitability for them. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2a: The positive effect of cloud computing on firm profitability is stronger for firms in manufacturing industries than those in service industries.

Given that market value is different from firm profitability, we further investigate how industry type moderates the impact of cloud computing on market value. We conjecture that cloud computing has a greater effect on the market value of firms in service industries than those in manufacturing sectors. Different from firm profitability, market value is a forward-looking performance measure which reflects the intangible value and future growth potential of firms [45]. Prior research has highlighted the capability of cloud computing to improve service quality and establish favorable customer relationships that are essentially valued in service industries [38,55]. Under this scenario, the adoption of cloud computing tends to be more effective for service firms that dedicate to the improvement in customer relationships and intan gible value. This can raise the market’s anticipation about the future growth of service firms. As a result, it is possible that cloud computing can have a greater potential to promote the intangible value of service firms than that of manufacturing firms, thereby contributing to higher market value for service firms [45]. Therefore, we posit the following hypothesis:

H2b: The positive effect of cloud computing on market value is stronger for firms in service industries than those in manufacturing industries.

## 2.4. Heterogeneous performance effects between big and small firms

Previous IS studies have also emphasized the pivotal role of firm size in influencing the business value derived from IT-related investments [56]. Hence, we further examine the heterogeneous effects of cloud computing on the performance of big and small firms. Specifically, we posit that the effect of cloud computing on firm profitability is greater for small firms as compared with big firms. This is due to the structural advantages owned by small firms, which enable them to gain more benefits from cloud computing. Compared with big firms, small firms have less structural inertia, which is more beneficial for the efficient use of cloud computing services [57]. This enables small firms to attain more operational benefits from the implementation of cloud computing, thereby yielding higher profitability for them. In addition, cloud computing can benefit small firms with poor IT capacity by allowing them to gain better access to sufficient computing resources with no upfront IT investments [8,58]. This expands opportunities for small firms to further reduce operational costs and pay more attention to their core businesses, which can result in higher competitive advantage and firm profitability. Therefore, as compared with big firms, small firms are likely to acquire more operational and strategic benefits from cloud computing, thus achieving greater profitability. This leads to the

## following hypothesis:

H3a: The positive effect of cloud computing on firm profitability is stronger for small firms than big firms.

In addition, firm size is an influential factor that determines the extent to which the stock market recognizes the future growth potential of listed firms that adopted cloud computing. In this study, we further argue that the effect of cloud service adoption on market value is stronger for big firms than small firms. This is because compared with small firms, big firms tend to have higher visibility and popularity [59], and hence, they normally gain significantly higher market attention and expectation [8]. As a result, the adoption of cloud computing by big firms can be more appealing and influential to investors, which leads to greater market evaluation. In contrast, investors generally pay less attention to small firms due to lower publicity and visibility, which may lead to underestimated future performance and lower market evaluation for small firms. Overall, this suggests that the impact of cloud service adoption on market value of big firms is greater than that of small firms. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H3b: The positive effect of cloud computing on market value is stronger for big firms than small firms.

## 3. Data collection, description, and variables definition

## 3.1. Cloud computing adopters

This study attempts to identify the worldwide listed firms that adopted cloud computing during the period of 2010–2016 based on firms’ client announcements on the S&P’s Capital IQ Platform. We chose this period because it involves both the exploration and burgeoning states of cloud computing [6], which is an appropriate timing to eval uate the early success of cloud computing. Table 1 shows the detailed steps that we follow to identify cloud adopters from the client announcements.

Specifically, we first utilized a set of related keywords to search for the client announcements in the “Business Description” module on the S&P’s Capital IQ Platform, which is a professional financial database that provides abundant business and financial information for global firms. We searched for highly relevant keywords including “cloud,” “cloud computing,” “cloud service,” “on-demand service,” “public cloud,” “private cloud,” “SaaS,” “PaaS,” “IaaS,” “Software as a Service,” “Platform as a Service,” and “Infrastructure as a Service.” This pre liminary keyword search resulted in a total of 49,791 client announce ments. Then, we read these announcements one by one and carefully screened out relevant announcements. This step resulted in 22,017 an nouncements that contained information of both the provision and adoption of cloud computing service. Next, to further determine the set of adopters in a more complete and accurate manner, we also visited the company’s official websites, and searched for technical reports (e.g., “the State of Cloud”) and news from mainstream media (e.g., The Wall Street Journal, New York Times, Google News) and professional database (e.g., Factiva). Because this study focuses on publicly listed firms, we excluded announcements related to unlisted companies as well as governmental and other institutional adopters. Beside, for the sake of a clean estimation, we further removed adopters that are cloud providers simultaneously.

## 3.3. Measures and variables

Previous research suggests that firm performance is a multidimensional concept [20] and measures it with both accounting and financial market-based metrics [49–51]. Therefore, in this study, we employ both ROA and Tobin’s Q ratio as measures of firm performance. ROA is a measure of firm profitability and is defined as a firm’s net in come divided by its book value of total assets. Different from ROA that relies on historical accounting information, Tobin’s Q ratio is a financial market-based measure and has the ability to address the limitations of accounting measures due to its forward-looking and risk-adjusted nature [48]. Following prior literature [45,51], we define Tobin’s Q as the ratio of a firm’s market value to the replacement costs of its assets. We use the book value of total assets as the measure of replacement costs.

To study the performance effects of cloud service adoption, we also account for a set of control variables to rule out alternative explanations from firm fundamentals and contextual factors, such as industry and geographical location. Firstly, previous research suggests that it is important to control for the historical trends in explaining the de terminants of firm performance [45]. Thus, we take the historical trends of ROA and Tobin’s Q into consideration. Secondly, we incorporate firm-specific factors, such as financial slack, SGA (selling, general, and administrative) intensity, and employee scale because they can reflect the resource availability for IT-related investment and affect the po tential performance outcomes [61]. We also calculate the EM (emerging market) score based on Altman [62] as it reflects whether a firm is financially healthy [61]. In addition, since accounting factors, such as leverage and research and development (R&D) intensity reflect the level of managerial risk and intangible value creation associated with a firm [45,63], we control for their influence on firm performance. Lastly, we consider the impact of contextual factors, such as industry and geographical location because they are important sources of differences in explaining the level of firm performance [51]. Specifically, we use the top three-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes to account for industry-level heterogeneity. With regard to location-based factors, we use a dummy variable to indicate whether a firm is headquartered at North America and use the standard three-digit country codes to control for country-level heterogeneity. ## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Identification strategy

An ideal way to estimate the causal impact is to conduct randomized controlled trails or employ natural events that provide unplanned in terventions in the absence of random assignment. Both methods can ensure that the exposure to “treatment” (i.e., adoption of cloud computing services) is exogenous to the potential outcome $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , firm performance). However, neither random assignment nor natural experiment is feasible for this study because the decision to adopt cloud computing may be inherently related to observed and/or unobserved factors, which, as a result, leads to the endogeneity issue.

To overcome such identification challenges, we utilize a DID method to eliminate the model specification bias due to time-invariant unobseryables. DID models have been widely used in IS research to investigate the causal effect of a “treatment,” such as a policy or IT strategy. In this study, the treatment group contains the 329 worldwide listed companies that adopted cloud computing services during the period from 2010 to 2016. An ideal control group should be the same set of listed companies that did not adopt any cloud computing services during this period. However, due to such counterfactuals, we turn to derive a matched set of control firms that shared similar characteristics with the treated firms but did not adopt cloud computing during that time period. Our original set of control firms includes other publicly listed firms that are in the same industries (measured by two-digit SIC codes) and regions (measured by three-digit country codes) as listed firms from the treatment group.

## 4.2. Matching

We apply a PSM approach to derive a matched sample of control firms. PSM is a popular econometrical technique which is widely used for causal inference under quasi experiments. It can eliminate the po tential endogeneity concern due to self-selection based on observables [64]. Furthermore, as compared with other matching techniques, such as coarsened exact matching, PSM can overcome the curse of dimen sionality when companies are confronted with many observed cova riates. Therefore, this study implements the PSM technique. We first use a binary choice model $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , logit model) to predict the propensity score, which is the conditional probability for each company to be assigned to the treatment group given a set of observed covariates.

Previous literature suggests that firms usually spend one year to initiate and deploy an IT solution before they publicly announce its adoption or implementation [39]. This suggests that if t is the actual adoption year, then t − 1 will be the initiation time for the cloud project. Hence, it is reasonable to use information at year $t - 2$ to measure a firm’s propensity to adopt cloud computing. As discussed earlier, variables for the estimation of propensity scores are shown in Table 3, which include various factors influencing IT investment as suggested by prior literature [51,61,63].

Based on the estimated propensity scores, for each treated firm i, we perform a caliper matching to find the control set $J _ { i }$ within a small caliper distance (i.e., 0.01) as

$$
J _ {i} = \left\{\forall j \text {   st.   } \left| \widehat {p} _ {i} - \widehat {p} _ {j} \right| \leq 0. 0 1 \text {   and   } i, j \text {   in   the   common   support   } \right\}\tag{1}
$$

If $J _ { i }$ is not empty, we further apply a k-nearest neighbor matching with replacement to identify four matched control firms $( k = 4$ in this study) that have the nearest propensity scores with the treated firm i. Otherwise, we simply drop firm i from the treatment group due to the lack of matched counterpart.

## 4.3. Econometric models

## 4.3.1. Baseline model

Based on the treated and matched control groups, we propose the following DID models to estimate the effect of cloud computing adoption on firm performance.

$$
\text { Outcome } _ {i, t} = \alpha + \beta \text { Adopt } _ {i, t} + \delta \text { Post } _ {i, t} + \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}\tag{2}
$$

In Eq. (2), Outcome is $R O A _ { i , t }$ or Tobin $Q _ { i , t }$ for firm i at year t. Adopt is an indicator which equals one if firm i has adopted cloud computing services at year t and zero otherwise. For brevity, we have defined $A d o p t _ { i , t } = T r e a t _ { i } \times P o s t _ { i , t } ,$ where Treat is a binary variable indicating whether firm i is in the treatment group, and $P o s t _ { i , t }$ is an indicator of the post-treatment period, which equals one if year t is in the post-treatment period for firm i and zero otherwise $, \mu _ { i }$ and $\tau _ { t }$ are the firm- and time-fixed effects, respectively, and $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is the model residual. Note that the firmfixed effect $\mu _ { i }$ will absorb the time-invariant variable Treat , and thus, we do not include Treati into our models.

Previous literature advises on examination of the IT performance effects in a relatively longer horizon because it may take several years to translate the investments in IT (e.g., cloud computing) into the realized performance [39,45,65]. In line with this appeal, we aim to estimate the effect of cloud service adoption on firm performance for the current adoption year $\left( t \right) = t _ { 0 } )$ and up to five years after the adoption $( t = t _ { 1 } .$ $\cdot . . t _ { 5 } )$ , respectively. This also contributes to a deeper understanding about both the short- and long-term impact of cloud service adoption on firm profitability and market value.

## 4.3.2. Extended model

In Eq. (3), we extend the baseline model by accounting for a set of observed covariates $\mathbf { X } _ { i , t } ,$ including firm-specific characteristics $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ total assets, number of employees, financial slack, and EM score), in dustry (i.e., three-digit SIC code), and geographical location $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ country) to further rule out alternative explanations from firm characteristics.

$$
\text { Outcome } _ {i, t} = \alpha + \beta \text { Adopt } _ {i, t} + \delta \text { Post } _ {i, t} + \gamma \mathbf {X} _ {i, t} + \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}\tag{3}
$$

## 4.3.3. Models of heterogeneous treatment effects

We further explore the heterogeneity in the treatment effect resulting from cloud service adoption. Previous literature highlights the impor tance of sector differences in studying cloud computing because different industries $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ , manufacturing and service industries) can have distinct drivers of cloud service adoption [43,51]. Thus, we further investigate the heterogeneous treatment effects by comparing firms in manufacturing and service industries. In Eq. (4), Ind dummy<sub>i,t</sub> is a binary variable which equals one if firm i at year t belongs to the manufacturing industry and zero for the service industry. Thus, the estimated coeffi cient for the interaction term $A d o p t _ { i , t } \times I n d \_ d u m m y _ { i , t }$ indicates whether the effect of cloud service adoption is heterogeneous across manufacturing and service industries.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Outcome } _ {i, t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text { Adopt } _ {i, t} \times \text { Ind\_dummy } _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} \text { Adopt } _ {i, t} + \beta_ {3} \text { Ind\_dummy } _ {i, t} \\ & + \beta_ {4} \text { Post } _ {i, t} + \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

In addition, previous research attaches importance to firm size due to its importance in altering the effect sizes of performance [8,66]. Hence, we further investigate whether cloud service adoption has varying effects on the performance of big and small firms. Following Son et al. [8], we use the logarithm of total assets as a proxy for firm size and calculate the median value of firm size based on our sample. Therefore, firms above (below) the median firm size are defined as big (small) firms. In Eq. (5), $I s B i g _ { i , t }$ is a dummy variable which equals one if firm i is a big firm at year $t ,$ and zero otherwise. If the coefficient for the interaction term Adopt × IsBig is significant, then the impact of cloud service adoption on firm performance is heterogeneous across big and small firms.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Outcome } _ {i, t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text { Adopt } _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} \text { Adopt } _ {i, t} \times \text { IsBig } _ {i, t} + \beta_ {3} \text { IsBig } _ {i, t} + \beta_ {4} \text { Post } _ {i, t} \\ & \quad + \mu_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Evaluation of matching performance

## 5.1.1. Overlap assumption

A critical prerequisite of using PSM is that the propensity scores for treated and control groups should have a large overlap region before matching. Fig. 2 shows the distributions for the treated and control groups before and after matching. We find that before matching, the distributions for treated and control firms have a very large overlap area, which implies that the overlap assumption can be satisfied. We also observe that the overlap region expands greatly after matching. This implies that utilizing PSM to derive the matched control firms is feasible and appropriate.

## 5.1.2. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test

We also conduct a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test for the equality of distributions of propensity scores for treated and control groups before and after matching. Following its definition, we calculate the statistics $D = \operatorname* { s u p } \{ | F _ { 1 } ( p ) - F _ { 2 } ( p ) | \}$ as the supreme of the absolute difference in two empirical cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of propensity scores. A small value of D lends little support to the refutation of the null hypothesis that the two samples have equal distributions, whereas a large D can be more convincing to reach the conclusion that the two CDFs are different.

In Table 4, we find that before matching (see row 1), the distributions of propensity scores for treated and unmatched control firms are significantly different (p-value=0.000). However, after matching (see row 2), the treatment group and matched control group tend to have equal distributions of propensity scores (p-value=0.971). This implies that our matching procedure has successfully derived the matched counterparts of the treated firms. A further look at the unmatched and matched control firms (see row 3) suggests that the two groups have significantly different propensity score distributions (p-value=0.000). Overall, the results in Table 4 reveal that the difference in propensity scores between treated and control firms have been significantly reduced after matching, indicating that our matching approach is useful and effective.

## 6. Discussion

To our best knowledge, this research is one of the earliest attempt that empirically investigate the impact of cloud computing on both market and accounting-based firm performance. We propose econo metric models based on the DID method with PSM approach and esti mate the average treatment effect on worldwide listed firms that adopted cloud computing service during the 2010–2016 period.

## 6.1. Research contributions

This study has the following important contributions. First, we contribute to the research on IT business value by providing the strong evidence on the impact of cloud service adoption on firm performance in terms of firm profitability and market value. Prior research highlights the importance of linking IT investment with organizational perfor mance [72] and calls for more empirical investigations of the perfor mance effects of cloud computing other than the technical issues and adoption intention [6,23]. Our study contributes to this research stream by representing one of the first attempts to quantify the economic ben efits accrued from cloud computing.

Second, we empirically demonstrate that the economic impact of cloud computing is heterogeneous across different contextual factors, thus advancing the knowledge of how contingency factors play a role in affecting IT performance. We find that the effect size of cloud service adoption depends on contingency factors, such as industry type and firm size. Cloud computing has a significantly higher impact on the level of profitability of small firms and firms in manufacturing industries than that of big firms and firms in service industries, whereas it has a significantly higher impact on the market value of big firms and firms in service industries than that of small firms and firms in manufacturing industries. Overall, these results add to IT investment literature by elaborating that both firm and industry-level factors are crucial in determining the level of IT economic values and have differential moderating effects on firm profitability and market value.

Furthermore, this study also provides interesting findings about the impact of cloud service adoption on market value of firms measured by Tobin’s Q. On the one hand, our baseline results suggest that the positive impact of cloud service adoption on Tobin’s Q is a relatively short-term phenomenon. It is possibly due to the cost reduction and rapid elasticity capabilities of cloud computing, which leads to greater market expec tation that cloud computing can take effect promptly and boost financial performance in a short term. This result also implies the tendency that the investors evaluate cloud computing on its ubiquitous and shared nature that serves for timely problem-solving and decision-making rather than the strategic value creation which is supposed to be real ized in a longer time period. On the other hand, we find that cloud computing mainly facilitates the market value of big firms rather than small firms. This indicates that there is greater market expectation about the future growth potential of big firms resulting from the adoption of cloud computing. In this sense, our study is different from previous IT investment literature suggesting that the adoption of cloud computing is particularly beneficial to small and young firms [4,28]. We provide evidence on the impact of cloud service adoption on listed firms that are relatively mature and developed and further reveal the differential impact of cloud computing on the performance of big and small firms.

Beside, different from previous research that largely considers either market- or accounting-based performance measures, our study considers the multi-dimensional nature of firm performance by focusing on both firm profitability and market value, which, as a result, provides a more comprehensive understanding of the economic values of cloud computing.

Finally, from the methodology perspective, our study is one of the earliest attempts aiming to study the causal effect of cloud computing on firm performance based on a quasi-experiment with treatment and control assignments. Different from previous studies focusing on statis tical correlations which can be contaminated by confounders, our method can alleviate the potential endogeneity bias due to timeinvariant unobservables and self-selection upon observables, which, as a result, leads to a cleaner identification of the adoption effect, and provides more reliable and robust results.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

This study also provides some important managerial implications. First, the results shed light on the significant economic benefits accrued from cloud computing, which can help managers make better decisions on the implementation of cloud computing service. For example, our results can incentivize and persuade managers, especially those who are hesitant about the economic value of cloud computing, to migrate or integrate their business to cloud in order to achieve better firm profit ability and market value. Firms are advised to consider cloud service adoption as a strategic action to enhance their competitive advantage and performance.

Second, managers should be aware that the performance effects of cloud computing could vary across different situations. Our results reveal that small firms and firms in manufacturing industries can ach ieve higher profitability, and big firms and firms in service industries can realize higher market value after the adoption of cloud computing. Therefore, managers who seek to use third-party cloud service for pur suing different performance goals are recommended to pay close attention to both firm- and industry-level factors that affect the extent of value creation derived from cloud computing adoption.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

Although this study provides notable contributions, there are a few limitations that can be addressed by future research. First, this study only considers firms’ initial adoption of cloud computing services. Future research can further study the impact of repetitive adoption of cloud services on firm performance. Second, due to data limitation, we risk not having identified a few firms that adopted cloud computing. Future research can explore additional data sources other than an nouncements. Third, it is also interesting to investigate the differential impact of cloud service types (i.e., IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS) on firm per formance. Finally, although our methods have accounted for endoge neity due to omitted time-invariant unobservables and self-selection based on observables, there still remain other time-variant and unob served confounders that may contaminate the estimation. Future research can leverage randomized experiments or natural events to further strengthen the identification of the causality between cloud computing and firm performance.

## 7. Conclusion

The market of cloud computing is flourishing with facilitated speed, established elasticity, improved scale flexibility, and proven security, which has attracted enormous industrial and academic attention on its business value creation. However, extant research is mostly skewed to ward technical issues and adoption intention of cloud computing [23], thus resulting in the paucity of research that empirically examines its performance effects [6,28,58]. To extend this line of research, we empirically investigate the impact of cloud computing on both the ac counting and market-based performance of worldwide listed firms. We find that cloud service adoption has significant and positive impacts on firm performance in terms of profitability and market value. We further quantify the magnitude of such impacts in both short and long time windows. We also demonstrate that the effect size is different across firms with different sizes and in different industries. Overall, this study opens a door for empirical evaluation of IT business values and con tributes to the IS research in the empirical investigation on the perfor mance effects of IT investment.
