# Undesirable framing effects in information systems projects: Analysis of adjective usage in IS project business cases

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: IS project management Project documentation Language usage Framing

## A B S T R A C T

Many information systems (IS) projects encounter significant problems. The literature suggests that decisionmakers can be misled by overoptimistic estimates. We argue that such overoptimism may also be present in the choice of language in business cases. In this study, we analyzed the usage of such framing in 20 business cases for large IS projects of the Dutch government. Our findings show that newly proposed systems are systematically framed using positive adiectives, whereas the existing systems are framed using negative adiectives. This patterr of framing may subconsciously bias the decision-makers toward investing in new IS projects.

## 1. Introduction

Many information systems (IS) projects still fail to meet their targets regarding time, budget, or functionality [6,11,12,15,21,36,38,42,55]. Unfortunately, there are no real signs that this trend is changing [15,27]. The literature on project planning suggests that decision-makers can be misled by overoptimistic budget, schedule, and benefits estimates [26, 56], leading organizations to start projects which ultimately do not deliver the expected benefits or fail to do so on time or within budget [15,24,42,47].

We argue that such overoptimism may not only be present in the numbers given in business cases, i.e., estimates of budget, schedule, and benefits, but also in the choice of language used in these business cases. In other words, we suggest that what matters is not only what is said in business cases but also how it is said. Our analysis of twenty busines cases from the Dutch government reveals that newly proposed IS are systematically described, or framed, using significantly more positive language than the current IS that they are set to replace. This pattern of framing can lead to consistently more optimistic views of newly pro posed IS as well as more pessimistic views of the systems currently in place, which could cause organizations to prematurely replace IS.

Prior research has suggested that language usage can play an important role in developing IS and may contribute to some of the persistent issues plaguing IS projects [3,8,14,16,58]. Yet, surprisingly, there has been relatively little research on this subject. The literature on framing suggests that even minor differences in the wording of the same information can strongly influence preferences and decision-making (e. g., [5,28,43,44,49,59]). The specific wording (i.e., the frame chosen) can influence how positively or negatively an object is perceived. For example, a real estate agent could describe the same property as being either “small” or “cozy.” The latter can cast the same property in a more positive light than the former. In the same way, IS projects can be framed positively or negatively. This is illustrated in an article describing a large IS project failure in an authoritative Dutch newspaper [45]. At the beginning of the project, the organization describes the new system as “fast, modern, and available 24/7,” while the old system is described as “inefficient and error prone.” After a period of 13 years, the IS project fails and the organization continues to work with the old system that is “a lot simpler and easier to maintain” [45]. In this case, the same current system is framed negatively at the start of the project and framed positively after the new system development efforts failed. Such positive initial framing of a new system could bias decision-makers toward funding projects that are unlikely to pay off.

In this paper, we propose that positive and negative framing may be present in IS business cases, both in descriptions of IS that are currently being used as well as in descriptions of newly proposed IS. The focus of our study is not on whether the proposed system is factually superior to the existing system. Of course, if a new system is being proposed in a business case, it is expected that this system carries certain advantages over the existing system. However, the advantageous system could still be described using either positive or negative framing. Therefore, our focus is on whether framing is systematically used in IS business cases to make the proposed system appear more positive, while at the same time making the existing system appear more negative. Such framing effects could subconsciously bias decision-makers, much like with the real es tate example, and possibly cause organizations to prematurely replace IS.

The remainder of our paper is organized as follows: First, we provide an overview of prior research in this field as well as our research hy potheses. Then, we describe the process by which we collected business cases from the Dutch IS projects as well as the methodology that we employed to code the dataset and to analyze the outcomes. After that, we describe our research results. Finally, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our findings, as well as the limitations of this study, and we provide directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The role of language usage in IS projects

Barrett et al. [[2], p. 206] describe rhetoric as “the art of effective persuasion in different contexts to advance certain interests [1,13].” Rhetorical practices involve the use of rhetoric by “using persuasive language for the purposes of negotiating, obtaining and sustaining support and consensus” [[33], p. 113]. Rhetoric and rhetorical practice provide a theoretical basis for the study of the instrumental use of persuasive language usage and discourses [20]. Rhetoric has been studied in a va riety of contexts, including management research, where it is has been studied “as a managerial tool of influence and control; as a means of un derstanding agents’ interpretive schemes; as a way to understand institutional logics; as a means of organizational identity construction; and as a means of analyzing the role of organizational narratives [32]” [[2], p. 206].

In the context of people’s perceptions of technology, a rhetorical approach has been used as a means to enrich the understanding of phenomena such as the diffusion of innovative technologies [2] and as a means to increase compliance intentions with information security practices [34]. Green [30] indicates that rhetorical practice is an essential mechanism by which actors generate and associate meanings with regard to a technological innovation, and as such rhetoric can play a role in IS investments. Finally, the justification behind an IS invest ment decision is often politically charged and highly context dependent [54], and in these situations, rhetoric can play an important role.

The degree of congruence between various actors when making an investment decision then depends on the way in which technology is represented and perceived. Framing forms the discourse on a subject, such as a new technology, and can influence how it is perceived. In a broad sense, framing encompasses the communication about a specific subject. In the context of information technology, Harrison and Wind eler [31] describe that, in computer-mediated communications, people engage in framing to influence others in order to achieve their personal agenda. Similarly, in their rhetorical approach to understanding IT diffusion, Barrett et al. [[2], p. 207] describe framing as “a rhetorical strategy [10,57] for advancing a certain view of technology.”

Parties involved in the discourse on a technology can put forward or shape “technological frames” in line with their own views or underlying ideologies. As Barrett et al. [[2], p. 207] put it: “A frame can be consciously or unconsciously recognized and represented through words, phrases, or propositions. To frame an issue is to employ certain rhetorical strategies with intent to represent the issue in a certain light and invite others to see it in this light; for example, in positive or negative terms or with certain associations and connotations.” In their study, Barrett et al. [2] describe how such framing played a role in the perception and diffusion of IT. In this study, we propose that framing could play a similar role in the context of IS project business cases to represent IS “in a certain light and invite others to see it in this light; for example, in positive or negative terms” [[2], p. 207].

In line with the above, in the context of IS, language is also believed to play an important role [8,14,16,58] as it is needed to “describe the IS we build, to explain and justify their possible uses and implications, and to represent the data and information they contain” [[14], p. 192]. Given this important role, increasing attention is given to studying the role of language in relation to some of the persistent problems that plague IS [14,16]. For example, prior research has suggested that language bar riers between system developers and users can lead to miscommunica tion of system requirements, resulting in systems that do not live up to user needs [8].

Research by Gemino et al. [29] shows that achieving a shared un derstanding within a project team is important for managing IS projects. One of the challenges in managing IS projects is to share knowledge between actors in technical and management roles. Actors in different roles have specialized languages, which can lead to poor communica tion, thus making it difficult to establish a shared understanding. Capturing knowledge through the development of various documents, such as the technical design and an organizational change plan, can act as a binding agent [29], making it important to study the use of language in the project documentation.

Along these lines, Benschop et al. [3] found that using more abstract language in business cases is associated with a lower budget and schedule overruns of IS projects. Their findings suggest that abstract language usage in business cases can be an indicator for higher psy chological distance from a project, which is related to being less prone to optimism in planning. As Benschop et al. [3] point out, different kinds of adjectives may very well have different effects on decision-makers.

In this study, we aim to extend the existing research on the role of language in IS projects by (1) analyzing the usage of different kinds of adjectives in business cases of IS projects, (2) determining whether there is a systematic pattern in which language usage related to the proposed system is positive, while language usage related to the existing system is negative, and (3) describing how this may bias the decision to start an IS project.

## 2.2. Optimism about an IS project may be reflected in business case language usage

As discussed in the previous subsection, framing relates to how a subject is described. which can subsequently influence how it is perceived. Even minor differences in framing are capable of influencing people’s views. In other words, prior research has established that small differences in how things are said can have a big impact on people’s preferences and decision-making, even when the factual information given, what is said, is equivalent [59,43,44]. This type of framing effect is quite robust and has been confirmed across a variety of contexts (e.g., [5, 28,43,44,49,59]). A specific form of framing involving minor changes in wording is attribute framing. This refers to describing specific attributes or characteristics of objects using either positive or negative terms (e.g., using adjectives with positive or negative connotations) [28,44]. Attri bute framing can influence how positively or negatively people evaluate the object in question [44,60]. For example, Belardinelli et al. [5] describe that managers’ ex-post performance evaluations can be influ enced by whether performance evaluations are described in terms of the percentage of satisfied or dissatisfied customers. In the context of IS project escalation, Nuijten et al. [49] found that decision-makers are less likely to continue with a failing project, when the possible outcomes associated with continuing are described in terms of gains instead of in terms of losses. Karevold and Teigen [35] performed multiple experi ments which revealed that framing the progress of a troubled project could be interpreted as either an argument for continued investment or as an argument for pursuing a different project instead.

While most studies focus on the effects of framing on receivers of framed messages, less attention has been given to how people use framing and what we can learn from their framing usage. Research suggests that people’s choice to use either positive or negative attribute framing may be based on the prior state of the object in question. For example, Sher and McKenzie [52] use theory on information leakage to find that subjects were much more likely to describe a glass as “half full” when it was previously empty and as “half empty” when it was previ ously full.

Other studies suggest that positive or negative attribute framing may be based on people’s opinion of the object in question. When people have an optimistic view of an object such as an IS project, they are likely to communicate its characteristics using positive adjectives (i.e., attri bute framing) [4,52,53]. Speakers may use this as a form of implicit recommendation to the receiver of the message [52,53,60]. For example, in the context of project management, Sher and McKenzie [52] found that people were much more likely to describe a good project team in terms of their success rate, while they were more likely to describe a bad project team in terms of their failure rate, even when the number of successes and failures were equal for the two teams.

When writing a business case for an IS project, it seems logical that the writer of the business case perceives a newly proposed system to have advantages over the existing system. Indeed, there would be little reason to write a business case to develop a new system unless it is going to be superior to the existing system. This suggests that a new system will be more likely to be described using positive adjectives, whereas the existing system will be more likely to be described using negative ad jectives. Furthermore, this pattern of framing may occur even when the newly proposed system is not superior to the existing system. For example, it may occur when the writer of the business case has an overoptimistic view of the proposed system. Such overoptimism is also considered to be common in IS projects and can be caused by factors such as unrealistically optimistic budget and schedule estimates for the proposed system (i.e., the planning fallacy) [23,40].

Another line of research suggests that people select the frame that they believe to be most convincing. Van Buiten and Keren [60] argue that people use positive framing when trying to persuade others to make the suggested course of action appear favorable. This is in line with earlier research by Keren [37] who found that people tend to choose positive frames over negative ones to describe a product when they have the goal of maximizing sales of that product. Using a positive frame to convince decision-makers to authorize a project is a well-known phe nomenon in the project management literature. This type of framing could lead to strategic misrepresentation of a project, which is consid ered to be one of the main reasons for overruns of projects in a variety of contexts, including IS projects [9,25,23,40].

We propose that people systematically use attribute framing in business cases of IS projects, regardless of whether the positive opinion about the new system is accurate or the result of an overoptimistic view, or from strategic misrepresentation used with the goal of obtaining funding for the project. An optimistic view about an IS project may lead to a relatively positive framing of the proposed IS and a relatively negative framing of the existing IS. Based on this, we propose the following two research hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. The percentage of positive adjectives used to describe an IS in a business case of an IS project is higher for the IS that is being proposed as compared with the existing IS.

Hypothesis 2. The percentage of negative adjectives used to describe an IS in a business case of an IS project is lower for the IS that is being proposed as compared with the existing IS.

On the one hand, the literature suggests various reasons, listed above, why attribute framing, in the form of systematic usage of ad jectives, may occur in IS project business cases. On the other hand, the (over)usage of adjectives in this type of technical documentation is discouraged [7], since they can be imprecise and introduce abstractness or vagueness into the document [48,51]. The linguistic category model (LCM), which forms the basis of Benschop et al.’s [3] analysis into IS business case language usage, also classifies adjectives as a form of ab stract language usage. Similarly, from a decision-making standpoint, the use of adjectives that are vague and unclear may also not be desirable. One could argue that clear and precise descriptions, e.g., “costs of 1.9 million dollars,” are more informative to a reader of a business case than descriptions based on vague or unclear adjectives, e.g., “enormous costs.”

Despite such adjective usage not being encouraged from both a technical writing standpoint, as well as a decision-making point of view, adjective usage in relation to IS is not uncommon in business cases. As an example, Benschop et al. [3] counted an average of almost 150 adjec tives per IS project business case that were used in relation to either the budget or project schedule. For that reason, we aim to provide insight into both the relative and absolute usage of certain types of adjectives. Specifically, we investigate how common the usage of the various types of adjectives is and how they are used in relation to either existing systems or proposed systems. Furthermore, we illustrate how adjective usage varies across the IS project business cases in our dataset.

## 3. Research design

The approach for our study follows a sequential exploratory mixedmethod design and consists of a qualitative, exploratory phase that provides input for a subsequent quantitative analysis [19]. According to Creswell et al. [[19], p. 181], “the purpose of this design is to use quanti tative data and results to assist in the interpretation of qualitative findings.” The first phase of our study consists of a linguistic analysis of the lan guage usage in business cases of IS projects, specifically the usage of adjectives. The second phase consists of a statistical analysis to deter mine whether the usage of adjectives is significantly different when the system that is being proposed is described in these business cases as compared with the existing system.

## 3.1. Dataset

In order to perform our linguistic analysis, we needed to collect business cases of IS projects in which both the proposed system as well as the existing system are explicitly discussed. Through collaboration with the Dutch government, we were able to collect a homogenous sample of IS projects. This resulted in a dataset of 20 business cases of IS projects and, thus, 20 descriptions of proposed IS systems as well as 20 de scriptions of existing IS systems.

Data on the 20 governmental IS projects were collected between April and September of 2018. Some of these projects were started as early as 2006, whereas the most recent project in the dataset was started in early 2018. Some of these projects had already concluded at the time that the research was conducted, whereas others had not. On average, these projects had a budget of 33 million euro. The IS projects included in the dataset are a mix of projects that use traditional waterfall meth odologies as well as agile methodologies and hybrids of both. Using a mix of project management methodologies is not uncommon in practice [18]. As the Project Management Institute describes [[50], p. 11]: “Success starts with the right approach to support project delivery. Organi sations will continue to use more than one project management approach and combine different techniques to cope with their own distinct challenges.” Dutch governmental IS projects are considered to be a relevant sample since a parliamentary investigation concluded that many of these IS projects encounter significant problems and that, based on a conserva tive estimate, between 1 and 5 billion euro is being wasted each year [17].

## 3.2. Coding and qualitative analysis process

Since our study focuses on the usage of adjectives related to either the proposed system or the existing system, we specifically selected only those (sub)sections of the business cases that were aimed at describing either of the systems or making a comparison between them. The text analysis software ATLAS.ti was used to facilitate the coding process, which forms the basis for our linguistic analysis. The coding process consisted of three main stages:

(1) identifying all the adjectives in the selected sections of the IS business cases,

(2) using manual coding to assign each instance a code to indicate whether the adjective relates to the proposed system or the existing system, and

(3) using a combination of automated and manual coding to reflect the type of adjective, e.g., positive or negative adjective.

In the first stage, all the relevant sections of the 20 business cases were gathered and then processed using ATLAS.ti’s “word cruncher” functionality, which identifies every unique word which occurs in the dataset. Since we are specifically interested in the usage of adjectives, all other words were removed from the list. For some words, it was not immediately clear whether they were used as an adjective in the text. For example, the word “increased” is used as an adjective in the sentence “the increased costs are a concern,” but it is used as a verb in the sen tence “the costs have increased since last year.” A subsequent manual analysis was performed to determine whether in these instances, the words were used as adjectives or not. Each occurrence of the remaining words was automatically flagged in the business cases using ATLAS.ti. In the second and third stages of the linguistic analysis, each of these ad jectives was assigned two codes, as described below.

In the second stage, the 20 business cases were manually analyzed, and each instance of these adjectives was assigned a code to indicate whether the adjective referred to the proposed system or to the existing system. In the third stage, two coders were provided with the word list of the unique adjectives from the business cases. They were asked to independently assign each of these adjectives a valence score. This was first done at the general level, one score for each unique adjective in the word list, and not each instance of these adjectives in the text. Most adjectives were rated on a five-point scale ranging from very negative to very positive with neutral as the middle value. Some adjectives, how ever, were classified as a sixth category of adjectives, named “dependent on context,” meaning that their valence differs based on the object that they relate to. An example of an adjective in this category is “enormous.” If benefits are enormous, then enormous can be considered to be a very positive quality; however, when we are instead talking about enormous costs, then this is not the case. As such, in a subsequent step, the coders examined each instance of these adjectives and categorized their use on the five-point scale ranging from very negative to very positive. In the third stage, any disagreements between the coders were discussed and resolved by the coders among themselves.

As a final step in our coding process, we examined the context in which all the adjectives were used to check that the coding was appro priate. This was necessary because the list of words that formed the basis for the initial coding process lists does not account for word combina tions. For example, the adjective “beneficial” has the opposite meaning when it is phrased as “not beneficial.” In instances such as this, the valence score was inverted, e.g., a “very positive” adjective combined with a denial was recoded “very negative” and a “negative” adjective combined with a denial was recoded as “positive.”

## 3.3. Qualitative and quantitative analyzes

The qualitative coding process, described above, yielded a dataset containing all instances of adjective usage with attributes reflecting the corresponding valence score, the business case from which the adjective originated, and a dummy variable that indicated whether the adjective was used in relation to an existing system or a proposed system. This dataset formed the foundation for our qualitative linguistic analysis as well as our subsequent quantitative analysis in which we tested whether the valence scores differed significantly between those two types of systems. The first step of this quantitative analysis was to determine whether the obtained differences in frequencies of the various categories of adjectives, between those used in relation to the proposed system and those used in relation to the existing system, are statistically significant. To this end, a two-way frequency analysis was performed.

In order to test our hypotheses, a paired-sample sign test was per formed. The advantage of using this non-parametric method of statisti cal analysis is that it compares for differences between the adjective usage related to the proposed and the existing system within each case rather than across cases. Differences between business cases that could influence the usage of adjectives such as individual characteristics of the writer, length of the business case, project management method, or other unique circumstances of the project are automatically controlled for by only making comparisons within the same business case. Rather than using the absolute frequencies of each of the categories of adjec tives, we quantified the usage of each of the categories as a percentage of the total amount of adjectives used in relation to a specific system. This allowed us to control for differences in the total number of adjectives used, for example, due to one system being discussed more extensively in a business case than another.

In addition to the percentages of usage of each of the five categories of adjectives, we also created a composite score that reflects the overall level of positivity or negativity of adjective usage related to a specific system in a specific business case. These composite scores were also compared within business cases to determine whether adjective usage is indeed more positive overall when the proposed system is discussed as compared with the existing system.

## 4.1.2. Pattern of usage of adjectives in relation to the proposed system and the existing system

The 820 unique adjectives appeared a total of 2951 times throughout the 20 business cases in descriptions of either the proposed system or the existing system. These 2951 instances of adjectives formed the basis for the analyses in our study. Each instance was coded on a five-point valence scale, ranging from very positive to very negative, following the procedures described in the “coding and qualitative analysis pro cess” subsection of this study. For example, all instances of the adjective “dependable,” which was classified as a very positive adjective, were assigned a code indicating that they were instances of a very positive adjective. Each individual instance of adjectives that were categorized as being “dependent on context” was looked at separately and inde pendently assigned a code on our five-point scale ranging from very positive to very negative. Through this process, each of the 2951 in stances of adjective usage was assigned a code reflecting their valence score. Next, each of the 2951 instances was coded as being used in the context of describing either the proposed system or the existing system.

## 4.2. Quantitative analysis of adjective usage in business cases of IS projects

## 4.2.1. Two-way frequency analysis

The next step of the analysis consisted of testing whether the observed differences in the usage of the five categories of adjectives, which emerged from the linguistic analysis and are listed in Table 4, are statistically significant. Specifically, we wanted to test whether adjective usage is different in relation to systems that are being proposed as compared with existing systems, i.e., whether there is a relationship between the system type and the five categories of adjectives. Further more, we wanted to establish whether the observed pattern was consistent with our hypotheses. To this end, a two-way frequency analysis was performed, using the data in Table 4 as a basis.

A two-way frequency analysis can be performed to test whether there is a relationship between two variables that are either categorical or ordinal in nature. Specifically, a chi-square test for association was performed. This test compares the distribution of frequencies of two variables and compares the frequency for each combination of both variables against the frequency that would be expected if both variables are unrelated. Thus, the chi-square test involves rejecting or not rejecting a null hypothesis, which assumes that both variables are un related. If the outcomes of the test are statistically significant, then the null hypothesis is rejected.

Table 5 provides an overview of the observed frequencies of the five categories of adjectives for both types of systems, listed in the rows labeled “actual count,” as well as the frequencies that would be expected under the assumption that there is no relationship between the two variables. The categories of the system type variable, “proposed system” and “existing system,” are represented by different rows, whereas the columns represent the five categories of adjectives, ranging from very negative to very positive. Furthermore, the total frequencies for each row and each column are listed. Since the chi-square test for association compares the actual observed frequencies, i.e., the actual count, with the frequency that would be expected, the most relevant data in the table are not the “actual count” frequencies themselves but rather the differences between the “actual count” values and the corresponding “expected count values.”

As can be seen in Table 5, the number of very negative adjectives used in relation to the proposed system that we observed during the linguistic analysis of the business cases was much lower than would be expected (101, where 169 were expected), whereas the actual observed number of positive and very positive adjectives was much higher than would be expected (300 and 595, where 241 and 490 were expected, respectively). When looking at the adjective usage in relation to existing systems, an opposite pattern emerges. Here, the observed frequency of very negative adjectives is much higher than would be expected (187, where 119 were expected), and the observed frequency of positive and very positive adjectives is much lower (111 and 240, where 170 and 345 were expected, respectively).

These patterns are consistent with our hypotheses. Also in line with our hypotheses, we find that the frequency of negative adjectives is lower than expected for proposed systems (63, where 77 were expected) and higher than expected for existing systems (68, where 54 were ex pected). However, this effect is less pronounced than for the very negative, positive, and very positive categories of adjectives. The fre quency of neutral adjectives is lower than expected for proposed systems (674, where 755 were expected) and higher than expected for existing systems (612, where 531 were expected).

The chi-square tests for association show that these differences be tween actual counts and expected counts are statistically significant at the p< 0.001 level, which indicates that the usage of adjectives is significantly different for systems that are being proposed as compared with existing systems, and, thus, that these two variables are related

## 4.2.2. Paired-sample sign test

The findings described above show that adjective usage in relation to proposed systems was more positive overall than adjective usage in relation to existing systems. To test the robustness of this effect, we further tested this effect within each of the business cases using a pairedsample sign test. This non-parametric test compares the usage of ad jectives in relation to both systems within the same business case, thus holding constant and statistically controlling for differences between business cases, writers, and IS projects. The paired-sample sign test in volves a median comparison of the relative frequency of the five cate gories of adjectives, expressed as a percentage of the total number of adjectives used, which tests whether the relative frequency for each of these five categories is significantly different for systems that are being proposed as compared with existing systems. Furthermore, the pairedsample sign test can provide an indication of the robustness of this ef fect by showing how many of the business cases in the dataset follow this pattern.

The dataset for the paired-sample sign test was composed using the outcomes from the linguistic analysis as a basis. This dataset contains 20 entries, one for each business case. Each entry contains information about the usage of the five categories of adjectives in that business case in relation to both the proposed system and the existing system. We used the relative frequency of each adjective category, rather than the ab solute frequency, as this allowed us to control for differences in the number of adjectives used to describe both systems.

In addition to information about the relative frequencies for each separate adjective category, we also created two composite scores which represented the overall level of positivity or negativity of adjective usage in relation to a specific system. To control for differences in the number of adjectives used to describe both systems, these scores were oper ationalized as relative values. Specifically, the first composite score was calculated by multiplying the absolute number of adjectives in each category with a certain score and subsequently dividing by the total number of adjectives used in relation to that system. Very negative ad jectives were assigned a score of -2, negative adjectives a score of -1, neutral adjectives a score of 0, positive adjective a score of +1, and very positive adjectives a score of +2. As a result of this method of scoring, positive composite scores reflect adjective usage which is more positive than negative and negative scores reflect usage which is more negative than positive. The second composite score consists of the ratio of the sum of the frequencies of positive and very positive adjectives to the sum of the frequencies of negative and very negative adjectives. As such, a higher score indicates a higher ratio of positive and very positive ad jective usage.

Table 6 provides an overview of our findings. Looking first at each of the categories of adjectives individually, we find statistically significant results for four out of five categories. Consistent with our hypotheses, we find that the relative frequency of very negative adjective usage is significantly lower when discussing proposed systems as compared with existing systems (median proposed systems = 4.59, median current systems = 13.57, p= 0.001). Also in line with our hypotheses, we find that the relative frequency of positive (median proposed systems = 17.46, median current systems = 9.07, p< 0.001) and very positive adjectives (median proposed systems = 31.66, median current systems = 16.35, p<0.001) is significantly higher. In addition, we find that the two composite measures, which provide an indication of the overall level of positivity or negative of adjective use, follow the same pattern. More positive scores are associated with systems that are being proposed as compared with existing systems for both composite measure 1 (me dian proposed systems = 67.80, median current systems = 3.15, p<0.001) and composite measure 2 (median proposed systems = 5.57, median current systems = 1.28, p<0.001). Furthermore, these results all appear to be quite robust, with between 17 and 20 out of 20 indi vidual business cases demonstrating the same pattern, as can be seen in Table 6.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Implications for research

With this study, we aim to make several contributions to the existing literature. Our study contributes to the field of IS project management. Many IS projects still encounter significant problems or even fail completely [6,11,12,15,21,36,38,42,55]. Findings from our analysis of business cases of 20 IS projects show a consistent pattern, where lan guage usage, in the form of adjective usage, is more positive when proposed systems are discussed as compared with descriptions of existing systems. In other words, the information provided about these systems appeared to be consistently colored.

It is important to note that the observed pattern relates to how sys tems are framed in business cases, independent of the actual benefits, risks or value of these systems. In other words, a pattern of negative framing used in relation to a system does not mean that the system in question is a bad system. Framing, by definition, involves casting the same information or characteristics in a different light using small dif ferences in wording, as in the case of the real estate agent describing the same property as “tiny” or as “cozy.” Also think back, for example, to the newspaper article in which the old system was initially described as “inefficient and error prone” and later as “a lot simpler and easier to maintain” [45]. We observed similar effects in our study. For example, a system was being referred to as the “old” system (very negative valence) or even a system that is “end-of-life” (also very negative valence) rather than as the “current” system (neutral valence). Proposed systems, on the other hand, were typically framed more positively as the “desired” system (very positive valence). If it were simply the case that the existing systems are inferior to the proposed system in the examined business cases, and there was no overoptimism about the project or a conscious intention to paint these systems in a certain light, then one would expect each of the categories of adjectives (i.e., framing) to occur in more or less the same frequency (for example, “old” system would be expected to be used about as often as “current” system). What we observed, however, was a robust and systematic pattern in which framing was used to make the information related to the proposed system appear more positive while making the information related to the existing system appear more negative.

The theory on attribute framing [44] indicates that the specific pattern, which we observe in business cases of IS projects, could sub consciously bias decision-makers to perceive the system that is being proposed in a business case more positively while simultaneously biasing the perceptions of the existing system to be more negative. As such, this could subconsciously push decision-makers to be more likely to fund IS projects.

Our study also contributes to prior research on the role of rhetoric and language usage in relation to technology and in IS projects specif ically. Barrett et al. [[2], p.216] propose that studying framing can be a valuable means to gain insight into “nonrational, interest-related, emotional factors” which can improve our understanding of why parties acted as they did with regards to technological developments (in their case, IT diffusion).

Language also plays an important role in IS projects specifically, as it is suggested that it may play a role in some of the persistent problems facing IS projects [8,14,16,58]. In line with this, findings from Benschop et al. [3] suggest that linguistic analysis of IS project documentation could provide valuable insights. While their study looked at adjectives, as a form of concrete language usage, they did not distinguish between different adjectives nor did they look at the valence of these adjectives. They discuss this as a limitation of their study.

With our study, we extend the literature on rhetoric and the role of language in relation to technology, particularly in relation to IS projects.

Barrett et al. [2] suggest that the use of framing can influence the diffusion of IT and that this may explain why even technologies with a high organizational value may be resisted. We extend these findings by showing that framing is also prevalent in business cases of IS projects, in the form of attribute framing. Prior research has shown that such posi tive attribute framing can subconsciously lead people to adopt more positive evaluations of the subjects in question as compared with when the same objects are described using negative framing instead. The pattern observed in our study may cause organizations to prematurely replace IS. Thus, in addition to increasing resistance to adopting bene ficial technology, as Barrett et al. [2] suggest, our findings indicate that framing can also lead to serviceable existing systems being replaced prematurely.

Our findings further suggest that, in addition to studying broad “technological frames” and the overall contents of the discourse as well as the arguments applied [2], there is also value in studying framing in the form of minor differences in wording, such as the usage of adjectives. Specifically, the pattern of framing observed in our results suggests that rhetorical practices may not only just involve setting the right content for the message [33] but also choosing the right words to convey that message. Specifically, writers of business cases, either intentionally or subconsciously, consistently use certain types of adjectives when describing either existing or proposed systems. The adjectives are applied in such a way that they are likely to influence the perceptions of the receivers of these messages, in favor of starting the development of the new system. Thus, we propose that by combining a broad view of the technological frames applied and arguments used, with an analysis of the specific wording used to convey these messages, our understanding of the roles and effects of discourse related to technology, e.g., in the context of IS projects, could be further enhanced.

Third, our study contributes to prior research on the role of framing in project management. Prior research has shown that framing effects also play a role in the context of project management (e.g., [35,49]). While various studies have focused on how minor differences in framed information can influence receivers of that information, relatively little research has been done on how people use framing in a project man agement context. Earlier work in this area has suggested that people use more positive framing when they have a positive, rather than a negative, view of a project team [52] or a project [4], or when they intend to continue, rather than abandon, a project [35]. However, to our knowl edge, our study is the first to look at the usage of such framing in the documentation of actual IS projects. Our findings extend these existing studies by demonstrating that IS project documentation appears to contain a consistent pattern of attribute framing in which systems that are being proposed are described more positively as compared with existing systems.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

The main contribution of this paper to practice is that our study is the first to show that framing is being used, both systematically and consistently, in business cases of IS projects. We uncovered a pattern in which systems being proposed in these business cases are framed in a way that could make them appear more positive than they may actually be, whereas the existing systems are being framed in a way that could make them appear more negative than they may actually be.

Prior research has suggested that the information in project business cases may be overoptimistic, either as a result of strategic misrepre sentation or as a result of an overoptimistic view of the time and budget required to finish the project [23,40]. Such strategic misrepresentation and overoptimism at the start of a project are seen as an important explanation for why so many projects fail to meet their targets [25,23, 40]. The findings of our study suggest that decision-makers need to not only be wary of unrealistic estimates of budget, schedule, and benefits in business cases but also of the language used to frame the systems described in business cases, which may make a system appear more positive (or negative) than it really is.

For practitioners, it is important to be aware of this apparently persistent pattern of attribute framing, since prior research has proven that framing can systematically bias decision-making in a variety of contexts (e.g., [5,28,43,44,59]), including IS projects [49]. Specifically, the theory on attribute framing [44] suggests that the pattern of framing that we observed in our study can subconsciously bias decision-makers to be more likely to fund a project. Decision-makers might be influenced by such framing without them even being aware of it.

Individuals tasked with writing proposals or business cases, such as technical writers and business analysts, should be aware that not just what is written but also how it is written could subconsciously influence readers. The literature on technical writing does not emphasize the concept of framing and how minor differences in wording could sub consciously bias readers. Our findings suggest that more attention needs to be placed on the role of adjective usage in IS project business cases. From a technical writing point of view, the use of adjectives is typically discouraged [7], since they can be imprecise and introduce abstractness or vagueness into the document [48,51].

From a decision-making standpoint, such imprecise, unclear, or vague adjectives may also not be desirable. Specially, more detailed or exact descriptions related to factors such as project costs are likely more informative to a reader of a business case than vague or abstract de scriptions such as “enormous costs.” Despite such adjective usage not being encouraged from both a technical writing standpoint, as well as a decision-making point of view, adjective usage in relation to IS is not uncommon in business cases. This is further supported by our research findings. While adjectives made up a minority of the words used in relation to either system in the business cases that we studied, each business case still contained almost 100 adjectives, on average, that were used in relation to either the proposed or the existing system. Our findings are in line with results from a study by Benschop et al. [3] who counted an average of almost 150 adjectives per IS project business case that was related to either the budget or project schedule. The findings from our study give further weight to the advice to avoid overusing adjectives in writing business cases, not specifically because they can be imprecise or vague, but, perhaps more importantly, that they could systematically bias decision-makers.

Writers of business cases should be more aware of these potential negative implications of adjective usage. The most important implica tion of our study for these writers is that they should be careful and reserved in their use of adjectives in these documents. When they do want or need to incorporate adjectives into their writing, it is important to consider the subconscious effects that the choice of adjectives could have on a reader. Therefore, we recommend that if adjectives are to be used, very positively or very negatively valenced adjectives should be used cautiously in business cases, as they could subconsciously evoke either a more positive or a more negative view of the system. Neutrally valenced adjectives, on the other hand, are less likely to subconsciously bias a reader’s assessment of either system. At the very least, writers of business cases should analyze their overall adjective usage to avoid the robust pattern observed in our dataset, where the proposed system is systematically described using more positively valenced adjectives than the existing system.

## 5.3. Limitations

Of course, our study is not without limitations. First, the focus of our research was specifically on attribute framing in the form of positive and negative adjective usage. However, in addition to attribute framing, other forms of framing exist such as risky choice framing, goal framing, and progress framing [44,35]. We did not consider these other forms of framing in our study. To establish a more comprehensive view of framing usage in IS projects, in all its forms, further research is required which explicitly considers these other types of framing in addition to attribute framing. Furthermore, there might be other ways, beyond these forms of framing, in which language usage might influence decision-making in IS projects. To establish a more comprehensive overview of such effects, future research could look at other forms of language usage, such as the usage of adverbs of degree, for example.

Second, thanks to a collaboration with the Dutch government, we were able to analyze business cases of 20 Dutch governmental IS pro jects. This provided us with a homogenous dataset of sufficient size for both our qualitative linguistic analysis as well as our subsequent quan titative analysis. This category of IS projects is also interesting to study, given the frequent examples of public IS project failure in the news as well as findings from a Dutch parliamentary investigation, which in dicates that governmental IS projects are plagued by significant prob lems [17]. Nevertheless, further research, which explores the language usage of IS projects in other contexts, such as in the private sector or in different countries, would be very fruitful in either providing further evidence for the robustness of these effects or in identifying potential differences in attribute framing usage in different contexts.

Third, our analysis was limited to business cases, but these are not the only documents that play a role in IS project management and framing might also occur in other IS project documentation. While there is no theoretical reason to suggest that people are prone to frame in one type of document but refrain from framing in others, further research on this topic could be valuable in determining whether the specific pattern in framing is different for different kinds of project documentation or that the pattern that we observed in our study is replicated and rein forced across documents.

## 5.4. Directions for future research

In addition to further research aimed at addressing the limitations of our study, we propose several other avenues of future research that we believe to be promising. First, future studies could further explore the various reasons behind this systematic pattern of language usage in business cases that we observed. Prior research provides some possible reasons for this pattern, including an overoptimistic view and strategic misrepresentation [9,25,23,40]. Our analysis, however, did not allow us to determine the specific reasons for the pattern that we observed. Thus, further research is needed in order to identify the causes of systematic framing. To start, it would be helpful to determine whether writers of business cases are even aware of their framing, i.e., to determine whether this pattern of framing occurs subconsciously or whether writers intentionally use framing. Subsequently, the underlying reasons for this conscious or subconscious pattern of framing could be identified.

Second, our study could be further extended by exploring what can be done to address the consistent framing pattern that we observed. However, the effectiveness of countermeasures may depend on the root causes of framing in a business case. As Flyvbjerg [[25], p. 6] describes: “The cures to optimism bias are different from the cures to strategic misrep resentation.” Specifically, an unrealistically optimistic perception of a system could possibly be corrected by avoiding framing that can induce such an overoptimistic view, e.g., the usage of very positive adjectives, or by challenging people’s perceptions of the system and providing ac curate information about the system’s cost, benefit, and risk. If the framing is intentional, then the problem becomes more challenging to address, and managers may need to explore other countermeasures. For example, Flyvbjerg [25] suggests that to combat strategic misrepresen tation incentives must be aligned to reward honest and accurate esti mates and representations of the project, rather than dishonest ones.

Third, it could be fruitful to further explore instances in which writers, intentionally or subconsciously, use framing to provide implicit recommendations or to convince a reader to undertake a certain course of action, such as to initiate a project. In itself, it would help our un derstanding of this phenomenon if we better understand why, and when, they do so. Future research could study whether such a framing pattern is perhaps more likely in certain circumstances than in others. If so, the usage of this pattern of framing could leak valuable information to a trained reader, as suggested by prior literature on information leakage [52].

Fourth, the effects of these framing patterns on the performance of IS projects warrant further study. Benschop et al. [3] identified a rela tionship between language usage in business cases and IS project over runs. They suggest that specific language usage in IS business cases could serve as an early warning indicator that the project planning may be overoptimistic, which increases the risk of overruns. Large-scale research into completed IS projects could help determine whether pro jects in which the framing effect of adjectives is strongest ultimately perform worse than projects in which this pattern of framing is weaker or not present at all. Such information leakage might help organizations to identify potential issues at an early stage and/or might help them in selecting the right projects to fund.

Fifth, while our study looked at framing usage at one point in time, namely in the business case at the start of the project, future research could identify how framing is applied at other moments in the project life cycle. It would be particularly interesting to determine whether the framing usage differs in various stages of the project and what the un derlying reasons for these differences could be. Finally, we see value in performing case studies where framing usage is assessed longitudinally to determine how, and why, the framing changes, for example, by relating it to critical events.
