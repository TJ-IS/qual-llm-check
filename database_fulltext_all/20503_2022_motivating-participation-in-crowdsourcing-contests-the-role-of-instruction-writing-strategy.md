## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Crowdsourcing Contest participation Task instruction Writing strategy Text analytics

## A B S T R A C T

Task instructions are seeker-generated content aiming to disclose information and persuade solvers to participate in crowdsourcing contests. There are various writing strategies for task instructions, with different levels of informativeness and persuasiveness for solvers. This work examines how requirement-oriented and rewardoriented strategies affect solver participation. The empirical results reveal a U-shaped relationship between the usage of restrictive words and the number of participants. In contrast, an inverted U-shaped relationship is found between terminology usage and participation. Emphasizing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards through issuerelevant information statements and emotional appeals also proves to be effective in motivating solvers involvement.

## 1. Introduction

Currently, enterprise borders are dissolving, and the value creation process is changing from centralized to decentralized and from closed to open [1]. Companies facing external competition and internal resource shortages are thus seeking crowdsourcing contests to channel crowd capital into the innovation of products and services [2–5]. As a core concern in crowdsourcing systems, solver participation has proven to be critical to the diversity and success of crowdsourcing contests [6–11]. The number of participants is thus widely regarded as a proxy for contest quality to measure the utility that solution seekers obtain from the contest [12]. As an incentive for participant motivation, task in structions and, more precisely, the way the problem statement is crafted are a uniquely interactive medium bridging seekers and solvers [13,14]. For seeker firms, it is a top priority to design an attractive crowdsourcing contest to elicit attention from the solver community [15,16].

Recent research has provided evidence for the implications of in struction design for participation in crowdsourcing contests. Task in struction types (i.e., unbounded, suggestive, and prohibitive) [13], content sufficiency, solution examples [14], and seeker identity disclo sure [17] have proven to influence solvers’ willingness to participate.

However, this body of research does not provide the evidence for how instruction-writing strategies influence crowdsourcing behaviors from the perspective of linguistic cues. First, previous research has focused on the impacts of instruction attributes on task outcomes but has not considered how to describe these attributes in terms of proper wording techniques. Second, findings on how solver participation is affected by narratives of requirements and rewards in task instructions remain controversial, especially in regard to two questions: What level of restrictive words and terminology should be used to disclose informa tion without scaring off solvers, and how can task instructions motivate participation by conveying motivation-based signals through linguistic cues? Third, the qualitative analysis, manual classification, and ques tionnaire approaches in previous studies are not directly applicable to knowledge-intensive crowdsourcing contests involving massive textual data. For most research, the task instructions consist of semistructured texts or a few sentences, which may affect the generalizability of the findings.

In fact, with the attributes (e.g., reward type, competition rules, and instruction component) of task instructions already determined, adopt ing proper writing techniques to disclose factual information and persuade solvers is vital to promote communication and motivate participation. The reason for this is twofold. First, participation in a crowdsourcing contest is largely driven by the subjective intentions of the individual [18–20], and the text composition of task instructions acts as a major communication signal affecting an individual’s cognitive process. According to expectancy theory [21], during the reading of instructions, individuals measure the extent to which the rewards are of importance to them and the likelihood of achieving higher performance if they spend efforts [22]. Second, task instructions are designed with a clear purpose to disclose information and persuade the solvers. Unlike literary works emphasizing artistic expression, written instructions are typically more informative and persuasive. They are intended to convey messages and guidelines and persuade the audience being addressed [23,24]. Prior studies [25–28] from various fields have shown that the persuasiveness of texts will change the attitude of individuals and consequently lead to behavior differences.

By drawing on expectancy theory, we posit that instruction writing impacts the solver’s decision by affecting his or her mental representa tion, i.e., perceived expectancy and perceived valence. Perceived ex pectancy is mainly influenced by statements concerning task requirements, while perceived valence is affected by the narrative of rewards. It is also observed that task requirements and rewards are the most common focus when crowdsourcing seekers pitch tasks to the crowd. As a result, we focus on two types of writing strategies: require ment-oriented and reward-oriented strategies. In terms of writing purpose, requirement-oriented strategies, i.e., using restrictive words and using terminology, are informative. In contrast, reward-oriented strategies, i. e., emphasizing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards by stating issue-relevant information and making emotional appeals, are persuasive.

This study examines the effects of task instruction-writing strategies on solver participation in crowdsourcing contests. Syntactic analysis and semantic computing are conducted to quantify the extent to which task instructions adopt a particular writing strategy. By building on text analytics, machine learning, and empirical analysis, our study suggests a U-shaped relationship between the usage of restrictive words and the number of participants. In contrast, an inverted U-shaped relationship occurs between terminology usage and participation. The two curvi linear relationships are stronger in tasks with higher complexity. We also apply the dual-process theory of persuasion to the crowdsourcing context. Emphasizing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards by stating issuerelevant information and making emotional appeals has proven to be effective in engaging solvers.

This study contributes to the growing literature on the implications of the formulation of task instructions for participation in crowdsourcing contests. First, from the perspective of linguistic cues, we conduct pio neering work examining the effects of the instruction-writing strategy on solver participation. This study provides insights for the formulation of crowdsourcing task instructions, that is, to integrate with writing sci ence and linguistics. Second, we propose a procedure that can quantify writing strategies for project instructions in online platforms. Our approach can process a large number of knowledge-intensive crowd sourcing tasks, and can generalize to the analysis of writing strategies for other online platforms. Third, we also make contributions regarding specific issues related to the participation of crowdsourcing contests. Previous studies have not reached a consensus on the effects of task restriction on solver participation. Our findings reveal a U-shaped relationship between using restrictive words and participation, and the curvilinear relation may be explained by the existence of two groups of potential solvers with different attitudes toward task restriction. Prac tically, this study enables an in-depth understanding of how to write instructions that can effectively convey factual information and persuade the targeted solvers through better textual communication.

## 2. Theory and hypothesis development

## 2.1. Crowdsourcing contests and the importance of mass participation

Defined as contests among a crowd of individuals involving their skills, ideas, and creativity, crowdsourcing contests are becoming increasingly efficient for enterprises to spur innovation in product development and marketing [29,30]. By collecting solutions for creative and challenging problems, enterprises can stretch their external intelli gence to develop new products or services [29–31], reduce costs [19, 32], and strengthen customer relationships and brand visibility [31,33]. Meanwhile, crowdsourcing changes the way solvers work by offering them digital platforms to earn rewards, display talents, and even satisfy their psychological, emotional, or social demands [20,34,35].

User participation has long been regarded as an antecedent for the success of information systems and has been examined in various types of crowdsourcing platforms, such as InnoCentive, Zooppa, Tongal, and Freelancer. Numerous studies have shown that the successful initiation and sustainable development of crowdsourcing contests largely depend on mass participation [6–11]. Hence, Steils and Hanine [14] suggest that companies attract valuable contributors in an open call for crowd sourcing participation. If a contest designer fails to provoke intense public interest, there will not be sufficient alternative solutions to ensure the quality of ideas. Given the randomness of the quality of submitted solutions, innovation seekers thus look for enough engaged participants to produce high-quality ideas [36].

Similar to most researchers, we regard the number of participants as a proxy for contest quality to measure the utility that solution seekers obtain from the contest [12]. This operation is also supported by extreme value theory [37], which posits that the maximum value of all draws can improve with more draws. In a crowdsourcing contest, as the number of participants (representing the draws from the solver pool)

## 2.2. Effects of task instruction on participation

As a primary source of contest information, task instructions can provoke solver interest. Thus, seekers may feasibly elicit solver attention by rigorously formulating task instructions. One research stream ex amines the impacts of restrictions in instructions on participation be haviors. Regarding how restrictive requirements in task instructions affect participation, a consensus has not yet been reached. Gillier et al. [13] manually categorize instructions into three types: unbounded in structions (solvers can contribute any ideas they want), suggestive in structions (solvers are asked for an idea contribution to improve current product benefits), and prohibitive instructions (solvers are asked for ideas that do not involve current product benefits). The empirical results show that idea originality and value are statistically equivalent for un bounded and prohibitive tasks. Gillier’s research [13] is an early exploration of the effects of task restrictions on crowdsourcing partici pation, and it provides insights for identifying requirement-oriented writing strategies. Gillier manually divides task restriction into three categories but does not suggest how to describe restrictions from a wording perspective or quantify writing strategies for massive tasks. To examine how the design of task instructions affects solver intention, Steils and Hanine [14] conduct a survey with 507 respondents. They observe that greater restriction negatively impacts the participation intention of highly reward-oriented participants but does not affect that of individuals with a low level of reward orientation.

In terms of the task statement, Pollok et al. [17] analyze the role of the quality of problem articulation in attracting participants, and the results confirm that a well-articulated problem statement reassures po tential solvers and consequently increases their willingness to partici pate. In the context of microtask crowdsourcing, Gadiraju et al. [39] conclude that the clarity of task instructions enables more participation, but poor formulation of task instructions hinders worker enthusiasm in both obtaining rewards and building a good reputation. Through a survey experiment, Steils and Hanine [14] encouraged seekers to pro vide sufficient instructions to attract creative solvers. The subsequent moderating effect indicates that lengthy task instructions may nega tively affect intention to participate. Moreover, disclosing the seeker’s identity in task instructions is beneficial for attracting solver attention because status signals from high-status seekers help solvers reduce their uncertainty about the consequences of participation [17].

Much attention has been given to the role of task instructions in encouraging mass participation. However, the task instructions in most studies [13,14,17] have been in the form of semistructured texts or a few sentences, which may impact the generalizability of the findings. For instance, in knowledge-intensive crowdsourcing platforms such as Zooppa and InnoCentive, the task instructions are very complex and look more like persuasive essays. Analysis of structured data and ques tionnaires is insufficient to derive implicit information from such long texts. Evidence from other domains [25–28] indicates that the persua siveness of texts may influence user participation. To the best of our knowledge, however, the impacts of task-instruction writing on will ingness to participate in crowdsourcing contests has never been investigated.

## 2.3. Effects of instruction-writing strategy on participation

Contest participation is a process of self-determination [40] in which individuals form evaluations through critical thought processes [41]. Thus, participation is primarily motivated by the subjective intentions of individuals [18–20]. Cognitive science reveals that decisions are often made by using a combination of descriptive and experiential informa tion [42]. When a crowdsourcing task is simple, individuals can un derstand the task based on experience, with little cognitive effort required [43]. In contrast, in knowledge-intensive crowdsourcing con tests, individuals are more likely to be influenced by instruction de scriptions because the required experience is too complex to be learned easily. Accordingly, the question of how to formulate instructions is the seeker’s main concern in affecting a solver’s decision to participate.

Expectancy theory [21] has become a commonly accepted scheme for explaining how individuals make decisions regarding various behavioral alternatives. When an individual decides whether to partic ipate, he or she undergoes a cognitive process that involves three ele ments: expectancy, instrumentality, and valence (Fig. 1). In this process, individuals measure the extent to which the rewards are important to them and the likelihood of achieving high performance if they exert efforts [22]. The first subprocess between effort and performance is related to the question, “What is the probability of accomplishing the task as required if I work hard?” An individual’s perception of this probability is defined as expectancy. The second subprocess between performance and reward corresponds to the individual’s concern about whether his or her efforts will be rewarded, i.e., “What is the probability of getting paid as promised if I accomplish the task well?” In practice, instrumentality is determined by the extent to which a solver’s perfor mance is finally transformed into rewards. Thus, instrumentality refers to the subjective assessment of the probability that a given performance level will lead to a reward [44], and this element is mainly influenced by solvers’ beliefs about the crowdsourcing platform’s trustworthiness and normality. The third subprocess involves the relationship between reward and personal goals and answers the question, “Is it possible to achieve my personal goals once obtaining the rewards?” This subprocess also reflects the attraction degree of the reward valence to an individual solver.

We only focus on expectancy and valence, not considering instrumentality in our research. The reasons are as follows. Each platform has set up a sophisticated awarding mechanism to ensure seekers’ promise keeping. Additionally, the reputation of a platform is not affected by an individual solver.

Writing is an important form of communication [45]. Writing stra tegies refer to the skills or techniques used in text composition. Expe rienced writers are skilled at using different writing strategies to achieve their expression purposes. Unlike literary works that emphasize artistic expression, task instruction-writing for a crowdsourcing contest typi cally entails informative and persuasive writing. Informative writing [23,46], also known as expository or explanatory writing, makes readers aware of a given fact. This writing uses evidence-based information from credible sources to support a focused and logical explanation of the topic at hand. Persuasive writing [23,24] considers the reader’s perspective and aims to influence others to take some action or generate change. It requires a clear awareness of what arguments might most affect the audience being addressed and often uses emotive language.

By drawing on expectancy theory, we hypothesize that the writing of task instructions has impacts on participation by affecting a solver’s mental representation, i.e., perceived expectancy and valence. First, a solver’s perception of expectancy, i.e., the probability of achieving good performance, is influenced by the statements concerning task requirements. Task requirement disclosure is a sort of informative writing for the purpose of informing the readers of factual information about the task. Such a disclosure will affect solvers’ beliefs in their ability to undertake a task because individual decision-making depends not only on the cognition of one’s ability but also on perceptions about the task requirements [47]. Solvers psychologically form their self-efficacy [48] by comparing perceived task requirements with their cognition of their own abilities. In addition, a solver’s perceived valence, i.e., the attractiveness of the reward to the solver, is often influenced by the narrative on task rewards. Narratives that emphasize rewards are a sort of persuasive writing because they aim to influence solver attitudes by conveying motivation-based signals through linguistic cues. According to motivation psychology, individual motivation is often activated in a certain situation and subsequently causes a particular behavior. In the scenario of browsing crowdsourcing tasks, a reward description in the instructions is the trigger that activates solvers’ needs and motivations [30]. Accordingly, this study focuses on two types of writing strategies, i.e., requirement-oriented and reward-oriented writing strategies (Table 2), given their impacts on different elements involved in a solver’s cognitive process.

To convey the requirements of a task, seekers commonly use restrictive words to limit participants’ behaviors and let them know what to do and what not to do. Terminology is also inevitably used to describe what expertise is needed in the task. Thus, using restrictive words and using terminology are two types of requirementoriented writing strategies that we intend to study. In addition, a reward-oriented description should stimulate the motivation of solvers to improve their perceived valence of the expected rewards. Given that motivation is mostly divided into intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, we categorize reward-oriented writing strategies as emphasizing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards. In terms of the purpose of instruction writing, a requirement-oriented strategy is informative, while a rewardoriented strategy is persuasive.

## (1) The strategy of using restrictive words

This strategy refers to using restrictive terms such as “should” and “must” to limit participant behaviors, with the purpose of providing the crowd with clear and intuitive insight into the expected solution. Scholars have studied the effects of instruction restrictions on partici pation behaviors but have not yet reached a consensus. Existing studies have manually classified the restrictiveness of task instructions into different types. However, it is still worth exploring how wording tech niques are employed to describe the restrictiveness of tasks. Under the background of knowledge-intensive crowdsourcing contests, our study focuses on the impacts of using restrictive vocabulary in instruction writing. Regarding whether task constraints have a positive impact on mass participation, one research stream argues that tasks without con straints lead to a higher quantity of brainstorming ideas [49]. A high level of contest autonomy may enhance the solver’s intrinsic motivation [38]. Nevertheless, some scholars suggest the opposite view: restrictive instructions do not inhibit willingness to participate and may even improve the quality of ideas. In this view, providing solvers with pro hibitive or suggestive requirements is helpful to eliminate a fixed mindset and generate more original ideas [50]. Steils and Hanine [14] find that greater restriction does not discourage participation for those with low levels of reward orientation. When individuals process infor mation during creative tasks, both requirements and material con straints are helpful to stimulate creativity [51,52].

Given the two opposing views, disagreement might arise from the two groups of potential solvers with different attitudes toward task re striction. The first group (hereafter called restriction-averse solvers) dis likes task restrictions. This type of solver might prefer task autonomy, be highly money-oriented, or take a risk by participating in the contest and expend less effort. In contrast, the other group (hereafter called restric tion-friendly solvers) shows a positive attitude toward task restrictions. Restriction-friendly solvers might be competitive players on the plat form or rational players who tend to avoid uncertainty in winning. Thus, they do not make decisions in a hurry.

Evidence supporting the existence of these two divergent groups is found in many online communities. Steils and Hanine [14] show that instructional constraints have different effects on reward-oriented solvers and intrinsically motivated counterparts in crowdsourcing con tests. Among crowdfunding and microloan platforms, sophisticated investors and less-experienced crowds are regarded as two different groups, with the latter often exhibiting herding behaviors [53,54]. If funders possess great ability and motivation, they will delve into careful consideration of issue-relevant information in task instructions [41]. Given the influence of group differences, the relationship between restrictive description and the number of participants in crowdsourcing contests may not be linear but may possibly be curvilinear.

To communicate effectively, seekers often signal the implicit cues of tasks to potential solvers by using restrictive words in the instructions. The text-based signal illustrates how one party may undertake actions to signal its underlying cues to other parties and has often been exploited to understand hiring and fundraising [55,56]. In general, a task with very few restrictive descriptions may attract many restriction-averse solvers who treat restrictive words as an indicator of task constraints. As the number of restrictive descriptions increases, restriction-averse solvers will decide not to participate. However, the overall churn rate may gradually decrease or even rebound because increasingly restrictive descriptions simultaneously attract restriction-friendly solvers who re gard restrictive terms as a signal of task clarity instead of constraints.

Specifically, a broadly defined task leads to more diverse solutions, but a clearly defined task may facilitate more focused efforts from solvers [32]. Unlike microtasks, crowdsourcing contests prefer solvers with high-level skills and extensive involvement in the task, thereby increasing solvers’ cost of participation. To avoid cost risk, restriction-friendly solvers need to have a thorough understanding of task objectives and requirements. They psychologically form their self-efficacy [48], which is defined as one’s belief in his or her ability to perform a task, by comparing perceived task requirements with their cognition of their own abilities. Existing studies [57,58] show that in dividuals are more willing to expend effort when self-efficacy is high, which suggests a positive relationship between self-efficacy and effort. Accordingly, we posit that requirement disclosure can act as a reference for restriction-friendly solvers to measure their self-efficacy and that the use of restrictive words to describe these requirements will improve task clarity. Clearly defined tasks contribute to helping restriction-friendly solvers measure self-efficacy accurately and reduce uncertainty, thereby facilitating their engagement.

In short, with an increase in restrictive descriptions, we expect the number of participants to gradually decrease with decreasing speed until a certain threshold is reached, and we expect the positive restriction effect from task clarity to start to outweigh the negative restriction effect from task constraints. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

H1a. . A nonlinear (U-shaped) relationship exists between the writing strategy of using restrictive words and the number of participants in crowd sourcing contests.

Moreover, given that the necessity of using restrictive words may vary depending on the complexity of the task, a U-shaped relation be tween restrictive description and participation may be affected by complexity. First, highly complex tasks are often accompanied by high returns (either monetary or nonmonetary), a feature that is undoubtedly attractive to restriction-averse solvers. However, restriction-averse solvers are more resistant to the restrictive description of more diffi cult tasks because they have to work harder to win. That is, increasing restrictions in complex tasks would further aggravate the loss of restriction-averse solvers at the beginning. Second, high-complexity tasks involve high standards for participants and thus require more restrictive descriptions. High complexity is accompanied by great un certainty, so potential restriction-friendly participants prefer high task clarity and thus are more tolerant of constraints. Additionally, restriction-friendly solvers prefer tasks with a higher challenge [38] because they want to earn money, develop skills, or obtain a sense of achievement. As a result. if a seeker uses more restrictive words to describe complex tasks, the enhancing effect on task clarity will be more significant, such that restriction-friendly solvers will have greater con fidence in winning. ## (2) The strategy of using terminology

The writing strategy of using terminology (specialized vocabulary) is another way to disclose requirements. A crowdsourcing contest is an innovation platform open to crowd talent with professionalism in various industries. For example, tasks in InnoCentive involve chemistry, life science, business, computer science, and clean technology, among others. In addition, quite a few solvers on the platform hold bachelor’s degrees or above. Without a professional vocabulary, the requirements of a task cannot be disclosed clearly and efficiently. Incorporating specialist language and terminology into the instructions will help solvers understand the concepts, phenomena, and relations of discipline; moreover, such an approach is intrinsic to learn disciplinary knowledge [59]. Therefore, seekers will inevitably use some domain terminologies in problem statements. Based on interviews across seven companies, Sieg et al. [60] explain that the formulation of a problem statement serves to enable potential solvers with qualified expertise to perceive a similarity between the current problem and past tasks they have encountered. When experienced seekers narrow down the problem ac cording to their domain knowledge, solvers may focus on the specific elements of a solution to allow the task to be solved by following a particular route. If seekers adopt particular terminologies that are inaccessible to outside problem solvers [60], task instructions can attract more solvers with relevant technical backgrounds [14].

Terminology usage can help solvers understand the factual infor mation of a task; however, excessive use of terminology may lead to negative effects. For the crowd on the platform, nontechnical terms may be more acceptable than technical terms [61,62]. Too much terminology could make the crowd perceive the complexity of the task. Processing such complicated information is also time-consuming [43]. As [38] shows, a higher level of complexity generates higher cognitive efforts, and an overload may exceed the cognitive capacity of the solver, thereby hindering his or her intrinsic interests. In light of the observations above, we expect that too much or too little use of terminology is not conducive to motivating solvers to participate. Instead, the appropriate usage of terminology would maximize participation. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2a. . A nonlinear (inverted U-shaped) relationship occurs between the writing strategy of using terminology and the number of participants in crowdsourcing contests.

Similar to the case for H1b, the inverted U-shaped relation between terminology usage and participation may be moderated by task complexity. The harder the task is, the more terminology is needed to explain the problem. Therefore, initially increasing the amount of ter minology in complex tasks would further accelerate the increase in the number of participants because the increased task clarity benefits solvers’ understanding of the task and their confidence in winning. On the other hand, the overuse of terminology in more challenging tasks would also aggravate the negative effects (i.e., the loss of solvers) because of the greater perceived uncertainty. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2b. . The inverted U-shaped relationship between the writing strategy of using terminology and the number of participants is stronger in tasks with higher complexity than in tasks with lower complexity.

## 2.3.2. Reward-oriented writing strategy

In addition to disclosing information, the writing of task instructions also serves to persuade solvers by highlighting rewards. Since a solver’s perceived valence is affected by the textual description concerning re wards, the emphasis on attractive rewards is actually aimed at satisfying personal needs. As individual motivation is a reflection of needs, examining individual motivation has always been a prerequisite to examining participation behaviors in crowdsourcing contests. Studies confirm that the language used by senders in word-of-mouth crafting is primarily targeted at receivers’ motivation [26]. To improve solver satisfaction with expected rewards, a reward-oriented description should consider solver motivation as the reference. Most researchers divide motivation into intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (Table 4). Intrinsic motivation refers to undertaking a task for spontaneous satis faction derived from engaging in the task itself; in contrast, extrinsic motivation refers to undertaking a task for an external outcome or compensation (e.g., acting for money or reputation) [20,40,63,64]. In response to these motivations, we focus on two kinds of reward-oriented writing strategies: emphasizing intrinsic rewards and emphasizing extrinsic rewards (Table 3).

Obtaining a monetary reward is the direct motivation for individuals to participate in crowdsourcing tasks. However, studies [67,68] have shown that even during early microtask crowdsourcing, solvers act not just to obtain money. This finding is especially true in crowdsourcing contests characterized by learning, challenge, and innovation. From the intrinsic perspective, some solvers participate in hedonic contests out of interest, fun, and curiosity [8,35]. They enjoy competition and thus obtain a sense of satisfaction. From the extrinsic perspective, some solvers are active in utilitarian contests for the purpose of developing personal skills [10,19], accumulating social capital [35], and seeking job opportunities [34]. Winning a crowdsourcing contest is viewed as one way of demonstrating capabilities and skills and as a means of self-advertisement for job seekers [30]. As crowdsourcing platforms gradually attach importance to the operation of online communities, solvers can also win peer reputation [10,38] and social capital [34,35] if they achieve good performance.

Since different types of rewards focus on different solver needs (for spontaneous satisfaction or utilitarian compensation), the descriptive techniques in task instructions should vary correspondingly. As the stimuli for solvers’ motivation, task instructions are expected to persuade the audience by conveying motivation-based signals through linguistic cues. That is, the writing strategy of emphasizing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards is basically a technique of persuading solvers to take action. As a dual process theory of attitude persuasion, the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) provides a framework for understanding the fundamental processes underlying stimuli processing and, consequently, the effectiveness of persuasive communications [69]. The ELM proposes two distinct routes for stimuli processing in the process of attitude for mation: a central route and a peripheral route. Under the central route, persuasion likely results from the careful and thoughtful consideration of the true merits of the information. Under the peripheral route, persuasion and attitude formation result from an individual’s associa tion with negative or positive cues in the stimulus or from making an inference about the statement. When the ELM is applied to website design [70] and persuasion in crowdfunding [41], central cues consti tute issue-relevant information that includes the specific attributes or merits of the focal topic, while peripheral cues typically consist of af fective components in the texts.

Informed by this, we further apply the dual-process theory of persuasion to the context of crowdsourcing. The ELM assumes that in dividuals can differ in how carefully and extensively they process a message and the position, object or behavior that the message is advo cating. That is, in any given context, the amount of elaboration or pro cessing a person does about a message or issue can vary from low to high along an “elaboration continuum” [69]. Individuals can think a large amount, a moderate amount, or even very little about a message, and the amount of thinking they engage in goes a long way in explaining how they will be persuaded. Given people’s dynamic positions along this contin uum, solvers may be inclined to go down both the central route and pe ripheral route when processing task instructions of crowdsourcing contests. To stimulate reward-related motivations, the instruction drafter, on the one hand, conspicuously emphasizes the tangible merits of the task and thus describes the task rewards in the form of issue-relevant infor mation statements (i.e., the central route). On the other hand, the drafter typically adopts the method of emotional appeal (i.e., the peripheral route) to evoke the resonance of the solver or stimulate scenario associ ation. This dual-route approach to persuasion is the most common persuasive factor in the form of linguistic cues in online platforms.

Specifically, a drafter first tends to show attributes (e.g., work au tonomy and skill enhancement) directly related to rewards in response to the rational judgment of the solver. This issue-relevant message is typically presented by objective language and is related to the argument quality, thereby evoking a central route to persuasion. Second, the drafter may use positive affective language to encourage solvers’ posi tive judgments along the peripheral route, thus allowing them to reach conclusions without elaboration. A positive emotional appeal tries to put solvers in a hedonic frame to increase their responsiveness to the re wards that make them feel good [71]. Positive linguistic cues usually help generate positive thoughts and lead to a high likelihood of desirable consequences among solvers [72].

For example, if drafters want to emphasize intrinsic rewards in in structions, on the one hand, they can state issue-relevant attributes such as “fun,” “creative,” and “autonomy” to respond to the rational judg ment of solvers. On the other hand, they may use positive affective words such as “incredible,” “crazy,” and “compelling” to convey a joyful working scene and indicate positive emotions. Similarly, when drafters emphasize extrinsic rewards, the focal issue-relevant attributes could be “skill enhancement,” “career development,” “social bonds,” etc. Addi tionally, positive affective words such as “glory,” “potential,” and “peer” could be used to indicate positive emotions. In a later section, we will follow the central (i.e., issue-relevant information statements) and pe ripheral (i.e., emotional appeal) routes when assigning initial keywords for the strategy of emphasizing extrinsic and intrinsic rewards.

We observe that some drafters already use issue-relevant information and positive affective language to indicate intrinsic and extrinsic re wards. For example, at the beginning of task instructions, the drafter may introduce the seeker company and highlight the development prospects. Emotive and positive language tones may also be applied to build a relaxing and interesting contest atmosphere. In fact, the strategy of emphasizing rewards may not necessarily change the factual content of the message but can attract attention and influence solvers’ percep tions of the rewards. Although some tasks enable solvers to learn new knowledge, build a reputation in the community, and even win potential employment opportunities, most drafters do not express those benefits in a scientific way. To investigate whether reward-oriented writing strategies affect crowdsourcing participation, we formulate the following hypotheses:

H3. . An emphasis on intrinsic rewards through central and peripheral routes has a positive relationship with the number of participants in crowd sourcing contests.

H4. . An emphasis on extrinsic rewards through central and peripheral routes has a positive relationship with the number of participants in crowd sourcing contests.

## 3.2. Measures

## 3.2.1. Dependent variable (DV)

Following earlier work [10,17,73], the DV (NumParticipant ) was set as the number of participants in a task. Zooppa grants everyone access to a non-confidential task after the award announcement. Here, solver participation only refers to solution submission excluding continued refinement or community feedback. For each task, only the solver who submitted a solution successfully was considered a participant.

## 3.2.2. Independent variables (IVs)

The four writing strategies constitute the four core explanatory variables. For each instruction, we exploit both syntactic analysis and semantic computing to quantify the extent to which a drafter adopts a particular writing strategy.

## (1) Keyword assignment of each writing strategy

On the basis of word frequency analysis of the collected instruction corpus, experts in crowdsourcing and linguistics assisted us in assigning an initial set of descriptive keywords to each writing strategy (Table 3). In terms of requirement-oriented strategies, we identified the restrictive keywords limiting solvers’ behaviors (e.g., “only,” “must,” and “mandatory”) and terminology related to video production (e.g., “script,” “resolution,” and “rig”). For each reward-oriented writing strategy, we identified issue-relevant and emotional-appealing key words following the central and peripheral routes.

## (2) Keyword expansion

We expanded the initial keyword lists by using the WordNet lexical database and a keyword expansion algorithm. By invoking WordNet in Python, we obtained a set of cognitive synonyms (synsets) for each input word. Synsets are interlinked through conceptual-semantic and lexical relations.

In addition to using a lexical database, we identified more keywords from the local knowledge, i.e., the collected instruction corpus. The expanded keywords are expected to have underlying connections (se mantic meaning, definitional relationship, or contextual contiguity) with the initial keywords. Based on the co-occurrence relations of initial keywords, we applied Mass Diffusion Algorithm [74–76] for keyword extraction to discover the hidden words that are semantically or conceptually related to the given initial keywords. The keywords expanded by Mass Diffusion are presented in Appendix A.

Next, an expert in linguistics and a rater from a non-linguistic field classified each expanded keywords in Appendix A (as correct or not). The two persons did not know the purpose of this experiment. Statisti cally, the Cohen’s kappa coefficient for the classification results of these two raters was 0.823 (p < 0.001), thereby showing strong agreement. On the basis of this classification, we further screened the expanded results. The identified keywords that can correctly describe writing strategies are presented in bold italics in Table A. Appendix B shows the final keywords for describing the four strategies, among which bold words are expanded by WordNet and italicized ones are obtained through the keyword expansion algorithm. Lemmatization and lowercase conver sion were performed for all the descriptive keywords.

## (3) Syntactic analysis

Syntactic analysis was conducted to extract the descriptive words from each task instruction. To ensure that the extracted words were used to describe the task, submission, or participants, we set up a set of descrip tion objects (except for the strategy of using terminology) as listed in Table 3. The text processing was implemented in Python. By importing the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK), we segmented the instruction text into sentences and tokenized the words. According to the pre-processed text, we further conducted syntactic analysis by using the Stanford Parser [77] in Python. The Stanford Parser is a natural language pro cessing package that ascertains the grammatical structure of sentences, for instance, which groups of words go together (as “phrases”) and which words are the subject or object of a verb. The Stanford Parser enabled us to identify dependency relations among the words in a sentence. Figure 3 illustrates an example of sentence parsing, in which the letters above the words are part-of-speech tags, and the arrow between two words repre sents a dependency relation. For each sentence, we extracted words with qualified part-of-speech tags. That is, the terms must have a qualified dependency relation (either direct or indirect) with the objects. The qualified tags and dependency relations are shown in Table 5. Note that the auxiliary verbs (such as “have,” “do,” and “should”) in the extraction results must be removed. Also, lemmatization and lowercase conversion were performed for the extracted descriptive words.

## (4) Semantic similarity computation

To quantify the extent to which an instruction adopts a particular strategy, we used Word2vec [78], a shallow neural network model, to produce word embeddings and calculate semantic similarities. Word2 vec takes as its input a large corpus of text and produces a vector space, typically of several hundred dimensions, with each unique word in the corpus assigned a corresponding vector in the space. With 4.7 million articles, the Wikipedia documents<sup>1</sup> were used as the corpus to train the Word2vec model. We imported the Gensim, a Python natural language processing library, to implement Word2Vec training, i.e., learning the semantic vector representation of hidden text layer from Wikipedia texts. The semantic similarity of two words was calculated according to the cosine similarity of the Word2vec vector representations. For each instruction, we calculated the average similarity (AveSim) between a descriptive word and all the keywords of a writing strategy. Finally, the average AveSim (AveSim) of all descriptive words in an instruction were considered as the degree to which this instruction adopts a certain strategy, i.e., the IV.

## 4. Analysis and results

## 4.1. Factorial design test

This study involves four factors of writing strategy. To pre-examine whether the effect of each factor at different levels on DV is statistically significant and whether an interaction occurs between factors, we con ducted a four-factor two-level factorial design. Each of the four factors was assigned into two levels. For curvilinear relationship, the difference between the DVs is obvious in the two cases when the IV is in the middle of the value range and at both ends of the range. To test the significance of the curvilinear relationship between requirement-oriented writing strat egy and task participation, we treat moderate use (high level) and less or extensive use (low level) of requirement-oriented strategy as two distinct levels. As for strategies that emphasize intrinsic and extrinsic rewards, a high level represents high use of the strategy; while a low level suggests no or less use of this strategy. We used a full factorial test design with two replications for each combination of the systematically varied factor levels and got $2 { \cdot } 2 ^ { 4 } = 3 2$ instances. The 32 most representative (the feature of a certain level-combination is very strong and representative) samples were selected from the total samples. On the basis of 32 repre sentative samples, the multivariate analysis of variance in Table 6 shows that the effects of all four writing strategies on participation were statis tically significant. None of the two-by-two interactions between these strategies were significant. That is, the effect of one writing strategy did not significantly depends on the other strategies. Table 6 also presents the effect size as measured by partial $\eta ^ { 2 }$ . Effect sizes are standardized mean differences that provide a quantitative estimate of the size of the effect of a specific IV. The effect size values of the four IVs are all greater than 0.14, thereby demonstrating that differences are not caused by sampling error but arise from heterogeneity between groups.

## 4.2. Descriptive statistics and model specification

The descriptive statistics and the correlations of the main variables are presented in Table 7. The variable “RestrictiveWordUsage” represents the extent to which an instruction adopts the strategy of using restrictive words, and “TerminologyUsage” denotes the strategy of using terminol ogy. ## 4.4. Robustness check

We conducted a variety of robustness checks to assess the stability of our main results. We began by adding cubic terms into the model to test whether the curvilinear relationship is perhaps S-shaped rather than Ushaped. The influence of the cubic term is not statistically significant and does not improve model fit, thereby providing stronger support for quadratic relationships. Furthermore, we treated outliers using the winsorizing method to prevent the possibility that a few extreme ob servations are driving the results. For each analyzed variable, the ob servations with values above the 95th percentile took the value of the 95th percentile, and all observations with values less than the 5th percentile were set to the value of the 5th percentile. We then reestimated the model, and the main findings of the six hypotheses remain unchanged.

Finally, following previous studies [82,84], we split the data ac cording to the empirically determined turning point to check whether two linear regressions generate slopes that are consistent with the pre dicted shape of the curve. For the RestrictiveWordUsage variable which has a U-shaped relationship with participation, the regression on the subsample with RestrictiveWordUsage-values below the turning point (i. e., the left half of the U-curve) indicates a negative relationship between RestrictiveWordUsage and participation. By contrast, the regression on the subsample above the turning point (i.e., the right half of the U-curve) demonstrates a positive relationship (Table 9). As for TerminologyUsage, which has an inverted U-shaped relationship with participation, the regression on the subsample with TerminologyUsage-values below the turning point (i.e., the left half of the inverted U-curve) shows a positive relationship between TerminologyUsage and participation. Conversely, the regression on the subsample above the turning point (i.e., the right half of the inverted U-curve) shows a negative relationship (Table 10). These tests provide more robust support for quadratic relationships, and we did not detect a sigmoidal relationship. In addition, the positive ef fects of emphasizing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards are still significant in the regression on the subsample.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Key findings

By building on text analytics, machine learning, and empirical analysis, our study suggests that the proper use of requirement-oriented and reward-oriented strategies in the drafting of crowdsourcing task instructions helps increase the number of participants.

First, existing studies on the effects of task restriction on participation behaviors have not yet reached a consensus. With an emphasis on text composition, we discover that the relationship between the use of restrictive words and the number of participants is not a simple linear relationship, but a U-shaped relationship. The approaches of using very few or many restrictive words in instructions are both likely to engage participants. The curvilinear relation may be explained by the existence of two groups of potential solvers with different attitudes toward task re striction. With an increase in restrictive descriptions, the number of participants will gradually decrease with a decreasing speed because of the loss of restriction-averse solvers who treat restrictive words as an indicator of constraints and the entry of restriction-friendly solvers who regard restrictive terms as a signal of task clarity. In addition, increasing restrictions will further aggravate the loss of restriction-averse solvers and strengthen the entry of restriction-friendly solvers in tasks with higher complexity. That is, the U-shaped relationship between using restrictive words and participation is stronger in tasks with higher complexity.

Next, although terminology is commonly used in task instructions, to the best of our knowledge, no scholars have empirically studied the impact of terminology usage on crowdsourcing participation. Using terminology can attract more solvers with relevant technical back grounds; however, too much terminology may generate higher cognitive efforts. Our results show that either too little or too much terminology in task instructions is not conducive to motivating solvers. An inverted Ushaped relationship exists between terminology usage and the number of participants, and the inverted U-shaped relationship is more signifi cant in tasks with higher complexity.

Finally, we investigated the persuasive effects of a reward-oriented strategy. Task instructions aim to persuade the audience by conveying motivation-based signals through linguistic cues. Thus, the writing strategy of emphasizing rewards is basically a technique for persuading solvers to take action. Drawing on the dual-process theory of persuasion, the results demonstrate that emphasizing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards by stating issue-relevant information and making emotional appeals is an effective means of promoting participation.

Our findings contribute to a better understanding of how crowd sourcing task instructions can be scientifically formulated through a combination with writing science and linguistics. Kellogg [85] outlines three stages of the development of writing skills in which three core elements become involved: the author, the reader, and the text. In the initial stage of knowledge-telling, the author expects to tell what he or she knows to readers. The second stage involves transforming what one knows for the author’s benefit. The final stage refers to crafting what one knows for the reader's benefit. Notably, in this third stage. the author must hold in mind his or her initial writing purpose, the words of the text itself, and the imagined reader’s interpretation of the text, thus creating an interaction and integration of the three elements. In this study, we advocate the use of both the requirement-oriented strategy for the au thor’s benefit and the reward-oriented strategy for the reader’s benefit. Our findings are in line with Kellogg’s definition of the most advanced stage of writing skills. The drafter of task instructions not only needs to maintain an interaction between their representation of writing purpose and the text representation but also must imagine how potential readers would interpret the text. Such crafting helps to form triangular in teractions between authors, readers and text. ## 5.2. Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the growing literature on the implications of the formulation of task instructions for participation in crowdsourcing contests.

First, from the perspective of linguistic cues, this work is the first to examine the effects of the instruction-writing strategy on solver partici pation in crowdsourcing contests. Previous research has focused on the impacts of instruction attributes on task outcomes but has not considered how to describe these attributes by using wording techniques. Based on expectancy theory, this study analyzes the cognitive process of individual decision-making and proposes two types of writing strategies for the first time. Our work sheds new light on the formulation of crowdsourcing task instructions by integrating instruction writing and linguistics.

Second, we propose a procedure for quantifying writing strategies for project instructions in online platforms. In previous studies, commonly used approaches such as qualitative analysis, manual classification, and questionnaires were not applicable to knowledge-intensive crowd sourcing contests because such task instructions with massive textual data may affect the generalizability of the findings. We were thus motivated to integrate text analytics, machine learning, and empirical analysis to quantitatively analyze task instructions in knowledgeintensive crowdsourcing tasks. Moreover, the proposed approach can generalize to writing strategies for other platforms, such as crowd funding and P2P lending.

Third, we also make contributions to specific issues related to the participation of crowdsourcing contests. Past studies have not reached a consensus on the effects of task restriction on solver participation. We identify two groups of potential solvers with different attitudes toward task restriction, and the findings reveal a U-shaped relationship between the use of restrictive words and participation. In addition, this study provides a new explanation for why solvers participate in crowdsourcing contests because we provide a better theoretical understanding of solver preference for task restrictions, specialization level, and reward type.

## 5.3. Practical implications

Overall, our study contributes to seeking an understanding of how to write task instructions that are more informative and persuasive to solvers. Specifically, we provide empirical evidence that using very few or numerous restrictive words makes it possible to encourage participants. However, attitudes toward task restriction vary from person to person. Therefore, before contemplating how to use restrictive words, drafters must ascertain their targeted solvers to adopt a proper writing strategy. Additionally, the excessive use of terminology is not suggested because the negative impact of terminology in increasing cognitive efforts would be more significant than the positive impact in improving task clarity. In addition to informative writing, drafters should adopt a reward-oriented writing strategy to persuade solvers. To attract more alternative solutions, drafters could state issue-relevant information and adopt emotional ap peals to increase the audience’s perceived extrinsic and intrinsic valence.

In practice, different writing strategies may produce different persuasive effects, even when describing the same attribute, and further affect solvers’ willingness to engage. For example, the sentence “This is your chance to become a music star” emphasizes nonmaterial rewards, which is more attractive strategy than simply telling the solvers how much they would be paid. Certainly, crowdsourcing could change the nature of social employment in the future [18]. To a certain extent, a crowdsourcing contest acts more like a process of staff recruitment for a company. Firm managers should indicate both internal and external rewards through linguistic cues to meet solvers’ psychological, social, or emotional needs.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

As with all studies, this work is subject to several limitations that provide great opportunities for future investigations. First, the data of this study come from a single crowdsourcing platform. At present, many reward-based crowdsourcing platforms exist around the world, each of which is tailored to a specific field. These platforms may vary in terms of complexity and awarding rules. However, we believe that the similar ities between platforms render our results generalizable, particularly in terms of instruction design and the competition process, both of which are generally standardized across platforms. Second, writing strategies are proposed according to expectancy theory and thus aim at the cognitive process of individual decision-making. This approach is surely not the only way of categorizing writing strategies in a crowdsourcing contest because the strategy can be defined from other perspectives. For instance, future research could extend the writing strategy to include exaggeration or analogy from the perspective of rhetorical devices if the data are available.

## 6. Conclusion

Instruction writing is both a science and an art. To elicit attention from the solver population, a task instruction drafter has the re sponsibility of conveying factual information and persuading the audi ence to participate through better textual communication. Given the nature of participating in activities, which involves solver engagement in opportunities for benefits, we drew from expectancy theory to pro pose two types of writing strategies, i.e., requirement-oriented and reward-oriented strategies. On the basis of text analytics, machine learning, and empirical analysis, our research suggests a U-shaped relationship between the use of restrictive words and the number of participants in crowdsourcing contests. In contrast, an inverted U-sha ped relationship occurs between terminology usage and participation. Providing issue-relevant information and making emotional appeals has also proven to be effective ways to emphasize intrinsic and extrinsic rewards. In practice, instruction drafters need to comprehend the crowdsourcing market and solver psychology. In this way, they can properly exploit linguistic writing strategies apart from meeting basic writing standards such as readability and usability. This study is a pre liminary exploration of the role of linguistic cues in motivating crowd sourcing participation. We believe that more interesting issues should be explored in this line of research.
