## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Recommerce Mixed-methods research Comprehensive action determination mode Green information technology Sustainability

## A B S T R A C T

A consumer electronics recommerce platform (CERP) is a customer-to-business online marketplace for trading in and recycling used electronic products. Its success depends on the usage of many customers. Employing a sequential mixed-methods approach, this paper aims to identify and examine the factors influencing CERP usage. Proposing and validating a contextualized comprehensive action determination model, we find that monetary value and convenience indirectly affect intention to use CERP. through attitude toward CERP. while attitude toward sustainability and online trading habit directly affect intention to use CERP. The results offer instruction for practitioners to develop appropriate solutions for boosting CERP use.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, the rapid development of technology has shortened the life cycles of consumer electronics such as mobile phones, laptops, tablets, and wearable devices, resulting in a greater need for replace ment and disposal as compared to other products [1–3]. According to a recent study, China alone generated 781 million units of obsolete mobile phones in 2015. This number will increase to 937 million units in 2025 [4]. Similarly, statistics also reveal that around 350,000 mobile phones are thrown away every day in the USA.<sup>1</sup> The burgeoning number of obsolete digital devices has triggered strong growth in the global refurbished and used consumer electronics market [5]. As reported by Forbes.com, global revenue in this sector is expected to rise from 19.7 billion in 2017 to 38.9 billion dollars in 2025<sup>2</sup> .

Consumer electronics recommerce platforms (hereinafter referred to as “CERPs”) are increasingly being established globally to enable users to trade their obsolete digital devices online ([5, 6]; Tong et al., 2018; [7]). For instance, Gazelle, which was launched in 2006 in California, recycled about 4.1 million pre-owned phones on its platform in 2017.<sup>3</sup> Similarly, Aihuishou (meaning “love recycling” in Chinese) processed

10,000 used digital devices every day through its facility by the end of 2017.<sup>4</sup> For most CERP, its business model – customer-to-business (C2B) – is exactly the opposite of the traditional business-to-customer (B2C) e-commerce model. In the C2B model, a consumer sells his/her old de vices to the company for a fair price, while in the B2C model, companies sell products to the consumers [5, 6, 8]. Moreover, this C2B model is different from a classifieds website like Craigslist, where customers have to post an ad and wait for someone to buy the device [9]. Trading in a CERP is simple, after a customer submits upfront quality statements, a system-generated price pops up for the device immediately. The CERP will arrange third-party logistics to pick up the device if the customer accepts the offer [5, 10].

Identifying strategies to attract potential customers and retain cur rent ones is a vital objective for the continued success or even survival of CERPs. However, current research has been primarily devoted to dis tinguishing CERP with traditional consumer electronics recycling channels such as Craigslist, eBay, street peddlers, and automatic vending machines, introducing the business model of CERPs, or designing the unique reverse supply chain management systems for CERPs (e.g., [5–7, 10–12]). There is little research examining the motivations behind the usage of such systems. Without a clear interpretation of the drivers for system usage, CERPs are onerous to attract enough users and will lose in the biting market competition.<sup>5</sup> Thus, there is a clear need to advance the current literature by theoretically building and empirically testing a research model of CERP usage motivations.

The aim of this study is, therefore, to address the above issue by exploring the following research question: “What factors drive in dividuals’ intention to use CERP?” To answer this question, we ground our research on the comprehensive action determination model (CADM) [13] to develop a research model that would improve our understanding of factors that drive the usage of CERP. Originally developed in the environmental psychology literature [13], CADM incorporates inten tional, normative, situational, and habitual factors to explain people’s environmentally friendly behaviors such as electronic cars adoption [14], green traveling [15], and recycling behavior at the workplace (Ofstad et al., 2018; [16]). Obsolete consumer electronics contain a mix of hazardous chemicals such as mercury, lead, and cadmium and are harmful to both people and the environment. By reducing obsolete consumer electronics at home, using CERP can bring significant envi ronmental benefits. An individual’s decision to use CERP to recycle used customer electronics is similar to one’s pro-environmental decision. Therefore, we believe that CADM can be usefully employed to offer an insightful perspective for investigating individuals’ intention to use CERP. Given the fact that systematic and conclusive studies are missing in the current literature on the CERP phenomenon, this study employs a sequential mixed-methods approach [17]. Following Zhang & Ven katesh [18], Hua et al., [19], and Wunderlich et al., [20], this research starts with qualitative content analysis to identify the key CERP-specific features by coding 340 online comments. Subsequently, a research model was built based on CADM and the antecedents identified earlier and then tested using a quantitative survey of Chinese consumers.

This study is expected to make important theoretical and practica contributions. First. although CERPs have been widely founded world. wide in recent years, research attention on the CERP phenomenon continues to be limited and fragmented in the literature [5]. Our study makes unique contributions to the literature by proposing a theoretically grounded and empirically validated model to explain individuals’ mo tivations to use CERP. Such a research process consequently deepens our understanding of the CERP phenomenon. Second, while rich and extensive studies in the fields of information systems, marketing, and computer science have explored users’ motivations to participate in e-commerce (e.g., [11, 21–23]), the existing literature has offered relatively little insight into the C2B e-commerce business model. To the best of our knowledge, our study is among the first to provide theoretical and empirical insights into the factors that affect users’ participation in the reverse e-commerce phenomenon. Third, by incorporating inten tional, normative, situational, and habitual factors into a comprehensive research model, the present study responds to recent calls to investigate the antecedents of green information technology (GIT) adoption from a lens other than rational choice frameworks such as the theory of planned behavior (TPB) and technology acceptance theory (TAM) [24, 25]. Fourth, while the CADM has been widely used to explain one’s envi ronmentally friendly behaviors in an offline context, this research con tributes to this theory by adapting it (and hence extending its applicability) to the online context. Finally, the empirical findings of our study are expected to offer rich practical guidelines for CERP operators and managers to attract potential customers and retain current users.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Consumer electronics recycling

Discarded consumer electronics such as mobile phones, laptops, and tablets are currently the world’s fastest-growing waste problem and must be addressed urgently [1, 26]. It’s no surprise that, in recent years, research on consumer electronics recycling has gained more and more attention from scholars globally. Extant literature on this question can be divided into two distinct but related streams. The first strand of work, which is the most pertinent, focuses on exploring enablers and inhibitors of an individual’s recycling behavior. Demographics variables, eco nomic incentives, and psychological factors were revealed to influence people’s intention to participate in consumer electronics recycling ac tivities (e.g., [1, 27, 28]). For example, by employing residents in Bei jing, China, as research participants, Wang et al. [27] suggest that people who have higher incomes are unwilling to recycle their used digital devices because they care less about the economic benefit of recycling. Mishima & Nishimura [28] indicate that incentives/rewards such as a discount on purchasing new products play an essential role in enhancing the mobile phone recycling rate in Japan. Moreover, based on the TPB, Wang et al. [1] reveal that environmental awareness, the convenience of recycling, and attitude are positively and significantly related to citizens’ intention to recycle electronic wastes.

The second strand of work revolves around the determinants of choosing consumer electronics recycling channels (e.g., [2, 29]). Chi et al. [29] point out that producer and municipality collection models are extensively adopted in developed countries such as the USA, Ger many, and Japan, while for many less developed countries, consumer electronics collection is dependent on the informal sectors such as un qualified workshops. Tan et al. [2] indicate that the most used recycling channels for Chinese customers are street peddlers, maintenance shops, waste collection stations, retailers, and producers. Some studies have also begun to describe the value of CERP as a new emerging recycling channel. The background information and current studies on CERP are discussed in the next section.

## 2.2. Consumer electronics recommerce platform

An online CERP is a type of online marketplace that allows people to sell, trade-in, or recycle their obsolete digital devices such as cell phones, tablets, and computers to companies or professional buyers [5–7, 10, 11]. Compared with the regular recycling channels such as Craigslist, eBay, and waste collection stations, CERP provides users with a convenient recycling process and fair prices without the need to haggle [5, 11]. CERP has experienced extensive development and popularity in recent years. For instance, with strong support from government policies and rapid development of the Internet, China alone has more than 50 CERPs including Alibaba-backed Huishoubao (www.huishoubao.com) and JD.com<sup>6</sup>-backed Aihuishou (www.aihuishou.com) [6]. Other CERPs operating with a similar business model include Gazelle (www.gazelle. com) in the USA and FLIP4NEW (www.flip4new.de) in Germany.

Using CERP to recycle is relatively simple. First, a customer logs on to a CERP website and requests to sell his or her used digital device by entering information about the item and its current condition. The CERP offers a price based on metrics of age, wear and tear, and market de mand. After accepting an offer, the customer can either mail the device in their own box or request a third-party logistics provider to pick it up. Upon arrival, a technician will inspect the device and determine its quality. If the device matches the quality stated by the customer, then the CERP notifies the customer and pays the previously agreed price. If the quality of the device deviated from what was promised (e.g., due to a smaller storage capacity, a different color, damage), the CERP issues a counteroffer, and the customer can choose to accept or reject the deal. If no deal, the device is networked to the customer [5, 6, 11]. A typical web page of Aihuishou.com is shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.3. Comprehensive action determination model

The “CADM” was originally designed for explaining proenvironmental behavior by Klockner ¨ & Blobaum ¨ [13]. It has been adapted and verified in various behavioral domains such as recycling, green products adoption, waste separation behavior, travel model choice, and energy conservation behaviors (e.g., [14–16, 33]). The CADM model postulates that individual environmentally relevant behavior is determined by four primary factors: intentional, normative, habitual, and situational [13]. The “intentional” factor claims that human behavior is usually determined by cognitive-structured attitudes or intentions [34]. The TPB informed the intentional components in the CADM. The “normative” factor contends that the driving force of behavior is a feeling of moral obligation [35]. The norm activation model (NAM) informed the normative components in the CADM. The “habitual” factor suggests that through regular repetition, a behavior becomes automatic or habitual [36]. CADM noted that frequent envi ronmental behaviors were best predicted by habit, whereas infrequent environmental behaviors were better predicted from intentions [13, 16]. The “situational” component suggests that one’s decision to perform an action is determined by certain situational facilitators/constraints [37]. Various situational facilitators/constraints are identified in previous environmental psychology literature such as availability of alternatives (e.g., energy-efficient equipment) and financial and behavioral costs of energy curtailment [16]. All four factors do not exist independently of one another but interact in a complex way over time [13, 16]. For example, Klockner ¨ & Blobaum ¨ [13] find that normative factors influence ecological behavior indirectly through situational, habitual, and inten tional factors.

The CADM model is suitable for this study for the following two reasons. First, the CADM model has been extensively employed in earlier investigations on pro-environmental behaviors (e.g., [14–16, 33]). For instance, by using CADM as the basic framework, Ofstad et al. [33] revealed that employees’ waste separation behavior is determined by perceived behavioral control, personal norms, social norms, attitude, and habits. Similarly, by surveying 1793 Norway car owners, Nayum & Klockner [14] found that situational factors (e.g., household income and license holders in the household) and intentional factors (e.g., attitude) impact the purchase of a more fuel-efficient car directly, while norma tive processes had an indirect influence. van den Broek et al. [16] pointed out that CADM was able to explain a large amount of variance in household energy-saving behavior, while normative and intentional factors had limited predictive power. Recycling obsolete digital devices can have significant benefits for the environment by reducing landfill waste, conserving natural resources, and decreasing energy consump tion. Therefore, we believe that a user’s decision to use CERP to recycle used customer electronics is similar to an individuals pro-environmental decision. Second, by including the assumptions of the TPB, NAM, habit, and situational factors, CADM dodges the weak ness of the single models and could be appliable in plenty of contexts including CERP. For example, the TPB neglects the influence of personal norms, while the NAM fails to account for the non-normative impacts. The CADM accommodates both normative and non-normative con structs [38]. We believe that the CADM provides a robust yet parsimo nious theoretical foundation to understand the drivers of CERP usage.

## 3. Methodology

To examine the antecedents of CERP usage by potential consumers, this study employs a sequential mixed-methods approach. First, a qualitative content analysis was performed to unearth the CERP-specific antecedents. In other words, the qualitative study (study 1) is used to identify those CADM factors that are directly related to the CERP context. Afterward, a research model was built based on CADM and the antecedents identified earlier and then tested using a quantitative sur vey of Chinese consumers. The mixed-methods approach is chosen based on the following reasons: (1) few studies have examined the adoption or diffusion of CERP, and an understanding of the underlying antecedents of a user’s motivation to use CERP is lacking. The qualitative analysis allows us to acquire in-depth insights into the targeted phenomenon, and (2) by employing a combination of qualitative and quantitative methods, mixed-methods design is not only able to explore both explanatory and confirmatory research questions but also offer stronger inferences than a single method approach [18–20, 39–41]. We believe such a design is well suited to the current study.

According to Hong et al., [42], contextualization is the act or process of putting the characteristics and usage contexts of the technology artifact into our research model. Contextualization can bring various benefits to our research, including (1) it makes the models more accu rate and the interpretation of results more robust; (2) it is better able to explain anomalous findings; and (3) it identifies how context enhances or modifies understanding of a common phenomenon across contexts [42–44]. ## 3.1. Study 1: Qualitative research

A sample of posts and comments for content analysis was identified from the Zhihu website (http://www.zhihu.com), which resembles Quora (https://www.quora.com/), where mainland Chinese users created, answered, and edited questions. The number of daily active users of Zhihu had exceeded 26 million as of June 2018.<sup>7</sup> In Zhihu, people can ask questions about anything and can get answers from anybody in the world who sees the question. Moreover, unlike Yahoo Answers (https://answers.yahoo.com/) or Baidu Knows (https://zh idao.baidu.com/) which focus on short and quick answers, Zhihu en courages users to provide high-quality answers in a detailed way ([45]; Zheng et al., 2020). According to Kuang et al. [45], Zhihu is the largest knowledge exchange platform in China, and over 70 million users are discussing diverse topics such as business, technology, psychology, and culture in Zhihu regularly. Neuendorf [46] points out that data available in various interactive media platforms are valuable sources for content analysis. Compared with traditional qualitative approaches such as semi-interview, interactive media data offers advantages such as being easily acquired and indexed, randomization, and large sample size [46]. We identified a thread posted in Zhihu: “How to evaluate the CERP Aihuishou?” All the posts and comments related to this thread were collected using R Studio and stored in a CSV file. The final dataset included 340 comments in total.

Content analysis was employed to analyze the comments with the help of a coding team consisting of two graduate students who are native Chinese speakers and familiar with Aihuishou. This method was employed because it provides a deep understanding of a phenomenon from the user’s perspective [47]. This approach was widely accepted by previous literature using a mixed-methods design (e.g., [41, 48]). When a discrepancy arose, the authors and the two coders discussed the context together to reach a consensus. Table 3 offers a detailed description of the findings from the qualitative data. ## 3.2. Quantitative research

The empirical investigation integrates the findings from qualitative study and the factors identified in CADM. According to CADM, one’s environmental behavior is determined by four groups of factors: inten tional, normative, habitual, and situational [13]. In this study, we conceptualized attitude toward CERP, attitude toward sustainability, and intention as intentional factors, personal norms as a normative factor, online trading habit as a habitual factor, and perceived security, monetary value, convenience as situational factors. CERP characteristics are considered situational factors in the current study because they could furthermore act as situational facilitators/constraints that could promote/limit a user’s perceived ability to use a CERP. This is consistent with previous literature that employs CADM as a framework to study the diffusion of sustainable technology [50]. Fig. 2 depicts the research model.

## 3.3. Hypothesis development

Following previous literature, in this study, we defined attitude to ward CERP as the extent to which a consumer likes trading using CERP and considers it to be a good idea [21, 22, 51]. According to TAM, attitude toward using a particular technology is a crucial facilitator of the intention to use that technology [51]. Considerable existing in vestigations in the e-business context have demonstrated that in dividuals’ attitude significantly impacts their behavioral intention to employ a particular e-business application (e.g., [21, 22]). For instance, by surveying 253 American consumers, Chen et al. [21] indicated that a customer’s attitude toward using an online store positively impacts his or her behavioral intention to use the online store. Similarly, researchers have also established the influence of attitude on GIT acceptance [20, 52]. For instance, by analyzing survey data from 950 Northern European residents, Toft et al. [52] revealed that attitude toward smart grid technology is related positively and significantly to residents’ accep tance of smart grid technology. We believe customers who have a pos itive attitude toward CERP are more likely to adopt the CECP service.

H1. Attitude toward CERP has a positive effect on intention to use CERP.

Attitude toward sustainability or environmental attitude is the most often used construct in environmental psychology as a powerful pre dictor of pro-environmental behaviors [1, 53]. According to Wang et al. [1], individuals exhibiting such an attitude tend to make an effort to solve ecological problems. Attitude toward sustainability has repeatedly been reported to be an important determinant of many pro-environmental behaviors such as energy-saving, towel reuse, recy cling, and paying for environmental goods [1, 54, 55]. For instance, Gao et al. [55] revealed that employees who form a positive attitude toward energy conservation are more likely to save energy in their workplaces. Similar mechanisms could also be expected with regard to the relation between attitude toward sustainability and the intention to use CERP. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2. Attitude toward sustainability has a positive effect on intention to use CERP.

Online trading habit refers to the habit of trading on the Internet in general [36, 56]. Studies have reported that those with online shopping habits would turn to online stores when they have needs for shopping without further consideration [56, 57]. Moreover, Chou and Hsu [58] also revealed that online customers’ trading habit moderates the rela tionship between their evaluation of online services and repurchase intention. Habit can drive to repurchase intention by removing the need for individuals’ rational evaluation of uncertainty. Following this logic, people who have an online trading habit may think using an online recycling platform is safe and lacks uncertainty. We believe that users with online trading habits would turn to the CERP to trade in their de vices other than traditional recycling channels. Thus, the following hy pothesis is proposed:

H3. Online trading habit has a positive effect on intention to use CERP.

Monetary value is defined in this study as the degree to which an individual believes that CERP provides satisfying trading prices [59]. It is known that product quality and price are two criteria employed by most customers in deciding whether to buy a product or adopt an online service [60]. Researchers have also revealed that economic incentives play a critical role in recycling behaviors [61]. In the context of mobile phone recycling, Liu et al. [3] suggest that recycling price is one of the most influencing factors on users’ decision of phone recycling.

Compared with irregular recycling channels, the price offered by CERP is fairer. We believe that if a person perceived that the CERP provides higher recycling prices than traditional recycling channels, he or she is more like to formulate a positive attitude toward CERP. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H4. Monetary value has a positive effect on attitude toward CERP.

In previous studies, convenience has been defined as the degree of “consumers’ perception that they can use technology to finish their work at a convenient time, in a convenient place, and in a convenient way” ([62], p.477). Thus, it is not surprising that many studies supported the positive relationship between convenience and new information tech nology (IT) adoption (e.g., [62–64]). For instance, based on the valence theory, Ozturk et al. [64] found that convenience and utilitarian value significantly influence one’s mobile payment technology acceptance. Liu et al. [62] demonstrated that consumers' perceived convenience and personality factors jointly impact their intention to adopt mobile coupon applications in China. The importance of convenience on recycling has also been reported by researchers. For example, Wagner [27] find that 28% of respondents think that increased convenience would prompt them to recycle. CERP frees consumers from the price negotiations with street peddlers and enables them to recycle their used digital devices at home and at any time [7. 111. These conveniences save significant time and effort for consumers and increase consumers’ perception of the value of CECP. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5. Convenience has a positive effect on attitude toward OERP.

In this study, perceived security refers to the degree to which an individual believes that using the CERP will be safe [65]. In the e-commerce context, perceived security has been considered as the key antecedence of online shopping behavior [63]. Researchers have agreed that the lack of security as perceived by online consumers is the main obstacle to the development of e-commerce. Different from traditional systems, CERP deals with private and sensitive data that are directly related to identifiable persons [5]. Liu et al. [3] argued that people would refuse to recycle their used smartphones because of privacy concerns. We believe that if a person perceived that the risk of trading with CERP is low and has a conviction that CERP will deep clean their personal information stored in the digital devices, he or she is more like to formulate a positive attitude toward CERP. Thus, the following hy pothesis is proposed:

H6. Perceived security has a positive effect on attitude toward CERP.

Personal norm is a set of self-set moral standards on what ought to be done or not [24, 25, 55]. According to NAM, people’s pro-environmental or pro-social behaviors are driven by not only the evaluation cost and benefit but also their personal norm [1, 24, 35]. In the context of energy-saving behavior, individuals with a high level of personal norm have demonstrated a higher level of moral obligation and responsibility to save energy in the workplace [55]. Moreover, in the context of in formation systems adoption, Dalvi-Esfahani et al. [24] revealed that executives with a higher level of personal norm on environmental preservation are more inclined to form a positive attitude and intention to adopt green IT in their organizations. Taken together, it can be speculated that ones with a higher level of personal norm will be more likely to generate a positive attitude toward sustainability, which may further impact the intention to use CERP. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H7. Personal norm has a positive effect on attitude toward sustainability.

## 4. Study design, procedure, and participants

To empirically test the proposed research model and hypotheses, an online survey was conducted in the present study. We recruited re spondents in Mainland China, the largest consumer electronics market, and producer in the world. Detailed information about the research setting, measures, and data collection is given as follows.

## 4.1. Questionnaire design

When developing the questionnaire, we adapted existing validated scales from the past literature to fit the context of CERP. All constructs were measured using multi-item perceptual scales and were measured on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree.” Specifically, items for intention to use CERP were derived from Dickinger et al.. [66]: items for attitude toward CERP and personal norm were adapted from Gao et al., [55]. The items of perceived security were drawn from Schierz et al., [65]. The items of monetary value were modified from Turel et al., [59]. The items of convenience were taken from Liu et al., [62]. Attitude toward sustain ability was measured using four items adapted from Wang et al., [1]. The online trading habit was measured using three items adapted from Khalifa & Liu [56]. An expert panel of two business professors and three doctoral students assessed the face validity of these measures and found the items to be adequate.

As the respondents of our online survey were from China, we fol lowed the back-translation approach [67] to translate the questionnaire into Chinese. We then invited 25 participants from a large Chinese university to check for any ambiguity in wording and format and established the validity of the questionnaire. The final questionnaire used in the survey is presented in Appendix A.

## 4.3. Common method bias and non-response bias

We performed two statistical tests to evaluate the common method bias. First, Harman’s single factor test was conducted using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) across all variables [68, 69]. The results showed that the largest variance explained by an individual factor was 36.14% which did not account for a majority of the variance. Second, a common latent factor test was performed by adding a direct latent common method factor [68]. The results of a chi-square test were below the threshold of 3.84. Thus, the results of the above tests indicate that common method bias is not a significant problem for the current study.

To address the non-responsive bias, we followed the suggestion of Armstrong & Overton [70] to compare the demographics of early and late responders. Participants who completed the survey in the first week were considered as early responders (N = 228) of this study, while the rest were viewed as late responders (N = 134). No significant differences were found between the two groups suggesting non-response bias was not a concern for our study.

The proposed research model and hypotheses were empirically vali dated by analyzing the data that were collected from an online survey among Chinese consumers. The findings of current research provide important implications for both research and practice.

This finding is in agreement with those hypothesized in TAM that customers’ attitude toward using a new information system influences behavioral intention to use, which leads to actual system use [51]. Similarly, our study also confirms the positive association between attitude toward sustainability and intention. People who have a positive environmental attitude tend to have a greater willingness to recycle their obsolete digital devices using an online channel. This finding is consistent with those reported in previous studies which suggest that people exhibiting such attitudes tend to display sustainability behaviors [1, 54, 83]. Online trading habit shows the second greatest direct impact on intention to use CERP. Customers who used to buy goods or services online are more inclined to use CERP to trade their used digital devices. This finding supports the notion proposed by Khalifa and Liu [56], who suggest that people with an online shopping habit would turn to the online store other than a physical store when they have needs.

Monetary value and convenience drive the attitude toward using CERP, which translates to use intention. When individuals recognized that CERP is a more convenient and efficient way to handle used digital devices compared with traditional recycling channels, they are more inclined to formulate a positive attitude toward CERP. This finding is in line with previous studies which suggest that convenience is one of the key drivers of sustainable behaviors such as recycling and composting [84]. Similarly, when people perceived that the CERP provides higher recycling prices than traditional recycling channels, he or she is more like to formulate a positive attitude toward CERP. This finding is consistent with Wang et al., [1] which revealed that economic incentives could motivate ones’ pro-environmental behavior effectively. Personal norm also shows a positive effect on the individual’s intention to use CERP through attitude toward sustainability. People who feel some degree of personal responsibility toward recycling obsolete digital de vices are more likely to generate an environmental attitude and be interested in using CERP. This finding highlights the importance of in dividual moral responsibility on green IT adoption [25, 52].

Yet, one hypothesized relationship does not reach significance, that is, perceived security is not a significant predictor of attitude toward CERP. If a person perceived that the risk of trading with OERP is high, he or she is more like to formulate a negative attitude toward OERP. This finding supports the notion proposed by Liu et al. [3], who found that fear for personal information disclosure is the biggest barrier for obso lete smartphone recycling.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

The theoretical contributions of this study can be interpreted in the following ways. First, the most fundamental contributions of the current investigation come from studying important determinants of users intention to use CERP. While the use of information system/IT has received plenty of attention from earlier literature (e.g., Davis et al., 1989; [21, 22]), the literature reveals no rigorous effort studying the factors that impact user intention to accept CERP. Current literature on CERP has been qualitative and mainly focused on distinguishing CERP with traditional recycling or trading channels, describing the business model of CERP, or designing the reverse logistic systems [5–7, 11, 12]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first research effort to predict users’ intention to use CERP which advances our understanding of the CERP phenomenon and bridges the research gaps in the CERP literature.

Second, the robustness of CADM is established in an unexplored context, namely, the user’s intention to use CERP. While CADM has received much attention from researchers as a valid lens for explaining pro-environmental behavior [14–16, 33]. This research contributes to CADM by adapting it from offline to the online context. Specifically, this study identifies seven constructs that are most relevant to CERP. The findings pinpoint that most of the constructs in the proposed model are critical for promoting users to use CERP.

Third, electronic commerce has been a growing area of research fo some time. Previous studies on e-commerce are either focused on dis cussing the benefits of e-commerce to individuals, organizations, and society or users’ motivations to participate in e-commerce (e.g., [11, 21–23, 63]). The existing literature has paid limited attention to the C2B e-commerce business model. By incorporating attitudes, norms, habits, and technical features of CERP into a research model, this study offered a theoretically grounded lens to better interpret the facilitators of user’s behavioral intention to trade their used digital devices back to com panies through online channels, thereby responding to recent calls for investigating the reverse e-commerce phenomenon [5].

Fourth, this investigation also extends the existing literature on the antecedents of green IT adoption. Prior studies within this area focus on the influence of rational trade-offs between cost and benefit [24, 25, 52]. The present study represents one of the first studies focused on the in fluence of rational choices such as attitude, as well as irrational factors such as personal norm and habit on the adoption of Green IT. Future studies are recommended to integrate the above-mentioned perspectives to develop a comprehensive explanatory model for understanding sus tainable IT adoption and continued use.

## 6.3. Practical implications

This study also offers some precious suggestions for IT practitioners and policymakers. First, our study highlights the importance of CERP as a channel for e-waste recycling practices. Government agencies and policymakers should consider policies to encourage and inspire the development of CERP to achieve their sustainability goals. Second, attitude toward CERP emerges as the most powerful facilitator of the intention to use CERP relative to the other predictors. This points out the significance of developing and managing one’s attitude to guarantee the successful implementation of CERP. CERP operators might work together with online marketing companies to generate positive word-ofmouth which will be effective in developing a favorable attitude toward CERP.

Third, considering the importance of personal norm and attitude toward sustainability, regular environmental protection training pro grams and lectures can be launched by CERP operators. These measures can make individuals realize the importance of environmental protec tion and sustainability, therefore, reinforcing the social desirability and ethical rightness of adopting CERP.

Fourth, this study also highlights the effects of online trading habit to enhance intention to use CERP. In other words, individuals who formerly shopped or traded online are more like to switch to using CERP. Therefore, CERPs can place their ads or promotions in traditional online stores to attract potential users.

Fifth, the strong effect of convenience on behavioral attitude in dicates that users are seeking easy navigation and a convenient online experience. CERP designers should focus on ease of use. Moreover, a convenient CERP should minimize customer effort, facilitate fast completion of a transaction, and offer a short response time.

Sixth, the monetary value was found to have a significant effect on the intention to use CERP. Therefore, managers need to ensure that the CERP provides higher recycling prices than traditional recycling chan nels such as Craigslist and street peddlers. Moreover, CERPs can consider providing economic incentives to individuals to encourage their CERP adoption.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

Although we strive to conduct a rigorous study, limitations are inevitable. First, given the scope of the study, constructs that are not related to CACM were not included in our research model. We encourage future investigations to integrate other important factors that may elucidate CERP usage behavior from different angles. Second, we did not measure actual usage behavior in our research model, which is the limitation of studies employing a cross-sectional design [85, 86]. Future investigations can extend our research model by integrating actual usage behavior and employing longitudinal design and other methods. Third, we collected data from a single country, which introduces a po tential threat to the generalizability of this study [87]. Thus, the con clusions of our study may have been biased to some extent by the high-power distance and collectivist culture of this country. It would be interesting to see future research extending our model to a broader set of geographical contexts or societies other than China. Finally, to yield better insights into the understanding of CERP usage behavior in different situations, moderating factors such as gender, education, oc cupations, income, and personality can be considered in future investigations.

## 7. Conclusion

Despite its growing prominence across the world, CERP’s survival and success are threatened by various challenges including limited adoption by consumers. The purpose of this investigation is to predict CERP usage via the perspective of CADM. Through a mixed-methods design, this study finds that attitude toward CERP, attitude toward sustainability, online trading habit, monetary value, convenience, perceived security, and personal norm have differential effects on users behavioral intention to use CERP. These findings can serve as a springboard for future research to build a broad understanding of the factors that influence users’ usage of CERP and provide insights for re searchers and practitioners.
