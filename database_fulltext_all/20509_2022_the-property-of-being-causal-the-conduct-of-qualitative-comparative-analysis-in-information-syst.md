## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Qualitative comparative analysis fsQCA csQCA IS research methodology Literature review

## A B S T R A C T

Grounded in configuration and complexity theory, qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) combines the ad vantages of case-based and variable-oriented methods for rendering complex information systems (IS) phe nomena comprehensible. Given its manifold benefits, the QCA method has attracted considerable attention in IS research, with an increasing number of studies employing it as their methodological approach. Based on a comprehensive review and synthesis of recent QCA practices from the IS field, covering 12 years of research, we outline the most prevalent research gaps and limitations concerning QCA’s methodological application prior to identifying issues for further improvement as well as highlighting future research directions.

## 1. Introduction

Due to digitization and the increasing convergence of information technology (IT) and information systems (IS) with business and society, IS research has the challenge of untangling the rising complexity of the ever more closely intertwined relationship between the components of sociotechnical systems [1,2]. Such intricacy of interrelated factors re quires a paradigm that goes beyond approaches encompassing a linear perspective (e.g., multiple regression analysis or structural equation modeling) to understand these cause-effect relationships [3]. In recent years, qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) has become the approach of choice for unraveling the causality behind complex IS phenomena [1, 2,4,5]. Grounded in configuration and complexity theory, QCA com bines factorial logic inferred from theory and combinatorial logic drawn from set theory to make cause-effect relationships understandable [1,2, 4,5]. The configurational tenet of QCA stresses the conjunction of influencing conditions on an examined outcome by taking a holistic stance. With complexity theory as its theoretical underpinning, the application of QCA is a means to theorize and derive configurations that affect an observed outcome by accounting for multiple causal pathways [1,6,7].

QCA was first introduced by Ragin [8,9] in the context of political science to address case-based research for intermediate-sized samples, where the “sample sizes [are] too small for regression techniques but too large for systematic cross-case comparisons” [10], p. 258]. Therefore, QCA encompasses a hybrid character, combining the advantages of case-based (qualitative) and variable-oriented (quantitative) methods that enable IS scholars to approach configurational research [2]. From an epistemological standpoint, the qualitative paradigm in QCA pre vails, since the systematic comparison of cases founded on qualitative rationales is a major aspect of QCA [5,11]. However, the formalized and replicable nature of QCA also embraces principles of quantitative methodologies [11]. Regardless of the methodological characteristics, QCA must be understood as more than just a means to data analysis; rather, it represents an approach that must be incorporated into the theoretical grounding of a research project [12].

Given QCA’s manifold potential to explain and explore complex phenomena, extant research has already pointed to its strengths and pitfalls and has provided numerous recommendations for its application in the realm of IS [2,4,5,13,14]. In addition, there is extensive seminal work that deals with the methodological guidance of QCA in detail [12, 15,16]. Our study does not aim to complement this literature corpus with another paper highlighting the potential of QCA for our research discipline nor does it intend to add novelty to the wealth of methodo logical guidelines. Rather, as the popularity of QCA increases in the IS domain [2], we attempt to reflect on the patterns in QCA conduct in IS research by providing an overview of recent QCA practices and their methodological expression. Although deeper insights into these prac tices may help to advance the proper use of QCA in future research, only two attempts have been made to provide such a systematic review of QCA practices in IS research [17,18], albeit with a rather small literature sample and a limited analysis of selected theoretical and methodological issues. We aim to close this gap by answering the following research questions (RQs):

RQ1: What are the main patterns of QCA practices in IS research and how have they evolved over time?

RQ2: To what extent do QCA studies in the IS field show deviations from the recommended best practices in the extant literature and what are issues for further improvement?

By answering these RQs, we point out avenues for methodological improvement and possible research endeavors, aiding IS scholars to avoid “the risk of misusing the technique and producing spurious con figurations without theoretical advancement,” as recently cautioned by Park et al. [[2], p. 35]. To achieve this goal, we perform a systematic literature review [19] to identify and analyze the IS literature published in high-impact journals and conference proceedings since the first QCA association in the IS literature in 2004 [20]. We identified and analyzed 119 relevant papers that use QCA as primary or complementary research method. Based on the main findings, we critically elaborated implica tions for research and practice and formulated 19 recommendations for further improvements. While interested readers can use these implica tions and recommendations to identify research gaps worth studying, IS scholars, who seek to conduct a QCA study, can follow them throughout the research process to avoid major pitfalls and strengthen the relevance and rigor of the approach.

We first introduce the theoretical background of QCA in the IS literature in Section 2, related works in Section 3, and we present our methodological approach in Section 4. We provide the results of our analysis in Section 5. We then discuss the theoretical and practical im plications drawn from these findings as well as the limitations of this study in Section 6, prior to providing concluding remarks in Section 7.

## 2. QCA–The property of being causal

QCA encompasses a concept of causality which relies on empirical consistency so “that any given type of cause influences outcomes in a similar way across all relevantly similar cases” [[3], p. 40]. To explore this empirical regularity and map the complexity therein, the configu rational approach of QCA makes use of three pillars: asymmetry, conjunction, and equifinality $[ 1 , 6 , 7 ]$ . The QCA property of asymmetry is the counterpart of the notion of linear variance-based models, which assume a causal symmetric relationship between the occurrence of an observed outcome and its causal conditions [4].<sup>2</sup> In contrast, causal asymmetry implies that the presence or absence of a causal condition does not implicitly cause the occurrence or absence of an outcome [21]. Instead, the effect is dependent on the property of conjunction, which refers to the set-theoretic approach of combining predictive conditions using Boolean operators that form configurations explaining the occur rence or absence of an outcome [5]. Thus, QCA does not focus merely on the effects of bivariate relationships but emphasizes conjunctural causation that holistically affects observed outcomes. The essence of the last pillar, equifinality, emphasizes that multiple and alternative con figurations of causal conditions can cause the same outcome [22]. The formalisation of the observed causality can be used to form statements about reality that can be further analyzed to build or refine theory [2].

Transparency is vital in QCA studies, since the methodological pro cedures generate configurations whenever researchers include a set of conditions, whether or not they are meaningful [12]. Thus, the QCA conduct must allow IS scholars to verify whether the configurational results are grounded on a theoretically and methodologically sound foundation [23]. Our goal is to shed light on how QCA is conducted in the IS literature and to examine its methodological and theoretical rigor.

## 3. Related work

With the increasing prevalence of QCA research in the IS literature, IS scholars have engaged with QCA and its application to present methodological aspects in the form of tutorials on how to conduct QCA in IS studies [13,14] or to highlight theoretical aspects and their utility for IS scholars [2.24]. As noted in the introduction, the primary goal of this study is to reflect on the theoretical and methodological patterns in IS research by providing a comprehensive overview of QCA studie published over the recent decades. Soto Setzke et al. [17] and Mattke et al. [18] followed a similar research focus, yet with one major limi tation. Both works have a strict focus on studies published in journal from the AIS Scholar’s Basket of Eight, and a set of other selected journals and IS conferences, which resulted in rather small study sam ples of n = 32 [18] and n = 64 [17]. We, by contrast, include all pub lication outlets as well as leading IS conferences according to VHB JOURQUAL3 in our literature search, with a total of n = 119 QCA studies. This comprehensive sample enables us to draw a more repre sentative picture of the QCA literature landscape in IS research. Another major value offered by our study relates to the sound theoretical framing that is lacking in the related works. For example, Soto Setzke et al. [17] rely on the general research phases of study design, model design, and data analysis for structuring their study results, while Mattke et al. [18] distinguish between research context and approach as well as data analysis and results when presenting their findings. In our view, these approaches are helpful to gain a general overview of the QCA method, but these approaches are not sufficient to systematically address the specific theoretical particularities associated with this method, espe cially for OCA novices. Thus. we account for these specific characteris: tics and features by relying on Park et al.’s [2] multiplicity framework when formulating the results of our systematic literature review, as described in Section 4. Aside from narrative and content analysis as applied in the related works, we additionally include a bibliometric analysis as a review method of choice to detect patterns within QCA studies in the IS field, including citation analyses and in-depth analyses of publication practices as well as their emergence over time. Moreover, our multi-perspective view helps us to identify the most prevalent research gaps prior to highlighting worthwhile future research di rections. We have included a more detailed overview of the similarities and distinctions between this study and the related work in Appendix A.

## 4. Systematic literature review

With a focus on reviewing the use of QCA in IS research, we con ducted a systematic literature review in August 2021, following the guidelines of vom Brocke et al. [19], in leading IS journals and confer ence proceedings. Therefore, we searched for peer-reviewed studies in publication outlets ranked according to the VHB JOURQUAL3 [25] by applying the search term “qualitative comparative analysis” in a full-text search. We intentionally focus on peer-reviewed literature in the respective ranking list because our study does not claim to be exhaustive nor is it intended to provide a representative sample of all QCA studies in IS research. Rather, our primary purpose is to provide deeper insights into the QCA practices in the leading IS journals and conferences that encompass a considerable body of knowledge in the IS domain. We refrained from including more search terms (e.g., abbreviations such as “QCA” or terms related to more specific QCA techniques such as “fuz zy-set qualitative comparative analysis” or “crisp-set qualitative comparative analysis”) into our literature search, since the search for “qualitative comparative analysis” in the full texts of the publication outlets enables us to identify all relevant studies that may mention related terms.

Our literature search yielded 263 publications, whose titles and ab stracts we read to identify those relevant to our analysis. The assessment step was based on a consistent set of inclusion and exclusion criteria. We included studies that conducted QCA as a main or complementary part of their research approach, and we excluded studies that considered QCA as part of a research commentary [26] or studies that briefly summarized the QCA concept [1,20], presented methodological guide lines [e.g., [5,13,27]], published research in progress [e.g., [28,29]], employed multi-value QCA (mvQCA) [30,31],<sup>3</sup> or only mentioned QCA but did not apply the methodology [e.g., [32,33]]. Nevertheless, we considered valuable theoretical and methodological contributions of several excluded studies [5,13,14,26] for analyzing the conduct of QCA research in the IS literature. This assessment step resulted in 119 rele vant studies covering 12 years of QCA research in the leading IS journals and conferences, representing an important fragment of knowledge in IS research. More detailed information on the literature search and selec tion procedure is provided in Appendix B.

The iterative multi-step approach of QCA can be broadly divided into three main steps, which we discuss in detail when presenting the results. The first step includes the preliminary theoretical considerations that comprise the theoretical framing and explication of the outcome under study, includes strategies for selecting causal conditions, and identifies cases that capture the empirical basis of the phenomenon of interest (see Section 5.2.1). The second step is concerned with data preparation and analysis, which includes substeps such as ensuring data validity and reliability, dichotomizing, or calibrating the data as well as performing truth table analyses and robustness tests (see Section 5.2.2). The third step deals with the presentation and interpretation of the configura tional results against the (multifaceted) theoretical background (see Section 5.2.3). We study how this iterative multi-step approach is con ducted in the identified IS literature, steered by the methodological guidelines of seminal work [12,15,16,23,34]. To synthesize and struc ture the results of our systematic literature review, we are guided by Park et al.’s [2] multiplicity framework, including the concepts of theoretical multiplicity, causal recipes, and configurational multiplicity.

Theoretical multiplicity reflects that complex IS phenomena often need to be considered through multiple theories to examine and identify the competing and complementary relationships among factors in order to study an outcome [2]. As shown in Fig. 1, we use this concept as a guiding framework to examine the theoretical considerations for building comparative research designs within the reviewed studies. The concept of causal recipes reflects the determination of the factorial and combinatorial logic in QCA studies; hence, this concept considers the formalisation of conjunctural causation. We use causal recipes as a framework to investigate processes and design decisions to derive con. figurations in published IS research. According to Park et al. [2], theo retical multiplicity supplies the causal ingredients, while causal recipes provide the cooking instructions resulting in the finished “dishes” of configurational multiplicity. We use the concept of configurational multiplicity to explore and synthesize the reporting and interpretation habits of the ecology of configurations within the examined studies along with the QCA pillars asymmetry, conjunction, and equifinality. On the foundation of the research framework depicted in Fig. 1, we devel oped a coding scheme, which two researchers have applied indepen dently on a concept-centric approach, to examine the full texts of the included 119 studies. The multiplicity framework serves as a compre hensive concept matrix encompassing numerous important units of analysis that enable us to analyze and synthesize the literature corpus in a structured manner in accordance with common guidelines [35,36]. The deductively extracted data from the analyzed literature corpus along with a detailed coding scheme are included in Appendix B and C. For assessing and confirming the reliability of our coding procedure, we calculated the interrater agreement of 0.89 and the inter-coder reli ability of 0.72 [37,38]. We discussed discrepancies among authors until a consensus on the coding was reached.

## 5. Qualitative comparative analysis in IS research

## 5.1. Bibliometric analysis

As can be seen in Fig. 2, the QCA method has become more prevalent in the IS literature since 2009. Notably, since 2015, the QCA approach has been increasingly applied across studies in leading IS publication outlets.

Interestingly, with $5 7 \% ,$ the majority of the QCA studies from our literature sample was published in conference proceedings such as the Americas Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS, 18 publications), Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS, 17 publica tions), European Conference on Information Systems (ECIS, 12 publica tions), International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS, 18 publications), and Pacific Asia Conference on Information Systems (PACIS, 5 publications). The other 43% of publications are distributed across leading IS journals, with the International Journal of Information Man agement (5 publications), MIS Quarterly (4 publications), Electronic Commerce Research and Application (4 publications), and International Journal of Information Systems (4 publications) recording the highest number of QCA studies. Since 2019, however, the number of QCA studies published in IS journals has exceeded the number of the pub lished conference proceedings.

With regard to their impact, QCA studies in IS research have as yet attracted only moderate attention in the IS community. The top five studies with the highest impact according to their citation counts per year include Rivard and Lapointe’s [39] study on user resistance during IT implementation (302 citations, 33.56 citations per year), Park et al.’s [40] study on the impact of business intelligence and communication technologies on organizational agility (160 citations, 40 citations per year), and Ordanini and Maglio’s [41] study on decisions for successful new service development (138 citations, 11.50 citations per year). The other two studies in the top five show even fewer citations per year, including Bouwman et al.’s [42] study on the impact of business model innovation practices in small and medium-sized enterprises (105 citations, 52.50 citations per year) and Tan et al.’s [43] study focusing the impact of electronic service failures on websites and customers (91 ci tations, 18.20 citations per year).

Another interesting picture is offered when analyzing the countries of origin of the QCA study authors. Germany provides the largest output of QCA studies with 48 authorships, followed by the USA (37), China (23), Canada (15), and Spain (13). Remarkably, authors from these top five countries (out of 27 countries) are responsible for 50% of all QCA studies in our sample. The top ten authors and their affiliations are shown in Table 1. The QCA study landscape is dominated by several IS scholars from the University of Bamberg and the Norwegian University of Science and Technology, followed by a few researchers from Canada and the USA. This predominance is also reflected in the fact that the most productive ten authors (according to their first authorship) are responsible for 37% of all studies, and almost half of the study samples (46%) were published by the most productive 20 authors (from a total of 240 authors).

5.2.1.1. Research area and theory. The selection of cases and variables in QCA studies is driven by the outcome under investigation. Therefore, the first step in building the comparative research design is determining the research endeavor and the explication of the outcome of interest [11]. Upon analyzing the selected study sample, we found that QCA studies in IS research always explicate the outcome under study and use QCA mostly to investigate RQs at the organizational level (63%), the individual level (35%), and even at the macro level (2%).

Given the case-based nature of QCA [2], the observed dominance of organizational-level studies is not surprising. However, regarding the evolvement of QCA studies over time according to the level of analysis (cf. Fig. 3.), it can be observed that individual-level studies have considerably increased since 2016. In 2020, individual-level studies accounted for about 42% of all published articles in this year (11 of 26 studies). Another interesting view is offered when analyzing the sample size of the studies according to the respective unit of analysis. The me dian sample size of individual-level studies is n = 204, whereas organizational-level studies show an intermediate median sample size of n = 70 and macro-level studies a slightly higher median sample size of n = 89.

The individual explicated outcomes of the 119 studies can be found in Appendix C. To provide an overview of the diverse subjects of inquiry, we draw on Grover et al.’s [60] classification scheme (an adaption of Vessey et al. [61]) for the diversity of IS research areas and topics. In their classification scheme, Grover et al. [60] differentiate between eight distinct research areas covering a wealth of topics ranging from issues concerning organization concepts, problem domain-specific concepts, or societal concepts, to name a few. When assigning the QCA studies into the different research areas and topic categories, we identified six broad research areas and 18 topic categories, with “6.0 organization concepts” being the most researched area, accounting for 83% of the whole sample (see Fig. 4). Among others, studies on IT impacts, technology transfer (including innovation, acceptance, adoption, and diffusion), and IT usage/operations are the most focused topics of this research area. The remaining 17% of studies deal with topics from the research areas “5.0 system/software management concepts” (7%), “2.0 system/software concepts” (5%), “7.0 societal concepts” (4%), and both “3.0 data/ information concepts” and “4.0 problem domain-specific concepts” with 1% each.

As mentioned, the underlying strategies for building the comparative research design in QCA studies need to be derived from theory [11]. This recommendation is consistent with the common view in IS research that theoretical framing contributes to a deeper understanding of diverse phenomena of interest, regardless of the research topic [60,62]. Most QCA studies rely on configuration and complexity theory, in which QCA is implicitly embedded. However, this theoretical foundation does not provide variables to choose from for a particular research project; thus, researchers usually use a “mixed bag of conditions” [[11], p. 26], in which researchers adopt constructs from previous related works that are well suited for their research project [e.g., [63–66]]. We also examined to what extent IS scholars draw on complementary theory to inform their QCA research. The findings indicate that the theory of dynamic capabilities [e.g., [6,67,68]] and the resource-based view [e.g., [55,69, 70]] are by far the most frequently used theoretical lenses, followed by the transaction cost theory [e.g., [71,72]], the service-dominant logic perspective [e.g., 41,73,7441,73,74], the IS success model [e.g., [45, 47]], and agency theory [e.g., [76,77]]. The observed popularity of these theoretical lenses corresponds to the finding presented above, according to which organizational concepts are the focus of most QCA studies in IS research, including topics on IT impacts, adoption, and use. Among the frequently applied theoretical lenses, we also found some borrowed theories from psychology and other social sciences, such as the theory of reasoned action [78,79] and social exchange theory [72, 80].

## 5.2.1.2. Variable and case selection strategies

As Park et al. [2] emphasize, approaching QCA atheoretically and analyzing and interpreting data with QCA simply because it is available for certain conditions does not contribute to well-grounded configura tions. Nevertheless, variables can also be derived inductively in a QCA approach by analyzing empirical data and linking the derived variables back to theory [15]. Moreover, QCA is particularly well suited for abductive approaches to the convergence of deductive and inductive inference [2.40.49.72]. OCA can incorporate a priori theorized variables into the causal recipe and output a data-based explanation of reality in a parsimonious manner, reducing complex phenomena to their core, and generating new propositions that complement or contradict existing theories [15]. According to Habermas [[81], pp.113–114], “abductive thinking impels the process of inquiry onward…. The content of our theories about reality is extended abductively through the discovery of new hypotheses, whereas we inductively check the agreement of our hypotheses with the facts.” In this way, QCA as an abductive approach is potent for formulating new hypotheses and propositions, which is especially important in IS research because of the lack of theorizing novel concepts [82].

When researchers theorize factorial relationships, they can leverage the instruments of Boolean algebra and the distinction into necessary and sufficient conditions for an outcome [12]. From the perspective of set theory, sufficient conditions constitute a subset and necessary con ditions a superset of an outcome. Therefore, the conditions “may be considered necessary if they must be present for an outcome to occur, and sufficient if they can produce an outcome by themselves” [[21], p. 802]. This distinction is an essential feature of QCA, as variance-based approaches assume that conditions are both sufficient and necessary for an outcome [26]. We found papers that incorporate hypotheses (25 studies) or theoretical propositions (16 studies) in their comparative research design, however, predominantly without incorporating the basic concepts of sufficiency or necessity in formulating the relation ships. Moreover, we found 10 studies that provide empirically derived propositions, thus exploiting and explicating the abductive potential of QCA.

The number of examined conditions is not irrelevant. One problem with a large number of conditions is related to limited empirical di versity, since each additional condition exponentially increases the possible conjunctive links between the examined conditions, which in dividualizes empirical cases and blurs the core interconnections [11]. The phenomenon that only a small fraction of potential configurations is also empirically observed is not uncommon in the social sciences but should be reduced as much as possible as it renders the interpretation of configurational results difficult [34]. We discuss this issue and corre sponding strategies in more detail in Section 5.2.2 as part of the truth table analysis. The data analysis of the IS literature showed a median of 6 conditions with a minimum value of 3 and a maximum value of 14 conditions. A large proportion of 72% of the studies falls into a recom mended [11] range of 4 to 7 conditions and 27% either give no infor mation or consider a very small or very large number of conditions.

The findings reveal that 66% of the examined studies applied QCA as the sole method of data analysis. Although there is no objection to using OCA as the main method depending on the research goal. “OCA does not in itself open up the ‘black box’ of complex phenomena and processes […]; it rather acts like a flashlight that points at some crucial spots in the black boxes of the cases under investigation” [[83], p. 170]. Therefore, validity and explanatory power in IS research could be augmented by using QCA in mixed-method approaches, complementary to qualitative methods [84,85] or methods of statistical inference [48,86], along with triangulating the results [48,83]. Despite the frequent calls for a com bined use of QCA in mixed-method research designs, our analysis shows that only 34% of studies used QCA as complementary to other qualita tive (e.g., case studies, interviews [39,41,87]), and quantitative (e.g., statistical inference such as regression analyses [48,88,89]) methodo logical techniques.

The complementary approach enables deeper insights into the data basis and is therefore especially relevant for IS research, where QCA is predominantly applied in intermediate-N (38% of the analyzed studie are based on 16–100 cases) and large-N scenarios (57% of the analyzed studies have >100 cases).

## 5.2.2.2. Calibration

When it comes to calibration, we found that the IS literature pre dominantly uses the direct type of calibration for fsQCA, which means that three qualitative anchor points have to be determined [4,86].<sup>5</sup> We identified many different anchor determination approaches in the IS literature. One common approach is to utilize the thresholds of the applied Likert scales in empirical surveys as qualitative anchors; for example, for a 7-point Likert scale, 1 is for full non-membership, 4 cross-over point, and 7 for full-membership [e.g., [44,47,49,68]]. Several studies regarded the data distribution based on percentiles or min-max values as outer limits and the median or mean as a cross-over point [e.g., [52,55],103–105]. We could not retrieve a rationale or a description of the applied calibration for 8 studies (7%). Furthermore, we observed 10 studies that bypassed the calibration value of 0.5, that is, the point of maximum ambiguity, by adding or subtracting a constant of 0.001 to the condition values [e.g., [45,49,58,75]]. This intervention in the calibration avoids dropping ambiguous cases from further analyses.

## 5.2.2.3. Truth table analysis

Followed by this step, the dichotomous or calibrated set membership values are subject to analysis to investigate the role (sufficient, neces sary, or neither) of the examined conditions under the configurational conjecture. The basic analytical tool for this procedure is a truth table, which lists all possible configurations (combinations of causal condi tions) affecting an outcome. Each column of the truth table depicts the causal conditions under study, and each row represents a particular configuration, where each condition is encoded 1 for condition = TRUE and 0 for condition = FALSE and is fitted to its dichotomized or cali brated values. From this, it follows that the number of possible combi nations increases exponentially with the number of included conditions (2<sup>k</sup> possible combinations, where k is the number of incorporated con ditions) [12]. However, not every emerging configuration can always be observed empirically, leading to so-called logical remainders.

We found that the IS literature rarely explicitly addresses the chal lenge of logical remainders and, as such, the strategy for the attendant problem of limited empirical diversity. We only identified 8 studies in our literature corpus that reported the extent of limited diversity [65,66, 72,87,99]. Nevertheless, disregarding theoretically logical but empiri cally unmeasured configurations can strongly influence the resulting configurative implications [12,83], thereby rendering the configura tional solutions complex, because the inclusion of plausible logical re mainders provides additional logical components to minimize the formula that explain sufficiency for an examined outcome [16]. Logical remainders are generally implicitly addressed and utilized in the IS literature following a strategy of counterfactual analysis (83 studies; hence, 70% reported results from counterfactual analysis). Counterfac tual analysis is a plausibility assessment of logical remainders. It mini mizes the Boolean solution formula for an outcome by incorporating simplifying assumptions by adding (indicating easy counterfactuals) and removing (indicating difficult counterfactuals) redundant causal con ditions from configurations without affecting the known outcome [106]. The output of the counterfactual analysis is a complex solution (solution neglecting counterfactuals), a parsimonious solution (solution consid ering all types of counterfactuals), and an intermediate solution (solu tion considering only easy counterfactuals) [22]. We found that 62% of studies use the Quine–McCluskey algorithm to conduct this logical minimization process by exploiting the fsQCA software (50%), the “QCA” package in R (7%), Tosmana (1%), or a combination of these tools (4%) [107]. However, 45 studies did not provide any information on the analysis tool or the employed algorithm.

Before conducting the Boolean minimization, it is possible for a researcher to define thresholds for the strength of evidence, operation alized by a minimum frequency of empirical observations, and a con sistency value under which the configurations in the truth table are handled as logical remainders.<sup>6</sup> The frequency cut-off attributes more relevance to the empirical evidence while dealing with deviant case errors [108]. The rationale for determining the frequency cut-off is often aligned with the sample size, as recommended in seminal works [16, 102]. This alignment is mostly set to 1 (in 24% of the studies) or 3 (in 25% of the studies) in the IS literature dependent on whether N is small or large. The consistency value indicates “the degree to which the cases sharing a given combination of conditions (i.e., a row in the truth table) agree in displaying the outcome in question” [[16], p. 44]. For the consistency limit, a value close to 1 should be preferred; Ragin [109] suggests a minimum value of 0.75, because below this value the set-theoretic property of sufficiency (subset of an outcome) is no longer justifiable. Accordingly, the consistency value in the IS literature is predominantly within this range, between 0.75 and 0.89 (68%).

A further important consistency measure, the proportional reduction of inconsistency (PRI), should be taken into account for assessing con figurations within the truth table, since disregarding this indicator can greatly affect the explanative implications of the solution formula [34, 110]. A low PRI for a configuration indicates a high “degree to which a configuration is as sufficient for an outcome as it is sufficient for the negation of this outcome” [[110], p. 3]. Seminal works recommend a PRI above 0.75 [12]; therefore, we were surprised that 97 studies (82%) have not considered or reported the PRI value in IS research.

Another surprising finding is that 52% of studies did not test for a superset relationship between a condition and an outcome (necessary condition). In most cases, the lower consistency limit for a condition considered necessary is set at 0.9, and explanatory power is assessed by ensuring coverage values above 0.6 once the consistency threshold is met [109,111]. Identified necessary conditions can be omitted from further truth table analysis, since this analysis is designed to find suffi cient conditions. Omitting variables that are superfluous from further sufficiency analysis reduces the case-condition ratio [12,112].<sup>7</sup> Never theless, some IS scholars are integrating necessary conditions within their reporting scheme of sufficient conditions for an outcome [e.g., 49, 100,113]. Moreover, we can confirm Soto Setzke et al.’s [17] findings of a false understanding of necessary conditions among some authors [114, 115].

## 5.2.2.4. Measurement model and robustness checks

Given the sensitivity of QCA to case selection, dichotomization, and calibration [4,15,23], it should be a desire of IS scholars to establish validity in their results, especially when generalizing over the estab lished configurational findings (i.e., external validity) [108]. In the following, we elaborate on some selected findings.<sup>8</sup>

First, QCA is in principle not designed for latent constructs. Ac cording to Liu et al. [4], multi-item constructs should not be used directly without appropriate measurement model testing. Therefore, in the case of IS studies that include latent constructs using survey methods, measurement model tests in the form of internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity must be con ducted and reported. Nevertheless, we identified studies that utilized multi-item constructs and yet did not report results of a measurement model test [e.g., [116,117]] or studies that performed confirmatory factor analysis but lack detailed insight (e.g., bivariate correlations be tween constructs) [e.g., [68,118].

Second, model misspecification errors in the form of calibration er rors should be controlled via robustness tests, as the concluded set re lationships could change with small changes in calibration thresholds We identified 15 IS studies performing a calibration sensitivity test, but still 83 fsQCA studies did not test (or report) for calibration robustness. In addition, the influence of deviant cases can be controlled by con ducting sample sensitivity tests by adjusting the frequency cut-off [47, 87,119,120]. Condition errors can be controlled by performing set relationship sensitivity tests, i.e., adjusting thresholds for raw consis tency and PRI [e.g., [48,69,70,121]]. Other robustness tests applied in the IS literature refer to predictive validity [e.g., [58,69,80,121]] and design validity [87,89,113,122,123]. Overall, we identified only 27 studies (23%) that relied on some kind of these robustness tests.

## 5.2.3.2. Conjunction

The obtained solution formula(s) from the analyses capture(s) the factorial and combinatorial logic. Each condition within the formula(s) plays its part in the holistic effect on an outcome which is formalized utilizing Boolean syntax. The Boolean operators enable researchers to explicate the individual “complementary, substitutive, or suppressive relationships by allowing comparison of the constellation of elements across different configurations” [[2], p. 1499]. Furthermore, as exem plified by Park et al. [2], Boolean algebra is not merely a representa tional vehicle; it is a simple and straightforward means of highlighting intersections between theorized and empirically observed configura tions and, based on this, explaining and highlighting implications and propositions in regard to theoretical multiplicity. We identified only 37 studies (31%) that explicitly reported their solution formula(s) in Boolean notation.

Instead of relying on Boolean notation, the IS literature predominantly relies on the graphical representation brought forth by Ragin and Fiss [86] to introduce the solution formula(s). Such an exemplary representation is depicted in Table 5. The notation comprises the elements “●”, “⊗”, and blank spaces. The black dot indicates that a specific condition within a particular configuration must be present for an outcome to occur. The crossed-out circle represents the negation of the black dots; hence, it indicates that the absence of a condition within a configuration adds to the cause of the outcome. Blank spaces are so-called “don’t care” conditions explaining that their presence or absence is of no importance for the cause of the outcome. This type of representation allows to incorporate the results of the counterfactual analysis by displaying the output of the parsimonious and the interme diate solution. The intersectional elements of those solutions indicate the core conditions and the remaining elements peripheral conditions [22]. The distinction between core and peripheral conditions dictates “causal coreness in terms of the strength of the evidence relative to the outcome, not connectedness to other configurational elements” [[22], p. 403]. Core conditions are symbolized by a larger size of the notation elements compared to the peripheral conditions.<sup>9</sup> We were able to identify 96 studies (81%) that used the notation of Ragin and Fiss [86] to present the conjunctural nature of their configurational results.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Implications for research and practice

## 6.1.1. Theoretical multiplicity

Guided by Park et al.’s [2] multiplicity framework, we systematically reviewed and analyzed QCA studies from the IS field in accordance with the concepts of theoretical multiplicity, causal recipes, and configura tional multiplicity. The findings in the context of theoretical multiplicity revealed the diversity of research topics, research designs, and meth odological features on different levels of analysis in the IS discipline in recent decades. We discovered both similarities and differences compared to QCA practices in other research disciplines. While the similarities could be reasoned by the fact that complex phenomena prevail in all business and management research disciplines, the dif ferences could be attributed to the specific focus of the IS discipline on human-machine systems, which places the interaction between IS arti facts and human users at the center of all studies [62]. This specific focus is reflected in the unique body of knowledge in the IS field, which in cludes a broad set of concepts, theories, processes, and applications for the development, management, and design of IS [128,129].

In line with Soto Setzke et al. [17], we found a strong predominance of large-N studies (N > 100) and a smaller proportion of small-N and intermediate-N studies. Therefore, IS studies often employ condition-based approaches, that is, they attempt to identify pattern and insights from cross-case inference rather than relying on a case-based approach, as is the case in most small-N scenarios [23]. Condition-oriented approaches often use probabilistic elements to approach the method. Prior literature reviews of QCA practices from related research disciplines such as the business and management [10, 130,131] or entrepreneurship and innovation research fields [132] have found a similar tendency toward the use of QCA to analyze large-N studies. Thus, this trend is a phenomenon that has also emerged outside the IS discipline. Several management scholars criticize this tendency, arguing that dealing with large-N samples does not allow to achieve closeness to the cases [10] or to address the lack of actionability [130]. Therefore, the use of complementary methods could provide deeper insights into a large sample. However, we found that only one-third of the selected studies use QCA complementarily with other qualitative or quantitative methodological techniques, whereas the majority of the studies rely on QCA as a single-method design. Related works on QCA practices have shown that a complementary approach is far more common in other disciplines [130]. For example, in their study of QCA practices in management research, Seny Kan et al. [130] found that nearly 60% of all QCA studies use the approach in combination with other conventional methods. Misangyi et al. [10] confirmed this finding and even reported an increase of QCA usage in mixed-method studies, especially in combination with regression analysis.

Recommendation 1: Especially for condition-based scenarios, it is worthwhile to complement the research with qualitative data to learn about the causes within the configurational pathways and connect and link them to empirical data $( i . e . ,$ cases) and theory [23,34,83,133].

With regard to research areas and topics, we observed that QCA i mostly applied as a method for investigating phenomena of interest in organization research (e.g., IT impacts, technology transfer, and IT usage/operation), while it is rarely applied to examine nonorganizational topics. This finding is plausible regarding the casebased nature of QCA, which fits well into organization-level study de signs, especially for the purposes of in-depth case analysis. However, as mentioned above, IS research often lacks this in-depth insight when refraining from integrating complementary qualitative or quantitative research methods. Beyond this, the question arises as to why other research topics and areas are under-represented in QCA research. With its origins in political science [8], QCA could be a helpful method in several other contexts, such as studying the cultural, legal, ethical, and political implications of IS/IT for society or for analyzing issues in problem domain-specific concepts in which an in-depth analysis is required. Furthermore, across research disciplines, QCA is considered worthwhile for studying multi-level phenomena, where causal complexity can be found at different units of analysis [134]. In inter national business research, for example, QCA is considered suitable for seeking answers to social, economic, and ecological questions that have configurational drivers [134]. In education research, QCA can help to study educational issues subject to intersectionality and multi-level analyses [135]. Consistently, QCA can be applied to answer complex issues in IS research by accounting for the holistic effect of various drivers at different levels

Recommendation 2: RQs on the cultural, legal, ethical, and political implications of IS/IT constitute the most prevalent research gaps in the IS field that are worth examining with the help of the QCA method. In addition, some well-researched areas can profit from the explanatory capabilities of QCA.

Recommendation 3: Using QCA for in-depth analyses in multi-level research contexts enables IS scholars to delve into the logic by which lowerlevel factors (at the individual level) result in outcomes at higher-level fac tors (team, group, or organizational level) [136].

With respect to the underlying theoretical foundation, we observed a high level of popularity with theoretical lenses that support the exami nation of organizational concepts, including topics on IT impacts, adoption, and use. The findings reveal that theoretical multiplicity has been widely acknowledged by IS scholars. However, given the major role of theoretical framing in contributing to a deeper understanding of diverse phenomena of interest [60,62], IS scholars should not only keep on with this consequent theoretical framing strategy but also aim to integrate theories that enable them to study the prevalent research gaps as mentioned above.

Recommendation 4: Theoretical framing is essential for finding the appropriate “ingredients” to investigate the prevailing research gap. How ever, researchers should not include too many theories and derived condi tions, as the issue of limited empirical diversity increases with the number of conditions. Berg-Schlosser et al. [11] recommend between 4 and 7 condi tions, which however is not a static guideline, as the ratio to the sample size and the proportion of limited empirical diversity should always be considered [11,34]. When researchers theorize factorial relationships, they should incorporate the basic causal concepts of QCA captured by conjunctive Boolean notation and the distinction into necessary and sufficient conditions for an outcome [12].

## 6.1.2. Determining causal recipes

When analyzing the applied QCA techniques, we found that QCA studies in the IS field largely rely on fsQCA, with only few studies employing csQCA. Also in related research fields, the fsQCA technique is a popular approach and among the most commonly applied techniques [130,135]. Due to the ambiguity in social sciences, the transition be tween memberships often cannot be determined precisely to allow a dichotomization. In addition, the strong focus on quantitative data within the analyzed studies allows for a finer calibration of the degree of membership. Therefore, the use of fsQCA in such settings fits the pur pose. Our results also show a trend in IS research to incorporate binary sets within an fsQCA approach.

Recommendation 5: The choice of applied QCA technique should be fitted to the research context and the type and availability of data. If the conditions can be precisely dichotomized (e.g., gender), binary coded sets can be used. More ambiguous contexts should resort to fuzzy sets. A combination between binary and fuzzy sets is also possible [e.g., [40,57]].

When it comes to calibration, particularly in large-N settings, re searchers often use a mechanistic approach in determining calibration thresholds, for example, by simply using the applied Likert scales or percentiles of the data distribution to set the qualitative anchors of the calibration [e.g., [66,100]]. This approach can be legitimate but re quires justification and consideration of the pitfalls associated with the calibration process [14,137]. The calibration rationale of IS scholars is often not sufficiently transparent and often simply provides threshold or an overview of the calibrated data.

Recommendation 6: We encourage the IS community to place more emphasis on sound calibration and dichotomization by considering empirical, theoretical, or substantive knowledge for setting the values that fit the context [12,15,34]. The calibration rationales must be transparent.

Furthermore, we observed that, in some cases, calibration was intervened by adding or subtracting a constant of 0.001 to the measures, avoiding ambiguous cases of exactly 0.5. This is a recommended practice by Fiss [22], but its consequences should be validated critically, since this intervention could bias a case to a particular membership direction [137]. Moreover, the calibration should be checked for sensitivity and robustness by setting conservative and relaxed calibration thresholds [108].

Our analysis of necessary and sufficient conditions revealed some shortcomings that should be considered by the IS community when conducting QCA. First, we found that more than half of the considered studies did not test for necessary conditions. Given that the distinction between necessary and sufficient conditions is an important explanatory benefit of QCA setting it apart from other procedures such as statistical regression, this result is somewhat surprising. The distinction is impor tant, because sufficient conditions imply the occurrence of an outcome, whereas necessary conditions entail an outcome. “Therefore, a neces sary cause is a constraint, a barrier, an obstacle, a bottleneck that must be managed to allow a desired outcome to exist” [[138], p. 11]. IS theories and RQs generally entail necessity [e.g., [40,139]]. Thus, when disregarding this aspect, the potential for explaining causality in many IS phenomena and theories cannot be exploited.

Recommendation 7: If theoretical grounding suggests a necessary con dition, the analysis of necessary conditions should be undertaken. The analysis should further be conducted prior to the testing of the sufficient conditions, as the two condition variants must be considered theoretically and methodologically differently [12]. In this context, we also advise to consider the recommendations of Mattke et al. [14] regarding the handling of trivial necessary conditions.

Second, we discovered that truth table analysis in IS research is often conducted without transparency and disclosure of the rationale behind each step. This is reflected in the strategy for handling logical re mainders, which is rarely explicitly addressed in QCA studies in the IS field. Instead, logical remainders are generally addressed implicitly and utilized by conducting counterfactual analysis as the plausibility assessment of logical remainders. In addition to the strategy of handling logical remainders, IS scholars need to disclose the procedure for dealing with contradictory configurations to avoid configurations that explain the presence and absence of an outcome. Another issue is the setting of thresholds for the strength of evidence in truth table analysis, which is often conducted by adopting the thresholds of other studies without explaining or justifying the estimates. Thus, we observed many studies that applied the QCA method in a mechanistic manner without ac counting for the specific study conditions and context. The tendency to “blindly” apply QCA mechanistically and according to step-by-step in structions is also evident in other research disciplines. Berger [131] expresses concerns about QCA practices applied in this way, because it prevents scholars from developing an in-depth understanding of the QCA method.

Recommendation 8: When conducting truth table analysis, the strategy of handling logical remainders should not be neglected. Mattke et al. [14] emphasize the need to counteract the problem of limited diversity with a variance-rich sampling strategy (while ensuring a balanced distribution). In any case, it is recommended to report the proportion of empirically observable configurations to non-observable instances in order to establish the validity boundary of the final results [12].

Recommendation 9: In setting the thresholds for the strength of evidence in the truth table analysis, the rationale for the frequency cut-off should not be merely based on the sample, but adjusted to the “nature and quality of the evidence” [[112], p. 118]. Greckhamer et al. [34] recommend to define a threshold that allows to retain at least 80% of the total cases. As with the frequency score, it is not advisable to adopt a general estimate for the con sistency cut-off; rather, scholars should be guided by the context of the research and the data basis [108,112]. For example, exploratory studies rely on less strict values than hypothesis tests [108].

Recommendation 10: To further improve the transparency of truth table analysis, IS studies must improve the reporting of the employed analysis tool or Boolean minimization algorithm, as this is essential information for replicability [12].

Notwithstanding the major role of robustness and sensitivity tests for analyzing the impact of uncertainty on the study results, our findings also indicate that robustness or sensitivity tests have so far been largely neglected in QCA studies from the IS field. Performing robustness tests and concluding that there is no significant difference in the explanatory power of the configurations can greatly improve the validity of IS studies [34]. Besides, we found that the transparency of reporting on mea surement models could be improved.

Recommendation 11: We recommend IS scholars to include and transparently report measurement model tests (for latent constructs) and to control for robustness in regard to calibration, set relationship, and predictive validity to strengthen configurational results. More detailed guidelines for conducting robustness tests can be found, for example, in the recent publi cations by Pappas and Woodside [13] or Mattke et al. [14]. Comprehensive guidelines for good reporting practices regarding measurement model testing can be found in numerous seminal works (e.g., Hair et al. [140]).

## 6.1.3. Configurational multiplicity

We observed a general lack of leveraging the QCA properties asym metry, conjunction, and equifinality in the conduct of QCA in IS research. Given the asymmetry between causal pathways and the occurrence of an outcome, there is a consensus on the necessity of conducting separate analyses to investigate the causes of the occurrence and absence of an outcome [12,34]. A large proportion of IS studies largely ignores the causal pathways leading to the absence of an outcome.

Recommendation 12: IS scholars should take advantage of the asym metric property of QCA to examine the presence and absence of an outcome in separate analyses. This provides an enriched view of the causality of IS phenomena and strengthens the propositions for theory.

With respect to conjunction, most QCA studies do not provide the solution formula in Boolean notation, but predominantly rely on the graphical representation brought forth by [86] to introduce the solution formula(s). This is rather surprising, when considering that Boolean algebra is considered the traditional presentation mode in QCA [131]. In business and management research studies, most scholars clearly prefer Boolean algebra over other presentation modes [131].

Recommendation 13: Following the common view in the extant litera ture, we advocate to (complementary) state the solution formula in Boolean notation [12,15]. The Boolean notation, i.e., Boolean algebra, has the advantage that intersecting or contradictory aspects of the resulting config urations can be easily identified and highlighted, which promotes the “dialog between data and theory” [[2]p. 1499].

Obtaining one or more configurations does not implicitly demon strate causality; instead, the causality and validity of the findings need to be assessed by the researcher by two mechanisms: set-theoretic mea sures and theoretical reasoning [12,23]. When analyzing the reported equifinality, we found that most IS studies adhere to the best practices of reporting set-theoretic measures, but some fail to explicitly indicate to which solution the reported set-theoretic measures apply.

Recommendation 14: For the sake of transparency, the type of solution should be explicitly stated (parsimonious, intermediate, or complex) and, if applicable, also follow the best practice of Schneider and Wagemann [12] by reporting all three solutions (in the case of considering logical remainders). Besides, all described set-theoretic measures (unique coverage, raw coverage, raw consistency, solution coverage, and solution consistency) should always be part of the reporting scheme [34].

Nevertheless, IS researchers should consider that exceeding the recommended consistency and configuration thresholds is neither clear evidence of theoretical significance and thus soundness of the theorized model in deductive studies nor evidence of new causal relationships that can be easily raised from inductive approaches. The set-theoretic mea sures are rather empirical indicators that should be interpreted against the background of theoretical multiplicity.

Recommendation 15: IS scholars should examine the cases in-depth and explain the interrelationships of conditions and the competing or comple mentary nature of configurations on the basis of pertinent theories [2,12,34].

## 6.1.4. Bibliometric findings

Regarding the number of published QCA studies in IS research, we observed a strong tendency toward publications in conference pro ceedings. At the same time, our detailed analysis uncovered that QCA studies in IS have low levels of transparency and documentation and neglect many mandatory measures and information that are considered vital for assessing the validity of study results. One reason for low transparency could be owed to space restrictions [12,34]. For example, we found 18 studies published in the AMCIS Proceedings, which usually allows 10 pages including abstracts, references, figures, and tables. Within these 10 pages, it is extremely challenging to embed configura tion findings in a comprehensive dialog between theory and empirical cases.

Recommendation 16: We recommend to change the submission practice at IS conferences and to allow more space for supplementary documentation and reports, not only for QCA papers, but also for other space-intensive research approaches, as is the case with quantitative approaches such as meta-analyses or structural equation modeling.

Recommendation 17: IS scholars should select publication outlets for their QCA studies that allow submission of extensive documentation and thus enable transparency.

In examining the impact of the selected study sample based on citation frequency, we found that QCA studies receive far less attention in IS research than studies based on other methodological approaches. The reason for this may be that the QCA method is still emerging and under-used in the IS field [2,65], so many IS scholars who are not familiar with it are still skeptical toward QCA results. This assumption can be confirmed when analyzing the countries and affiliations of the study authors. The results of the bibliometric analysis in Section 5.1 indicate that the QCA study landscape in IS is dominated by only a few experts, while others apply QCA as novices. This would also explain the lack of methodological rigor and the low level of transparency and documentation in many QCA studies. However, the desire to establish best practices and standards toward more rigor and transparency does not only hold for IS scholars. Mello [141] consistently found only few examples for “good practices” of QCA studies in his study sample and suggested the provision of supplementary information in online appendices as a method of choice to comply with transparency requirements.

Recommendation 18: When applying QCA as a methodological approach, IS scholars should rigorously adhere to the common guidelines for good practice in QCA research suggested by Schneider and Wagemann [12] and Ragin and Rihoux [15]. To ensure transparency, the detailed steps and rationales should be at least documented and provided, for example, as supplementary data in online appendices.

Moreover, given that expertise in using QCA is obviously held by only a small number of authors, available resources for peer-review processes in IS journals and IS conferences are rather scarce. As a result, QCA studies may not be adequately reviewed and assessed during their peer-review process, which could explain the lack of methodo logical rigor and transparency.

Recommendation 19: To ensure the quality of peer-review processes, journal and conference editors should only select reviewers who have suffi cient expertise in QCA.

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

Similar to prior studies of this kind, our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. These limita tions may affect the claims of validity, objectivity, and generalizability that are central to any study in social research [142].

First, the findings presented above are limited to the selected liter ature sample that may not be representative of the IS field due to the strict focus on peer-reviewed articles from publication outlets with a VHB ranking [25]. Although we believe that this sample provides a holistic picture of the QCA landscape in IS research, future studies may include non-peer-reviewed publication outlets for comparison. Such a comparison would help IS scholars to identify the limitations imposed by editorial practices of individual publication outlets in order to further improve these practices.

In this context, another fruitful avenue for future research is to compare the QCA practices from the IS field with those from other disciplines in a more systematic and detailed manner. Although we in tegrated some interesting findings regarding the QCA practices from related disciplines such as business and management research, inter national business studies, or innovation research, a more granular crossdisciplinary comparison of QCA practices is beyond the scope of our study. Such cross-disciplinary comparisons may uncover insightful similarities and differences with respect to the methodological features that can help to improve the use of the QCA method in our discipline. Another limitation concerns potential selection biases that may arise from the literature assessment, selection, and coding process. However, we attempted to overcome this limitation by strictly adhering to the developed coding guidelines based on the best practices of seminal works and steered by the multiplicity framework.

Although Park et al.’s [2] multiplicity concept comprising theoretical multiplicity, causal recipes, and configurational multiplicity has proven to be useful as a guiding framework for the analysis and interpretation of findings, our study is not intended to completely address all methodo logical features of a QCA study in every detail. Instead, we have focused on highlighting the most salient patterns of QCA research in the IS field prior to identifying paths for further improvements.

As a final note, we would like to point out that despite our attempt to integrate a multi-perspective view of the use of QCA, including issues concerning the theoretical foundation, research areas, and research topic, some relevant aspects in QCA research have not been taken into account in our study. For example, the question of the extent to which the QCA method has helped IS scholars to answer urgent RQs, resolve inconsistencies in ambiguous research areas, or contribute to theory development and theory building was outside the scope of this study and thus remained unanswered. Future studies could address this issue at a more granular level.

## 7. Conclusion

In recent years, QCA has attracted considerable attention in the IS field as a method of choice to render complex IS phenomena compre hensible [1,4,5]. To delve deeper into this stream of research, we per formed a systematic literature review including a bibliometric analysis to provide a comprehensive and consistent picture covering 12 years of QCA practices in the IS literature. The most prevalent patterns in the analyzed QCA studies indicate plenty of limitations and weaknesses in the conduct of the method itself concerning the dimensions of theoret ical multiplicity and determining causal recipes and configurational multiplicity. For interested readers, our derived implications and rec ommendations can be considered as a starting point for identifying research gaps that are worth being studied. For IS scholars wishing to conduct a QCA study, the propositions can serve as a guideline throughout the entire research process to avoid major pitfalls and strengthen the relevance and rigor of the methodological approach. Beyond this, we aim to advance the use of QCA in the IS field to further enhance methodological diversity in IS research.
