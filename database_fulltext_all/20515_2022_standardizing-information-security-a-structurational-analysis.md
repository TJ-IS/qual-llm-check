## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Standard development information security legitimacy structuration theory ethnography

## A B S T R A C T

Given that there are an increasing number of information security breaches, organizations are being driven to adopt best practice for coping with attacks. Information security standards are designed to embody best practice and the legitimacy of these standards is a core issue for standardizing organizations. This study uncovers how structures at play in de jure standard development affect the input and throughput legitimacy of standards. We participated as members responsible for standards on information security and our analysis revealed two structures: consensus and warfare. A major implication of the combination of these structures is that legitimacy claims based on appeals to best practice are futile because it is difficult to know which the best practice is.

## 1. Introduction

Information and information systems are important assets in both private and public organizations. These assets are central for practices on operational, managerial, and executive levels of organizations. Innovative use of information and information systems creates new opportunities for organizations [1,63]. It is hardly surprising that the same assets also become highly sought-after targets of foreign govern ments, organized cyber-criminals, and hacktivists. Sadly, information security incidents are a perennial problem for organizations [12,13,40, 41].

The consequences of these incidents can be severe. Disclosed infor mation can cause great financial losses [13,21], have negative effects on trust [10], and an organization’s reputation [51]. In addition, in frastructures, such as fuel pipelines [56], food supply chains [37], and water supply systems [14] are at risk as they nowadays are integrated with advanced information technology [61]. It means that if antagonists gain access to such systems, they can create severe damage and disrupt production or services in organizations and society [33].

As a result, organizations are compelled to invest in information security [3,34] to safeguard their information assets. Organizations are fencing information security risks by implementing advanced informa tion technology and information security management systems [17]. In fencing risks, information security standards play an important role, where they advise on how these kinds of countermeasures are to be designed and implemented [58]. It means that organizations adopt best practices expressed in international information security standards, such as the ISO-27,000 series [23,36,47,48]. The ISO-27,000 series is an example of a de jure standard, i.e., published by legislative bodies, government agencies, or independent standards development organi zations, such as the International Organization for Standardization (ISO).

Standards are documents that provide “requirements, specifications, guidelines or characteristics that can be used consistently to ensure that materials, products, processes, and services are fit for their purpose” ([26], para 1). Their main purpose is to contribute to an organization’s efficiency and effectiveness [16]. Information security standards pro vide a generic set of controls, acting as a baseline for systems and practices in organizations [17]. Thus, standards govern what we do, what we have, or what we are [8], and as such, they have a powerful position in society. The trustworthiness and authority of standards and the interest to adopt them are based on positive views of standards organizational effects. It means that establishing the legitimacy of standards is a core issue for standardizing organizations, as legitimacy is seen as positive in the collective perception of all relevant stakeholders [54].

This line of reasoning, relating to standards and their role, is also found within the area of information security. Much research in this area revolves around user compliance with standards and policies (e.g., [6, 11,57]) where employees’ attitudes are said to positively affect infor mation security [46]. However, international information security standards are no silver-bullets, and they are not without criticism. For example, they have been criticized for being a one-size-fits-all-approach to information security [47], and for a too narrow focus on the existence of information security processes over the content of these process [45]. There have also been concerns that information security standards are charged by political interests [5]. Siponen and Willison [[47], p. 269] argued that the legitimacy of international information security stan dards is established through “appeal to common practice and authority.” That said, they mean that the argument that de jure information security standards build on best practice is invalid as “[n]either the evidence for the reasoning behind them nor the underlying research processes were given or made public and visible.” (ibid., p. 269). This includes the use of authority or experts for the validation and development of information security standards. Also, previous research on standardization illustrates a potential conflict between efficient standard development and the involvement of experts, where increased expert participation might reduce efficiency in standard development [8]. These concerns address the struggle to establish the legitimacy of standards, which is a major research concern on standard development by organizational bodies [54].

Standardizing organizations borrow their decision-making logic on democratically based claims where, for example, deliberative proced ures are used as a basis for the development of standards [29]. We, therefore, need to address standardizing organizations as the regulatory authority they set out to be by analyzing the important work for establishing legitimacy. Despite this importance, there are very few studies, as we will show in Section 2, on standard development and legitimacy within information security. Silva et al [44]. illustrate how different types of legitimacy are at play in the establishment of infor mation security standards, and Backhouse et al [5]. argue that the legitimacy, and thus the success, of an information security standard is dependent on the active participation of experts. Our purpose is to complement the previous studies by adding a legitimacy perspective on information security standard development.

Against this background, the present study aims to uncover how structures in standard development affect legitimacy of de jure infor mation security standards. Specifically, our study addresses the research question: how do different structures affect the input and throughput legitimacy of de jure information security standards? Legitimacy can be viewed as “generalized perception or assumption that the actions of an entity are desirable, proper or appropriate within some socially con structed systems of norms, values, beliefs, and definitions” ([52], p. 574). As legitimacy is a key factor for the authority of standards [54], standardizing organizations establish legitimacy through several different legitimacy strategies, such as transparency or using consensus and experts in the decision-making process [54]. These legitimacy strategies are often discussed in terms of input legitimacy, throughput (or procedural) legitimacy, and output legitimacy [29]. These strategies are materialized as re-occurring actions that, for some reason, are considered valid and work well for the establishment of legitimacy. They constitute patterns of basic assumptions and can be understood as structures that constrain and enable agency [20]. Therefore, we approach the legitimacy of information security standards as the result of enacted structures in standard-developing organizations. We use structuration theory [20] as the analytical lens when analyzing 34 months of data from participating in information security standard development. This allowed us to analyze how the uncovered structures affect input and throughput legitimacy.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. The next two sections introduce related research on legitimacy strategies in stan dardization in general and standard development research in informa tion security in particular Section 4. gives an introduction to structuration theory. In the fifth section, we present the contextual setting and our research method. The sixth section describes our anal ysis. The seventh section comprises a discussion of the research findings, our theoretical contributions, implications for practice and research, and ideas for future research.

## 2. Standard development and legitimacy strategies

## 2.1. Legitimacy strategies

One way of achieving legitimacy in the eyes of future adopters is to strive for consensus among the different experts who participate in the formation of a certain standard [9]. Input legitimacy, therefore, refers to the importance of stakeholder participation and the possibility to have equal opportunities to represent interests in the decision-making process [29,42]. Throughput legitimacy is concerned with the quality of the decision-making process and questions, such as transparency and openness [29,43]. This term refers to the process of decision-making with a focus on “responsiveness of the decision-making authority” and procedural fairness ([29], p. 16). Thus, throughput legitimacy requires mechanisms of transparency and processes that ensure “that actors have a realistic chance of being heard” ([29], p. 26). Furthermore, it has to do with effective participation “through sufficient language skill and the right vocabulary,” and the use of consensus for decision-making ([54], p. 141). Finally, output legitimacy is related to the results of the decision-making process, i.e., whether it is effective or not in solving the problems at hand as well as preventing abuse of public power [29,42]. This is the reason why we are not addressing output legitimacy in this study; implementation of standards in organization, such as addressed in the work of Smith et al [48]., does not provide us with insights on how structures in standard development affect the legitimacy of information security standards.

## 2.2. Standard making and tensions

There are at least three types of tensions, particular for the formation of standards concerning legitimacy. The first type concerns the struggle between an efficient standard development process and experts involvement, where increased expert participation might reduce the efficiency of the standard development process [9]. In the area of in formation security standard development, previous research on dis courses showed that the desired consensus-reaching practice made the decision-making less efficient and was, therefore, in practice, aban doned [4]. The second type of tension raised by Brunsson et al [9]. concerns the potential conflict related to the role of the experts participating in the development of standards simultaneously as they represent certain interest groups. Previous research on information se curity has argued that standard development is a political process where many different interests may collide [5].

A third type of tension concerns tensions between different legiti macy strategies. For instance, Botzem and Dobusch [7] investigated the dynamic found in standard development as a tension between input and output legitimacy, i.e., between stakeholder participation and the effectiveness of the resulting standard [7]. Based on a case of global standardization, they showed how increased standard diffusion replaces input legitimacy with output legitimacy, where a decreased focus on stakeholder participation will increase the focus on various consultation procedures during standard diffusion. They conclude that stakeholder participation is a key factor for input legitimacy and authority affecting initial adoption. In contrast, output legitimacy is a key for long-term use, “bringing about stable social order through standards” (p. 755). Botzem and Dobusch [7] argue that “future research is needed to map out in more detail how different forms of legitimacy are related to each other by the actors involved” (p. 755). Standard development can thus be investigated through the different legitimacy strategies at play to establish the legitimacy of standards.

## 3. Legitimacy and standard development research in information security research

Our research concerns how standards are developed as products of formal organizations. This means that both input and throughput legitimacy is of interest. In this section, we will cover research on in formation security standard development and its relation to legitimacy.

## 3.1. Information security standard making research and legitimacy strategies

Information security researchers have not shown a great deal of in terest in standard making neither in general nor from a legitimacy perspective despite its pervasive role in developing and using new technology Table 1. summarizes information security research on stan dardization, focusing on issues of importance for the legitimacy of standards and standardization involving de jure standards. There are, however, very few articles with an explicit focus on legitimacy [5,44]. Therefore, we have also included research studying standard develop ment focusing on trust to establish standards [28] as we see trust as closely related to legitimacy.

Backhouse et al [5]. investigated the role of power and politics in setting information security standards. They found that the process was politically charged, with different stakeholders’ interests influencing the process. They concluded that the development of standards is not always the effect of economic and strategic decisions; it can also be derived from exogenous contingencies and power structures. Their study highlights the importance of industry representatives for legitimating standards, where standard development requires the active participation of in dustry to ensure legitimacy and credibility (i.e., input legitimacy). Their research results, which were based on an analysis of the development of the British security management standard BS7799, showed how power and social relationships influenced the institutionalization of a standard. The British standard became in 1995 established as the international standard ISO/IEC 17,799.

The work presented by Silva et al [44]. builds on the same case and theoretical perspective as in Backhouse et al [5].. In the 2016 study, they continue their quest for understanding the role of power for standardi zation, but here with a focus on the acceptance or rejection of standards. Drawing mainly on the fourteen interviews in their case study, they analyze why the information security scheme “c:cure” failed establish ment. They conclude that the reason for the lack of adoption and ulti mately withdrawal of c:cure can be explained by three “circuits of power”: resistance from actors, presence of contradicting discourse, and lack of disciplinary techniques [44]. In the 2006 study and the 2016 study, legitimacy is an important theme in their research on standardi zation. Legitimacy is seen as a result of power. Silva et al [44]. illustrate how the strong market discourse dominates over the less prevailing technology discourse and how these two discourses interact and strug gle. They also conclude that potential adopters seem to lean on market legitimacy as the determining factor when deciding whether to adopt a specific standard.

Kallberg [28] studies the need for creating and maintaining trust to establish the international ISO standard Common Criteria for Informa tion Technology Security Evaluation (CC) as a global standard. A nation must be part of the Common Criteria Recognition Agreement (CCRA), guaranteeing that IT security equipment’s technical requirements are met to participate in CC. This requirement builds the base for mutual recognition and trust. However, if this cooperation fails, CC will not be established globally. Kallberg [28] concludes that for CC to be successful as a global standard, it must be aligned with politics, establishing trust. Nevertheless, as Kallberg [28] continues, regional interests will always override global concerns, the standard will fail to become established globally.

We conclude, based on our review of research on de jure information security standard development, that there is a strong focus on input legitimacy emphasizing stakeholder participation and involvement of experts [5,44,28]. Although Backhouse et al [5]., as well as Silva et al [44]., uncover the power struggle and process of standard making, they do not focus on throughput legitimacy as the quality of the decision-making process. Thus, our study complements these previous studies as we are addressing both input legitimacy and throughput legitimacy, and the tensions between them. Silva et al [44]. also stress the prevailing power of the market discourse to establish de jure infor mation security standards. Using structuration theory [20], our study thus adds to the understanding of how structures are developed through and form standard development

## 4. Structuration theory and the process of legitimating standard development

Structuration theory [20] is used as the theoretical lens to study the process of establishing legitimacy for de jure information security standard development. Structuration theory has frequently been used in the information systems field [27]. Due to structuration theory’s high level of abstraction, the information systems field has made some theoretical development of the theory in order to better fit technology and to facilitate empirical application of the theory. Notable examples are the “Duality of technology model” and the “Practice Lens” by Orli kowski [38,39], the “Adaptive Structuration Theory” by DeSanctis and Poole [15], and the operationalizations made by Halperin and Back house [22]. Regarding information security, structuration theory has, for example, been used for advancing our understanding of information security awareness and behavior in organizations [55,24] and for understanding privacy attitudes in relation to location sharing [64]. For this study, we have used the original Giddens’ theory (1984) and its operationalizations made by Halperin and Backhouse [22].

Structuration theory builds on the assumption that social life is more than the aggregation of random human interactions, but not only determined by social forces. Thus, it addresses that human actions and social structure are interdependent and intimately related and cannot be understood in isolation. It means that structures are not only constraints that shape what standard developers do, but rather it is their actions, or practices, that constitute these structures. Thus, structures are standard developers’ shared memory traces that influence their actions and are also (re)produced. Structures can differ in strength, and strong struc tures are characterized by things taken for granted, i.e., structures are reinforced by “routinization.” Giddens described these structures as having a “habitual, taken-for-granted character of the vast bulk of the activities of day-to-day social life” (1984, p. 376).

Some of the fundamental concepts of structuration theory are illus trated in Fig. 1, where social structure and human interaction are captured as three interlinked dimensions. Starting in the left-most part of the figure, signification focuses on standard developers’ sense-making of their actions. The signification aspect of structuration creates mean ing in standard developers’ communication concerning information security standard development. Standard developers draw on interpre tative schemes to make sense of their own and other standard de velopers’ actions. These beliefs, or ways to perceive the world, refer to the specific standard development at the time and all “knowledge” influencing the actions, e.g., thoughts on how decisions, in general, should be made, beliefs about how the information security standardi zation world is working. In doing so, standard developers produce and reproduce signification of specific ways to develop information security standards. We wanted to examine the significance of different inter pretative schemes’ impact on achieving the claimed legitimacy strate gies for information security standard development. An interpretative scheme that stresses and achieves participation during standard devel opment contributes positively to the input legitimacy strategy. In cases where standard development is governed by an interpretative scheme that does not stress transparency and openness, it contributes negatively to the throughput legitimacy strategy.

In Fig. 1, legitimation refers to normative regulations for carrying out information security standard development. It means that standard de velopers reflexively monitor the flow of standards development actions and where normative elements are “contingent claims” ([20], pp. 30, 31). In seeking legitimacy for development actions, standard developers constantly refer to their shared way of working to ensure that their ideas fit the norms. Norms can be broken down into formal norms (i.e., official rules and guidelines documented in written form) and informal norms (i. e., conventions reflected in common behavior – “shoulds”). In addition, norms can differ concerning their sanction level – i.e., how strongly or weakly a norm is understood and enforced [22]. This fact means that strongly enforcing norms that are not in line with participation will negatively impact the input legitimacy strategy. On the other hand, weakly enforced norms aligned with transparency and openness would have the same impact on the throughput legitimacy strategy.

Domination in Fig. 1 happens when standard developers use their skills, expertise, and influence in a given development situation, i.e., draw on facilities to exercise power, while at the same time complying with the norms and achieving recognition and approval of other stan dard developers for their development work. Power is, according to Giddens [[20], p. 257], “the capacity to achieve outcomes,” and it is exerted through resources that come in two kinds: allocative and authoritative. Allocative resources refer to the domination of material things (or the “natural world”), whereas authoritative resources refer to the coordination of human activity ([20], p. 31). In our case, allocative resources drawn upon could be money or time, whereas authoritative resources may include language skills or the ability to charm and convince. Differences in facilities between actors in information security standard development can create asymmetries in a structure regarding power and domination, impacting the legitimacy strategies for infor mation security standard development. Lacking an allocative resource, such as money, affects the possibilities to participate in development work, affecting the input legitimacy strategy. Having an authoritative resource, such as the ability to charm and convince, can, on the other hand, be a way to dominate the decision process while being non-compliant with existing norms and affect the throughput legitimacy strategy.

Although much of the discussion above focuses on one single struc ture, Whittington [60] shows that multiple structures can exist and intersect in organizations. In acknowledging the possibility that multiple structures can exist and intersect concerning information security standard development, we include Giddens’ often over-looked concern for the intersection and tension between different structures, where these structures can reinforce or undermine each other. It means that the inner workings of a structure impact the legitimacy strategies for in formation security standard development. However, it is also important to recognize the interrelationship between the structure and its relative strengths.

## 5. Material and methods

This research is qualitative and interpretive [59], building on the ontological assumption that reality is socially constructed and produced by humans. The objects of study are the mental models, or structures, that underlie the work with creating de jure information security stan dards. We must identify the instances where these standards are nego tiated and understand why actors make the claims about the negotiations that they do. For this assessment, we needed to get close to actors that are part of this process, and we therefore adopted the interpretive ethnography research method [2,19,30,35]. The ethno graphic method calls for the researcher to be closely engaged with the daily life of another community [32]. In our case, it meant participating and engaging in the process of developing standards as a member of the Swedish technical committee responsible for standards on information security.

## 5.1. Contextual setting

The research conducted in this study takes place within the Swedish Institute for Standards (SIS). SIS is the representative body for Sweden within ISO, a non-government association of national standards orga nizations [53]. Standard development within the ISO is carried out by technical committees, each with its area of responsibility. The ISO de velops standards for a vast variety of areas – e.g., ISO 31,000 (risk management) and ISO 22,000 (food safety management). Its mission is to “ensure that products and services are safe, reliable and of good quality” [26].

The national standard organization, in our case, the SIS, works at both the national and the international levels. SIS was founded in 1922 and has been working internationally since the 1960s [49]. By 2020, SIS had 1067 companies, agencies, and organizations as members [50]. Although SIS works globally with international standards, the organi zation has an interest in promoting the national solutions. The SIS mission is to “take initiatives and collaborate on best practices that promote Sweden’s competitiveness and encourage the smart, sustain able development of society” […] and strive “to increase Sweden’s in fluence on international collaboration and to ensure that best practices are shared and leveraged throughout Sweden.” [50].

Most proposals for a new standard, or revisions of a standard, come from the international level. After that, comments and suggestions are made from each country and are communicated via a designated website for all countries to take part of. Later, the standards are voted on at international meetings. At the national level, the committee that we were a part of, is made up of different actors in society that are considered experts in the area of information security well enough to contribute to sound standards. During our time of investigation, the committee had 65 members representing 49 public and private organi zations. The private companies listed themselves as dealing with secu rity work, telecommunications, and software development. The public organizations ranged from the army and police to the Swedish transport agency and the Swedish public television body. Whereas, the full member list in this way includes a large variety of diverse stakeholders, we found that the few active members only represented small (often single person) private consultant firms. It was these same male private consultants that sent most comments, attended national meetings, and traveled to the international ISO meetings. Since this background bears importance, we, when presenting our results, have specified each member's role when statements are made instead of just calling them “members.”

During office hours at the SIS headquarters, there are national meetings where members can participate in-person or via video-link. During these meetings, national decisions on specific changes to stan dards are made, and the committee’s budget is discussed. The more frequently visited annual national meeting is hosted over two workdays and takes place at a conference establishment and is more informal. Decisions on standards are made during these days, but most focus is on strategies for upcoming international meetings and issues, such as how the committee should be made more powerful. These discussions take place not only during meetings, but also during less formal situations, such as breaks and dinners.

## 5.2. Data collection

The first author conducted the ethnographic data collection, who, as a member of the SIS technical committee, was engaged in the develop ment of standards over 34 months from February 2013 to November 2015. As researchers, we obtained the support of the project manager of SIS to carry out this study and the members of the committee were informed about the purpose of the research project and agreed to participate. The first author participated by attending national meetings and seminars as well as traveling with the committee to international ISO meetings. During the period, all actions and observations were documented in a diary. The ethnographic material was collected by participant observations, unstructured interviews, informal conversa tions during, e.g., meetings, meeting breaks, dinners, and bar visits. For instance, when traveling to Malaysia for an ISO meeting, she took notes on conversations taking place during check-in at the airport, drew sketches on the large conference rooms at the conference venue, and used her mobile phone notes to write down conversations and obser vations during informal dinners and bar-visits. When she found it not to be too intrusive, she would ask if she could use her mobile phone to record a meeting. The material was also collected via e-mail and on designated websites where official and unofficial documents were available. She collected, for instance, e-mail correspondence, minutes from meetings, travel reports, and strategies for upcoming meetings. Whereas, this variety of data collection made the data rich, it also made the analysis quite cumbersome. Texts were copied and pasted into a word document based on the chronological order, often with our com ments on how they were related – e.g., “this message was sent out directly after the updated webpage (see p. 68).” Overall, several hun dred pages of diary entries, transcribed recordings, e-mails, and docu ments were available for the analysis.

## 5.3. Analysis

Our structurational analysis aimed at tracing structuring processes by letting the modalities in Fig. 1 guide the analysis. We used the emails, interview transcripts, and diary entries to identify which facil ities, interpretative schemes, and norms actors drew upon when making information security standards. As an example of how the in terpretations were made, we use part of a transcript from a standard development training session:

“To be successful in an international context, you need […] contacts – have coffee with them, eat with them, attend the dinner-party … much is decided in the bar.”

A norm in this statement is that one should participate during din ners and social activities. The interpretive schemes which this norm draws on are that liaisons and informal decisions about standards are made outside the formal meetings. The facilities for accomplishing this are interpreted as having an ability to be social (authoritative resource) and having time and money to be social (allocative resource). In relation to the norm, which is informal since it is not written down as a rule or appear in guiding documents, we also wanted to see what level of sanction the norm has. The norm is informal, and there is no punishment if you do not participate during dinners, which could imply that the sanction level is weak. However, as we continued analyzing all material, we found that this kind of norm was very much taken-for-granted. This way of working is just “a must.” We, therefore, ended up labeling the norm as strongly sanctioned. In this way, we moved from smaller units of analysis to the whole data-set and made changes to the analysis as the full picture was made visible to us. This iterative analytical work and constant comparison of the smaller units of analysis made us aware of the two structures presented in the “Result” section; structures that differ in their interpretative schemes, norms, and facilities.

We then moved on to analyze the identified structures regarding which legitimacy claims can be made. More specifically, we looked at input and throughput legitimacies, where input legitimacy is achieved through broad stakeholder participation. Throughput legitimacy is achieved through, e.g., transparency and openness in the decisionmaking process. Using the structurational analysis of the example above, we found that throughput legitimacy was weak since the pre scribed behavior (norms and interpretative schemes) was to take de cisions in the bar that undermines transparency or any documentation of the process made. The structurational analysis also revealed that the facilities drawn upon in making these kinds of decisions were the ability to be social and have time and money to be so, which hinders broad participation and undermines input legitimacy.

Our guiding questions for analyzing input legitimacy were: How many persons participated in the work to develop information security standards? How many different stakeholders participated (i.e., people representing different interest groups)? For instance, when SIS writes that they have to make sure “that as many relevant stakeholders as possible are invited to participate,” it shows how important input legitimacy is for the organization. In structuration terms, as described in Fig. 1, we found the interpretative scheme for the legitimacy strategy to be that having many participants is important for the quality of stan dards, the norm for this is that many participants should therefore be invited and, as the results will show, the facility for this is the capacity of the organization to invite many participants.

For the throughput legitimacy analysis, we asked ourselves: How open and transparent is the decision-making process? Do we know who participated in the decision-making? Do we know on which grounds a decision was taken? Is the decision communicated to us? Do all actors have a realistic chance of being heard (e.g., possibility to participate, language skill, and the right vocabulary)? How are agreements made? On what grounds are decisions made (e.g., are all opinions addressed and listened to)? For instance, a statement, such as “If we don’t reach a consensus it just happens that we don’t reach consensus” indicates that throughput legitimacy is not highly prioritized. In relating to Fig. 1 again, we analyzed the interpretative scheme for the legitimacy strategy to be that a consensus-reaching practice can be overlooked in the making of standards.

We ended our analysis by comparing how the two structures – consensus and warfare – affected each other with their different legiti mation processes. We did so by returning to our structuration theory analysis to see how the facilities, norms, and interpretative schemes stipulated a certain behavior and how it could strengthen or weaken a behavior prescribed in another structure. Since we did this on the mo dality level, the granularity of analysis became very low (e.g., high level of detail) while we at the same time included the strategies for legiti mation (see Fig. 1). For example, when we compared the behavior of non-participation in the warfare structure, we could see that the weakly sanctioned norm partly explained this behavior in the other consensu structure that stipulated participation. Even though the norm in the consensus structure was that one should participate, nothing happens if you do not. In this way, the norm’s weak sanction in the consensus structure enabled a contradictory interpretative scheme in the warfare structure, saying that broad participation is unnecessary and that a selected few should take decisions.

## 6. Results

We can now uncover how the structuration of information security standard development enacts input and throughput legitimacy. We use the input from our diary entries and transcribed recordings from the ISO and committee meetings, seminars, training sessions, and document analysis of rulebooks, e-mails, and other documents as committee members. Our analysis uncovered two structures: the consensus struc ture and the warfare structure. The former structure emphasizes the participation of many different stakeholders and sees standard devel opment as a process to reach consensus. The latter structure – warfare – uses military metaphors where standard development is described as going to war. This structure is not aligned with the consensus structure communicated by the SIS. We start by analyzing the consensus structure and, after that, the warfare structure concerning the criteria for legiti macy. Finally, we analyze the combined effect of the two structures

## 6.1. The consensus structure

The consensus structure builds on the assumption that de jure in formation security standards result from negotiations between different stakeholders that finally reach a consensus. This structure revolves around interpretative schemes of significance for standard developers sense-making of information security standard development: inclusive ness, transparency, consensus, participation, and commenting. Throughput legitimacy is thus high, but, as the analysis will show, input legitimacy is weak.

## 6.1.1. Input legitimacy

Input legitimacy concerns how many persons participated in the development and, in this case, how the participants represented different interest groups. The interpretative scheme of inclusiveness is present in most of the official communication carried out by SIS and ISO and actors coordinating standard development activities. There are clear instructions on how standard development should be based on inclu siveness, voluntarism, and stakeholder-governance in the guiding doc uments from ISO and SIS. For example, SIS writes in the rules for working in technical committees:

“When establishing an SIS/TC [technical committee of SIS], SIS has to make sure that as many relevant stakeholders as possible are invited to participate and that these stakeholders represent diverse organizations of the society. Private companies, interbranch orga nizations, authorities, consumer- and environment organizations, union organizations, and public administration, when relevant, should all be represented. Producers and consumers must be repre sented as well as that law-setting authorities also take an active part in the work.” (Rules for working in a technical committee in SIS/TC, 2011)

These instructions clearly show that the SIS aims for high input legitimacy. The communication is based on the interpretative scheme that de jure information security standards based on input from many stakeholders yield better standards. Hence, it shows that inclusiveness is important. The norm is that information security standard development should include as many different interests and organizations as possible. However, the norm is rather weak, as membership is voluntary. Ac cording to the SIS head office, few members took an active part in the standard development; out of 65 members, they normally received re sponses from the same two to three members – thus not including “as many relevant stakeholders as possible.”

In this structure, we find an interpretive scheme emphasizing that it is important to participate in the development and comment on the de jure information security standards. When discussed, however, it is always in the light of a lack of participation and comments. For example:

“I want to encourage the whole committee to contribute, these are highly relevant standards, and they become more relevant if more people contribute. If this workgroup is to remain, then more people must participate. Now it is only [name of person] that is comment ing. There is no point in having meetings if no one participates.” (Private security consultant, 2015)

The norm is that more people should participate because the inter pretative scheme is that a high level of member participation is beneficial to the development of standards. It makes the standards more relevant. Although this is a significant interpretative scheme, as pointed out above, the norm is weakly sanctioned since nothing happens if you, as a member, do not participate. While being a committee member for three years, not one single reprimand was sent out for not voting or com menting on the standards. Hence, the low degree of participation and commenting harms input validity. The powers that the standarddeveloping organization could draw upon here are the facilities to be able to stimulate members to participate (authoritative resource) and time (and money) for members to participate (allocative resource). During our analysis, it was very clear that time is a limiting factor:

“I am also interested, but I’m in meetings all day tomorrow. Can we make a new appointment?” [Information security manager at the municipality]

[…]

“I am also interested, but I am away at the ISO-meeting in Korea this week. Can we please try to find another time? [Information security researcher].” (E-mail conversation between members 2013)

“To put it simply – there are far too few persons that have the time to engage in the development of international standards – leading to the standards ending up already finalized on the table for voting.” (In formation security manager at a large manufacturing company, 2014)

## 6.1.2. Throughput legitimacy

Throughput legitimacy concerns how open and transparent the decision-making process is. The interpretative schemes of transparency and consensus are present in most of the official documents carried out by SIS and ISO and actors coordinating standard development activities. When a new member joined a standard-developing committee, he or she received instructions about the work accompanied by the following instructions:

“Standard making within ISO, CEN and therefore also SIS is based on four important principles: openness, voluntarism, stakeholdergovernance and consensus practice.” (Rules for working in a tech nical committee in SIS/TC, 2011)

When ISO goes into detail and expands on the guidance to standard developers, they also stress standard development transparency and the importance of reaching consensus.

“The chair should ensure that all points of view have received adequate attention, that consensus is reached, and that all resolu tions are worded in a clear and precise manner and are made available – by the secretary – for confirmation.” [25]

This guidance addresses throughput legitimacy. This communication draws on the interpretative scheme that transparent and consensus-based standard development with many stakeholders yields better standards. Stressing that the chair should ensure that all viewpoints receive attention that decisions are consensus-based, and make resolutions available correspond to a high degree of responsiveness and procedural fairness. Thus, the norms are that decision-making in the standard development should be based on transparency and consensus. The power to achieve this is manifested through facilities based on the ability of the standard-developing organization to manage transparency (authoritative resource), and having the time for consensus-reaching (allocative resource).

The interpretative scheme of inclusiveness, transparency, and consensus, and its significance, was also shown each time when mem bers were invited to comment on a proposal where the following stan dardized formulations are made:

“By providing your opinions on the attached referral, you contribute to making the upcoming standard more accepted and thereby more useful. Your opinions will be balanced with the opinions of other referral-recipients that will mutually constitute the Swedish answer to ISO.” (SIS e-mail, September 19, 2013)

We find the interpretative scheme to be that the standard is more accepted and useful if members have commented on them. Therefore, the norm is that all members should provide opinions on standard pro posals. Claiming “opinions will be balanced” is an expression of proce dural fairness. Hence, it contributes to increased throughput legitimacy, although it does not clearly state how this will be done. During a members’ meeting in 2014, many different members criticized the SIS for not “practicing what they preach”:

“The premise is that this should be a cooperation that is open, that we should be transparent in our work, that we should involve as many as possible […] there is a democratic failing here – it [the standard development] is too internal and needs to be opened up. You [referring to SIS] need to show that the standards are objective, neutral, and well-grounded.” (Information security manager in a small municipality, November 13, 2014)

Another aspect of throughput legitimacy is that all actors should have a realistic chance of being heard. Members need the ability to give opinions on the topic at hand, i.e., to use their existing knowledge base on information security to evaluate the proposal (or parts thereof). Members also need the ability to communicate these opinions in an understandable way. At several times during international meetings, we witnessed that a lack of English skills prevented participation. For example, during an ISO meeting, the chair of the secretariat and par ticipants found it difficult to understand the Chinese representatives since their English was so poor. The chair (2015) asks the Chinese to

“Kindly write down your opinions in Chinese and then have someone translate them to English. Because your opinions are important.”

The interpretative scheme is that many different opinions make stan dards better, and the norm is that we should listen to the opinions of all countries. Thus, the power to draw upon is the standard-developing organization’s facility to connect a wide variety of stakeholders and be skilled in the English language. Thus, the standard-developing or ganization needs the ability to manage members’ opinions and stimulate members to provide opinions. All these facilities are authoritative re sources. In addition, members need to have the allocative resources of time (and money) to give opinions and comments. However, our field work showed that the norm is very weakly sanctioned – nothing happens if a member does not provide comments; not even a reminder is sent out.

A final aspect regarding throughput legitimacy is if the decision is communicated to us. While being a member of the committee in charge of information security for three years, we found that the final stage of the standard development process, when a standard is ready for publi cation, was always communicated via e-mail: “Dear member, Attached, please find the approved [name of the standard].” As a committee member, you did not know who made the decision or on what ground the decision was taken. Decisions taken up to that point were seldom communicated.

## 6.2. The warfare structure

The warfare structure builds on the assumption that de jure infor mation security standards result from different forces, be it the industry or other countries, that compete and where the winner gets a proposal for a standard through or most comments and changes into an existing standard. Input legitimacy is weak because participation is low and seen as unproblematic. Throughput legitimacy is also weak since the struc ture revolves around how the standard development is based on a not very open political process and where efficiency is highly stressed.

## 6.2.1. Input legitimacy

Input legitimacy in the warfare structure is weak and that few members participate in the standard development is not seen as prob lematic. It is justified with the interpretative schemes that we need to move fast and that the few participating members’ special skills are needed. One such skill needed is “political schooling” in order to be able to build alliances with other organizational actors:

“It is also practical political schooling that is needed – that is one thing that we must acknowledge. Standard development is not only about creating documents; it is about real politics in the real world.” (Private security consultant, 2014)

The interpretative scheme is that standard development is about pol itics and the informal norm is that one should be schooled in practical politics if one is to develop standards. The level of sanction for this norm is weak – people who work in committees do so voluntarily, and there are no requirements for certain experiences or educations. A member’s power to draw on here is the authoritative resource to be skilled in politics.

The warfare structure’s focus is not on broad participation or stakeholder-governance; rather, the focus is on getting members into strategic positions. Members talk with envy about how, e.g., Microsoft or IBM has full-time positions just monitoring what different standard developing organizations are doing. Another strategic position is to hold a secretariat for a standard. As a holder of a secretariat, you act as a chairperson and call members for meetings related to those particular standards. This position is also seen as very important because “it gives influence and power over the agenda.” During an international meeting, where we followed the development of a certain de jure information security standard, we found one such holder of a secretariat to call us for a meeting in the hotel lobby with only 30-minute notice. Another characteristic of the few that participate is that they need to be socially skilled because decisions and agreements are made “in the bar”:

“To be successful in an international context you need […] contacts – have coffee with them, eat with them, attend the dinner-party … much is decided in the bar.” (Private security consultant, 2015)

The interpretative scheme is that liaisons and decisions about de jure information security standards are made outside the formal meetings. Therefore, there is an informal norm that members should participate during dinners and social activities. The power aspects here, the facil ities to draw upon, are (1) having the ability to be social (authoritative resource) – e.g., not being shy or having a social phobia and (2) having time (and money) to be social (allocative resource) – e.g., not being a single parent that needs to pick up children after work or not having the money to pay for a babysitter.

## 6.2.2. Throughput legitimacy

In the warfare structure, the consensus is not used for decisionmaking. Rather, getting standards through is seen as a politically charged process with lots of pressure from the industry and other na tions. As two members from two smaller manufacturing companies discussed during a meeting in 2014:

“We are subject to distasteful pressure from some industries and sometimes multinational companies when it comes to pushing re quirements that are favorable for their products. They can find out something – a requirement or a cogent law that can favor their products – and then they show up with 100 delegates for a meeting. They mobilize people and open up subsidiaries in different countries – people are bursting everywhere and working at the same place. And they can afford to fund commissioned research that supports their claims and ignores reports that do not benefit their interests. There is a democracy deficit, and where the only counterweight is commenting, auditing, and working very hard in the committees. But it is very hard to stand up against those people.”

## “Yes, no one would argue against IBM.”

The interpretative scheme is that standard development is subject to pressure from the industry and multinational companies. This strong belief is taken for granted – “no one argues with IBM.” The power that the members have to counteract this pressure is to work hard (authori tative resource), and for this work, they need time and money (allocative resources). The norm, albeit informal, is that the committees should work hard with their commenting and auditing work. However, the level of sanction of this norm is weak since nothing happens if members do not comment – not even a reminder is sent out.

During the same discussion, a standardization consultant drew a picture of how other countries are demonstrating their strength:

“The surrounding world is demonstrating its strength. If we take Germany as an example, they have had a clear strategy to develop standards that are beneficial for the German industry for several years. That is the guiding principle in Germany; they have a very streamlined and effective organization, I have to admit. It is an impressive country when it comes to pushing standards.” (Private security consultant, 2014)

Another way of counteracting this pressure, apart from working hard in the committees, is to play the same game by building alliances with other countries:

“We should go back to using the Nordic countries as a base for standard development in order to become stronger.” (Private secu rity consultant, 2014)

“Nordic meetings do occur as a preparation for ISO-meetings as a way to get a shared opinion. They call it the Nordic mafia.” (Private security consultant, 2015)

The norm is that the Nordic countries should unite because the belief, or interpretative scheme, is that together with other Nordic countries, Sweden will be stronger internationally. The sanction level for this norm is weak – partly because few efforts are made to cooperate systemati cally and because the norm is ambiguous since the Nordic countries are also competing with each other. An underlying belief is that being strong internationally is a good thing and that the surrounding world is demonstrating its strength. The facilities for power that the actors can draw on here are the authoritative recourses of working together with other countries and having skills in coordinating trans-national work. That standard development is not built on consensus, but rather on strategic warfare moves, was very evident during the international ISO meeting where countries created alliances before meetings in order to get their will through:

“This Japanese guy presented his proposal for a cloud standard at plenum last Tuesday, but he was completely killed. Australia and England had looked at the proposal beforehand and said that it was not needed. I mainly feel sorry for this Japanese professor who speaks really poor English and who has lots of Japanese people in the room that nod and agree. For no good at all” (Private security consultant, 2015)

The facilities made visible here are: having the ability to work together with other countries; coordinate trans-national work; and be skilled in the English language (all authoritative resources). The norm is that countries should make liaisons, and this is due to the belief, or interpretative scheme, that building liaisons improves your ability to affect de jure information security standards.

It is obvious from the descriptions above that the decision-making process is neither open nor transparent. Adding to that the fact that many decisions are taken in the bar (as discussed concerning input legitimacy), we do not know who participated in the decision-making or on which grounds a decision was taken. Overall, this makes throughput legitimacy weak, if not absent.

An additional explanation for this lack of transparency is the norm in this structure that standards should be developed quickly and efficiently and where time is seen as a scarce resource. Standards have to be timely because resources are scarce and “to ensure that they respond to market needs” [25]. During our inquiry, the development cycle of a standard was set to a maximum of three years. However, there were discussions about new directives for an even shorter development cycle:

“We need to keep pace – we need to be quicker because there is a need to deliver new standards or revise existing standards much quicker. The commission has demanded that we cut the development time on a standard in half – from 3 years to 1 ½ years.” (SIS consultant, 2014)

People consistently talked about how the development needs to be speeded up, and we observed many secretarial chairs at international meetings trying to rush through the decisions, e.g., (2015):

Chair: “We lack time; we need to move on. If we have time, we can discuss this after lunch. [Name of British member], I just want you to explain in detail what you have done quickly.”

Country B: “[explains what he has modified] and ‘track changes doesn’t work …”

Other nations: “Are we supposed to read this now?”

Country B: “No, no – let me just put this in context … [hastily explains]”

Country S: “What concerns me is that this is not what we decided yesterday … now there is a lot of text here …”

Chair interrupts: “Let’s move on. We do not have time.”

Country F: “Yes, let’s move on – that discussion is not relevant now.”

We find the interpretative scheme that if standards are on time, they respond to market needs, and based on that assumption, the formal norm is that standards should be on time. The norm is strongly sanctioned – being on time was continuously stressed during our years of participa tion and had taken on a taken-for-granted character. The power to accomplish this is having an ability for effective management of projects (authoritative resource). Often, we found time to be the main determi nant if a standard should be changed completely or revised, e.g., (2015):

Country S: “Change it completely? That will take four years! It is better with corrections, minor corrections …”

Country B: “I talked to colleagues in the UK, and they estimate that this will take up to 5–7 years – if revised in full.”

Chair: “We are lacking time; we need to move on. If we have time, we can discuss this after lunch."

Country G: “This is the 3rd Working Draft. We do not want a fourth or fifth!”

Chair: “We have to be quick with time. Don’t spend time on perfect wordings – the point is that we should agree with the general idea.”

The underlying belief is that a good standard is a finalized standard; time is scarce, and; standards that are completely revised take a long time. Because of these interpretative schemes, the norms are that standard should be quickly developed and that full revisions should be avoided. Again, the level of sanction is very strong. This norm is based on the facility to have the skills to write standards in an efficient way (authoritative resource).

Just as was the case in the consensus structure, the final stage of the standard development was always communicated via e-mail. The members did not know who made the decision or on what grounds the decision was taken.

## 6.3. Interaction between the two structures

Based on our structurational analysis of the process of legitimation, we found two contradicting but interacting structures. Our analysis revealed that the consensus structure enabled, or even created, the warfare-structure by having weakly sanctioned norms. For example, the idea, or interpretative scheme, in the warfare structure that standards should be developed quickly and efficiently undermined the consensus structure that stipulates that the development should be based on stakeholder-governance and a consensus-reaching practice since the norms stipulating this practice were weakly sanctioned, and lack of consensus was taken very lightly. This fact means that while the norms connected to the idea of consensus are formal, by being stipulated in the guiding documents (“rulebook” as the Swedish version is called). In our material, we find that the sanction level for these norms is weak – our analysis showed that these statements are nothing more than lip service. Thus, we could see that the attempt to establish throughput legitimacy through a consensus process and input legitimacy through many participating stakeholders was not achieved or even considered impor tant in practice.

## 6.3.1. The interactive structures effect on input legitimacy

The major tension concerning enhancing input legitimacy to de jure information security standards is related to the norm that many persons should participate in the development but do not. The persons who do participate should represent different stakeholder groups but do not. The interpretative scheme of inclusiveness is present in most of SIS and ISO’s official communication. However, tension is found in how the norm stipulating inclusiveness is weakly sanctioned. It is accepted that few participate, and the development goes on as usual.

Our analysis also revealed that the few who do participate have similar backgrounds and professions and cannot represent different stakeholder groups. The second tension concerning stakeholder groups concerns the potential conflict related to the role of experts participating in the development of standards simultaneously as they represent certain interest groups such as Microsoft, Google, or, as in our case, private consultant firms. Not only does the lack of broad stakeholder participation influence input legitimacy negatively because few stake holders are representing a variety of views present, the fact that these stakeholders often are biased, and may therefore act in their own in terests, makes input legitimacy even more absent or lost.

## 6.3.2. The interactive structures effect on throughput legitimacy

Tensions to the establishment of legitimacy can also be seen in at tempts to enhance throughput legitimacy. Throughput legitimacy in de jure information security standards is believed to be achieved through an interpretative scheme that states that voluntary participation creates the best, or at least unbiased, standards. In the consensus structure, we also found norms supporting this interpretative scheme, but since the standard-developing organizations cannot force members to participate, throughput legitimacy is never gained.

Even when the practice of consensus is discussed (a consensus structure practice that would increase throughput legitimacy), it is obvious that it has no real value. The following examples illustrate how weakly sanctioned the norm for this practice is:

“Usually consensus means that two-thirds agree. If we don’t reach consensus it just happens that we don’t reach consensus” (Chair at ISO-meeting Malaysia, 2015)

“We can still move on with this. We did not have continued long standing resistance.” (Another chair at ISO-meeting Malaysia, 2015)

These official comments from editors at the ISO meetings in Malaysia, show how consensus is devalued. The first editor statement indicates that reaching consensus is not that important and the state ment was uttered with a shrug of indifference – “if we don’t reach consensus it just happens that we don’t reach consensus.” In the second editor statement, she has instead re-defined consensus to a lack of “continued longstanding resistance.” In this way, consensus has a different meaning than how most people would define it.

To sum up, as mentioned in the section above on the consensus structure, the SIS tries to establish legitimacy for de jure information security standards by appealing to transparency, stakeholdergovernance, and a consensus-reaching practice. In the structure of warfare, however, tensions to this perceived legitimacy of standards are made apparent. The first tension concerns the struggle between the development of standards being efficient and the involvement of ex perts, where increased expert participation might reduce efficiency, hampering the possibility to reach consensus.

## 7. Discussion and conclusion

Our aim with this study was to uncover how structures in standard development affect the legitimacy of de jure information security stan dards. Our interest in standard development meant that we in particular focused on input and throughput legitimacy. We did so by analyzing the structures that underlie de jure information security standard develop ment in the Swedish technical committee. With regard to the examined case, some notable lessons can be learned and below we discuss the implications for research and practice.

## 7.1. Implications for research

Silva et al [44]. argued that legitimacy of standards is the result of power and the reputability of the involved organizations and experts. As standardizing organizations borrow their decision-making logic on democratically based claims. we complement this institutional perspective with a view where the establishment of a legitimate de jure standard follows the logics of a democratic process, involving three legitimacy strategies: input legitimacy, throughput legitimacy, and output legitimacy. Our choice to carry out a structurational analysis on de jure information security standard development revealed two iden tified structures – consensus and warfare – where input and throughput legitimacy are treated very differently. Showing a co-existence of these structures is a contribution to existing research. Although it is previously known that power is an important element in de jure information se curity standard development and that it undermines legitimacy [4,5, 44], this structure is not the only one present. We show that a structure with more consensus-oriented values is present, but we found that the norms’ sanction level plays an important role in which becomes the stronger structure. In turn, this shows the norms’ sanction levels importance for achieving legitimacy of de jure standards.

In our examined case, it is clear that the umbrella organization SIS had a clearly defined structure based on values of openness, consensus, and stakeholder participation. In this structure, the legitimacy for de jure standards was achieved by appealing to transparency, stakeholder governance, and a consensus-reaching practice. As previous research has pointed out $( \mathsf { e } . \mathsf { g } . , [ 7 ] )$ , stakeholder participation is a key factor for input legitimacy, and throughput legitimacy is achieved through mechanisms of transparency and the use of consensus for decision-making [29,54]. Given this structure, legitimacy for de jure standards, such as the ISO information security standards, should thus be high. However, as our analysis in Section 6.1 shows the formal norms of this structure had weak sanction levels.

The second structure we found is based on military values focusing on protecting, defending, and being quick and efficient. In this structure, two tensions to the legitimacy of de jure standards were made apparent. The first tension concerned the struggle between standard development being carried out efficiently and experts’ involvement. Here we, in line with, e.g., Brunsson & Jacobsson [8], found that increased expert participation, i.e., an increased number of participants, was seen as it might reduce efficiency, thereby hampering the possibility for demo cratic and consensus-reaching practice. A second tension was found in how experts participating in the development of de jure information security standards represent certain interest groups. This finding is in line with the observations made by Backhouse et al [5]., who, in the setting of information security standards, found that the work was politically charged, with different stakeholders’ interests influencing the process. Given this structure, legitimacy for the ISO information security standards becomes problematic. Compared to the consensus structure, we found that informal norms in the warfare structure were accompa nied with strong sanction levels.

Thus, we found that conflicting values underlie de jure information security standard development. Silva et al [44]., in their study, pointed towards presences of “contradicting discourse” as one of the reasons for the lack of adoption of de jure information security standards, and our study demonstrates that this contradiction is in play already at work with developing these standards, i.e., long before any adoption of the same. Tamm Hallstrom ¨ and Bostrom ¨ [54] argued that “values of effi ciency and legitimacy may not be easily combined, although the establishment of authority requires them both” (p. 6). However, during our analysis, we found that the SIS management somewhat sanctioned the warfare structure. For example, during the training session with SIS, they explained the consensus-reaching development. Although they pointed to how important it was in order to maintain high-quality standards, but at the same time, they pointed to how informal meet ings and liaisons was how to get things done (see Section 6.2).

Our analysis shows that success for a national standard committee is most often described based on getting a proposal for a standard through or getting as many comments and changes as possible into an existing standard. In order to make this happen, a more military structure is needed. For committee members, these different kinds of structure are often present at international meetings where they need to push to get Swedish comments through. This confirms the suggestion of Brunsson et al [[8]; 2012] that increased expert participation might reduce the ef ficiency of the standard development process. At the same time, these members have simply learned what works in the international setting, and this is in line with structuration theory’s notion of people being seen as knowledgeable and capable. This “learning in the situation” would be explained by a “reflexive monitoring” of action ([20], p. 30), which means that the members continuously monitor, or reflect over, what they are doing, the sanctions received and the results. Members claimed that in most cases there were better results from the warfare structure than from the consensus structure defined by SIS.

We also contribute to research on structurational analysis [20]. By analyzing the norms’ sanction level, we could see that formal norms do not make a structure stronger by default. We found that whereas the structure of warfare was strong, it contained no formal norms, only informal norms. These informal norms, however, all had a strong sanction level. The consensus structure contained many more formal norms, but these norms had a very weak level of sanction. This case shows that when conducting a structuration theory analysis, there is a need to identify norms as well as analyze these norms’ sanction level to determine the strength of a structure. An additional implication for research is how we captured legitimacy strategies using structuration theory’s modalities of norms, interpretative schemes and facilities as tools (see Fig. 1).

## 7.2. Implications for practice

The first implication for practice is that all legitimacy claims of de jure information security standards based on appeals to best practices are futile, given the strong presence of the warfare structure. Our study has shown that, today, very few members take part in de jure informa tion security standard development. We, therefore, cannot know which the best practice is, as pointed out by Siponen & Willison [[47], p. 269], just “because some organizations are using certain practices, does not prove that they are best practices.” Another implication of the few participating members is that diversity of organizations is neglected where the established de jure information security standards are “generic in scope, while organizations need methods tailored to their environment and operations” ([47], p. 267). Our call for broader participation in de jure information security standardization supports the (albeit slower) consensus structure, thus supporting the legitimacy of these standards on capturing best practices in general and for a diverse set of organizations.

During the years following this study, we have also noticed a prac tical implication that, at least in part, stems from this study. After we presented these findings to SIS and the informants, both SIS and indi vidual members of the information security committee have started a “journey for change” and devised a strategy for how to make sure that larger and broader participation is made available. Another visible change has been seen in the e-mails during 2019 and 2020 where chairpersons of committees follow up on members who do not partici pate during meetings or on members that do not cast votes on proposals. In structuration theory terms, we would say that the norms to participate have become more strongly sanctioned.

The second practical implication concerns the information security risks of a powerful warfare structure. As can be seen from our analysis, the market needs is one of the driving forces in the warfare structure. On one hand, meeting marked needs is a good thing; it is important that standards are up-to-date and relevant. On the other hand, keeping the pace to meet these needs may result in certain topics not being as well elaborated in the standards as could be expected if the consensus structure actually were in the forefront of the development process. For example, our empirical data shows that when voices were raised about clarifications, the standard-making processes were still being pushed forward to keep the deadlines. Furthermore, there is also a risk that when specific industry or country agendas take over in a warfarestructure some suggested information security topics and solutions might be down-played in favor of solutions driven by specific agendas. Thus, this might in the end lower the relevance of the developed stan dards, something that in the end could impact on information security in organizations world-wide.

The third practical implication of our study is to raise the awareness among managers in general, and information security managers in particular, on how existing and future structures of standard develop ment impact on the input and throughput legitimacy for standards. As Giddens pointed out, managers are both creators and victims of struc tures, so that, they need to understand what structures are at play during development of de jure standards in order to make informed choices in the future, both with regards to the use of standards and if they intend to change these structures. The two structures at play when developing de jure information security standards, confirm that Giddens’ [20] concern regarding different social systems is highly relevant when analyzing structures and their impact on individuals or society at large. As we made clear in Fig. 1, from a structuration theory point of view, there is no problem with accepting that differing and contradicting structures are brought into play within an organization’s context. Having said this – that different structures can co-exist – managers need to be aware that the differing structure may start to take over the structure defined by the organization, in this case the consensus structure, which SIS is said to stand for. This fact is particularly important for the legitimization of SIS work and. in the end. the standards that are supposed to be adopted by organizations in our society at large. We would argue that there is a need for managers of standard organizations to be aware of different struc turing processes within their organization and, based on this awareness, make informed choices on aligning them.

## 7.3. Limitations and avenues for future research

As with all ethnographic research, the obvious limitation of this study is that it lacks breadth. An ethnographer typically researches one organization or one structure – in our case, we examined the structure of one national standard-developing committee. Hence, one may claim that this limits the potential for generalizing our findings. However, a case study may reveal important information about the object under study through the rich details it provides [62]. Consequently, case study research allows for generalization, not to other cases, but rather to theoretical constructs [18,30,31]..

An avenue for further research would be to research the tension between input and throughput legitimacies within the field of infor mation security. Whereas, there is some research on input–output legitimacy in the field, the tension between input and throughput legitimacy is to a large extent unexplored. Whereas, this study has demonstrated its significance, further granularity to the analysis should be welcomed. For example, whereas, this study concludes that far too few actors are involved and that the decision-making is hidden from members, further analyses could explain how the decision process can be made more transparent and how many actors need to be involved given a certain kind of decision in order to increase legitimacy. A final avenue for future research is to extend structuration theory’s notion on norms concerning their level of sanction. This study showed that by analyzing norms’ sanction level (i.e., how strongly or weakly a norm is enforced) we could gain a deeper understanding of what makes a structure stronger. It would be interesting to add this dimension to structuration theory analyses not only in the field of information secu rity standard development, which was investigated here, or the field of e-learning where it has previously been used (see e.g., [22]), but also in other areas of information systems and even beyond.

## 7.4. Conclusion

Our aim with this study was to uncover how structures in standard development affect the input and throughput legitimacy of de jure in formation security standards. We found two structures – consensus and warfare – that affected input and throughput legitimacy very differently. The consensus structure strengthened input and throughput legitimacy for de jure standards. In the case of input legitimacy the consensus structure appealed to inclusiveness, voluntarism, and stakeholder participation and throughput legitimacy was achieved through trans parency of the process and a consensus-reaching practice. The warfare structure had a more problematic effect on both input and throughput legitimacy of de jure standards, and reasons given were that there is a need to be efficient and get things done. Few, homogeneous, partici pating members weaken input legitimacy and quick decision-making weakens throughput legitimacy. Showing a co-existence of structures with conflicting values is a contribution to existing research. We also contribute to research on structurational analysis by demonstrating that when conducting a structuration theory analysis, there is a need to identify norms as well as analyze these norms’ sanction level. A contribution to practice is to show that all legitimacy claims of de jure information security standards based on appeals to best practices are futile, since very few members take part in de jure information security standard development. Another contribution to practice is making in formation security managers aware how existing and future structures of standard development impact input and throughput legitimacy for standards
