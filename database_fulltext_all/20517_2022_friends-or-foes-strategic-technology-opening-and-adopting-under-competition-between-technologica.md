# Friends or foes? Strategic technology opening and adopting under competition between technological firms

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Technology opening competition and coopetition technology development capacity technology transformation rate

## A B S T R A C T

This study investigates the motivation behind an incumbent firm’s strategy to open up its technology and an entrant’s decision on whether to adopt this technology. We model the interactions between firms’ strategies toward coopetition and the entrant’s technology development capacity, as well as the technology transformation rate, which occurs in the technology adopting process. We find that the two technological parameters exert significant influence on the coopetition decisions of firms. Moreover, consumer surplus and social welfare are compared under different configurations. We further verify the robustness of our main results in two extension cases.

## 1. Introduction

In the technology industry, the growing complexity of technologies and the intensifying competitive environment are continuously bringing new challenges for competing enterprises. In addition to continuous innovation and investment in improving technologies, firms should also launch appropriate technology management strategies to maintain the competitive edge. Under such circumstances, an increasing number of high-tech firms, especially information technology-based firms, are opening their proprietary technologies through licensing the technical documentation or source code. For example, Facebook released the 360- degree 3D panoramic camera “Surround 360” in 2016 and subsequently opened its hardware design and stitching algorithm (Huang et al. [1]). As another example, OPPO, a leading smart device manufacturer, is reported to have licensed its proprietary rapid-charging technology, “VOOC,” to 14 industry partners as of July 2019.<sup>1</sup> Major technology firms, such as Google and IBM, also joined forces in opening their pro prietary technologies. Not only is the technology opening strategy widely adopted in the information technology sector, but we also have observed such a strategy by other technology-enabled industries. For example, in 2014, the CEO of Tesla made an official announcement to open its patents to anyone and any company to use, making it the first automaker to open its technology.<sup>2</sup> In June 2018, BYD opened its electric vehicle-developing platform (e-platform) worldwide.<sup>3</sup> Interestingly, although the technology opening strategy creates convenience for other carmakers in their new product development, it was reported that au tomakers, such as General Motors, Ford, and Chrysler, showed little interest in using Tesla’s open patents.<sup>4</sup> As shown in an interview with Bill Wallace, GM’s director of global battery systems, GM chooses not to use Tesla patents just because GM has enough technology and resources to create something even better than a Tesla in the future.<sup>5</sup> At the same time, many manufacturers in the automobile industry continue to invest heavily in their intellectual property (IP) and closely protect their technological patents, despite the availability of open technologies. The above-proposed examples show that some technology owners may be motivated to open technology to their competitors while others are closely protecting their technological patents. As for the firms who have access to these open technologies, interestingly, not all of them have incentives to adopt these technologies. Exploring the principles and motives behind these phenomena can provide practical implications for decision-makers in determining the optimal technology strategies that maximize their profits.

The technology opening and adopting strategies between competing firms may help them become better off but may also present a new set of challenges. An incumbent can benefit from technology opening by reducing a potential entrant’s competitiveness if the latter owns a suf ficiently high-technology level. However, opening proprietary technol ogies can be detrimental to the incumbent if the potential entrant is a great learner. Adopting open technologies allows entrants to lower their technology development costs, but meanwhile, hinders the innovation of their own technologies. Thus, identifying key factors and uncovering the appropriate conditions for technology opening and adopting stra tegies are of great significance for market participants to maximize their profits.

Although previous studies have provided valuable findings in the technology opening field, they do not address all aspects of this issue. Existing papers on technology opening and licensing, such as that of Sun et al. [2], Parker et al.[3], Parker and Van Alstyne [4], and Niculescu et al. [5], focus mainly on the demand side. In these studies, the tech nology firm’s opening strategy is attributed to the consumer-side network effects. However, there might be some other considerations in the firms’ decisions of opening their technology. For example, in a market where the incumbents face threats from potential entrants, they may open their technology to avoid that the new comers may come up with superior technology and gain competitive advantage. Even though Huang et al. [1] already have considered the supply-side factor, their study shows that an important reason for a firm to open its technology is the information effect, which can save competition cost by informing the firm of competitors’ technology value. However, such a conclusion does not fit the scenario where the incumbent faces threats from a potential entrant with a certain level of technology development capacity. Tech nology enterprises are constantly facing challenges from newcomers despite their strong positions in the current market. For example, even though Yahoo was a successful company in the early days of the Internet, it was soon supplanted by Google. owing to the latter's superior search-engine technology. Similarly, MySpace, Nokia-Symbian, and other traditional firms that had once dominated the market are no longer popular due to the advent of new entrants. Due to the complexity and diversity of realistic technology opening decisions, previous research cannot fully reflect all the rationales behind such problems. Challenging previous assumptions will complement existing literature on better explaining the motivations of firms’ technology opening and adopting strategies and providing more comprehensive implications for decision-makers.

Our purpose is to integrate the characteristics of both the technology owner (the incumbent firm) and the potential technology adopter (the entrant firm), conceptualize the interaction between the two parties, and identify the appropriate conditions for them to cooperate through technology opening and adopting. Our study makes several novel con tributions to the previous literature. First, differing from the studies that primarily focus on the technology owner’s opening and licensing de cisions [3–5], our study considers the potential adopter’s technology adopting decisions. Second, we relax the assumption widely adopted in the literature [2, 5] that the entrant cannot enter the current market without adopting the incumbent’s technology. We consider a more realistic scenario where the entrant can form a significant competitive threat to the incumbent. Third, we consider technology opening as a means for a firm to hinder the development of its potential competitor.

According to the former analysis, our research questions arise: (1) Under what conditions would the incumbent open its proprietary tech nology and under what conditions would the entrant adopt this tech nology? (2) What role does the technology development capacity play in firms’ technology management? (3) How would the coopetition relationship between incumbent and entrant affect the consumer surplus and social welfare? To solve these research questions, we propose a theoretic model consisting of an incumbent, who owns a proprietary technology and is deciding whether to open its technology and an entrant, who has a technology development capacity and is deciding whether to adopt the open technology or to develop similar technology in-house. Both the incumbent and the entrant are strategic when determining technology strategies related to competing aggressively or forming a coopetition relationship.

The key findings from our analysis are summarized as follows. The incentives of the incumbent and the entrant to form a coopetition rela tionship are primarily dependent on the entrant’s technology develop ment capacity (the potential technology level) and the technology transformation rate. Note that in our model, the technology develop ment capacity represents the entrant’s capability to develop a new technological product when it does not adopt the incumbent’s tech nology. The higher the technology development capacity, the higher the quality of the product. Technology transformation rate captures the entrant’s technology absorbing and transforming efficiency in the technology adopting process. Our results first show that if the new entrant has a medium technology development capacity, both the incumbent and the entrant are willing to cooperate $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the incumbent opens its proprietary technology, and the entrant adopts this technol ogy). With a high-technology development capacity and low-technology transformation rate, the entrant suffers a loss in the coopetition case and thus has less incentive to adopt the incumbent’s open technology than the former situation. Oppositely, a low-technology development ca pacity and a high-technology transformation rate make the incumbent prefer to keep its proprietary technology closed. Moreover, the coope tition case leads to greater consumer surplus and social welfare when the entrant’s technology development capacity is medium. Our key con clusions are robust in several extensions.

The remainder of the study is organized as follows. We present a review of related literature in Section 2. In Section 3, we describe the analytical model of our study, followed by the equilibrium results and analysis in Sections 4 and $^ { 5 , }$ respectively. We extend our analysis to examine the robustness of our study in Section 6. Finally, in Section 7, we provide concluding remarks and implications.

## 2. Literature Review

Our research is closely related to the literature on coopetition be tween competing firms. Researchers have studied these strategies from different perspectives, such as the intra-firm, inter-firm, and network levels [6]. Luo et al. [7] explore the coopetition relationship at the intra-firm level between differentiated functional areas within a firm and find that such coopetition improves the firm’s performance. Peng and Bourne [8], in a case study, focus on coopetition at the network level. They investigate coopetition between two healthcare networks in Taiwan and address the forces and network structures under which coopetition is allowed. Randolph et al. [9] find that technology facili tates knowledge exchange and plays a vital role in enhancing the stra tegic relevance of coopetitive options among firms. Previous literature on inter-firm coopetition involves various forms but mainly focuses on the operation and management level. Little attention has been paid to firms’ core technology management. For example, Cohen and Zhang [10] analyze a profit-sharing contract as a coopetition strategy between two-sided platforms in the ride-sharing industry and find that this strategy benefits all parties, including riders, drivers, and platforms. Zhang and Frazier [11] build a two-stage game model to explore the rationale for the formation of supply chain alliance among competitors and analyze the economic motivation of coopetition. Mantena and Saha [12] explore the network sharing strategies of two competing two-sided platforms. Compatibility with competitors is also adopted by firms as a form of coopetition [13–16]. Our study complements this literature stream by considering technology opening as a coopetition strategy between competing firms, which has the potential to either enhance or reduce firms’ competitive power by altering their core technologies.

Another branch of literature related to our study is sharing economy. Table 1 presents a summary of this literature stream. In the sharing market, asset holders share access to their assets with other participants in the exchange of payments without transferring asset ownership [17, 18]. However, this literature stream focuses primarily on sharing idle resources, such as rooms, cars, and labor, which is facilitated by online platforms [18–21]. An emerging stream of literature pays attention to the sharing (opening) of firms’ proprietary technology, a strategy by a technology firm or other organization to license the organization’s core technology or IP to others [3, 22-24]. More studies focus on technology opening between competing firms. For example, Wu [25, 26] focuses on firms’ pricing and royalty modes decisions in the technology licensing process between competitors. Huang et al. [1] explore competing firms incentives to cooperate through technology openness and thus coexist in the market. The major finding of their research is that a firm’s tech nology opening decision depends on the trade-off between the infor mation effect and access effect of the supply side. The impact of network effects on technology firms’ technology-opening strategies has been an issue of significant research interest [2, 5, 27]. However, these studies assume that a potential entrant cannot enter the market without adopting the incumbent’s technology. We consider a more realistic setting in which the entrant is able to develop a proprietary technology that may be either superior or inferior to that of the incumbent. Although technology opening has long been of scholarly interest, pre vious research predominantly considers characteristics of the incum bent—the owner of the specific proprietary technology or factors relevant for the market, such as network effects. In contrast, our study examines the important role of the entrant’s features regarding this issue since a potential entrant may be able to develop a superior technology [28–30]. Specifically, our study considers the potential competitor’s technology development capacity as a key factor and shows that it has a significant influence on the competing firms’ technology strategies.

Our research also contributes to the literature on duopoly competi tion, which mostly deals with interactions between existing firms [31–35]. We summarize the related studies and major findings in Table 2. Halaburda and Yehezkel [36] explore the impacts of the ex-ante uncertainty and ex-post asymmetric information of new technology on the competition between incumbent and entrant firms. Zhu and Iansiti [37] show that indirect network effects and customer expectations, but not platform quality, are of great importance to the entrant’s success in the competition. Wang et al. [38], Xiao and Qi [39] analyze the in cumbent’s pricing strategy in the competition with an entrant. Entry deterrence is an important topic in the competition between incumbents and entrants. Dilek and Top [40] show that, although entry barriers can benefit the incumbent by preventing competition, they can have an adverse impact on the incumbent due to opportunity cost. Huberts et al. [41] consider a dynamic competing framework between incumbent and entrant and find that entry deterrence is achieved by an optimal in vestment time rather than overinvestment. Our work contributes to this research area by considering coopetition between an incumbent and an entrant, where the incumbent takes the technology opening strategy to hinder the development of an entrant. In our model, technology plays a vital role in the two firms’ decisions about whether to form a competi tion or coopetition relationship.

## 3. Model

We establish a game theoretical model consisting of one incumbent (denoted by $i )$ which develops and sells technological products in the current market and one entrant (denoted by $e ) ^ { 6 }$ , the incumbent’s potential competitor that is capable of developing a substitutable tech nological product. The products are differentiated with respect to their vertical value due to the distinct technology levels of the two firms. They may also differ in appearances and features, which consumers with heterogeneous tastes have different preferences for. Therefore, we characterize the interaction between the incumbent and entrant using a Hotelling model to capture both the horizontal and vertical differences. In our model, the incumbent and the entrant are located at 0 and 1 on a line of unit length, respectively. A continuum of consumers with het erogeneous preferences for the products is uniformly distributed along the line, and each requires one product from either of the two firms. In addition, the market is fully covered. The notations used in our model and their descriptions are presented in Table 3.

The timeline of the game is as follows. In Stage 1, the incumbent, who owns a proprietary technological product, decides whether to open its patent technology by licensing its technical documentation or source code (hereafter referred to as “open technology”). Some previous liter ature assumes that the incumbent opens its technology for free [1, 5]. In contrast, we consider the case where the technology owner opens its technology with a charge to examine how the royalty rate affects the equilibrium outcomes. In Stage $^ { 2 , }$ observing the incumbent’s decision, the entrant chooses between adopting and not adopting the incumbent’s technology if it is open and develops the product in-house if it is not open. There are two possible market configurations: (1) Competition configuration: The incumbent chooses to keep its technology closed, or the entrant refuses to adopt the open technology and develop a new technological product. (2) Coopetition configuration: The incumbent opens its technology, and the entrant adopts and transforms the open technology into a product. We denote the two configurations as Cases C and O, respectively. In Stage 3, the two firms set the selling prices, p and $p _ { e } ,$ for their products simultaneously. In Stage 4, consumers choose to purchase one product that delivers a higher utility. We illustrate the sequence of events in Figure 1.

Consumer utility for each firm is the value a consumer derives from purchasing the product minus the price and disutility from the mismatch between the product and the consumer’s taste. Consumer $x ' s$ utilities from consuming both products in Case C are $U _ { i } = \nu - t x - p _ { i }$ and $U _ { e } = $ $\alpha \nu - t ( 1 - x ) - p _ { e } ,$ where v is the value of the incumbent’s product, which is assumed to be large enough so that the market is fully covered. Consumers incur a mismatch cost measured by the distance between locations of the specific firm and the consumer. A higher unit mismatch cost t means a higher level of horizontal differentiation of their products and a lower intensity of competition [1, 42, 43] Similarly, in Case $^ { 0 , }$ the consumer’s respective utilities are $U _ { i } ^ { o } = \nu - t x - p _ { i } ^ { o }$ and $U _ { e } ^ { o } = \theta \nu - t ( 1 -$ $x ) - p _ { e } ^ { o } . ^ { 7 }$ The entrant is characterized by two key parameters: (1) tech nology development capacity $\alpha ,$ which measures the entrant’s capability to independently develop a product that is substitutable for the in cumbent’s product. A larger (smaller) α indicates the entrant’s ability to develop a more superior (inferior) product; (2) technology trans formation rate $\theta ,$ which measures the product quality level that the entrant can achieve from adopting the incumbent’s open technology. Similar to the knowledge absorptive capacity which is positively related to firm performance in the work of Braojos et al. [44], an increasing technology transformation rate ensures improving performance of the entrant when adopting the incumbent’s technology. Note that in our research, θ is assumed to be lower than 1 since a firm is not likely to reengineer another firm's technical documentation or source code completely, given the lack of experience with understanding and incorporating the specific technology. For example, IBM’s cognitive computing technology platform, Watson, is not just a simple collection of technologies but, rather, a unification of technologies in an unprec edented way. Watson is highly dependent on open-source technology that everybody can access; only IBM, however, can run Watson.<sup>8</sup> In the car industry, even if other carmakers implement all of Tesla’s open patents, they may still encounter technical difficulties when trying to integrate various components seamlessly.

For the entrant, a higher α means a stronger pricing power. It also makes the entrant’s product more attractive to consumers and generates greater demands and consequently more profits. In the baseline model, we assume that only one entrant in the current market and that the entrant’s technology development capacity is exogenously given. For example, in the new energy vehicle industry, since Tesla opened its patent technology, many electric car companies have emerged. Among these companies, Chinese startups NIO and Xpeng Motors entered the market during the same period.<sup>16</sup> These startups were new threats to Tesla and promoted Tesla to take price measures to prevent competition. To explore the robustness of our main results in the case of multiple entrants, we extend our prior analysis by considering a market with two entrants. Both entrants make independent decisions on whether to adopt the incumbent’s technology if it is open. Therefore, the three firms form a competition relationship if the incumbent does not open its technology or a coopetition relationship if the incumbent opens its technology and at least one entrant adopts the open technology.

In this case, we use Salop’s circle model to characterize the spatial competition [47] among three firms. Without loss of generality, we as sume that one entrant has a relatively higher development capacity than the other. The incumbent (denoted by i), the entrant (denoted by $e _ { h } )$ with high-development capacity $h ,$ and the entrant (denoted by $e _ { l } )$ with low-development capacity l are evenly distributed on a unit circle with a perimeter of 1. The distance $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the arc length) between any two of the firms is 1/3. The consumers of measure 1 with heterogeneous prefer ences are uniformly distributed along the circle, and each requires one product from either of the two adjacent firms. The market is fully covered, as in our prior analysis. Moreover, a consumer’s utility from purchasing a product is still the product’s value minus the price and disutility from preference mismatch; however, in this case, the disutility is proportional to the arc distance between the firm and the consumers.

## 7. Conclusions

New entrants with a high-technology development capacity pose a significant threat to incumbents, whereas entrants often find it difficult to survive when competing against incumbents. Therefore, one common market observation, especially in the high-technology industry, is that incumbents and entrants may form a coopetition relationship. In this research, we explore the technology strategies of the incumbent and the entrant regarding whether to cooperate through technology openness and adoption. We build a theoretical game model to investigate this research issue and derive equilibrium outcomes of the two different cases.

We then characterize the conditions for the competition case and the coopetition case. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study exploring the impacts of technology development capacity on firms technology opening strategies. Our research reveals that the two firms form a coopetition relationship when the entrant’s technology devel opment capacity is medium. Keeping core technology closed is more beneficial to the incumbent for a low-technology development capacity and a relatively high-technology transformation rate. In contrast, a high technology development capacity beyond some threshold and a lowtechnology transformation rate make the entrant more likely to develop its own technological product to retain its competitive edge. Comparing the consumer surplus and social welfare in the two cases, we find that they are both higher in the coopetition case when the entrant’s technology development capacity is medium. The above findings show that competition might benefit competitive firms, consumers, and so ciety at the same time under certain conditions.

Moreover, we extend our analysis to two separate scenarios. We first consider network effects and show how this parameter influences competing firms’ technology strategies. We show that the main results in our base model are qualitatively robust in this scenario, yet there are some slight differences. When network effects are present, the incum bent can attract more consumers when opening technology. In this case, the incumbent has more incentives to open its proprietary technology, which the entrant becomes less willing to adopt. We also find that our main results are generally robust to the two-entrant scenario.

This study provides several managerial implications for decisionmakers in the technology industry. A key contribution of our study is that we demarcate the regions of the appropriateness of firms’ different technology strategies. In contrast with existing literature that constantly focuses on the factors on the demand side, such as network effects [2, 5], Our work unearths that a potential competitor’s technology develop ment capacity and the technology transformation rate in the adoption process play critical roles in firms’ technology strategies. Specifically, the incumbent and potential entrant will cooperate if the entrant’s technology development capacity is in an intermediate range, i.e., the technology level of both firms is comparable. Under such conditions, the entrant forms a threat to the incumbent, incentivizing the latter to open its proprietary technology. Meanwhile, the entrant can benefit from adopting this technology by saving development costs.

Some real-world examples can reflect such a finding. For instance, after General Motors opened its OnStar system, some equally competent automakers, such as Honda, Volkswagen, and Subaru adopted General Motors’ open technology. Adopting the open technology helps these firms save a great deal of development costs without losing competitive power. Note that either a sufficiently high- or low-capacity of the entrant will lead to the misalignment of the two firms’ coopetition incentives. For example, although Sony opened its Betamax technology, Matsushita or JVC launched their video formatting systems utilizing their hightechnology development capacities instead of adopting Sony’s open technology [27]. Sometimes, opening technologies can be risky for a technology firm if the competitors can smoothly transform these tech nologies. For example, IBM’s open PC architecture has helped its com petitors quickly absorb the key technology and develop a better architecture at a minimum cost, leading to its failure on PC business [1]. Previous literature suggests that incumbents can set entry barriers through timely investment, appropriate pricing, and branding strategies ([38, 39, 41]). In contrast, our study proposes that the incumbent’s technology opening strategy can be a measure to hinder the develop ment of an entrant and reduce potential competitive threats.

Our results suggest that a higher technology development capacity endows a firm with stronger pricing power and greater consumer attraction. Thus, a firm’s technology development capacity directly determines its competitive position in the market. When encountering a potential entrant with relatively high-technology development capacity, the incumbent should lower its product price to maintain the competi tive edge. Our study also shows that the incumbent should maximize the royalty rate within the entrant’s acceptable range and charge more for its product if a coopetition relationship is formed. Such measures enable the incumbent to extract the profits of its competitor and the consumer surplus to the largest extent. This result incentivizes the policymakers to limit the royalty rate charged by the technology owner within a reasonable range to improve consumer surplus. We also provide some practical implications for the social planners. In some industries, where the entrants’ technology development capacities are intermediate, so cial planners aiming to improve social welfare should facilitate coope tition between the incumbents and entrants. In practice, authorities may establish special economic zones, regional research and development centers, and collaboration and communication mechanisms backed by laws and policymaking to facilitate coopetition practices between firms. However, in the industries where entrants own either sufficiently high or low-technology development capacities, social planners need to promote competition between incumbents and entrants in the market.

There are several limitations to our research work, which may lead to opportunities for future research. First, we consider a one-period game in our research model. Future work can extend the analytical model to a multi-period model to examine how the factors between the two periods (e.g., customer loyalty) influence the technology-opening strategies. Second, we focus on the competition game model with only one incumbent firm in the market. Thus, an interesting direction for future work is to explore whether the firms open their technologies if two in cumbents are competing in the market before the arrival of new en trants. Finally, in our study, it is the incumbent who makes decisions about technology opening, whereas the entrant decides whether to adopt this technology if it is opened. Future work can focus on the opposite case, in which a competitive high-technology entrant de termines the technology opening state, and the incumbent responds to this situation. Considering the effects of the imitation behavior of the firm with a lower technology level, in this case, could be valuable.
