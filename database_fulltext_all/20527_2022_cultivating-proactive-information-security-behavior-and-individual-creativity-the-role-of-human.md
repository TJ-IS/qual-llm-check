## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Proactive information security behavior Individual creativity IT use governance Human relations culture

## A B S T R A C T

This study developed the concept of proactive information security behavior and examined its connections with individual creativity and two organizational context factors: human relations culture (values and beliefs that promote participation and autonomy in decision-making) and IT use governance. Reliability and validity of this construct were tested with survey data. Findings of this study support its positive relationship with individual creativity, human relations culture, and IT use governance, and show partial mediation effects of individual creativity on the relationships between human relations culture and IT use governance, and proactive infor mation security behavior. Theoretical and practice implications are discussed

## 1. Introduction

Employees, known as insiders, pose a serious threat to organizationa information security with a plethora of negative behaviors [77, 97, 137]. To address the insiders’ threat, past research explored computer abuse motivation [113, 119] and intention [67, 139], but more importantly, recommended approaches of deterrence [22, 41, 48, 119], prevention [121, 138], and detection and remedies ([121]; Chen et al., forth coming). Further, a large number of studies examined information se curity policy (ISP) compliance as an end result of organizational intervention [137], and sought to find out what factors would facilitate or hinder compliance (see, e.g., [11, 12, 45, 57, 68, 87, 115]). No doubt, such a deterrence and prevention approach would contribute to a reduction of computer abuse behaviors among employees if imple mented properly.

However, simply complying with organizational ISPs is just a mini mally important information security behavior for employees. ISPs do not exhaustively capture what behaviors must be performed by their internal employees. As new technologies are adopted and new ways of doing things are implemented, threats to information security constantly emerge. Established ISPs only prescribe behaviors that are deemed effective in dealing with threats or problems that happened in the past, but not those emerging in the work processes. More importantly, following ISPs is a passive behavior, not calling for creative solutions to security threats and problems. Passive behavior refers to directed ac tions prescribed to be right or useful, as opposed to proactive behavior that occurs to tackle an issue before it happens. Ideally, maximally contributing to organizational information security management, em ployees should be capable of identifying possible and new security threats and developing effective solutions in their work processes. These newly identified threats and problems and their corresponding solution behaviors that are shown to be effective will then help to update the ISPs. These behaviors are proactive in nature.

Proactive behavior contributes to a successful response to uncertain and turbulent environments [20, 93], idea generation and imple mentation [103], innovation and organizational change [60], and overall organizational performance [63]. Proactivity should be prac ticed in information security management [64] and enacted in infor mation security behavior as well. Proactive activities belong to positive and benevolent security behavior, which unfortunately received little attention in information security behavior research [99]. There is an urgent need to fill this research gap, as it will highly benefit organiza tions. Indeed, as information technology and security threats are constantly changing, being able to stay ahead of such threats really calls for proactive information security behavior (PISB) among employees.

Motivated thus, the purpose of this study is therefore to investigate employees’ possible proactive behavior in information security and organizational context factors that help to cultivate such behavior. We will draw on the literature of organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) in management research that characterizes and categorizes different citizenship behaviors to articulate the concept of PISB so as to advance our understanding of information security behavior and provide a new direction in information security behavior research. Among persistent concerns about insider threat to information security, this study will present some cheering findings that highlight a positive potential of employees, that is, creative problem-solving in information security, and how organizations can foster this potential with two mechanisms: human relations culture (HRC) and IT use governance (IUG). This study examines these two mechanisms, because they represent two major organizational context factors, culture and structure [55, 94], which can effectively shape organizational behavior [90] including proactive behavior [140].

With these findings, we intend to contribute to information security research in four ways. First, the introduction of the new concept of PISB will lead information security research to a new front, namely, exploring how employees can positively contribute to information security man agement. Second, and related to the first, this study will further advance our understanding of PISB by showing whether PISB has a creative element, thereby distinguishing it from information security behavior traditionally conceptualized in past research, which is mostly passive in nature. Third, this study will contribute to theory and research by exploring whether organizational context, more specifically, HRC and IUG, is conducive to the development of PISB. Fourth, the results of this study will help practitioners understand how to cultivate such proactive behaviors in their organizations.

## 2. Literature review, theory, and hypotheses

## 2.1. Theoretical framework

In this section, we will draw on the theory of OCB and review the relevant literature to introduce and develop the concept of PISB. More specifically, we will illustrate that proactive behavior is based on the participation obligation of OCB. Then, in reviewing the literature, we will discuss proactive behavior in the context of information security management and then develop the concept of PISB. Next, we will draw on the relevant literature to argue that PISB is shaped by organizational culture and structure, as they largely constitute an organizational context impacting information security behavior [71]. Organizational culture and structure determine moral beliefs with which individual employees use to justify their ISP violation behaviors [70], and their thought patterns that sustain such behaviors [100]. We will indicate that HRC and IUG, representing organizational culture and structure, are two organizational context factors/mechanisms that positively contribute to PISB as they influence participation. Finally, by showing the literature that suggests proactive behavior engages with creativity and innovation, we will posit that individual creativity (IC), another construct, predicts PISB. Thus, our research model consists of the constructs of HRC, IUG, IC, and PISB, and the hypothesized relationships between them. The definitions of these constructs are presented in the Appendix.

## 2.2. Proactive information security behavior and organizational context

In the workplace, employee work behavior, although not measured as job performance, is critical to long-term organizational success [25]. This provides a rationale for the rubric of OCB research. The concept of OCB can be traced to the notion of active citizenship syndrome [53], which consists of three interrelated obligations for civic citizens: obedience, loyalty, and participation.

Graham [36] applied this political philosophy perspective on civic citizenship to the organizational setting. Obedience, in the organiza tional setting, refers to abiding by organizational rules, regulations, and policies. Loyalty means identification with, allegiance and commitment to the organization, and subordination of local interests of individuals, groups, and departments to those of the organization as a whole. Participation, then, is about active involvement in organizational governance, staying informed of organizational issues and key concerns, and cooperating with fellow organizational members to collectively address those issues and concerns. In their participation process, em ployees would show behaviors that usually go beyond their job de scriptions, but are recognized as essential to their effectiveness and well-being in their organizations [96]. Within this framework, partici pation usually refers to extra-role or role-expanding as well as voicing behaviors [35].

These three facets of OCB can be all reflected in a major type of work behavior, information security behavior. To fulfill the obedience and loyalty responsibilities, individual employees would do something like ISPC. It is their participation obligation that motivates them to engage with proactive activities in information security practice. Proactive behavior is self-initiated and future-oriented, aiming to change and improve the situation [20, 125]. Self-initiation means doing something without being told or without an explicit role requirement, whereas long-term and future orientation is to anticipate future problems or opportunities. Moreover, it is to challenge the status quo (as opposed to passively adapting to current conditions), and to improve current cir cumstances [20]. Further, proactively behaved employees usually go beyond assigned tasks, develop their own goals, and adopt a long-term perspective to tackle problems [29]. Thus, proactivity is shown in both idea generation/implementation and problem solution.

Such OCB-based and participation-driven proactive behavior applies to information security. Given the extensive use of IT in the workplace, information security concerns become top priorities on organizations agenda. As responsible, committed, and dutiful citizens of their orga nizations, employees would share these security concerns, as well as their security experiences, thus practicing organizational security engagement [21]. Further, because of their participation obligation, employees would engage in brainstorming for ideas and measures for addressing these issues and concerns.

More specifically, the two dimensions of proactivity can both be manifested in information security behavior. To practice proactive idea implementation, employees would offer security improvement sugges tions and then discuss them with co-workers. For proactive problemsolving, they would act to prevent the reoccurrence of a problem or to solve it in an unusual or unconventional way [93]. Here is a scenario of PISB. The current practice in Organization A is that personnel are authorized to use the same user name and password to access their system. To enact proactive idea implementation, an employee would suggest that different usernames and passwords be created for those authorized personnel who do not know each other’s. A proactive problem-solving behavior in this scenario would be to change the system so that different usernames and passwords can be created for different personnel and their access records can be tracked.

Based on this discussion of proactive behavior in the context of in formation security, PISB is defined in this study as employees taking ini tiatives in foreseeing, perceiving, and identifying new possible threats to information security and addressing these threats and problems in an un conventional manner.

As previously shown, PISB may inherently be creative. Proactive idea implementation and problem-solving [93] both require the exhibition of creativity from the performer of these behaviors [126, 135]. For example, dispositional proactivity inherently involves idea generation, which is a creative behavior (Kim, Hon, and Crant [61]. More specif ically, proactive behaviors are linked to innovation [10, 110, 126, 135]. Motivated by their participation responsibilities, employees take ini tiatives in decision-making and problem-solving situations [30]. This can theoretically explain the link between proactive behavior and creativity (Giebels et al. [34]. This creative dimension of PISB suggested in the literature awaits to be tested. If such a connection between PISB and creativity is empirically supported, this study will reinforce the perspective of treating information security as an innovation [49]. Therefore, it would be an important contribution of this study to the literature.

Despite showing a connection between PISB and IC, we treat it as equally important to theoretically explain why PISB would possibly emerge in organizations. Organizations serve as an important context for individual employees [13]. More important, this context impacts their behavior [55]. Among a series of organizational context factors such as history, size, industry, and geographical location, in this study, we explore two factors: organizational culture and structure. We focus on these two factors because, compared to others, they significantly shape organizational behavior [55]. Particularly, influence on group behaviors such as collective security efficacy [56], can be attributable to organi zational culture. In this study, we examine one specific organizational culture, HRC, and one specific structure, IUG. The theoretical frame work is shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.3. Human relations culture as a fostering context

The review of the OCB literature suggests that PISB may not only happen in organizations, but also be conditioned by their culture. As a specific organizational culture, HRC is a collection of norms, beliefs, and values that emphasizes cohesion and morale that help group mainte nance and growth [101]. HRC provides fertile ground for OCB, as its core value, collectivism, promotes OCB [85]. It then can be argued that HRC fosters the growth of proactive behavior including PISB. Next, in line with the cultural tightness concept [33], HRC represents a loose culture that encourages a high level of discretion for members to perform their tasks, enabling creative solutions to problems [144].

To start with, HRC cultivates and fosters participation [24], just as it cultivates and fosters OCB [85]. As it cultivates a friendly, encouraging, welcoming, and facilitative climate, HRC fosters among employees positive feelings toward participation. More importantly, the partici pation mindset would serve as scripts guiding actual behaviors among employees in interactions [112]. Specifically, this is revealed in promoting employee proactive behavior [81]. First, participation in spires employees to self-manage their work and bolsters their confidence in handling challenging work [1]. Next, participation facilitates empowerment that motivates initiative-taking behaviors in their work roles [62].

As the research literature supports the link between HRC, especially the value of participation, and proactive behavior, it follows that HRC would positively influence PISB as well. Indeed, empirical research suggests such a relationship. More recently, Lowry, Cao, and Everard [78] found that collectivism positively influences privacy concerns which lead to the preferred use of technologies that protect privacy in online interpersonal communication. This suggests that people from an HRC have strong security and privacy orientations, which may translate into behaviors of proactively seeking measures to ensure security and privacy protection.

As HRC is more people-centric [24], it promotes the development of personal accountability for security among employees as well as an organizational climate that encourages employees to engage in mutual assistance in performing security duties [21]. Further, personal accountability leads to security engagement, practices of collectively sharing security advice and experiences among employees [21]. Next, an organizational security climate is one where co-workers, supervisors, as well as top managers interact with each other in their security engagement practices [21]. Such an organizational security climate would stimulate the development of extra roles in security practices [50], some of which (voicing behaviors) exemplify PISBs.

Further, HRC facilitates the development of three types of organi zational commitment (affective, continuance, and normative) [92]. From the organizational perspective, affective organizational commit ment helps to shape employees’ motivation to protect organizational information assets by ways of responding to security threats [98]. As employees with high affective organizational commitment are emotionally attached to their organizations, and are willing to do more than simply fulfilling their job requirements [91], they would be ex pected to show extra-role information security behaviors [50]. Although these security behaviors examined in the literature, which benefit from participation and organizational commitment are not necessarily pro active in nature, PISBs should also be outcomes of participation and organizational commitment. As organizational ISPs are guidelines or action maps for information security practices [19], proactive behaviors in this area can be initiating ideas and suggestions of how to improve best practices, and seeking creative solutions to security problems that have occurred despite remedy actions outlined in the policies were taken in the past.

H1: Human relations culture is positively associated with PISB.

## 2.4. Human relations culture as an antecedent to individual creativity

Factors that promote IC have been persistently identified in the research literature. Among them is the context where individual em ployees are embedded in Amabile and Conti [3, 46]. As a major con stituent of the work context, culture impacts IC [17, 141], because its constituents such as norms, values, beliefs, and shared knowledge, determine individual motivation, cognition, and problem-solving [16, 86]. For example, if a culture overly promotes strong rules and hereafter compliance, and discourages deviant behavior, it will cultivate indi vidual characteristics of caution, predictability, and discipline, which generally inhibit creativity [33].

More than other types of organizational cultures, HRC cultivates core values of trust, belonging, and participation [24]. These values then promote behaviors of open communication, employee involvement, participation, and teamwork [42]. Further, these values cultivate strong feelings of ownership and control over one’s own work and ideas, which then foster creativity [2]. Further, in an HRC, a highly supportive environment [24], individual employees are granted much flexibility and autonomy in decision-making as well as work practices. Autonomy highly facilitates IC [2]. When individuals enjoy autonomy in task assignment accomplishment, they produce more creative work [147]. In a supportive organization, the supervisor, the group, as well as the or ganization tend to encourage creative activities [2]. At the organiza tional level, this is usually shown in the encouragement of risk-taking, generating new ideas, supportively evaluating new ideas [145], enhancing task motivation [23]. Further, a supportive organization would tend to recognize and reward creativity [146]. At the group level, HRC is conducive to open communication, and therefore, promotes exposure to unusual ideas, constructive evaluation of new ideas, and critical thinking, which all cultivate creativity [2].

Further, centralization interacts with goal orientations in impacting creativity [46]. Centralization refers to power distribution in an orga nizational hierarchy and the extent to which employees are allowed to participate in decision-making [38]. Low centralization is a feature of HRC [24]. According to Hirst et al. [46], low centralization is positively related to learning orientation, an intrinsic interest in developing one’s competence and task mastery, all of which combine to contribute to creativity. Thus, Hirst et al.’s [46] findings regarding the interactions of centralization and goal orientations and their impact on creativity point to a strong link between HRC and IC:

H2: Human relations culture is positively associated with individual creativity.

## 2.5. IT use governance

As security concerns can be mostly traced to technology imple mentation and use, information security relates to IT governance. IT governance refers to the pattern of decision-making for all IT-related issues including IT investment, planning, management, and application [108, 134]. Further, the pattern of decision-making is mainly shown in the amount of autonomy or the extent of participation that employees have in decision-making. Thus, IT governance is a construct of organizational structure that is characterized by centralization and formalisation [104]. Although formalisation refers to the use of rules and regulations to control and prescribe behavior [40], centralization means the amount of power or autonomy that employees have in decision-making [127]. IT governance, based on its definition and operationalization in the liter ature, only captures the centralization dimension of the structure.

Among the IT-related issues, IT use management engages most em ployees, as they more or less use IT in their work. Indeed, a significant number of prior studies $( \mathrm { e } . g . , [ 1 4 8 , 1 4 9 ] )$ pointed to the central role of authority and rights in using IT in organizational IT governance. For the same reason, IT use management is the area where the highest number of employee information security behaviors emerge. Thus, this study focuses on IUG, a sub-area of IT governance, briefly defined as the extent to which employees participate in decision making in IT use management including addressing information security issues and concerns in IT use, in its exploration of how IT governance impacts individual creativity and proactive information security behavior.

IT governance positively impacts organizational performance [150]. More specifically, research strongly suggests that IUG influences infor mation security. For example, employee participation leads to multiple positive outcomes of information systems security risk management through some problem-solving behaviors such as “validating calcula tions and establishing password protection in financial spreadsheets” (p. 508, [116]). Furthermore, in a recent multi-method (experiment and survey) study, Kearney and Kruger [59] found that there are significant perceptual differences among the three groups of employees of a large Australian company (top management, IT professionals, and ordinary or line-level employees) with regard to who is responsible for information security and who is the solution for it. More specifically, while among the three groups, more line-level employees than the other two groups indicated that everyone is responsible for information security, but the IT department is the solution for it. This perceived information security management practice in the organization deviates from Kearney and Kruger’s [59] proposed organizational information security model that all employees are the solution for information security. The findings of this study suggest that IUG, autonomous decision-making at the line management level, which most likely was not the case in that organi zation, would lead to the practice of the proposed model in information security.

Further, from a governance-knowledge fit perspective, as pertinent knowledge usually is highest in business units, authority of decision making in IT-related issues should be collocated with pertinent knowl edge [54]. This is especially so in the area of information security, as in many organizations such as healthcare providers, employees at all levels are involved in information protection activities even on a daily basis [65]. Thus it mandates that they are empowered in decision-making in security-related issues.

Further, proactive behavior such as making improvement sugges tions [6] is highly associated with job autonomy [34]. At the individual level, PISB requires that employees take initiatives in addressing infor mation security issues. Doing that calls for their information security involvement, that is, spending time and energy, and making efforts to ensure a secure information environment for their organizations [107]. IUG allows for employee involvement in information security practices, as it provides enough room for decision-making in IT issues including information security. Recent security behavior research points to a possible link between IUG and PISB. Hsu et al. [50] showed that employee involvement, via social control, is not just positively related to in-role behaviors but also extra-role behaviors in information security. Some extra-role behaviors, such as voicing, are proactive in nature, because they involve offering ideas, suggestions, and comments for how to improve security management performance [50].

As employees at all levels are involved in the protection of infor mation security, organizations should have a mechanism that ensures employee participation in the overall organizational implementation of proactive security measures [65]. IUG is such a mechanism, which en ables and promotes decision-making in information security by em ployees even at the lowest level. When employees participate in organizational proactive security measures such as predicting possible vulnerabilities by way of penetration testing in the organization’s network, reviewing and updating current security procedures, and addressing security issues as they emerge [65], they perform PISB. This is another indication of a possible link between IT governance and PISB. Thus, we propose:

H3: IT use governance is positively associated with PISB.

Moreover, both culture and structure influence technological in novations [151]. IUG, especially a decentralized IUG structure, would also help to promote IC. As shown earlier, low centralization in structure may contribute to IC. It follows that when it is less restrictive for em ployees to use IT resources, they are more likely to develop creative solutions in their work. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H4: IT use governance is positively associated with individual creativity.

## 2.6. Individual creativity

In information security, an important part of PISB is to address se curity issues emerging in the work process. To do that requires em ployees to show a problem-solving orientation and competence, which are core components of IC. According to the componential theory, IC has the following indicators: high motivation, strong skills, and problem solving competence [2].

First, people who show IC must have a high motivation to complete tasks [4], and a “general and pervasive orientation toward to one’s work” ([2], p. 116). Such a high task motivation is usually associated with a significant amount and persistence of effort [4], and predicts commitment to tasks [118] as well as to problem-solving [106]. High task motivation is usually shown in idea generation. Strong task moti vation is needed in information security practice and should be shown in positive information security behaviors. For example, the aware assur ance behavior takes as a prerequisite a strong motivation to do well in protecting the organization’s information assets [117]. Such a protec tion motivation, which is both intrinsic and extrinsic [98], is an important assumption in information security behavior [131].

Next, IC is associated with the ability of acquiring domain-specific knowledge and skills [2]. These skills include factual knowledge, tech nical proficiency, ideas about various issues in the domain, knowledge of paradigms regarding the domain, standard problem-solving proced ures in the domain, and even esthetic criteria [106]. Such domain-relevant skills serve as a foundation for creativity. More importantly, they are also much related to and seriously needed in addressing information security issues. PISB mandates that employees must gain and show such domain-specific (information security) knowledge, a prerequisite to proactive information security manage ment, which serves as a foundation for self-efficacy, an important antecedent to information protection behavior [12, 57, 98].

Furthermore, problem-solving competence is inherent in IC, which “determines the flexibility with which cognitive pathways are explored, the attention given to particular aspects of the task, and the extent to which a particular pathway is followed in pursuit of a solution” ([2], p. 95). With a problem-solving competence, employees will take new perspectives on problems, applying heuristics to explore new cognitive pathways, and formulating a working style characterized by a strong persistence [2]. According to Ruscio et al. [106], behaviors strongly associated with problem-solving competence include, but are not limited to, goal setting, responding to challenge, and more specifically, getting prepared for group meetings, actively participating in group problem-solving activities, generating novel ideas, asking relevant questions, offering ideas, and building on others’ contributions, etc. As information security management involves problem-solving, PISB en tails such competence. In information security management, responding challenges, for example, are shown in employees’ response efficacy, employees’ intention, and belief in their competence of taking action to respond to threats and risks to information security [98]. Likewise, of fering help and constructive suggestions to fellow employees in infor mation security management for how to solve security problems is revealed in extra-role security behaviors [50]. This short review of the literature regarding IC with respect to its relevancy to and utility in information security management leads us to posit the following hypothesis:

H5: Individual creativity is positively associated with PISB.

In sum, we propose that the organizational context characterized by HRC and a structure of IUG fosters PISB, and IC that in turn contributes to PISB. This theoretical model therefore implies that IC mediates the effects of HRC and IUG on PISB. Fig. 1 presents our theoretical model that consists of the relationships between the constructs.

H6a: Individual creativity mediates the effect of human relations culture on PISB.

H6b: Individual creativity mediates the effect of IT use governance on PISB.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Data collection

This study used a questionnaire-based survey method to collect data. It went through a two-step testing process. The questionnaire items were developed based on the literature review as well as measures used in other studies. Following its initial development, the survey was pilot tested using a sample of 64 MBA students. The testing results were then used to refine the survey. Validity and reliability were further tested using the results of the final large-scale survey. The testing results will be reported in the Results section.

Finally, data were collected from individual employees working in organizations across several industries. Students in two MBA general management classes with enrollments of 50 and 45 and an undergrad uate business statistics class with an enrollment of 24 at a large Mid. western university were briefed about this study. As an option for extra credit, students were asked to send the survey as an email attachment to three of their business contacts or family members (parents and/or siblings) who work full-time and use computer technology substantially in their work duties. Students were also asked to provide contact in formation for these family members on a separate paper so that confirmation could be made with the survey takers if the researchers deemed it necessary to do so. The contact information was not identi fiable on the completed surveys. Students were also requested to inform their contacts that participation was voluntary, and they were assured of the confidentiality of their responses. This method of using student contacts to successfully recruit full-time employee participants in studies has been documented in prior research (e.g., see [37, 82, 123]).

Through this method, a total of 246 surveys was collected, with a response rate of 70%. With the removal of 11 incomplete surveys, the final sample size is 235 complete surveys. In 228 of the 235 complete surveys, respondents indicated which industries their organizations belong to. A total of 19 industries were reported with the following distribution: 51 healthcare, 43 manufacturing, 41 education, 7 govern ment, 15 retail, 12 finance, 9 construction, 11 technology, 5 energy, 6 marketing, 7 legal, 5 consulting, 2 insurance, 1 fashion, 4 utilities, 3 transportation, 1 agriculture, 4 nonprofit, 2 telecommunications, and 7 missing values. Organization size ranges from 7 to 250,000 with a mean of 7176 and a standard deviation of 28,600. Of the 235 respondents, the average tenure was 7.54 years with a standard deviation of 7.90. A total of 66 respondents were in leadership positions whereas 224 of them were non-leaders (11 missing values).

## 3.2. Measures

The measurement items were adapted from or developed based on extant literature to maximize their validity and reliability. Appendix 1 shows the constructs with their definitions, and the primary sources of the measurement items, and Appendix 2 presents the entire instrument.

## 3.2.1. Human relations culture

The HRC scale, which consists of four items measuring promotion of participation and open discussion, empowerment, attention to employee concerns and ideas, and promotion of human relations, teamwork, and cohesion, was adapted from Quinn and Spreitzer’s [102]. A sample item is as follows: “Our organization promotes participation and open dis cussion.” Each item was rated on a scale from 1, “strongly disagree,” to 7, “strongly agree.” The coefficient alpha for the HRC scale was 0.94.

## 3.2.2. Individual creativity

The IC scale was adapted from the one originally developed in Tierney, Farmer, and Graen [124] and slightly adapted in Farmer, Tierney, and Kung-Mcintyre [26]. It consists of five items asking re spondents to assess their ability to generate, raise, and try new ideas, provide creative solutions, and serve as a role model for creativity. Although Tierney et al. [124] and other studies such as Oldham and Cummings (1996), Zhou and George [143], and Farmer et al. [26] that adopted this scale used supervisor rating, the scale was based on self-report in this study. This is not unusual, as [152] used a self-report scale as well, from which some items of the scale used in Tierney et al. [124] were adapted. Each item was rated on a scale from 1, “strongly disagree,” to 7, “strongly agree.” (α = 0.88).

## 3.2.3. IT use governance

The IUG scale consists of three items. One is to ask about the level of management where decision-making in IT use management is located: corporate IS, divisional IS, or line management (with corporate IS as “1<sup>′′</sup> and line management as “3<sup>′′</sup>). The other two items are used to inquire about decision-making effectiveness in this area. The coefficient alpha (α) of this scale is 0.64.

## 3.2.4. PISB

We developed the PISB scale in this study based on the literature review of proactive behavior research. According to Parker et al. [93], proactive behavior involves idea generation and implementation, and proactive problem-solving. These two aspects of proactive behavior can be reflected in the context of information security. We used three items to measure idea generation and implementation, and proactive problem-solving in information security management practices. Idea generation is measured in the item of “I give suggestions on how to make improvements in information security practices in my organization.” Idea implementation is reflected in the item of “I discuss with my col. leagues and boss ways of improving our information systems security measures.” Lastly, proactive problem-solving is covered in the item of “I am active in taking efforts in identifying possible security risks or threats to my organization’s information systems.” Although this item focuses on problem identification, the action part of problem-solving is already covered in the other two items. Each of these three items was rated on a scale of 1, “strongly disagree,” to 7, “strongly agree.” The coefficient alpha of this scale is 0.88.

An exploratory factor analysis was performed for the construct of IUG, but the results (with loadings of 0.15 for one item, and 0.94 for the other two items) do not confirm the one-factor three-item structure. Thus, this construct should be reduced to a two-item structure. This change will be further subject to an ensuing confirmatory factor analysis test. The exploratory factor analysis results (0.82 and higher) confirm the one-factor structure for the construct of PISB. Besides HRC and IUG, other factors that may potentially affect PISB such as industry, tenure, age, and position are treated as control variables.

## 4. Result

## 4.1. Descriptive statistics, reliability, and validity

The descriptive statistics for all the variables analyzed in this study are shown in Table 1.

To test validity and reliability, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis using AMOS 23 software with maximum-likelihood estimation procedures, observing the factor structure of the focal variables. The expected four-factor solution (HRC, IUG, IC, and PISB) displayed a good fit with the data $( \mathrm { C M I N } / \mathrm { D F } = 1 . 5 0 , x ^ { 2 } = 1 0 4 . 8 2 , d f = 7 0 , \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 9 ,$ , TLI $= 0 . 9 8 , \mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 5 , \mathrm { S R M R } = 0 . 0 3 )$ . However, consistent with the previously mentioned exploratory factor analysis results, the results of the confirmatory factor analysis also indicate that item 13 that measures IUG should be removed from the construct as it has a loading of 0.08, far below the cutoff point of 0.5. Thus, from now on, this study uses a twoitem scale to measure the construct of IUG.

Construct reliability was tested using Cronbach’s alpha and com posite reliability score [27]. Cronbach’s alpha measures internal con sistency among all items used for one construct. Composite reliability serves a similar purpose but is considered a more rigorous reliability measure in structural equation modeling [105]. Both Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability values are presented in Table 2.

The Cronbach’s alpha values for all the constructs are equal to or higher than 0.87. The composite reliability values of all the constructs are greater than the threshold value of 0.70 (See Table 2) [8, 32, 88]. These results indicate that items measuring the same construct are highly intercorrelated and that the constructs have good construct reliability.

Construct convergent validity was assessed using item factor loading scores. High item factor loadings provide evidence of convergent val idity [[5]son & Gerbin, 1988]. The factor loadings (see Table 2) ranged from 0.80 to 0.91 for HRC items. 0.70 to 0.90 for IC items. 0.87 to 0.89 for IUG items (after removing one item with a loading value of 0.08), and 0.77 to 0.84 for PISB items. The test results indicate that the items measuring all the constructs have loadings of greater than the cutoff point of 0.5.

Discriminant validity was tested in four ways. First, discriminant validity was examined using average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct. Discriminant validity is supported if the square root of the construct’s AVE is greater than the correlations of the construct with others [27, 52]. In this study, the AVE values of the constructs indicate that the constructs have good discriminant validity (see Table 2). Sec ond, the cross-loadings of measurement items on latent constructs were examined. All the items load more highly on their intended constructs than any other construct with a primary loading at least 0.2 larger than secondary loading (see Table 3), showing good discriminant validity [153]. The third indicator of good discriminant validity is that all the correlations between constructs do not exceed 0.7 (see Table 1). Fourth, we checked the HTMT values of the constructs, which are all below 0.85 [44], further indicating that the constructs have good discriminant validity.

Following prior studies (e.g., [122]), we evaluated non-response bias, using a t-test. T-tests were performed comparing the responses of the first 30 and last 30 respondents with five items measured with an interval scale as dependent variables, which were randomly selected from the questionnaire. No significant difference was found between the two groups at the 0.05 level in all the five t-tests, suggesting no evidence of non-response bias in the data.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Key findings

This study articulated a view that rather than passively complying with organizational ISPs, employees can proactively address informa tion security in their organizations. This potential of individual em ployees is best captured in the concept of PISB developed in this study. This study finds that PISB is positively related to IC, suggesting that PISB has a creative element. This study also finds that HRC contributes to the development of PISB. It does that via several routes. Besides its direct contribution to PISB, HRC positively relates to IC, which itself is a positive contribution to PISB. In the same ways as HRC does, IUG stimulates the development of PISB as well.

## 5.2. Implications for theory and practice

One major contribution of this study to information security behavior is the introduction of the concept of PISB, which captures what employees should and can do in organizational information security management practice. The conceptualization of PISB was based on the review of the literature of OCB and proactive behavior research. This represents a move beyond past research in seeking to understand the role of employees in information security management in organizations.

Past research overly conceptualized employees as a major source of problems in information security management in organizations. In past and recent research. emplovees were mostly labeled as “organizational insiders” who misbehaved in handling organizational information assets (e.g., see, [22, 58, 129, 132, 137]), or the “weakest link in information security” [12, 84, 132]. Corresponding with such negative perceptions, approaches recommended in past research were unsurprisingly deter rence and sanction, and training and education (Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010). For deterrence and sanction, from the organizational perspective, more specific measures such as enhancing mandatoriness of security policies [11], taking disciplinary actions [120], punishment severity and detection certainty [45], and using fear appeals [57] were offered as effective solutions. For the training and education approach, although non-punitive in nature, it mainly targeted how to effectively motivate employees to comply with organizational ISPs [154]. Thus, it is no overstatement to argue that past research perceived employees as a target that should be put under organizational control or at most playing a very passive and reactive role in information security management practice. This line of theorizing would make it difficult for us to discover any positive contributions from employees in information security management.

In this regard, the conceptualization of PISB represents a new line of theorizing toward the role of employees in information security man agement. This line of theorizing enables us to discover a great potential in employees to make a positive contribution to organizational infor mation security management. Viewed from this light, this study contributed to information security behavior research by filling up a gap in this line of research because positive information security behavior were hardly identified in past research [99]. More important, following this line of theorizing, the image of employees would be turned around from being trouble makers to problem solvers. Thus, from a theoretical perspective, with the conceptualization of PISB, this study is, not just a departure from past research, but also an affirmation of the ethical mandate of implementing participative management in organizations [18]. More specifically, it affirms the right as well as the role of em ployees in significant participation in organizational information secu rity management. Based on Inkeles’ [53] categorization of citizenship obligations and Graham’s [36] application to the organizational setting, conceptualization of the role of employees as mostly ISP compliance in past research only captured their obedience responsibility, or avoidance behaviors [15]. Yet this study defined their role at the participation aspect, the highest in the hierarchy of OCB responsibilities.

Related to the first major contribution, this study contributes to the information security behavior research in its demonstration that PISB has a creative gene. Specifically, this study finds that IC is positively associated with PISB. This finding shows that as a participation re sponsibility level citizenship behavior, PISB is inherently creative. This creative element of PISB makes it markedly different from the security policy compliance behavior as it enables employees to significantly contribute to organizational information security management through proactive and voluntary problem solution activities. This creative element enables employees to solve problems emerging in organiza tional information security practices. Yet problem-solving is missing in security policy compliance behavior. By showing this creative element in PISB, this study represents a breakthrough in understanding and conceptualizing employees’ information security behavior.

Third, this study contributes to information security behavior research in that it reveals that HRC provides a positive environment conducive to the development of PISB. This study finds that HRC facil itates the development of PISB by promoting IC, which then contributes to PISB. HRC is found to be a direct path for developing PISB. A second path is a mediated one, which consists of the nodes of HRC, IC, and PISB. The validation of these two paths in this study indicates that HRC is a beneficial organizational environment for developing PISB. Consistent with past research on HRC [24, 42, 112], this study showed that HRC promotes participation or participative management, which facilitates proactivity, in this case, in information security behavior. This finding further echoes our previous assertion that organizations should promote a participative environment as it not only represents an ethical mandate but also constitutes a positive context for employees to develop and exercise their creativity, which can be shown in information security management activities.

In addition to HRC, IUG serves as another organizational context factor facilitating the development of PISB. Besides positively impacting PISB, IUG promotes the development of PISB via IC. The findings of this study show that both organizational culture and structure shape employee information security behaviors. More specifically, these findings indicate that PISB benefits from HRC and IUG. These findings offer some insight into what organizational antecedents are critical to developing PISB.

Apart from implications for theory and research, practitioners have some takeaway from this study. Organizations certainly need to enhance their technology investment to address information security and beef up their ISPs. Further, it is also important for organizations to educate employees about these policies. However, besides this bottom-line approach, organizations should treat employees as resources rather than liabilities in information security management. This study reveals an important potential in employees to positively contribute to organi zational information security management. Capitalizing on the creative potentials of their employees, organizations can be rewarded with extra results unexpected from their regular information security investment and planning. In their own work experience, employees, if culturally and structurally motivated and inspired, would proactively and creatively address various information security concerns as they emerge, using their work experience and knowledge. Organizational ISPs, no matter how minutely they are crafted, most likely do not provide information about such emerging concerns and problems regarding information se curity, and certainly do not specify such hands-on solutions. Because of this, organizations should strive to culturally and structurally provide a participative environment, which will, in turn, facilitate the develop ment of employees’ PISB.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

After reexamining our research procedure and methods, we found some limitations in our data collection procedure. We measured IUG by asking respondents to indicate what level of management in their or ganizations is authorized to make decisions in the domains of IT use management. Three levels of management were provided for them to choose one: corporate level, division level, and line level. Such a way of soliciting responses presumed that respondents all work in large orga nizations. But for respondents who work for small size organizations, this presumption is less appropriate as management may not be divided into such three levels.

As to whether and how these two plausible explanations make sense, we have to resort to future research. First, future research can investi gate whether centralization/decentralization and formalisation, two main constructs of organizational structure in its general sense, relate to PISB, just as HRC, a general organizational culture construct. Results from such research will help to ascertain whether PISB is predicted just by general organizational context factors and whether PISB is a behavior that is beyond IT use.

To address this limitation, future research can refine the concept of IUG and test whether it is conceptually consistent with the construct of centralization/decentralization in decision-making. More specifically, in research design, future research may tighten the requirement for qualified respondents and collect data only from employees of large size organizations. Further, some employees may not know what level of management has the authority to make decisions on IT-related issues especially security in their organizations. In future research, this possi bility should be accounted and clarification about this needs to be made in the instructions for the instrument.

In addition, this study examined the relationship between HRC and PISB. Future research may investigate whether other types of organi zational culture relate to PISB. More important, future research should examine how other factors influence the relationship between organi zational culture and PISB. This line of future research will generate more nuanced knowledge of what factors can be strategically counted on so as to buffer or boost the relationship between organizational culture and PISB. Results of such research endeavors will serve a broad spectrum of organizations, as culture varies from organization to organization.

## 6. Conclusion

This study concludes the investigation with the argument that, in addition to requiring employees to comply with organizational ISPs, organizations should seek and promote positive and beneficial behaviors in individual employees to tackle issues and threats to information se curity. In this regard, this study develops the concept of PISB, which shows one such positive and beneficial information security behavior and highlights that IC predicts PISB, and more importantly, that HRC and IUG are two organizational factors that positively impact PISB. By providing a new theoretical framework grounded in empirical evidence, this study contributes to our knowledge of information security behaviors.
