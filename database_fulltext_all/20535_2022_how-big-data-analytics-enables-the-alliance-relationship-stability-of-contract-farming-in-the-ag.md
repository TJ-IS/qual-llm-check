# How big data analytics enables the alliance relationship stability of contract farming in the age of digital transformation

<sup>a</sup> College of Economics and Management, South China Agricultural University, Guangzhou, China <sup>b</sup> Anderson School of Management, University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, New Mexico, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Big data analytics Risk management capability Data quality Alliance relationship stability

## A B S T R A C T

Notwithstanding the potential of big data analytics technology for alliance management, there is a lack of un derstanding of how such digital technology influences alliance relationship stability (ARS). Drawing on the in formation technology-enabled organizational capabilities (IT-enabled OCs) perspective, this study empirically verifies that big data analytics promotes ARS and risk management capability. Moreover, market risk manage ment capability (MRM) enhances ARS, and data quality moderates the relationship between big data analytics usage (BDU) and MRM. This research reveals the impact mechanism of BDU on the ARS. Implications for management and future research are presented as well.

## 1. Introduction

With the burgeoning growth of big data analytics technology, it has been regarded as a key and imperative tool in the age of digital trans formation [13,29]. Big data analytics refers to advanced data processing and analysis technologies combining tremendous computing power with greater intelligence, which can generate and visualize solutions for decision-makers through statistical analysis, open-source programming, and data management [13,30]. As big data analytics has become a widely used digital technology within and across organization bound aries, scholars have suggested that it is a key complementary resource that facilitates alliance management [12,56]. Alliance refers to the voluntary arrangement between supply chain partners on the exchange, sharing, and joint development of products, services, or technologies [65]. Big data analytics technology provides a set of infrastructure that can create multiple links, enable weak ties and increase communication and social interaction between alliance partners [56]. With open communication standards and cross-functional transparency enabled by big data analytics technology, the alliance can optimize the efficiency or accuracy of supply chain transactions [23], codify and mobilize tacit knowledge, as well as introduce new joint innovative processes [47]. With these advantages. big data analytics can not only promote the formation of strategic alliances but also enhance the value derived from alliances [26,44,57].

Contract farming has gained increasing attention for its increasingly significant role in the complementary and allied development of agri cultural supply chain [73]. As an alliance form characterized by vertical coordination [63], contract farming is defined as an institution for the production and supply of agricultural commodities under forward con tracts [51]. Such contracts are signed by farmers and agribusinesses<sup>1</sup> and specify that the farmer produces a certain amount of agricultural products for the company under some predetermined conditions, such as production processes and output prices [73]. Compared with other in dustries, agricultural alliances are faced with plenty of dual risks from the market and production [41,50]. The anticipated benefits of building alliances are often replaced with significant uncertainty and high probabilities of interest damage [35,73]. Low alliance relationship sta bility (ARS) has been often encountered in implementing contracts farming [19,58]. For example, the default rate of contract farming in China is as high as 70%. ARS describes the state and tendency of members to maintain stable, long-term, and secure alliance relationship [20,43]. Big data analytics technology can gather and connect agricul tural supply chain members [71], and strengthen communication and collaboration between focus companies and upstream suppliers [11]. Furthermore, big data analytics provides effective early warning and generates timely and accurate response strategies for potential risks [55]. Therefore, big data analytics technology may play a critical role in improving the ARS of contract farming.

Despite the fruitful potential of big data analytics for alliance, there is a lack of understanding of how such digital technology play role in alliance contexts. Although some studies have focused on the effects of digital technology in facilitating strategic alliance formation and enabling firms to derive value from alliances [44,56,65], there is not as much empirical evidence regarding the role of big data analytics as an enabler of ARS. Furthermore, prior information system (IS) research has predominately emphasized three kinds of antecedents of ARS: contract characteristics, partner characteristics, and relationship characteristics [19,27,52,58], leaving the technical drivers comparatively neglected. Given these gaps in existing research, we examine how big data analytics technology affects ARS in a specific contract farming context.

Grounded on the information technology (IT)-enabled organiza tional capabilities (IT-enabled OCs) perspective, we argue that big data analytics usage (BDU) can not only directly accelerate, but also indi rectly improve the stability of alliance relationship by enhancing orga nizations’ production and market risk management capabilities. Additionally, the effectiveness of BDU depends on data quality (DQ). An empirical survey among China’s provincial-level agribusinesses sup ported most of our assumptions. By focusing on the influences of big data analytics technology on the ARS in the situation of contract farming, this research extends existing understanding of the value of big data analytics in alliance management, provides a new technology standpoint for the existing discussion on ARS, and, at the same time, sheds light on how agribusinesses can realize digital transformation and reap competitive advantage from it.

## 2. Theoretical foundations

## 2.1. IT-enabled OCs perspective

Realizing the business value of IT is a popular topic in IS research [22]. The IS literature mainly demonstrates the impact of IT on corpo rate performance, thus forming the so-called IT-enabled OCs perspective [9]. This view proposes that IT resources affect corporate performance through the development of business/OCs; therefore, it emphasizes the enabling role of IT in creating business value [6].

IT-enabled OC is an appropriate lens to understand the impact of big data analytics technology usage in firms. IT-enabled OC has been a dominant and well-established perspective to explain how a company’s IT resources affect the development of competitive advantages [23]. It is also one of the most powerful and evident frameworks focused on solving the IT productivity paradox in IS research [10]. Many companies have invested in big data analytics technology but have not successfully reaped positive outcomes [14,30]. Given the growth in BDU and the mixed outcomes it has generated, this paper focuses on exploring particular capabilities, as the mediating mechanisms that govern the translation of a firm’s BDU into ARS.

Previous IS research using this framework has found that some OCs can play such mediating roles, including strategic and business flexi bility [8,16], knowledge management capabilities [10,16], new product development capabilities [53], corporate entrepreneurship [15], IT integration capabilities [8], supply chain management [2], operational capabilities [7], and online customer engagement [9]. Given that the ability to effectively identify, analyze, and respond to risks is critical to survival and success in a rapidly changing business environment, risk management capabilities are non-negligible mechanisms capable of improving business performance [5]. In this sense, this study builds on the IT-enabled OC framework to verify how BDU influences ARS via the mediating path of risk management capabilities.

## 2.2. Big data analytics usage

Big data analytics technology has become a key IT in organizations due to the increase in the amount, speed of change, and type of data in businesses [13,29]. It describes how the new generation of analytical techniques can store, manage, analyze, and visualize data in an advanced and unique way to generate meaningful and accurate insights in a low-cost manner $[ 1 4 , 4 9 ]$ . In this study, BDU represents the extent, scope, and ability of an organization to utilize such tools [30,75]. The widespread use of big data analytics provides companies with more opportunities to discover hidden patterns, associations, segmentations, and other insights from big data sets [12,31]. This brings opportunities for enterprises to improve products/services, optimize management and operations [28], improve and innovate business models, and enhance organizational competitive advantages [33,46]. Recent work has shifted from an internal focus to examining the role of big data analytics tech nology in external partnerships in supply chains $[ 1 2 , 3 2 , 3 6 , 6 6 ]$ . Big data analytics technology can gather and connect different participants in the industry (e.g., focus companies and upstream suppliers) together, strengthen the communication and collaboration between channel members in the supply chain [11], thus helping to improve the alliance relationship between the focus enterprise and its suppliers. In agricul ture, the alliance relationship between agribusinesses and farmers is similar to the relationship between manufacturers and suppliers [63]. Therefore, big data analytics technology is an appropriate and useful tool for alliance relationship management between agribusinesses and farmers [39,62,71].

## 2.3. Contract farming and alliance relationship stability

Contract farming is an agricultural alliance form of vertical coordi nation [63] that aims to respond to the demand for high-quality prod ucts and grapple with various market imperfections, such as information asymmetry, high transaction costs, and missing markets [40]. In recent years, contract farming has become a common and growing trend in developing countries [25], as well as in developed countries and regions [54]. Contract farming is defined as an institution for the production and supply of agricultural commodities under forward contracts [51]. Such contracts are signed by farmers and agribusinesses and specify that the farmer produces a certain amount of agricultural products on his or her land for the company under some predetermined conditions, such as production processes and output prices [73]. Contract farming allows enterprises and farmers to form a mutually-beneficial community based on shared interests [54], and can significantly reduce the high trans action risks and costs of the spot market [40]. Such alliance also makes it easier for farmers to access key inputs, markets, credit, technical assis tance, as well as to reduce price risk [52]. Moreover, agribusinesses can obtain the goods they need at the specified level of quality, in the desired quantity, and at a fair price within the expected timeframe without owning farms [73].

Nevertheless, low ARS has been encountered in implementing con tract farming despite its benefits [19,58]. ARS describes the state and tendency of members to maintain existing alliance relationships [20, 43]. In this study, ARS emphasizes the stable, long-term, and secure alliance relationship between agriculture firms and farmers [72]. Scholars have conducted research on the antecedents of ARS, mainly by considering factors related to contract characteristics [52], partner characteristics [19], and relationship characteristics [27,58]. However, few studies have explored the impact of emerging technologies, such as big data analytics, on the relationship stability of alliance from a tech nical perspective [41,50,54].

## 2.4. Risk management capabilities

Risk management capability requires special attention in agriculture because agriculture is inherently highly risky and faces unpredictable impacts [21]. Unexpected events associated with, for example, eco nomic fluctuations, climate change, and operations management, have become increasingly more frequent and severe [3,41], which also have significant impacts on agriculture, and change the nature of agricultural risk [38]. If an agribusiness wants to achieve stable development in such a rapidly changing and risky environment, it needs to focus on risk management and improve its risk management capability [5]. Risk management capability is considered a key factor in achieving organi zational goals and creating wealth [50]. It is conducive to improving the accuracy of expectations and decisions, achieving organizational goals, ensuring value appreciation, improving corporate governance effi ciency, and stabilizing company earnings [4].

In this study, we focus on the production risk management capability (PRM) and market risk management capability (MRM), because pro duction and market risks have become the predominant risks faced by farmers and agribusinesses in contract alliance [5,21,41]. Production risk originates from the uncertainty of the natural growth process [41], including any events that lead to variability in yields, such as weather and climate, pests, and diseases [21]. Market risk describes the risk related to monetizing the foregoing output and mainly focuses on the uncertainty of prices, costs, and market access [5]. They can arise from changes in output, the price volatility of raw materials, and asymmetric access to information, as well as international trade, liberalization, and protectionism [41]. Therefore, PRM and MRM are defined as the capa bility of an organization to systematically identify, analyze, and respond to, thereby effectively eliminate, mitigate, and control production and market risks, respectively [41,50]. Alliance is a risk strategy per se, so risk management is an aspect that cannot be ignored in an alliance [20]. However, only a few studies have investigated risk management in alliance contexts [35,73]. To expand the existing research, this article further explores the influence of organizations’ risk management capa bilities on ARS.

## 2.5. Data quality

DO, as one of the core research topics in the field of big data, has attracted considerable attention from scholars [1]. The concept of DQ revolves around the characteristics of big data. DQ is assessed based on its completeness, accuracy, format, currency, reliability, relevancy, representation, accessibility, and ease of treatability [18,28,60]. In this study, we follow the traditional definition of DQ derived from Wixom and Todd [70] and describe DQ as a combination of completeness, ac curacy. format. and currency of the information. Completeness refers to the degree to which all necessary information is provided [60]. The term accuracy refers to the user’s belief that information is correct [28]. The term format refers to the user’s perception of the quality of information presented [70], while timeliness refers to how up-to-date information is [18]. Data with these four characteristics can be considered high quality and can be used to extract competitive value from big data analytics technology for agribusinesses [18]. Previous research has shown that DQ significantly affects an organization’s IT usage and the accompa nying digital decisions [60]. Companies have difficulty extracting competitive value from big data analysis technology without high-quality data [26]. Big data analytics technology should, therefore, consider DQ as a condition to create advantages for organizations.

## 3. Theoretical model and research hypotheses

## 3.1. Big data analytics usage and the alliance relationship stability

Big data analytics technology is playing an increasingly significant role in enhancing the relationships among supply-chain partners $^ { [ 3 9 , 6 2 , }$ 71]. Big data analytics technology strengthens information sharing and exchange between agribusinesses and farmers [28]. It provides clearer and more accurate information for firms and farmers, which helps them understand each other’s plans and actions [62]. This is conducive to achieving close and effective collaboration, increasing the relationship satisfaction of both alliance parties and their willingness to continue the relationship [39,62,71]. Conversely, inadequate and asymmetric infor mation impairs the integration of resources and coordination within the agricultural supply chain [59]. This may damage the efficiency and relationship quality of alliance, limit the ability of members to meet the alliance requirements, and subsequently increase the likelihood of the contract breaking. Furthermore, the purchase and use of big data ana lytics incur considerable costs for agribusinesses. Big data analytics technology is a special asset for agribusinesses investing in their agri cultural production and operation. According to Yeung et al. [74], in vestment in specific assets strengthens and helps maintain a partnership. This helps increase the willingness of agribusinesses and farmers to maintain long-term alliance relationships. Therefore, the following hy pothesis is proposed:

H1:BDU improves the ARS of contract farming.

## 3.2. BDU and risk management capabilities

As a basic industry, agriculture is greatly affected by the natural environment [5]. Natural disasters, such as extreme weather, pests, and diseases, as well as geological disasters, can significantly disrupt agri cultural production [41]. On the production side, agricultural big data include information about, for example, weather, hydrology, soils, crop conditions, livestock and poultry growth, pests and diseases, and animal epidemics [71]. Through multi-source data fusion, efficient and real-time information processing, big data analytics technology can monitor agricultural conditions in a relatively timely and accurate manner [71]. Such advantage helps guide planting and breeding accu rately, assists agribusinesses in making production and operation de cisions [29], and thereby strengthens their ability to manage production risks. BDU promotes the construction of agricultural informatization, promotes the transformation of extensive agricultural production and operation into refined and intensive operation, and improves the informatization and intelligence level of the production process [39,76]. While controlling the uncertainty of production and operation, BDU also improves the ability of agricultural business entities to manage pro duction risks. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

## H2: BDU improves the PRM of agribusinesses.

In the process of agricultural production and operation, uncertainty is caused by various economic, social, and political factors, and changes in these factors eventually affect the market environment [41]. In con tract farming, market risks have not disappeared due to the cooperation of agribusinesses and farmers; instead, market risks have been shared by them [51]. Big data analytics technology can help agricultural com panies obtain more effective decision support on the market [17,67]. By analyzing crop and livestock growth information, past transaction and price data, and consumers’ consumption habits, agricultural companies can estimate the output and quality of agricultural products, make predictions on market prices, and adjust the direction of agricultural sales [48]. Thus, market risks, such as price fluctuations and imbalances between supply and demand can be effectively controlled, and MRM can be significantly improved [48,71]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3: BDU improves the MRM of agribusinesses.

## 3.3. Risk management capabilities and the alliance relationship stability

In contract farming, agribusinesses and farmers constitute an alli ance that shares risks and benefits [24]. However, agricultural risks are not removed from the alliance: instead. they are transferred and distributed between the two parties [51]. After the contract is signed, if the risk exceeds the ability of one party, this party is likely to default [73]. For agribusinesses, failure of the agricultural products provided by farmers to meet the quality and quantity stipulated in the contract af fects processing and sales. Agribusinesses are likely to choose to breach the contract with farmers to maintain their own economic benefits [73].

The losses caused by the occurrence of risks in the previous period in crease the risk expectations of agribusinesses in future transactions with farmers and reduce their willingness to continue contracts. If the PRM of agribusinesses is improved, the threats caused by production risks can be reduced, and the loss caused by production risk can be minimized or eliminated [35]. Agribusinesses might feel more satisfied with their alliance relationship with farmers in the current period, reduce their tendency to default and be more willing to continue the alliance rela tionship in the next period. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H4: The PRM of agribusinesses has a positive impact on the ARS of contract farming.

The formation of an agricultural alliance is conducive to the for mation of scale effect. However, it also increases the impact of market risks. When market volatility occurs, alliance members would be hit harder. [41], and the party whose interests are more damaged may breach the contract to protect its own interests [54]. In contract farming, it is often that a single agricultural enterprise sign with many farmers, so the agricultural enterprise bears greater market risks. When faced with market risks, if the agricultural enterprise’s MRM is weak, it will likely choose to quit alliance to protect its own interests [73]. Furthermore, agribusinesses might find that the risks of cooperating with farmers are hard to control in the future and their profitability would suffer; hence, they would be unwilling to continue the alliance relationship in the next period. If agribusinesses can withstand and respond to the market risks they face and have advanced MRM, they will be less likely to break their cooperation with farmers [52]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5: The MRM of agribusinesses has a positive impact on the ARS of contract farming.

## 3.4. The moderating effect of data quality

All aspects of agricultural production are high-risk [41]. Many fac tors can cause agricultural production risks, and some of them, such as biological disease, occur suddenly without much warning [5]. Although big data analytics technology collects and processes a large amount and diverse data about production risks, organizations definitely cannot obtain meaningful and applicative insights from incomplete, incorrect, presented in a variety of forms, or even out of date data in a low-cost manner [18,61]. Unruly data add difficulty and complexity to identify, analyze, and respond so that effectively control production risks [26]. Hence, the use of low-quality data hinders the improvement of the PRM enabled by big data analytics technology, and the following hypothesis is proposed:

## H6: DQ moderates (strengthens) the positive effect of BDU on PRM.

Similar to production risk, the management of market risk requires following certain rules [5]. Through the data analysis of past produc tion, prices, and transactions, market trends can be predicted to a certain extent [41]. However, the prerequisite for accurately assessing market risks is access to complete, accurate, formal, and current data [26]. As the quality of big data increases, it is more likely to provide accurate and relevant information about market risks and help identify, evaluate, and respond to these risks quickly, thereby assisting in avoiding or mini mizing the associated losses [29]. However, if the market data is not accurate, complete, timely, and not presented in a logical format, the storage, analysis, and visualization of these data by big data analytics technology would become a useless effort. It is impossible to say that big data analytics improve the management of market risk. [69]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H7:DQ moderates (strengthens) the positive effect of BDU on MRM.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Scale development

Since BDU, PRM, MRM, DQ, and ARS are latent variables that cannot be directly observed, it is necessary to design observation indicators to measure them. In the current study, to ensure that each measurement item has good content validity, all measurement items are adapted from the existing literature in the context of agribusinesses using big data analytics technology. The variable of BDU was adopted from the work of Yunis et al. [75], while PRM and MRM were adopted from the work of Mu et al. [50], Komarek et al. [41] and Behzadi et al. [5]. DQ was adapted from the work of Corte Real et al. [18], and ARS was adapted from the research of Yang et al. [72]. Appendix A shows the measure ment items for each variable and the corresponding references.

In this study, data were collected using a questionnaire survey to conduct empirical testing and analysis of the proposed research model. The questionnaire comprises two parts: (1) the basic characteristics of the sampled companies, such as their size and how long they have been using big data analytics technology and (2) five structural variables and their measurement items, measured using a 5-point Likert scale. A pilot test was conducted before the formal investigation. The preliminary questionnaire was distributed to 102 agricultural managers who participated in a training class, and 75 valid questionnaires were completed. After analyzing the data, the questionnaire was modified to increase the scales’ reliability and validity, and the final version was then utilized.

## 4.2. Data collection

China’s provincial-level agribusinesses were selected for the study because they play critical roles in promoting agricultural allied devel opment, and are the leaders of agriculture digital transformation [42]. In China, provincial-level agribusinesses maintain close ties with farmers. They cooperate with farmers in building standardized raw-material bases through, for example, order contracts and share cooperation, and have established a close interest-linkage mechanism. First, with the assistance of the Department of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of China, we obtained basic contact information (e.g., telephone numbers, email addresses, and physical address) of the 3,064 provincial-level agribusi nesses located across the country. Next, a web link of an online ques tionnaire was sent to each of these enterprises by email. The online questionnaire was generated through Wenjuanxing’s website (https://www.wjx.cn). Ultimately, 303 agribusinesses agreed to take the survey. Within 1 week after the questionnaire was sent out, these companies were reminded via telephone calls to fill out the question naire. A monetary reward was provided to each participant after the questionnaire was completed. The formal investigation began in June 2019, and a total of 281 questionnaires were collected within 3 months. Questionnaires were considered invalid if respondents (a) scanned the questionnaire in less than 3 minutes, (b) provided illogical answers to questions (e.g., all the answers are the same), or (c) were from com panies that had not used big data analytics technology. After excluding 37 invalid responses, 244 valid questionnaires were obtained. In terms of the regions where the agribusinesses interviewed are located (as shown in Appendix B), they are mainly concentrated in provinces and cities with greater economic development, in the eastern region of China. Basic information about the sampled companies and question naire respondents is shown in Tables 1 and 2.

We made our best effort to prevent the appearance of common method bias (CMB). First, we took some measures to minimize CMB at the data collection stage. We provided a detailed introduction at the beginning of the questionnaire, explaining the conceptualization of BDU in the questionnaire. The items were not assigned to any specific construct and were randomly organized in the questionnaire. Also, de mographic questions were randomly inserted between the question items. Second, we employed the marker variable technique to assess the potential effect of CMB on the empirical analysis [45]. The variable of gender was chosen as the marker variable because it is theoretically unrelated to the endogenous variables. The average correlation between the marker variable and the other variables was only 0.04, smaller than 0.100, indicating that CMB is not a serious problem.

## 5.2. Test of the structural model

The hypotheses were tested using SmartPLS software, and the result are shown in Fig. 2. BDU had a significant positive impact on ARS (β=0.208, p<0.01), PRM (β=0.444, p<0.001), and MRM (β=0.544, p<0.001). MRM had a significant positive effect on ARS (β=0.172, p<0.05), while production risk ability had no significant effect on ARS (β=0.102, t=1.642). DQ played a positive role in moderating the rela tionship between BDU and MRM (β=0.144, p<0.05), but it did not have significant moderating effects in the relationship between BDU and PRM (β= 0.071, t=1.166). Therefore, hypotheses H1, H2, H3, H5, and H7 were supported, while H4 and H6 were not. The variance explanation rates of PRM, MRM, and ARS were 19.7%, 29.6%, and 15.4%, respectively.

## 5.3. Mediating effects analysis

Bootstrapping analysis [68] was implemented to examine the mediating effect of MRM. A bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 sam ples was used to construct bias-corrected confidence intervals for indi rect effects. As shown in Table $^ { 6 , }$ the indirect effects of BDU (β=0.049) had 95% confidence intervals that did not include zero [lower level of confidence interval (LLCI) =0.009, upper level of confidence interval (ULCI) =0.100]. By adding the significant direct effects after controlling for MRM, the findings show that MRM partially mediates the relation ship between BDU and ARS.

## 5.4. In-depth moderation analysis

DQ moderates the effects of BDU on MRM, as indicated by the sig nificant interaction terms in the structural model. To demonstrate this moderating effect in more detail, the Interaction software package (www.danielsoper.com/Interaction) was used to plot it, with all the predictors standardized. The diagram in Fig. 3 demonstrates the positive relationship between BDU (x) and MRM (y). The slope becomes more positive when the level of DQ is higher (e.g., the line for +3 SD). In contrast, it becomes less positive (e.g., the line for –1 SD) or switches to negative (the line for –3 SD) when the level of DQ is lower. Except for the slopes of the lines with –2 and –3 standard deviations, all the slopes are significant $( \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ , which is further evidence that the proposed positive effect of BDU on MRM is significant. However, it weakens as DQ decreases. When the level of DO is below -1 standard deviation of the average, BDU is no longer a significant positive determinant of MRM.

## 6. Discussion

Our research reveals several significant findings. First, BDU can improve the risk management capabilities of agribusinesses. Previous research has found big data analytics technology can provide effective early warning and preparedness, as well as help develop proper response strategies for potential risks [55]. According to our results, agribusi nesses can improve their PRM using big data analytics technology. Agricultural production and management face considerable production risks [41]. Big data analytics technology plays a significant role in the meteorological disaster, disease, and pest identification, as well as agricultural product output estimation, agricultural product safety, and quality testing. It also helps agribusinesses prevent and reduce produc tion risks and improve their PRM. Besides, using big data analytics technology, agribusinesses can improve their MRM. Through BDU, ag ribusinesses can obtain more market-side historical data and forecast data. By processing and analyzing these data, agribusinesses can accurately and timely forecast market supply and demand, reduce and response to the market uncertainty.

Second, BDU can significantly improve the ARS of contract farming. In the current digital economy, big data analytics technology support better operational decisions and operational efficiency by collecting, mining, analyzing, and transmitting data [29]. By making better busi ness decisions and more accurately predicting future business situations, agribusinesses can increase the loyalty of their partners, such as farmers, and maintain the relationship stability of the alliance. In addition, the results of the mediating effect test show that MRM has a partial medi ating effect between BDU and the ARS. This shows that BDU, directly and indirectly, affects ARS. These results expand and thicken the posi tive effects of digital technology on alliances [44,56,65], and big data analytics technology on supply chains management [12,32,36,66] found in previous studies.

Third, though MRM exerts a significant influence on ARS, PRM does not have a significant impact on ARS. A possible explanation is that agribusinesses are generally at the processing and sales end of the in dustrial chain, while farmers are at the production end [73]. Thus, farmers are the initial victims of production risks. Improving the PRM of agribusinesses has minimal effects on farmers’ resistance to production risks. Furthermore, although agribusinesses can disseminate production risk information to farmers so that farmers can prevent and control them on the production side, this process also involves reciprocal communi cation and information-sharing between agribusinesses and farmers [52]. Farmers also need to effectively understand this risk information and apply it to agricultural production. Otherwise, the production risks in contract farming cannot be effectively controlled, and the alliance relationship remains fragile.

Finally, DQ plays a positive role in moderating the relationship be tween BDU and MRM. Nevertheless, DQ had no significant moderating effect on the relationship between BDU and PRM. A possible explanation is that the influence of big data analytics technology on improving the quality of organizational decision-making and bringing value or competitive advantage depends not only on the data itself but also on the organization’s big data analytic capabilities [37]. In addition to DQ, the maturity of data analysis tools and the analytical knowledge and skills of employees affect the role and value of big data analytics technology for organizations [64]. The production risks faced by the agricultural in dustry are more complex, changeable, and uncontrollable than market risks, requiring a higher level of big data analytic capabilities [21]. As a result, DQ cannot be a sufficient situational condition for big data ana lytics technology to affect PRMs.

## 7. Theoretical and managerial implications

## 7.1. Theoretical implications

This study makes several theoretical contributions. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explain theoretically and examine empirically the ARS in the IS field. Prior IS research on IT, and alliance has mainly focused on the effects of IT on alliance formation or value creation [44,56,65], but it remains unknown how digital tech nology can help maintain the stable relationship among alliance part ners. We contribute to this literature by uncovering the mechanism of how the use of big data analytics technology can both, directly and indirectly, increase the ARS via the mediating role of risk management capability, and the moderating role of DQ.

Second, our study provides a new technology standpoint for existing discussion on ARS. Prior literature has mainly considered characteristics of contract, partner, and relationship as the influencing factors of ARS [19,27,52,58]. However, our study contributes to alliance literature by explaining the big data analytics-enabled direct and sequential effect through risk management capability on ARS.

Finally, digital technology brings significant changes in agriculture and enables new possibilities for the discipline of agricultural economic management [42]. Our study contributes to multidisciplinary research development in IS field by revealing how agribusinesses can utilize digital technology to improve their collaboration with partners, realize digital transformation, and reaps competitive advantages from it.

## 7.2. Managerial implications

Through an empirical analysis of the theoretical model, some im plications for management were derived. First, companies can achieve competitive advantages through the use of big data analytics technolo gies, including building up risk management capabilities and improving the relationship stability of alliance. Second, in addition to strength ening the utilization of big data analytics technology, organizations should obtain as high-quality data as possible. When applying big data analytics technology, they should pay attention to the DQ and identify the most useful data to solve the problem. Given DQ does not play a role in improving the PRM, organizations also need to strengthen their re serves of technical talent and improve the construction of IT infra structure so that big data analytics technology can play greater roles. Third, promoting the adoption of big data analytics technology is beneficial for developing modern industries (i.e., modern agriculture). Relevant government agencies can encourage the opening and sharing of big data from various sources, the construction of big data-related infrastructure, and the big data talent-system cultivation to unleash the great potential of big data analytics technology in industry modernization.

## 8. Limitations and future research

Although the present study produced meaningful findings, it also has some shortcomings. First, this research examines the impact mechanism of BDU on the ARS of contract farming. Future research can expand the scope of the research objects and examine the influence of big data analytics technology on different ARSs, such as cooperatives alliance, community-supported agriculture. Second, the data are cross-sectional, but the relationships between alliance members are dynamic. Future research can collect longitudinal data for dynamic comparison and analysis. Third, the empirical data in this article are all from Chinese agricultural enterprises, which may limit the universality of our research findings to some extent. China has a unique cultural and economic sit uation, and Chinese agricultural companies possess idiosyncratic prod ucts, business, and alliance management (for example, compared with industrial firms). By collecting data from other countries or industries, future research can expand the findings of this research.
