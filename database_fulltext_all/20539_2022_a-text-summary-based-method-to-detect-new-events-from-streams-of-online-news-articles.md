## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: New event detection Online event detection Text summarization Text summary-based method Environmental surveillance

## A B S T R A C T

New event detection (NED), which is crucial to firms’ environmental surveillance, requires timely access to and effective analysis of live streams of news articles from various online sources. These news articles, available in unprecedent frequency and quantity, are difficult to sift through manually. Most of existing techniques for NED are full-text-based; typically, they perform full-text analysis to measure the similarity between a new article and previous articles. This full-text-based approach is potentially ineffective. because a news article often contains sentences that are less relevant to define the focal event being reported and the inclusion of these less relevant sentences into the similarity estimation can impair the effectiveness of NED. To address the limitation of the fulltext-based approach and support NED more effectively and efficiently, this study proposes and develops a summary-based event detection method that first selects relevant sentences of each article as a summary, then uses the resulting summaries to detect new events. We empirically evaluate our proposed method in comparison with some prevalent full-text-based techniques, including a vector space model and two deep-learning-based models. Our evaluation results confirm that the proposed method provides greater utilities for detecting new events from online news articles. This study demonstrates the value and feasibility of the text summarization approach for generating news article summaries for detecting new events from live streams of online news ar ticles. proposes a new method more effective and efficient than the benchmark techniques, and contributes to NED research in several important ways.

## 1. Introduction

Central to new event detection (NED) in the online environment is processing streams of news articles effectively and efficiently to identify the onset of new events [1,2]. Such detection is crucial to firms’ envi ronmental surveillance [3], and could significantly affect their perfor mance and competitiveness [4,5]. In the perception-action chain, NED (or also called online event detection) represents essential inputs that enable firms to adapt to changes in the environment and develop proper responses in an effective, timely manner [6,7]. Firms perform NED to keep abreast of changing market conditions, so that, they better antici pate emerging trends as an essential element of their business planning, strategy formulation, and decision-making [8–10]. Prior studies [11–13] have shown that high-performing firms tend to monitor the environment more frequently and thoroughly than less-performing ones.

The importance of NED has increased drastically with the intensi fying market competition and globalization, coupled with ubiquitous prevalence of the Internet as a source of news articles. To cope with the fast-changing business environment, firms must analyze staggering volumes of news articles concerning a wide range of topics [8,9] in less time, which requires sufficient knowledge and appropriate representa tion schemes to identify new events [8]. Manually detecting new events at their onset is especially challenging, because different institutions and websites disseminate many articles about each new event and its development [6,14], and firms must sift through all of them [2,15,16]. For example, Google News aggregates news from more than 4500 sources that publish an enormous quantity of articles, multiple times a day; NewsNow disseminates hundreds of newswires every few minutes; MSNBC features all the contents produced by the entire NBC News family; and Apple News+ houses hundreds of newspapers and maga zines. Non-syndicated websites, general or industry-specific, publish their own news articles at a high frequency too. The sheer volume and stringent response time requirements associated with NED make any manual approach ineffective, if even feasible, which indicates the need for automated methods to detect previously unforeseen events from streams of online news articles [17–19].

Most of existing techniques that support automated NED from online articles are full-text-based [1,2,15,17,18,20–26]. That is, they examine the full text of news articles, transform each news article into a feature vector, then assess the similarity between a new news article and pre vious ones according to their corresponding feature vectors, which are subject to the curse of dimensionality [24]. To filter out non-representative features, existing NED techniques generally perform feature selection to reduce the size of the feature space. Such feature selection process can potentially improve the effectiveness of NED by suppressing potential biases embedded in the original, untrimmed set of features. However, the utility of feature selection may be constrained when a news article contains (many) sentences less relevant to define the focal event being reported [6,27–29]. That is, because existing NED techniques rely on a full-text analysis, even with the use of feature se lection to potentially excluding non-representative features, these full-text-based techniques still are likely to encompass features from less relevant sentences. The inclusion of these features, which should be considered non-representative, into the corresponding feature vectors of news articles will risk biased similarity comparisons between new and previous articles, which in turn can impair their effectiveness for iden tifying new events.

To address these limitations of full-text-based techniques for NED, we propose a novel approach that incorporates an information filtering mechanism at the sentence level into the NED process. Through this sentence-level information filtering, in conjunction with a subsequent feature selection method (operating at the feature level), our proposed approach is capable of improving the effectiveness of NED. Specifically, we develop a summary-based event detection (SED) method that first selects essential, relevant sentences from each article, new or previously seen, as a summary. With the resulting summaries, it then determines whether a newly released article pertains to a new event. Text summa rization essentially involves a reductive transformation of a textual document to a summary through content reduction by extracting what is important in each document [30]. The use of summaries to detect new events promises greater effectiveness and efficiency, because it reduces the dimensionality of the vector space common to the full-text analysis and removes less relevant sentences that can create noises and biases in article comparisons.

Our method offers several novelties and thus research contributions in relation to existing NED techniques. First, our study proposes a novel approach that incorporates a sentence-level information filtering mechanism to address the limitation of existing full-text-based NED techniques. Specifically, instead of analyzing the entire document and using a feature selection method to determine representative features, we follow the text summarization approach to first identify relevant versus less relevant sentences in the document, select representative features from the relevant sentences, and use these features to compare the similarity of different documents. As a result, we filter the noises by removing less relevant sentences and thus will not only improve the effectiveness of NED, but also increase computational efficiency. Sec ond, by summarizing news articles and determining whether they pertain to new events, organizations and individuals also can use the summaries to understand the content of each news article rather than reading its entire text. Moreover, event summarization, or summarizing articles pertinent to a specific event, can benefit from the summaries produced by our method too, because it can focus on the identified relevant sentences without any further processing. In developing and evaluating our proposed SED method, we seek to address two funda mental questions:

1 Can the use of a text summarization model to generate article sum maries provide a viable approach for automated NED from streams of online news articles?

2 Is the proposed summary-based method more effective and efficient for NED than prevalent full-text-based techniques?

## 2. Literature review

Several streams of research are closely related to our study: NED, automated NED techniques, and text summarization techniques. In this section, we will review representative studies and techniques in each stream and identify the gaps that motivate the current research.

## 2.1. New event detection

NED identifies the onset of new events by analyzing the stories in streams of news articles as they become available. Various events get reported in news articles that are published at an accelerating pace, so that, many firms increasingly struggle to identify emerging events, monitor their developments over time, and respond appropriately [31, 32]. Such efforts are central for firms to stay abreast of important changes in the business environment. According to Liu, Shih, Liau and Lai [3], “As the business environment becomes increasingly compli cated, capturing environmental changes and being sensitive to the business environment is crucial to success in business” (p. 983).

Online news articles are now available in enormous quantities and accelerating frequencies, so that, the ability to detect new events from them can overwhelm human cognitive processing capacities. Simon [33] indicates that “what information consumes is rather obvious: it consumes the attention of its recipients” (p. 40). A wealth of information in effect creates a poverty of attention. To process (consume) important information, both individuals and organizations must find ways to allocate their finite attention and processing capacity adequately. As the Internet has emerged as the most important medium for information access and dissemination and, automated detection of new events from streams of news articles, at the moment they appear, has attracted a growing attention from researchers and practitioners alike [1,2,6,15, 24–26,34–36].

Furthermore, detecting new events from diverse, rich online contents also has important business implications. To illustrate, people are more accustomed to sharing their opinions and evaluative experiences (e.g., testimonials, reviews) online [37]. In addition to news articles, firms need to analyze these user-generated contents to detect potential new events related to their products, which could affect their brand or product reputation significantly [38]. Abbasi and Chen [39] call for efforts to develop analytics-enabled methods for effective, automated detection of new events, thus enabling timely identification, summari zation, and visualization of information available from various sources.

## 2.2. NED techniques

The development of existing NED techniques involves three major design issues: how to represent a news article, how to measure the similarity between news articles, and how to identify whether a new news article reports a new event or not. In the following, we review existing NED techniques using this framework.

How to represent a news article. Most of existing NED techniques adopt a vector space model for feature selection and document representation. Specifically, on the basis of the full text of news articles, they first select representative features using a feature selection metric and then use the selected features to represent each news article as a feature vector ac cording to a document representation scheme of choice. Existing NED techniques that follow the vector space model differ in their feature selection metrics and document representation schemes. For instance, Allan, Papka and Lavrenko [2] employ a term frequency × inverse document frequency (TF × IDF)-like scheme for feature selection and document representation, in which the IDF is estimated from an auxil iary collection, independent of the stream of articles. Yang, Carbonell, Brown, Pierce, Archibald and Liu [1] propose a single-pass incremental clustering algorithm (INCR) for NED, where INCR uses an incremental IDF scheme to cope with the continual arrivals (availability) of docu ments over time, which can affect both term weighting and vector normalization. As an alternative, some previous research exploits the event property-based model for NED. To address the noise problem common to the vector space model, Wei and Lee [6] propose the NEED technique that combines information extraction and text categorization techniques for NED. Specifically, NEED employs a supervised learning classifier (e. g., C4.5, CN2) to identify the most likely event topic (e.g., examples of event topics include merger and acquisition, new product introduction) to which a newly available article belongs. Subsequently, information extraction is performed to extract relevant event properties from the focal news article, according to the corresponding event topic template and information extraction rules (or models) developed for that event topic. The subsequent NED process is then limited to the extracted relevant event properties of a new article and those of previous articles that share the same event topic as that of the new article.

The third model to NED applies deep-learning methods for document representation in order to alleviate the problems of traditional TF × IDF representation that include the curse of dimensionality and feature in dependence assumption. For instance, Hu, Zhang, Hou and Li [24] use a large, independent news corpus to first train word embeddings. As a result, each word in a news article is transformed into a vector in a multidimensional space. Consequently, they perform word clustering (using the k-means clustering algorithm) to cluster words (according to their word vectors) referring to the same or similar meaning to obtain a latent semantic space. Subsequently, document vectorization is per formed. That is, each word in a news article is replaced with its corre sponding word cluster index and an article is represented by a vector of word clusters.

How to measure the similarity between news articles. Once the repre sentation of a news article is obtained (e.g., a vector of representative features), a similarity measure $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ , cosine similarity) is adopted to measure the content similarity of two news articles. In addition to content similarity, most of prior studies also consider the temporal proximity of news articles because events tend to appear in news bursts [2,34]. News articles about an event typically exhibit a loosely defined life cycle: Many articles appear in the beginning (close temporal prox imity), which ceases quickly (e.g., fading away in a matter of days). That is, articles appearing in adjacent temporal proximity are more likely to pertain to the same event than those appearing further apart in time. This temporal characteristic suggests the use of a combined measure of content similarity and temporal proximity as a criterion for event detection [2,34]. Moreover, a time gap between bursts of topically similar news articles often indicates that they discuss different events. Hence, some prior studies also incorporate a time window for event scoping when measuring the similarity between two news articles [2, 34]. For example, INCR [1] adopts a time penalty function when esti mating the similarity of a new news article x and an existing event c (consisting of one or more previous news articles). The evolution of different event topics may exhibit distinct temporal patterns [21]. To illustrate, news articles about a new product intro duction event likely fade out relatively quickly, whereas those about a business merger or acquisition event persist far longer. Techniques that adopt a definite time window to determine whether a new news article reports a new event or a known event thus might not be effective across different event types, especially those spanning a longer time horizon. To model the life cycle of an event, Chen, Chen and Chen [21] develop an energy-based algorithm that incorporates a self-adaptive event life cycle management mechanism premised in the aging theory.

How to identify whether a new news article reports a new event or not. The third design issue of NED techniques is about their detection algo rithms. To cope with the sequential availability of news articles, existing NED techniques (including INCR) generally leverage a single-pass clustering algorithm to detect new events [1,2,15,17,20,21,24]. Unlike traditional clustering algorithms that partition a set of documents at the same time, single-pass clustering algorithms, also known as incremental clustering algorithms [2], assume that source documents are input in sequence and assign them to appropriate clusters, one document at a time [41]. Typically, single-pass clustering algorithm begins using the first news article $( d _ { 1 } ) _ { : }$ , in a sequence of articles, as the centroid of an initial cluster $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ , event) $c _ { 1 } .$ . When a new article $d _ { i }$ arrives, the similarity $s _ { i j }$ between d and each cluster c is calculated. If the maximum similarity among $s _ { i j }$ for all clusters i exceeds a prespecified threshold, article $d _ { i }$ is assigned to an existing cluster that attains the maximum s $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ d is regarded as belonging to an existing event); otherwise, a new cluster is created with $d _ { i }$ being its centroid $( \mathrm { i . e . , } d _ { i }$ is identified as a new event).

Prior studies also propose other clustering algorithms for event detection, but for a context different from NED. Specifically, they target at supporting retrospective event detection, which aims to discover events from a chronologically ordered accumulation of news articles, by segmenting these news articles into clusters $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , events). To take into account the characteristic that events tend to appear in news bursts, the clustering algorithms for retrospective event detection generally follow three phases [2,34]. The first phase is bucketing, which divides the chronologically ordered news articles into non-overlapping and consecutive buckets of fixed size. Subsequently, the second phase is to partition the news articles in each bucket into clusters (events). Some prior studies [2,34] employ a group average clustering (GAC) algorithm, whereas other studies [2,34] use the single-pass clustering algorithm for this phase. The third phase is to merge similar clusters (events) in different buckets to address the problem that an event may straddle the initial temporal-bucket boundaries.

## 2.3. Text summarization techniques

According to Jones [30], text summarization refers to “a reductive transformation of source text to summary text through content reduc tion by selection and/or generalization on what is important in the source” (p. 1). By selecting from the full text of a document important sentences that jointly serve as a summary, it helps alleviate the stringent time and computational processing requirements and thus enables an alyses of enormous quantities of news articles for timely event detection. Luhn [42] pioneers automated text summarization research, using sta tistical techniques anchored in term frequency and term normalization. Since then, different techniques have been developed, often using various features to determine the importance of each sentence in a document.

Existing techniques typically rely on linear functions, naïve Bayes, decision tree induction, or decision rule induction. Linear functions determine the overall weight of a sentence in a document according to a linear function of selected features. Sentences with an overall weight greater than a prespecified threshold or those k sentences with the greatest weights are selected and concatenated to create a summary of the document [43,44]. The use of naïve Bayes for text summarization is also prevalent. Given h features $f _ { 1 } , f _ { 2 } , . . . ,$ and $f _ { h } ,$ the probability that a sentence is included in a summary can be calculated according to Bayes theorem. The sentences with the highest probabilities then produce a summary of the document [45–48]. Decision tree induction methods employ a supervised learning algorithm to construct a decision tree from a set of training instances [49,50]. Furthermore, decision rule induction methods iteratively generate decision rules from a set of training in stances, where each rule consists of a conjunction of conditions (e.g., feature $f _ { i } { = } 0 \mathrm { A N D }$ feature $f _ { j } > 2 0 )$ as its antecedent and a specific class as its conclusion. The goal of decision rule induction is to seek a compact, “covering” rule set that can cover all or the maximum number of training instances [51,52].

## 2.4. Gap analysis

Our literature review indicates that existing NED techniques, no matter whether they take the vector space model, event property-based model, or deep-learning methods for news article representation, rely on the full text of news articles for NED. As we mentioned previously, not all sentences in a news article are equally important to define the event being reported in the news article. The inclusion of less relevant sen tences into the NED process would introduce noises and biases, which will undermine the detection effectiveness. Thus, the goal of this research is to propose a summary-based NED method to address the limitation of existing techniques. Through excluding less relevant sen tences in news articles, our proposed summary-based method can reduce noises and biases and thus is capable of improving NED effectiveness. In addition to effectiveness gains, the proposed summary-based method compares summaries of news articles rather than analyzing their full text, it reduces the processing requirements and hence can identify new events more efficiently.

The vector space model for document representation is commonly adopted by existing NED techniques, due to its simplicity and extensi bility. On the other hand, the event property-based model [6] directs the similarity analysis between news articles to the core properties essential to a specific event topic, which is less influenced by noises and biases in the content of news articles. However, the event property-based model involves a pipeline process of three tasks, including event topic classi fication, information extraction, and then NED. Thus, error propagation from previous tasks in the pipeline will propagate to later tasks and thus would hinder the effectiveness of this model. Moreover, the event property-based model is best suited to the scenario where the event topics to be surveilled can be predefined, so that, an event topic classi fication model, event topic templates, and an information extraction rules (or models) for each event topic can be constructed beforehand. Due to this requirement, the lack of extensibility with respect to new event topics to be incorporated represents a serious limitation of the event property-based model $[ 8 , 3 5 ]$ . Finally, the deep-learning model represents the most recent trend of event detection research, which exploits deep-learning methods to convert a news article (new or pre vious) into a representation in a multidimensional space. A cost asso ciated with this model is that it typically requires a large, independent news corpus to train word embeddings or a language model for docu ment representation. In addition, our literature review suggests that existing deep-learning-based techniques (such as [24]) generally are developed for the retrospective event detection scenario, rather than for NED. Thus, they are not directly applicable to this study.

In this study, we will take the vector space model, due to its simplicity and extensibility, as the underlying scheme for document representation when designing our summary-based NED method. Moreover, because our study targets at NED rather than retrospective event detection, we will adopt an incremental clustering algorithm commonly employed by existing NED techniques for detecting new events from live streams of online news articles. In addition, following the current research trend, we will develop deep-learning-based methods specifically for NED as our performance benchmarks.

## 3. Our proposed summary-based method for NED

Our proposed summary-based event detection (SED) method first develops a text summarization model to identify relevant sentences in each news article and generate a summary of it for the subsequent NED. Our proposed method’s reliance on relevant sentences for NED can improve detection effectiveness, because noise-prone features linked to less relevant sentences are excluded from the similarity analysis be tween a newly available article and already known events (i.e., distinct article clusters). Moreover, by detecting new events from the resulting summaries, instead of the full text, our proposed method can achieve computational efficiency by reducing the feature space. As we illustrate in Fig. 1, the overall SED process consists of three main phases: news article summarization learning, news article summary extraction, and NED. The first phase constructs a summarization model based on a training sample of news articles and their summaries. In the second phase, the summarization model constructed in the previous phase is employed to identify relevant sentences for creating a summary of each news article, new or previous. Finally, the third phase involves a com parison of the summary of a new (incoming) article with those of pre vious articles to determine whether the focal, new article pertains to a new event. We elaborate on each phase in the following subsections.

## 3.1. News article summarization learning

In the first phase, SED takes a training set of news articles and their summaries as inputs to build a summarization model for news articles. In this research, we formulate text summarization as a sentence-level, dichotomous classification task, such that, we classify each sentence in a news article as either relevant (to be included in the summary) or less relevant (not to be included in the summary). The sentences appearing in the summary of each training article serve as the positive training instances (i.e., belong to the “relevant” class), whereas those that do not appear in the summary of each training article are negative training instances (i.e., belong to the “less relevant” class). We design several salient features (attributes), in line with previous research $[ 4 3 , 4 5 - 4 8 ,$ 53], to represent all training instances.

Cue phrase features. Cue phrases in a sentence can be identified by the presence of pragmatic words (e.g., significant, impossible, hardly) [43, $4 5 , 4 6 , 4 8 ]$ . We categorize cue phrases as positive or negative. Accord ingly, we develop the positive and negative cue phrase features. For each training sentence, we consider three alternative representation schemes for each cue phrase feature: top-N, binary, and proportional. With the top-N representation scheme, the top N sentences that have the highest number of positive (or negative) cue phrases in the article is set to 1, and 0 otherwise. With the binary scheme, the value of a positive (or nega tive) cue phrase feature of a sentence is set to 1 if the sentence contains any positive (or negative) cue phrase, and 0 otherwise. With the pro portional scheme, the ratio between the number of positive (or negative) cue phrases in a sentence and the sentence length determines the value of the positive (or negative) cue phrase feature for that sentence.

Thematic word feature. Thematic words signify the theme of a docu ment [42–48], so that, a sentence containing more thematic words should be more relevant. Again, we employ a rule-based, part-of-speech tagger to identify nouns and noun phrases in each training article, because thematic words usually are nouns or noun phrases. Our SED method then employs a standard $\mathrm { T F } \times \mathrm { I D F }$ selection metric to measure the weight of each word in a training article and selects the top $n _ { t w }$ words with the highest (within-document TF) × IDF weights as the thematic words for that article, according to the characteristics of thematic words, such that, they frequently appear in a document but seldom occur in many other documents. Similar to the cue phrase features, we also consider three representation schemes $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \mathrm { t o p } { \cdot } N ,$ , binary, and propor tional) for the thematic word feature to represent each sentence in a training article.

Title word feature. Words in the title of a document tend to circum scribe its subject matter and frequently indicate what the document entails. Therefore, title words offer an essential basis to assess the sig nificance of different sentences in a document $[ 4 3 , 4 6 , 4 7 ]$ ]. As we show in Fig. 2, SED uses a stop word glossary to remove all stop words in the title, then uses the remaining words to create a list of title words for each article. As with the aforementioned features, we also consider the three representation schemes $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , top-N, binary, and proportional) for the title word feature.

Location feature. The location of a sentence in a document can signal its significance too [53]. Previous studies disagree about the relative significance of a sentence’s location within a document [43–47], but they seemingly concur that sentences in paragraphs closer to the beginning or the end of a document tend to be more content-loaded and thus more appropriate to be included in the summary than sentences in other parts of the document. Accordingly, we represent the location feature of a sentence in a news article according to where it appears: in the first (F), middle (M), or last (L) third of the article.

Cohesion feature. Cohesion feature reflects the centrality of a sen tence, in relation to the entire document; it is a measure of the similarity between a sentence and other sentences in the document. In a news article, sentences that are not sufficiently significant to be included in the summary often have low cohesion with the others in the article [44, $^ { 4 7 , 4 8 ] }$ . We use an existing measure developed by Neto, Alexandre, Santos, Kaestner, Alex, Freitas, Nievola and Conceio [48] to assess the cohesion of a sentence s in a news article d: $C O H ( s , d ) = \sum _ { s ^ { \prime } \in d \mathrm { ~ a n d ~ } s ^ { \prime } \neq s } \frac { s i m ( s , s ^ { \prime } ) } { n - 1 } ,$ where n is the number of sentences in d. We consider two representation schemes for this feature: numeric and binary. With the numeric repre sentation scheme, the cohesion feature of each sentence in a training article is represented using its COH value, whereas with the binary representation scheme, the value of the cohesion feature of a sentence is set to 1 if its COH value is greater than the average COH across all sentences in article d, and 0 otherwise.

Sentence length feature. In a document, sentences with only few words tend to be less significant than long sentences and thus are less likely to be included in its summary [45,46]. Specifically, we adopt the binary representation scheme to represent the length feature of each sentence in an article. It equals 1, if the length of the sentence exceeds a pre specified threshold $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ 5 words), and 0 otherwise.

In addition, previous research suggests the advantageous perfor mance of $\mathsf { C 4 . 5 , }$ a decision tree induction algorithm, for constructing a text summarization model, compared with other techniques [44,48]. We therefore apply C4.5 for summarization learning to construct the sum marization model for dichotomous classification; that is, whether a sentence is either relevant or less relevant. As we will discuss in the second phase of SED, to be able to adjust the length of each summary, we incorporate a compression ratio, calculated as the number of sentences in the summary divided by the total number of sentences in the original article. We then extend the prediction mechanism of C4.5 using a Lap lacian accuracy measure [55] to estimate the accuracy of each path in the induced decision tree, calculated as $\frac { n _ { p r e d I n s } + 1 } { n _ { t o t I n s } + n _ { c l a s s } }$ , where $n _ { c l a s s }$ indicates the number of decision classes $( \mathrm { i . e . , } 2$ in this study), $n _ { p r e d I n s }$ is the number of training instances in the predicted class specified by the path, and $n _ { t o t I n s }$ denotes the total number of training instances covered by the path. Accordingly, when a sentence in a news article is covered by a decision path in the summarization model, the Laplacian accuracy of this deci sion path serves as the weight of the focal sentence.

## 3.2. News article summary extraction

The second phase of SED, news article summary extraction, involves the selection of relevant sentences to be included in the summary. An article, new or previously seen, serves as the input to the SED, and its summary is the output that comprises relevant sentences in that article. As detailed in Fig. 3, SED performs two tasks. First, in the sentence representation task, SED uses the features and their representation schemes, determined in the news article summarization learning phase, to represent each sentence in the focal article. Second, SED performs a reasoning task, using the constructed summarization model to deter mine the weight $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , Laplacian accuracy) of each sentence in the focal article and then create a summary. Specifically, each sentence traverses through the decision tree $( \mathrm { i . e . } _ { \cdot }$ , the summarization model), according to its feature values, and the Laplacian accuracy of the path it traverses serves as the sentence’s weight. Subsequently, according to a pre specified compression ratio and the weight of each sentence in a news article, a desirable number of sentences is selected from the news article to form a summary for the article.

## 3.3. New event detection

Fig. 4 depicts the final phase, which determines whether a newly arrived news article pertains to a new or a known event, using an NED technique to compare the summary of the focal news article against those of all previously seen articles. In this study, we use the INCR for NED, mainly because it can adapt to live streams of online news articles and is easy to implement without the requirement of an independent, large news corpus to estimate the IDF of each feature (term) in newly available articles. We preprocess news article summaries, new and previously seen, to extract and select the representative features and then use them to represent each summary as a feature vector with an adequate representation scheme, before identifying whether the new article pertains to a new event or otherwise.

For feature extraction and selection, we use a rule-based, part-ofspeech tagger [54,56], a noun phrase parser [57], and the TF × IDF feature selection to extract and select representative features from the summaries of previous articles. After we have selected a set of k repre sentative features, the summary of a newly available article and those of the previous articles are represented with an incremental TF × IDF representation scheme. Next, the feature vector for the summary of the new article is compared with that for the summary of each previous article, using the similarity measure of INCR that combines lexical similarity and temporal proximity. For generalizability, we modify the similarity function of INCR by changing the time window, from the number of news articles to the number of days, such that, i equals the number of days between x and the most recent article in c, and w is the time window (measured in days) before x. Finally, if the maximal sim ilarity between the new article and any previous article is equal to or greater than a prespecified threshold $t _ { n } ,$ the new article is identified as belonging to a known event; otherwise, the new article is considered reporting a new event.

We empirically evaluated the effectiveness of our proposed SED method, using several prevalent full-text-based NED techniques, including a vector space model and two deep-learning-based models, as the performance benchmarks. This evaluation involves two sets of news articles: a training sample for summarization model construction and a testing sample for evaluating the effectiveness of all NED techniques examined. In the following subsections, we first describe the two news corpora used in our evaluation study. We then discuss our performance benchmarks, evaluation criteria and procedure, and, finally, model training and parameter tuning analyses.

## 4.1. Two news corpora

The first news corpus for training purpose was collected from excite. com, a popular online news website, over a 2-month period. We gathered 506 training articles that pertain to six different event topics: adjustments of interest rate, initial public offerings, business mergers, new product announcements, businesses partnerships, and computer viruses. For the second news corpus, we collected another set of news articles as the testing sample from various websites (e.g., The New York Times, Washington Post, Financial Times, Yahoo! Finance). The identified (and annotated) events were reviewed by an infor mation systems researcher to ensure their appropriateness. Specifically, INCR adopts the vector space model for feature selection and document representation, uses the combined measure of content similarity and temporal proximity to determine the similarity between news articles, and employs a single-pass clustering algorithm for identifying whether a new news article discusses a new event.

As we discussed previously, existing deep-learning-based techniques [24] are not directly applicable to this study, because they generally are developed for retrospective event detection scenario, rather than for NED. Thus, in this study, we developed two deep-learning-based NED methods specific for NED as additional performance benchmarks. Recent natural language understanding research often leverages the Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model [58], a pretrained language representation model containing multiple self-attention layers (e.g., 12 or 24 layers) as the encoder [59]. Specif ically, a passage (e.g., a sentence or a document) is represented as a sequence of input tokens, where each token is a word or wordpiece (i.e., subword). The first token of a passage is always a special classification token ([CLS]), which is used as the aggregate representation of the input sequence and often is employed for downstream classification tasks. Accordingly, our first deep-learning-based NED method employs BERT as a document representation encoder for news articles (or summaries of news articles). We adopted $\mathrm { { B E R T _ { b a s e } } , }$ which allows an input sequence up to 512 tokens, contains 12 self-attention layers, and generates a 768-dimensional hidden vector for each token (including [CLS]). We took the hidden vector of [CLS] as the document representation of a news article. Subsequently, we employed the cosine similarity on two news articles’ hidden vectors of [CLS] to measure the content similarity and also considered the temporal proximity when determining the overall similarity between two news articles. Finally, as with INCR, we used the single-pass clustering algorithm for identifying new events. We refer this performance benchmark as BERT-NED.

As described, BERT-NED uses BERT as a language model. BERT is pretrained on documents pertaining to general domains (including the BooksCorpus and English Wikipedia) [58], which may not effectively understand news articles. To address this possible limitation, our second deep-learning-based performance benchmark employs BERT as a sen tence encoder and then adopts long short-term memory (LSTM) [60] to capture the context of sentences and thus attain more effective repre sentations of news articles. An LSTM unit, composed of a memory cell and several gates (input, output, and memory gates), has the ability to retain important information over a sequence of tokens. In our design of the second deep-learning-based performance benchmark (referred to as bi-LSTM-NED) used BERT to encode each sentence in a news article. The resulting hidden vector of [CLS] serves as the representation of the input sentence. Accordingly, a news article then becomes a sequence of sen tence representations, in their original order within the article. We then implemented three layers of bi-directional LSTMs, where each bi-directional LSTM consists of two hidden layers running in two different directions (forward and backward). We then took the last hidden vector (with a dimension size of 150) produced by each direction in the last layer of bi-directional LSTM and used the concatenation of these two hidden vectors as the representation of the focal news article.

The training task of our bi-directional LSTMs in bi-LSTM-NED is defined as follows: given two randomly selected news articles, the task is to classify whether these two articles pertain to the same event. There fore, on top of the bi-directional LSTMs, we designed two layers of fully connected networks for this classification task, where the input layer to the fully connected networks is the concatenation of the representations of two news articles, the hidden layer contains 50 hidden nodes, and the output layer has two nodes (one node for that the two news articles pertain to the same event and another for different events). We adopted the cross-entropy method as the loss function to update the weights in the fully connected layers for classification and the weights in the three layers of bi-directional LSTMs.

The bi-directional LSTMs, once trained, were adopted by the bi LSTM-NED method to obtain the representation for each news article. The designs of the remaining tasks (including similarity measure and clustering algorithm) of the bi-LSTM-NED method were identical to those of the BERT-NED method.

## 4.3. Evaluation criteria and procedure

In line with Allan, Papka and Lavrenko [2] and Schanda, Sanderson and Clough [14], we used miss and false alarm rates to measure NED effectiveness. The former indicates the probability of failing to detect a new news article pertaining to a new event and the latter reveals the probability of falsely determining a new news article about an already known event as a new event. A low miss rate enables firms to respond to external changes in a timely manner, thereby increasing their adapt ability to the changing environment. On the other hand, a low false alarm rate reduces the time and effort requirements for processing and analyzing news articles about an already known event. As environ mental surveillance becomes increasingly complex and dynamic, responsiveness and adaptability arguably might be even more essential for firms than efficiency, which suggests the miss rate relatively more important than the false alarm rate. In principle, firms should strive for the lowest attainable miss rate while maintaining an acceptable false alarm rate [6].

As mentioned. we evaluated the proposed SED method and our performance benchmarks using the testing sample (i.e., the second news corpus that consists of 215 news articles). We randomly sampled 70% of news articles as a testing dataset, fed them in a chronologically order into an NED technique under investigation, and measured the miss and false alarm rates attained by each technique at each level of the novelty threshold $t _ { n } ,$ which ranges between 0.01 and 1 in increments of 0.01. To obtain more reliable performance measures, we repeated this procedure 30 times. The average performance across the 30 trials showed the overall detection effectiveness of our proposed SED method versus its benchmark techniques.

## 4.4. Model training and parameter tuning analyses

To fine-tune the parameters for the proposed SED method or our performance benchmarks, we split the training sample (i.e., the first news corpus that includes 506 news articles) into articles collected in the first versus the second month. Specifically, articles gathered in the first month served as “previous articles” and those collected in the second month were used as “newly released articles” in subsequent parameter tuning experiments. For the summarization model construction of the proposed SED method, we also used the first-month “previous articles” and their relevant and less relevant sentences to fine-tune the parame ters involved in the news article summarization learning phase of SED.

For the SED method, we first targeted five essential parameters in the news article summarization learning phase: upper-selection ρ , $\rho _ { u } .$

lower-selection $\rho _ { l - s e l e c t i o n } ,$ support-ratio $\rho _ { m i n s u p }$ thresholds for positive and negative cue phrases, the number of thematic words $n _ { t w } ,$ , and the sen tence representation schemes for each feature used in the summariza tion model construction. Constructing the summarization model requires training articles, with relevant and less relevant sentences, so that, we randomly sampled 70% of the previous articles and used them for the summarization model construction. Subsequently, we employed the remainder (i.e., 30% of the previous articles) for the model evalu ation, using recall and precision as the metrics for evaluating the effectiveness of the summarization model. The recall and precision rates represent common classification performance metrics. The former re veals the percentage of manually coded relevant (or less relevant) sen tences are correctly identified by the summarization model, whereas the latter indicates the percentage of relevant (or less relevant) sentences identified by the summarization model are indeed manually coded as relevant (or less relevant) sentences. According to our parameter tuning results, we set $\rho _ { m i n s u p }$ to 0.02, $\rho _ { u - s e l e c t i o n }$ to 0.3, and $\rho _ { l - s e l e c t i o n }$ to 0.25 to generate the positive and negative cue phrase glossaries. We examined the number of thematic words $n _ { t w }$ over the 10–20 range, in increments of 5. We also examined different representation schemes for all features adopted for learning a news article summarization model. The results of our tuning experiments revealed that the news article summarization learning phase of SED achieved the best effectiveness with the following parameter values or design choices: 20 for $n _ { t w } ,$ the top-3 representation scheme for positive and negative cue phrase features, the top-5 repre sentation scheme for both the thematic word and title word features, and the binary representation scheme for the cohesion feature as well as for the sentence length feature (with a threshold of 5).<sup>2</sup>

INCR and BERT-NED do not involve any model learning. In contrast, the bi-LSTM-NED method needs to update the weights in the fully connected layers and the weights in the three layers of bi-directional LSTMs, using the training task of classifying whether two news articles discuss the same event. We employed the training sample (including 506 news articles in the first news corpus) to build the dataset for the model learning of the bi-LSTM-NED method. A pair of news articles that are from the same event served as a positive instance, otherwise a negative instance. When creating the training dataset, we maintained the ratio between positive and negative instances as 1:4. The setting of other key hyperparameters was as follows: 0.005 for learning rate, 0.5 for dropout rate, and 16 for batch size.

For the event detection phase, we needed to determine three important parameters: the compression ratio cp-ratio, the number of features $k ,$ and the respective time window (w) for SED. First, we heu ristically set cp-ratio to 40% and fixed the number of features k to 50 and examined the effects of w in $^ { 7 \cdot , }$ 14-, 30-, or 60-day window. The results show that SED exhibited the best performance when $w = 6 0 .$ . Then, we fixed k to 50 and w to 60 and examined the effects of compression ratio ranging from 10% to 100% in increments of 10%. The results show that SED achieved better performance as setting cp-ratio to 70%. Finally, we assessed the effects of the number of features k on the detection effec tiveness of SED, over a range of k = 25, 50, 100, 150, 200, and all words. The results suggest that SED performed better when we set k to 25. Accordingly, we employed these parameter values in the subsequent evaluations.

For INCR’s time window $w ,$ we employed the same tuning procedure and set it to 60, according to the tuning analysis results. Similarly, we also conducted a tuning experiment for the number of features k. Over a range of k = 25, 50, 100, 150, 200, and all words, INCR attained better performance when setting k to 25. For the two deep-learning-based benchmarks (i.e., BERT-NED and bi-LSTM-NED), we also set the time window w to 60. In the subsequent evaluations, we used these parameter values and representation schemes for the three benchmark techniques.

## 5.1. Comparative evaluation

We compared the detection effectiveness of SED and the three benchmark techniques by examining their miss and false alarm rates, across the different levels of the novelty threshold $t _ { n } ,$ ranging from 0.01 to 1 in increments of 0.01. As Fig. 5 illustrates, the two deep-learning based techniques (i.e., BERT-NED and bi-LSTM-NED) perform much worse than the two techniques that are based on the vector space model (i.e., INCR and SED). A plausible reason attributed to the ineffectiveness of BERT-NED is that we adopted the BERT model, pretrained on a large collection of documents of general domains, without further fine-tuning on news articles. This may seriously constrain BERT’s ability for detecting new events. In the bi-LSTM-NED method, we employed BERT as a sentence encoder and relied on three layers of bi-LSTMs to learn a language model specific for the news domain. As Fig. 5 shows, the detection effectiveness of bi-LSTM-NED is considerably greater than that of BERT-NED, confirming the utilities of learning domain-specific lan guage model as achieved by the three layers of bi-LSTMs and the training task that we developed for training the bi-LSTMs.

Although the bi-LSTM-NED method outperforms the BERT-NED method, its detection effectiveness still cannot reach the levels of the two vector-space-model-based techniques. It is possibly because the size of the training dataset we employed to train the bi-LSTMs was too small. Recall that we used our training sample (consisting of only 506 news articles from 346 events) to create news article pairs (positive or nega tive) as the training dataset. The total number of news article pairs that we created to train the bi-LSTM-NED model was only around 3400. Such a small-sized training dataset is likely insufficient for training a very large number of weights associated with the three layers of bi-LSTM and the two layers of fully connected neural network for classifying a news article pair into one of the two classes (i.e., belonging to the same event or different events). We expect that the increase of the size of training dataset will further improve the detection effectiveness of the bi-LSTM-NED method.

## 5.2. Supervised versus unsupervised summarization

The summarization of news articles conceivably can proceed in a supervised or unsupervised manner. The proposed SED ranks and ex tracts sentences using a supervised learning algorithm (i.e., C4.5) to construct the summarization model. Mihalcea and Tarau [61] instead develop TextRank, a graph-based unsupervised algorithm, to extract keywords and sentences from text documents by modifying the Pag eRank algorithm [62]. Given a document to be summarized, TextRank represents each sentence as a vertex and uses the similarity between each pair of sentences as the weight of the edge that connects them. The similarity of two sentences $s _ { 1 }$ and $s _ { 2 }$ is ${ \frac { \left| S _ { 1 } \cap S _ { 2 } \right| } { \log \left| S _ { 1 } \right| + \log \left| S _ { 2 } \right| } } .$ To compare the effectiveness of supervised versus unsupervised summarization for NED, we examined SED using the summaries of news articles created by TextRank, a representative unsupervised algorithm, and compared its detection effectiveness with that of SED. We refer this variant as TextRank-based SED. We followed the same tuning procedure to determine three essential parameters involved in the event detection phase for TextRank-based SED: the number of features $k ,$ the compres sion ratio cp-ratio, and the respective time window (w). Guided by the parameter tuning results, we set the number of features k to 25, cp-ratio to 80%, and the time windows w to 60 for the subsequent evaluation.

As Fig. 7 illustrates, the results confirm that SED outperforms TextRank-based SED, conveying the use of summaries produced by a supervised summarization model more effective for detecting new events than those created by an unsupervised summarization model. That is, the summaries produced by the supervised summarization model appear more relevant and utilitarian for detecting new events from news articles than those created by the unsupervised summariza tion model.

## 5.3. Impact of compression ratio on the effectiveness of SED

We also scrutinized the impact of the compression ratio of summaries on the detection effectiveness of SED. As Fig. 8 shows, the detection error trade-off curves of the SED shift toward the origin when the compression ratio increases from 10% to 70%, but its effectiveness de creases when the compression ratio increases from 70% to 90%. ## 6. Discussion

The proposed SED method outperforms the three benchmark tech niques (including INCR, BERT-NED, and bi-LSTM-NED) that depend on a full-text analysis for detecting new events, with greater effectiveness and efficiency. Specifically, our news article summarization learning method incorporates a set of appropriate features and representation schemes to construct a news article summarization model, which is then employed to generate the summaries for all news articles (including incoming article and previous articles). Because the summaries have excluded less relevant sentences in corresponding news articles, our proposed SED method is capable of reducing noises and biases and thus can improve NED effectiveness. In addition, the use of article summaries to detect new events also can greatly enhance efficiency in terms of time and computational processing requirements. Furthermore, according to our evaluation results, supervised learning-based summarization seems more effective than unsupervised summarization for extracting sum maries from news articles to support NED.

To keep abreast of emerging events in a timely manner, firms can benefit from using relevant, comprehensive summaries of news articles to detect new events. The proposed SED method, with its ability to identify and select relevant sentences from each news article effectively and efficiently, can inform continued efforts to support new event tracking, event summarization, and event episode discovery [63]. Tak ing event summarization as an illustration, the results show that sum maries produced by an effective text summarization model can filter less relevant sentences in a news article while preserving the information essential and representative of its focal event. At the same time, this study sheds new light on document clustering analysis. In a sense, NED represents a variant of traditional document clustering, in that both analyses involve the measure of content similarity between documents, before assigning them to appropriate clusters or identifying them as new events. Using the summaries produced by an effective summarization model, we can increase similarity comparison accuracy and reduce the computational processing requirements.

## 7. Conclusion and future research directions

Automated detection of new events from online news articles i crucial to firms’ environmental surveillance efforts undertaken to identify emerging events, trends, or changes in an effective and timely manner. To address the limitations of full-text-based techniques, we propose a SED method that filters sentences in a news article according to their relevance to the focal event reported in the news article, before performing NED. When applied to a sample of online news articles, our proposed SED method proves more effective than its benchmark techniques.

This study contributes to extant literature in several ways. First, we advance NED research by developing a novel approach more effective and computationally efficient than existing full-text-based techniques. The empirical results indicate the viability of our method, as measured by miss and false alarm rates. The proposed SED method can effectively detect new events from online news articles using their summaries instead of their full texts, which also increases both time and processing efficiency. Second, we affirm the feasibility and practical value of text summarization for NED, which can identify and filter less relevant sentences from the subsequent analyses. Our summary-based method achieves detection effectiveness greater than that attained by some prevalent full-text-based techniques, including a vector space model and two deep-learning-based models. For news articles, the use of relevant sentences in summaries ensures access to valuable information for NED while preserving readability for manual inspections, if necessary. Third, we reveal that supervised summarization learning is superior than un supervised summarization for extracting summaries of news articles. Our empirical study also conveys the viability of constructing an effec tive summarization model from a relatively small set of training articles, thus offering practical values for various event detection scenarios. Fourth, we contribute to text summarization research by identifying an appropriate set of features and their representation schemes for news article summarization model construction. With these features and representation schemes, a summarization model can effectively identify relevant sentences from a news article.

This study has several limitations that indicate further research di rections. For example, we apply decision tree induction for summari zation model construction; future studies should consider other supervised learning algorithms, such as random forest or artificial neural network, and evaluate their effectiveness in comparison with the deci sion tree induction (i.e., C4.5) in the news article summarization learning method developed in this study. Also, the effectiveness of SED depends on accurate predictions of relevant sentences in news articles. While appropriate for our objective, we formulate our news article summarization task as a sentence-level, dichotomous classification problem and employ a supervised learning algorithm to identify relevant sentences. Continued efforts are needed to examine other means for determining relevant versus less relevant sentences in a news article. In addition, the summarization model’s performance that we report re flects the use of salient features for model construction. Further analyses of the relationships or interplays of these features could help better justify their use for the summarization model construction. Moreover, our proposed SED method exhibits a specific configuration that involves a particular supervised text-summarization model and an NED tech nique (i.e., INCR). Evidently, different combinations of existing text summarization techniques and NED techniques can be developed. The evaluation of these different combinations to assess the applicability and utility of text summarization as a sentence-level information filtering mechanism under various underlying NED techniques represents an interesting direction for further research. Finally, this study targets NED for firms' environmental surveillance, so that, more efforts are needed to establish the legitimacy and effectiveness of the text summarization approach for other tasks (e.g., event tracking, event summarization, and event episode discovery) and to extend the proposed SED method to additional tasks.
