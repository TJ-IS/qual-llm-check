# Role of users’ status quo on continuance intentions

Sandeep Goyal <sup>a</sup>, Viswanath Venkatesh <sup>b,\*</sup>, Xinpeng Shi <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Louisville, United States

<sup>b</sup> Virginia Tech, United States

<sup>c</sup> Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Associate Editor: Patrick Chau

Keywords: Continuance intentions Prospect theory Value of the status quo Commitment to the status quo Cognitive competency Habit Trust Satisfaction

## A B S T R A C T

Accelerated technological innovations have led to shorter product life cycles. Yet, consumers often decide not to discard the incumbent technology in favor of a new technology. To explain this decision-making process, a rich research stream has investigated subconscious motivations. However, there is little understanding of the role of conscious motivations and, more importantly, their interplay with subconscious motivations in their decisions. Using the value appreciation perspective, we controlled for the predictors of the second version of the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT2) and leverage prospect theory to enrich our understanding of users’ continuance intentions. We conceptualize status quo preference with two new constructs—namely, the value of status quo and users’ commitment to status quo—and integrate them with other known predictors of continuance intentions (related to an incumbent system)—i.e., trust and habit. We empirically test our model in two studies. Study 1 was conducted among 2,096 users of smartphones in Hong Kong. Study 2 reports longi tudinal data, across three waves of data collection over a period of six months. from 240 analysts introduced to a new technology to support their work in a large financial services organization. The findings support our model and suggest that (1) users’ status quo preferences significantly determine continuance intentions: and (2) trust and habit have an effect on users’ status quo preferences. These findings advance knowledge on continuance intentions by integrating the effects of users’ conscious and subconscious intentions to continue using the incumbent technology.

## 1. Introduction

Buyers expect technology companies to launch new versions of their products much more frequently than they have in the past [55,103]. These accelerated technological turnarounds have created an upgrade fatigue where organizations not only have to compete with their com petitors, but also compete with their own products [72]. Organizations often respond with increased marketing expenditure to motivate users to upgrade. Although such marketing efforts tend to enhance user aware ness of new technology, they have little impact on the user’s dilemma of choosing between the incumbent and the new technology [9,16]. Most users share strong opinions and resistance to upgrade because of un certainty, scarification, loss of personalized use, and experience nurtured in their incumbent technology [16]. Organizations lose billions of dollars when users fail to see the value proposition of the new tech nology relative to the existing technology and choose not to upgrade. Therefore, it is important to understand users’ decision-making pro cesses for replacing an incumbent technology with a new technology.

A wealth of knowledge has explained how and why users initially adopt a technology and then choose to continue using it or to discard it (e.g., [19,39,53,90,94,98]). Recent research in IS and reference disci plines have challenged the switching from incumbent technologies to new technologies by examining constructs such as inertia, relative advantage, transaction costs, habit, and satisfaction (see [35,41,47,66, 67,93]). The core argument in this stream of work is that the motivation to continue using an incumbent technology involves subconscious (e.g., habit) and conscious (e.g., switching costs) factors. Although this line of inquiry has explained subconscious motivations, there is little under standing of the role of conscious motivations and, more importantly, their interplay with subconscious motivations [33]. Although some users might consider subconscious factors to be more important, others might consider conscious factors to be more important, and some might weigh the cost and benefits of both. Therefore, relying on only one type of factor, rather than a holistic model that incorporates both conscious and subconscious motivations, may either present an incomplete or an inaccurate understanding of users’ continuance intentions. Against thi backdrop, the current research has two objectives as follows:

1 Present a holistic model of IS continuance that not only explains the effect of conscious motivations, but also their interplay with sub conscious motivations; and

2 Empirically test the proposed model.

We develop a model that not only unveils the effects of the status quo preference on users’ continuance intentions, but also elucidates the interplay between other known conscious and subconscious de terminants of users’ continuance intentions. Controlling for the pre dictors from the second version of the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT2; [93]) and leveraging prospect theory [38, 84], this research investigates how users’ continuance intention to use an incumbent technology can be a rational status quo preference, which is not a bias but a conscious decision-making outcome. Polites and Karahanna [66] argue that status quo bias presents a set of theoretical explanations on why users may stay with the incumbent technology instead of switching to a better alternative. We argue that this status quo bias has two distinct dimensions—namely, the value of status quo and commitment to status quo. Value of status quo is defined as a user’s rational and psychological assessment of the current technology with the incorporation of uncertainty and alternatives; and commitment to status quo is defined as a user’s attachment to the existing relationship with the current technology, and the need to continue the relationship without the sacrifice of current value and invested resources [1,3,62,78, 83].

Prospect theory postulates that losses loom larger than gains [38,84]. We contend that the users take their value appreciation of status quo as the reference state for comparing the gain, loss, and costs of alternatives. Specifically, the difference between the value of status quo and the value of alternatives determines users’ status quo preference to continue using a technology. The marketing literature (see [6]), in contrast, has shown that consumers tend to show commitment to the current product because of economic, social, or psychological costs. Even Brown et al. [11] and Szajna and Scammell [79] have presented evidence that users of technology tend to rationalize sticking to the incumbent technology even when better alternatives are available. Therefore, the commitment to status quo is expected to determine users’ intentions to continue using a technology.

In this research, we investigate customers' decision-making processes for replacing an incumbent technology with a new technology by leveraging the key tenets of the prospect theory and incorporating them into the accumulated knowledge on users’ continuance intention de cisions $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ UTAUT2). By doing so, this research makes two key con tributions. First, we present a holistic model explaining customers decision-making processes for replacing an incumbent technology with a new technology. We build on recent research that has investi gated several attributes that explain continuance intentions to use incumbent technologies—e.g., effect of status quo bias [41], habit [17, 47], status quo [66], and switching, transition, uncertainty, and sunk costs [41,66,69]—and go beyond this work by incorporating the conscious and subconscious motivations of users’ continuance in tentions to use a technology. We argue that some users might consider subconscious factors to be more relevant, whereas others might consider conscious factors to be more relevant (see [93]). Some users might weigh the costs and benefits of both, thus making the holistic model essential for an in-depth understanding of users’ continuance intentions. Second, we go beyond accumulated knowledge on users’ continuance intention decisions (e.g., [36,87,93]) and answer the call of Bagozzi [2] for a paradigm shift to expand the scope of technology adoption litera ture (see also [4,75,88]). We accomplish this through the addition of the multi-pronged role of status quo preference.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Unified theory of acceptance and use of technology 2 (UTAUT2)

In this section, we first briefly review the key tenets of UTAUT2 [93]. Venkatesh et al. [87], in their seminal work, integrated eight major technology adoption theories and models and presented UTAUT as an integrated theory of critical determinants of user adoption and use. UTAUT identified four core determinants of user adoption and use. They are performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Performance expectancy is defined as the degree to which an individual believes that using a system will enhance their job performance. Effort expectancy is defined as the degree of ease associated with the use of the system. Social influence refers to an in dividual’s perception that important others believe that he or she should use the new system. Facilitating conditions refers to an individual’s belief that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to sup port the use of the system. Venkatesh et al. [93], in presenting UTAUT2, extended UTAUT to the consumer context by adding three new con structs. They are hedonic motivation, price value, and habit. Venkatesh et al. [93] define hedonic motivation as the fun or pleasure derived from using a technology; price value as the cognitive trade-off a consumer perceived between the benefits of a technology and the cost of the technology; and habit as the extent to which consumers tend to perform behaviors automatically because of learning.

UTAUT2 has been successfully applied and extended to study the adoption of various technologies in a variety of contexts. However, Venkatesh et al. [93] noted that traditional predictors, such as perfor mance expectancy, are less relevant in a consumer context, and other drivers, such as price value, which are irrelevant in a work context to employees, will be important in a consumer adoption context. Relevant to the current research, UTAUT2 provides a powerful framework for the adoption and use of consumer technology. However, UTAUT2 does not fully provide for situations when an individual makes a continuance decision regarding an existing incumbent technology. Such decisions require a user to compare and contrast their preferences toward an incumbent technology with preferences toward a new technology. Still, UTAUT2 focuses on the new technology. To address this gap regarding perceptions of consumers toward the incumbent technology, we incor porate the role of status quo, and specifically, two aspects of status quo to provide a better understanding of continuance intentions compared with what would be obtained from UTAUT2 alone.

## 2.2. Prospect theory

The key assertions of prospect theory [38,84] are as follows: (1) people are more sensitive to losses/costs than gains/benefits; (2) in dividuals prefer risk aversion than risk-seeking action when they face certain gains or positive prospects and vice versa; (3) the decision is always a reference-dependent preference; (4) individuals have biased valuation—i.e., the endowment effect—“People who own a good value it more than people who do not” ([60], p. 339; [62,69]); and (5) the conservative principle that “we have reason to preserve valuable things that currently exist, even when we could replace them with things of equal or greater value” [10,38,62, p. 461].

Prior technology adoption literature has used prospect theory to explain continuance intentions. Brown et al. [10], for example, used prospect theory to argue that negative disconfirmation of perceived usefulness expectations has a stronger effect on continuance intentions than does the positive disconfirmation of the perceived usefulness ex pectations. In this work, using prospect theory allows us to present theoretical arguments about the situations where users make a choice that could possibly result in the largest gain and/or least loss.

## 2.3. Factors influencing continuance intentions and continued use

Technology adoption research has employed a multitude of vari ables, other than the critical predictors included in UTAUT and UTAUT2, to explain users’ initial adoption and continuance intentions. We present a brief overview of such variables. The literature on continuance intentions indicates that trust is a key antecedent in pre dicting users’ continuance intentions [20,24,40,49,56,73,85,97,99]. Trust is conceptualized as a trustor’s willingness to be venerable and rely on a trustee, as the trustor believes the trustee to be a trustworthy partner and has confidence in the trustee’s competence, benevolence, and integrity to fulfill his/her promises [12,44,49,54,57]. Trust has been a cornerstone of long-term relationships in social and economic ex changes [23,44,74,80]. IS research has consistently shown that trust has extended into the human-technology context [43,85,97]. Lankton et al. [43], for example, suggest that, in a human-technology relationship, technology can act as a trustee because technology nowadays presents a human-like nature or its humanness in the process of users’ social in teractions, and an artifact’s “smart” or intelligent characteristics (e.g., voices, animations, and sensors) can be anthropomorphized.

Habit is an individual’s behavior featured with automatic, efficient, and effortless actions [47]. Verplanken and Aarts [95] define a habit as the “learned sequences of acts that have become automatic responses to specific cues, and are functional in obtaining certain goals or end-states” (p. 104). In an IS context, users initially develop their habits with intentional efforts and goal-oriented actions, such as learning func tionality, interactions, and performing maintenance of a technology [17, 36]. Habit evolves when users become cognitively competent and create their own use, experience, and skills [17,21]. Eventually, well-established habit characterizes unintentionality, automaticity, lack of awareness, and efficiency [95,96,100–102]. Given the important role of habit, IS scholars have investigated its impact on continuance in tentions [8,67]. However, the findings of this stream of research provide unspecific effects. Some works indicate that habit has a direct effect on continuance intentions [42,46,93], others suggest mediating effects [24], and some suggest moderating effects on the relationship between continuance intentions and continued use [47]. Venkatesh et al. [93] argue that some technology consumers find factors related to subcon scious motivations to be more relevant to their continuance intentions, whereas others find factors related to conscious motivations to be more relevant. Testing one or the other might result in an incomplete, or even an inaccurate, understanding of users’ continuance intentions. Incor porating trust, habit, status quo preferences, as well as UTAUT/UTAUT2 predictors to predict continuance intentions allows us to theorize and test a holistic model to understand users’ continuance intentions.

## 2.4. Status quo preference literature

Prior literature has investigated the role of status quo preference in multiple contexts (for example, see [31,41,63,66,69]). Samuelson and Zeckhauser [69] argue that “most real decisions…have a status quo alternative—that is, doing nothing or maintaining one’s current or previous decision” (p. 7). This research stream has alluded to the com plex interplay between conscious and subconscious decisions. Polites and Karahanna [66], for example, note that users may consciously un derstand that the alternative technology is better, but these users may continue to use the incumbent technology because of subconscious factors such as habit or their emotional attachment to the current way of doing things. Nebel [62] similarly contends that individuals may pre serve status quo even when they can replace incumbent products with better alternatives. Finally, Samuelson and Zeckhauser [69] argue that users could make a psychological commitment to products because of the sunk costs as well as a need for cognitive consistency. Although this stream of research has shown the significant effect of status quo pref erences on decision-making, much of this effect has focused on commitment to status quo with little attention to the value of status quo even though the value has an important role. Sweeney and Soutar [78], for example, assert that few purchases are entirely about emotions, and consumer value is an important part of this decision process. Similarly, Turel et al. [83], highlighting the role of consumption value in tech nology adoption decisions, note that consumption value has several dimensions, such as emotional value and value for money, that are not captured by traditional technology adoption models.

Despite the evidence for two distinct dimensions of status quo pref erence, little research has investigated their distinct impact on con sumers’ decision-making processes for replacing an incumbent technology with a new technology. Maintaining such a distinction is critical for more nuanced theorizing of the consumers’ decision-making processes because consumers may react differently depending upon the dimension that is more relevant to them. For example, both Nebel [62] and Polites and Karahanna [66] note that sometimes fully rational users tend to preserve status quo, despite alternatives that are of greater value available to them. Perhaps for such users, it is the emotional factors—e. g., personal attachment—that are stronger drivers of their decision-making process. Bendapudi and Berry [6] as well as Sweeney and Soutar [78] have presented evidence for such attachment by arguing that consumers tend to show commitment to the current prod uct because of economic, social, or psychological costs. Venkatesh et al. [93], on the other hand, argue for the impact of consumer value on the decision-making process by noting that users make technology use de cisions based on a cognitive trade-off between the perceived benefits of a technology and the monetary cost of using it. In light of this literature, we anchor on the commitment to status quo and extend to add the value of status quo as an important yet distinct determinant of continuance intentions.

## 3. Research model

Fig. 1 presents our research model. This model offers a holistic causal chain predicting users’ continuance intentions. Specifically, the model contends that the users’ conscious and rational decisions are determined by the assessed value of status quo technology and users’ commitment to the status quo choice. The model also shows that users’ subconscious motivations of habit and trust in technology have an effect on their continuance intentions. These effects are conveyed by the dual media tors of the value of status quo and commitment to status quo. Therefore, this model integrates users’ determinants tied to subconscious and conscious motivations and emphasizes how and to what extent habit and trust in technology indirectly determine continuance intentions. ## 3.1. Effect of value of status quo

Prospect theory [38,84] provides the value function as a theoretical rationale to elucidate individuals’ conscious decision-making under uncertainty, risks, and alternatives. The theory postulates that people are more sensitive to losses/costs than gains/benefits such that they prefer risk aversion than risk-seeking action when they face certain gains or positive prospects and vice versa. Of particular importance to the current research, the value of a status quo object acts as the reference state for a decision-maker to assess the relative gains or losses of switching to alternatives and then make a risk averse or seeking decision.

We argue that the value function of the incumbent technology acts as the reference state for a comparative assessment of the value functions of alternatives [41.62.66.69]. A positive value usually leads to a decision-maker’s risk averse choice of preserving and using the technology. Users tend to highly evaluate the positive value of the incumbent technology and overstate the risks and losses of alternatives due to the endowment effect and the bounded rationality of individual cognitive limitations [71]. As a result, the value of the incumbent technology dominates users’ assessments to decide the preference for the status quo, as it is a valuable, secure, sensible, economic, and riskless choice [22,70, 81]. When the incumbent technology is valued negatively or the alter natives have breakthrough features [68], the user will make a possibly risk-seeking decision to switch from the incumbent technology to one of the alternatives.

To assess the value, users may take multiple tangible and intangible value attributes to evaluate and from the overall value of the status quo. For example, in extending UTAUT, Venkatesh et al. [93] introduce price value as another predictor of behavioral intention to use a technology. Specifically, Venkatesh et al. [93] argue that users perceive a cognitive trade-off between the perceived benefits of a technology and the mon etary cost of using it. When consumers perceive that the perceived benefits of using a technology are greater than the associated monetary cost, price value will have a positive effect on behavioral intention.

Following the same line of reasoning, we argue that, when presented with an alternative technology that does not offer groundbreaking new features, users might consider the incumbent technology to be a better economic choice compared to the alternatives. This risk averse strategy suggests that, if users ascribe a positive and high value for the incumbent technology, they will prefer the status quo, and this will put alternative technologies in a disadvantageous position. Thus, we posit the following:

H1. The value of status quo will have a positive effect on users’ contin uance intentions to use the technology.

## 3.2. Effect of commitment to status quo

We argue that commitment to status quo will have a positive effect on continuance intentions. Bansal et al. [3] conceptualize commitment to have three distinct dimensions—namely, affective. normative, and continuance. Affective commitment refers to a desire-based attachment to the organization; normative commitment refers to an obligation-based attachment, and it is the right thing to do; and continuance commitment refers to a cost-based attachment to stay with the organization [3]. Continuance commitment is relevant to the current context because it relates to how consumers perceive that they can end a relationship because of economic, social, or psychological costs [6].

Prospect theory addresses individuals’ commitment to status quo preferences [60]. Schwarz [70] argues that the commitment is mainly created when a user’s sense of psychological ownership is fostered in prior repetitive and interactive use processes. Ownership of a technol ogy creates a psychological tendency for users to commit to their pref erence and maintain a deliberate consistency. Examples of such a tendency are available in multiple contexts. For example, in the context of organizational behavior, Hambrick et al. [31] argue that organiza tions do not change because one values the current state and would incur a loss if there were changes. Similarly, Polites and Karahana [66] contend that users perceive a strong emotional attachment to the current way of doing things and find any changes to be stressful

When users make an investment in the ownership of the incumbent technology, they would be self-motivated to rationalize their decision with routine and continuous use [22.60.70]. Evidence for such decision rationalization is also present in cognitive dissonance theory [11]. For example, Szajna and Scamell [79] note that users in their experiments aligned their experiences with their initial expectations with technology to decrease dissonance. Further, the routine and interactive use of the technology engenders a user-technology-dependent relationship, which is user-technology specific and cannot be easily transferred to alternative technologies [3,28,58]. Users may be afraid to abandon the incumbent technology because of the fear of substantial loss of their enjoyable experience, familiarity, feelings, and dependence on the technology. Thus, we posit the following:

H2. Commitment to status quo will have a positive effect on users continuance intentions to use the technology.

Value of status quo will have a positive effect on commitment to status quo. Prior literature has provided several examples of value driving commitment. Moliner [59], for example, showed that by paying attention to perceived value, healthcare facilities were able to achieve a greater degree of commitment toward the hospital. The underlying logic in this relationship is that when users consider the incumbent technol ogy to be valuable enough that they are willing to exert extra effort to maintain it indefinitely, their commitment to status quo increases. Thus, we posit the following:

H3. The value of status quo will have a positive effect on users commitment to status quo.

## 3.3. Role of subconscious motivations

In addition to the value of status quo and commitment to status quo as the core drivers of the continuance intentions, subconscious motiva tions play an important role in continuance intention decisions. Next, we elucidate how and why these subconscious motivations have an effect on the value of status quo and commitment to status quo.

## 3.4. Effects of habit

Habit lets users fully utilize the functions and technological advan tages with high efficiency, build user-technology relationships, appre ciate the value of the technology, and eventually make users rely more on the technology. In developing UTAUT2, Venkatesh et al. [93] added habit as a predictor of behavioral intention to argue that users’ habits are behaviors and ways of using a technology. It is automatic, efficient, and free of users’ mental capacities to do multiple tasks [96]. Indeed, habitual acts occur in a familiar environment.

The development of habit requires a certain amount of repetition or practice [93]. In what they call proceduralization, Limayem at al. [47] argue that habit formation requires weekly repetition or practice in a habit conducive context. Once the habit is formed, the behavior is per formed automatically with minimal effort and minimal conscious effort. We argue that, when consumers reach such a habitual stage, any alter nate technology represents an endeavor rife with uncertainty and a need for additional effort. Such a rational and a psychological assessment would increase user’s value of the incumbent technology, thereby increasing their value of status quo.

Users’ familiarity with a technology enhances their efficiency in using the technology to accomplish routine tasks and reduces time and mental costs, all of which allows users to make a conscious and rational assessment that they value the technology they are using. For example, when individuals use their smartphone for a long period of time, they tend to learn the sequence of activities involved in achieving certain goals. When provided information about the availability of the nextgeneration smartphone, such individuals may fear that using the nextgeneration smartphone may inhibit stored automatic responses to spe cific cues (e.g., a keystroke sequence needed for a particular activity on the phone). Thus, we posit the following:

H4a. Habit will have a positive effect on users’ value of status quo.

With the development of habit, users may attribute part of their success to the technology. Limayem et al. [47] note that “due to its minimal requirements on cognitive processing and deliberate control, habitual behavior is both effortless and efficient …; consequently, it reduces response latencies and ‘smoothes’ responses” (p. 709). In such situations, users learn to work seamlessly with the technology to suc cessfully accomplish tasks. In doing so, these users would develop a relationship with the technology such that they would consider the incumbent technology a partner essential to accomplishing task at hand. The replacement of the incumbent technology with an alternate may make users feel handicapped such that they have to divert some of their cognitive resources to using the technology rather than the task at hand. If they face failure, these users may attribute their failure to the tech nology instead of their own effort.

Habit may engender an interactive and dependent relationship be tween users and the technology. Because of users’ knowledge about the sequence of acts to perform their tasks, they are able to develop famil iarity and a feeling of knowing the intricacies of the technology. Ven katesh et al. [93] view such use as automatic. Such automatic task accomplishment may cultivate affective feelings of psychological ownership, a user-technology-dependent relationship, and a commit ment to the technology. For example, a user who does not feel comfortable with a particular geographical area is expected to feel anxious. However, if that user is an avid user of smartphone GPS (global positioning system) technology, they may not feel any anxiety because they believe that their smartphone GPS technology is always there to guide them. Thus, we posit the following:

H4b. Habit will have a positive effect on users’ commitment to status quo.

## 3.5. Effects of trust

As technology advances with increasingly human-like characteris tics, studies have paid particular attention to investigate users’ trust in technology and its effects. In keeping with this, we focus on examining the effects of user's trust in such a human-like artifact. The context of such a human-like artifact for the current research includes a technology product produced by the vendor. The product for our first study is a smartphone and for our second study is a new financial services system developed for an organization.

Trust is a key factor in decreasing user’s perception about risk and uncertainty [20,43]. When faced with the prospect of adopting and using a new technology, users are likely to feel vulnerable. Such vulnerability may be further exacerbated if the users are presented with incomplete information about the new technology. Therefore, if the user finds the incumbent technology to be trustworthy, they would perceive a higher value in status quo because they find the incumbent technology to be reliable, dependable, and perform as expected. Thus, we posit the following:

H5a. Trust in technology will have a positive effect on users’ value of status quo.

Trust is expected to have a positive effect of commitment to status quo. First, in line with the commitment-trust theory [61], users’ trust leads to their psychological commitment to the technology and en hances their willingness to maintain the current user-technology rela tionship. Second, trust sustains the continuance of the relationship without the sacrifice of prior invested efforts in establishing the rela tionship. Third, users’ trust in the technology lets them confidently explore the technology’s capabilities and utilities, optimize the pre determined use to tailor personal needs, and appreciate the value of technological advantages. Thus, we posit the following:

H5b. Trust in technology will have a positive effect on users’ commitment to status quo

Prior research has viewed habit as the extent to which an individual believes that their behavior is automatic [47,93]. Such automaticity is reached with experience. Venkatesh et al. [93] argue that, with the passage of time and an increase in experience, users can develop a level of habit depending on their familiarity and interaction with the tech nology. As such, users with high trust would highly treasure the incumbent technology and appreciate the high value from their use, and these are likely to lead to strong intentions to continue using the tech nology. Specifically, trust makes users feel at ease and reassured to learn how to efficiently use the technology, design personal user interfaces, and develop personalized uses. Therefore, the trust provides users with psychological assurance to engender habit. Thus, we posit the following:

H5c. Trust in technology will have a positive effect on habit.

## 4. Method

We conducted two studies to test our model. Prior research (e.g., [93]) has argued that contexts can have an important effect on theories, such that differences in context could change the significance, direction, or magnitude of the originally theorized relationships. Moreover, prior research (e.g., [90]) has argued that, although a cross-sectional design is useful, longitudinal field studies are necessary to understand how continuance intentions change over time—such a central role for time, especially in technology adoption research has been underscored in prior research (see also [86,89,92]) over a period of time. Therefore, we tested our model in two different contexts—i.e., personal technology and workplace technology—and in two different research designs—i.e., cross-sectional field surveys and longitudinal field studies. The first study comprised cross-sectional field surveys of consumers using tech nologies for personal use. The second study was a longitudinal field study in an organization where a new technology was introduced in the workplace to replace an existing technology. Empirical support for the model across two different contexts would not only demonstrate greater generalizability, but also provide richness by explaining differences in the context of the studies. Below, we discuss the setting, participants, procedure, and measurement details for both studies. We present the full details of study 1 and study 2. We also present the key differences be tween the two studies.

## 4.1. Study 1

## 4.1.1. Setting

The smartphone was the target technology, and smartphone users were the target respondents. The data collection was conducted in Hong Kong. Smartphones and their users are an appropriate context to study the causal effects on continuance intentions to use technology. First, smartphones are typical technological devices to meet personal infor mation and communication needs. They provide users with compre hensive features, functionalities, and applications to meet information and communication needs. Second, using smartphones generates tremendous impacts on users’ cognition, psychology, and behaviors due to the worldwide adoption and users’ intensive daily use. Users continued use of smartphones has significant implications for sustain able demands, supply, R&D investment, and IT innovation. Third, smartphones have also been a popular consumer technology worldwide, especially for Hong Kong users—the respondents in our study. For years, users are likely to have had a personal preference for, satisfaction with, trust in, and commitment to smartphones. Finally, the competition for market share and consumers’ continuance intentions is fierce among smartphone brands in Hong Kong because smartphone users have a variety of alternatives for decisions related to continue using the same smartphone or switching to different smartphones or brands. As the market is well developed, smartphone users are cognitively competent and knowledgeable to make their own decisions. Thus, such contextual features fit well with the theoretical underpinnings and assumptions of this research.

## 4.1.2. Measurement

All constructs in the model were first-order reflective latent constructs with multiple observable variables. We adopted validated scales from prior literature and made minor modifications to suit our context. We developed new scales for the two new constructs proposed in the study—namely, the value of status quo and commitment to status quo. As we explain in more detail later, we based the measurement of the value of status quo on the value concept of prospect theory [37,38], value of status quo by Nebel [62] and Morewedge and Giblin [60], and studies about users’ perceived values of incumbent products [78,83]. We based the measurement of commitment to the status quo in line with the definitions and descriptions of psychological commitment by Allen and Meyer [1], Bansal et al. [3], Dunham et al. [18], Samuelson and Zeckhauser [69], and Schwarz [70].

The scale for continuance intentions was adopted from Bhattacherjee [7] and Venkatesh et al. [93]. We added one item to their three-item scale to enhance reliability and validity and strengthen model identifi cation. Habit was measured using the items from Polites and Karahanna [66] and Venkatesh et al. [93]. Trust was measured using a scale from Lankton et al. [43] and other related literature (e.g., [24,44,56,57]). In addition, control variables were included from UTAUT and UTAUT2. They were performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions, hedonic motivations, price value, and de mographic characteristics. Other control variables included gender, age, education, occupation, experience with using smartphones, and expe rience using the current smartphone. Appendix A provides the scales used in our studies.

We developed scales for the value of status quo and commitment to status quo. We based the measurement of the value of status quo on the value concept of prospect theory [37,38], value of status quo by Nebel [62] and Morewedge and Giblin [60], and studies about users’ perceived values of incumbent products (see [78,83]). We used these items to create a questionnaire in English. Each question was measured on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree (1)” to “strongly agree (7)”. Next, we followed Cohen [14] and Trochim et al. [82] to conduct Q-sort analysis to purify and validate our items. We invited 20 graduate students and divided them into four groups to conduct four rounds of sorting. In the first round, we did not provide any operational definitions and asked our participants to group items printed on separate index cards into any number of categories and label them. We asked the participants to notate ambiguity in scales and recommend changes. All respondents categorized items into two categories. We revised our items based on the feedback we received. In the second round, we provided participants with the operational definitions of the two constructs—i.e., the value of status quo and commitment to status quo—along with the items printed on separate index cards for a sort. In the third round of sorting, as we did in the first round, we did not provide the label, operational definitions, or the number of categories. Finally, in the fourth round of sorting, as we did in the second round, we provided labels as well operational definitions of the constructs. Consistently across all rounds, our participants placed items into two categories and the inter-rate Kappa of greater than 0.90, much higher than that 0.65 recommended by Cohen [14].

We conducted a pretest survey with 92 smartphone users in Hong Kong. Preliminary analyses were conducted with this survey data. Typical statistical criteria were used to examine reliability, validity, and the factor structures of loadings and cross-loadings of observable vari ables [13,15]. Two items each from the scales of commitment to status quo were dropped due to low loadings (less than 0.70). The purified measurement scales and subsequent analysis showed that the instru ment was reliable, valid, and ready for a full-scale survey. We conducted a pilot study with 1,186 smartphone users in Hong Kong, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen. The key results of this pilot are presented in Appendix B.

We conducted our survey in English among 5,000 smartphone users in Hong Kong. Paper questionnaires were distributed to evenly stratified smartphone users as follows: university students, company and institu tional staff, managerial or administrative staff, and professionals. Completed surveys were returned within two months of distribution. Completed surveys were returned within three weeks of distribution. In all, we received 2,187 responses. We dropped 91 incomplete responses for a response rate of approximately 42%. To test for nonresponse bias, we compared the demographic profiles of the respondents with the sampling frame and found them to be similar.

## 4.1.3. Results

We analyzed our data using the partial least squares (PLS) in

SmartPLS (version 3.2.4). We used the PLS algorithm and bootstrapping procedures to estimate the parameters related to the measurement model, structural models, and testing hypotheses in line with the sug gestions by Gefen et al. [25,26] and Hair et al. [29,30].

Table 2 presents the Cronbach’s alphas (CAs), internal consistency reliabilities (ICRs), descriptive statistics, correlations, and average variance extracted (AVE). We examined reliability using CAs and ICRs, and convergent and discriminant validity using standardized factor loadings and square root of average variance extracted $( \sqrt { \mathrm { A V E } } ) \ [ 3 0 , 5 1 ] .$ All CAs and ICRs were above the cut-off criterion $( \geq 0 . 7 0 )$ , thus sup porting the reliability of the scales. The PLS factor loadings of observable variables onto their own manifest constructs showed that all items had high loadings $( \geq 0 . 7 1 )$ on their own constructs and cross-loadings lower than the loadings, thus supporting convergent and discriminant validity. The high factor loadings $( \geq 0 . 7 1 )$ and the high AVEs $( \geq 0 . 5 0 )$ values support convergent validity for all scales. The $\sqrt { \mathsf { A V E } }$ values of all scales were higher than the correlations with scales measuring other con structs, thus supporting discriminant validity.

Because of our data collection methodology, we tested for common method bias. In line with the recommendation by Lindell and Whitney [48], Malhotra et al. [52], and Podsakoff et al. [65], we used rigorous instrument design and data collection procedures. In particular, the scales in this study did not involve respondents’ self-evaluation, per formance, judgment, comparison, or socially desirable variables. All items were related to smartphone use issues and respondents could freely express their views on their current smartphones. Further, we assessed common method bias in the dataset using Harman’s single factor test [32,66]. We added a construct termed as “Common Method Factor $( \mathrm { C M F } ) ^ { \prime }$ in the structural model and connected it with all of the observable variables. The construct served as the “common method bias” to control the common method variance among endogenous and exogenous constructs. We conducted analyses “with” and “without” the CMF in the structural models and found no significant differences on estimated parameters and variance explained. Therefore, common method bias was not an issue in our dataset (see [65]).

The structural model results are presented in Fig. 2. All hypotheses were supported. We hypothesized that the value of status quo (H1) and commitment to status quo (H2) will have a positive effect on users continuance intentions to use the technology. The status quo preference explained 62% of the variance in continuance intentions. Both the value of status quo $( \beta = 0 . 6 0 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 ; \mathrm { H 1 } )$ and commitment to status quo (β $= 0 . 2 4 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 ; \mathrm { H 2 } )$ significantly determined users’ continuance in tentions, thus supporting H1 and H2. We found that the value of status quo had a significant impact on commitment to status quo $( \beta = 0 . 4 8 ; p <$ 0.001), thus supporting H3. We hypothesized that habit and trust will have a positive effect on users’ value of status quo (H4a/H5a) and commitment to status quo (H4b/H5b). We also hypothesized trust in technology will have a positive effect on habit (H5c). We found habit and trust explained 53% of the variance in the value of status quo and 59% of the variance in commitment to status quo. These outcomes indicated that habit was a significant determinant to users’ value of status quo $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 ; p < . 0 0 1 )$ and commitment to status quo $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 ; p$ $< 0 . 0 1 )$ , thus supporting H4a and H4b, respectively. Trust was also a significant determinant of the value of status quo $( \beta = 0 . 3 7 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ users’ commitment to status quo $( \beta = 0 . 5 5 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and habit $( \beta =$ $0 . 2 4 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus supporting H5a, H5b, and H5c, respectively.

## 4.2.1. Results

Similar to study 1, we analyzed our data using PLS. Table 3 presents the CAs, ICRs, descriptive statistics, correlations, and AVEs. We again examined reliability using Cas and ICRs, and convergent and discrimi nant validity using standardized factor loadings and √AVE. All scales exhibited adequate reliability, with CAs and ICRs over 0.70. Like in study 1, the common bias tests revealed no problems.

The structural model results are presented in Fig. 3. Most of the hypotheses were supported. The status quo preference explained 44% of the variance in continuance intentions. Both the value of status quo $( \beta =$ $0 . 1 7 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 $ ) and commitment to status quo $( \beta = 0 . 1 9 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ had a positive and a significant effect on continuance intentions, thus sup porting H1 and H2, respectively. Value of status quo had a significant effect on commitment to status quo $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , thus sup porting H3. We found habit and trust explained 16% of the variance in the value of status quo and 21% of the variance in commitment to status quo. ## 5. Discussion

Incorporating the key tenets of UTAUT and prospect theory, we conceptualized value of status quo and commitment to status quo as two new constructs to explain continuance intentions. In doing so, this research answers the call for a paradigm shift in expanding the scope of the technology adoption literature by using a value appreciation perspective to explain continuance intentions. We conducted two empirical studies to test our proposed research model. The proposed model had strong empirical support in the first study and somewhat weaker in study 2. In study 1, we explained 62% variance in continuance intentions; and, in study 2, we explained 44% variance in continuance intentions. Our findings suggest the two new constructs propo sed—namely, the value of status quo and commitment to status quo—as determinants of continuance intentions. The significant effect of the value of status quo suggests that users’ conscious and rational evaluation outcomes of the use of technology and its alternatives act as key criteria in determining users’ continuance intentions. The significant effect of users’ commitment to status quo on continuance intentions suggests that the users keep their personal attachment and avoid the sacrifice of their cognitive competence and resource investment.

We present a holistic model explaining users’ decision-making pro cesses for replacing an incumbent technology with a new technology. This model not only examines the effects of the two new constructs proposed in this research (i.e., the value of status quo and commitment to status quo), but also highlights the interplay between other known conscious and subconscious determinants of continuance intentions (i. e., trust, UTAUT constructs, and habit). Both habit and trust were found to be determinants of the value of and commitment to status quo, expanding beyond the UTAUT constructs. This demonstrates that these subconscious motivations are the cornerstone to the sustained usertechnology relationship continuance and value appreciation. These findings indicate that not only system-like characteristics, such as reli ability and functionality, are important to users, but also human-like characteristics, such as ability and benevolence $[ 4 3 , 5 6 , 6 4 ]$ , are vital.

Users trust in the technology allows them to explore the technology’s capa bilities and customize the technology to their personal needs. Such customization would help build a user-technology relationship fostering commitment. Although such a relationship is likely in the context of personal technologies—e.g., a smartphone—it is less likely and relevant in the context of organizational technologies—e.g., a financial system —because the consequences of using a technology at work are more distal to the individual, especially from an emotional perspective.

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

This work makes several contributions to the literature on technol ogy adoption in general and continuance intentions in particular. First, this research builds on UTAUT2—a well-established and widely used theory that explains users’ adoption and subsequent use of tech nology—and used prospect theory—a well-known psychological and behavioral economic theory—as the theoretical bases to understand users’ decisions about continuance intentions. This research used the concepts of value function, risk aversion, and the status quo preference as the users’ assessment criteria to explain continuance intentions. Two new constructs of status quo preference—namely, the value of status quo and commitment to status quo—explained how users make their continuance intentions decisions. This research also clarified the ante cedents of the two status quo constructs. Given the empirical evidence supported our hypotheses that the value of status quo and users commitment to status quo had an effect on continuance intentions, our findings enrich the technology adoption literature.

Second, prior IS research has investigated the role of various con structs, such as users’ demographics, attitudes, beliefs, motivations, and affect as well as technologies’ benefits and social influence, to explain IS continuance intentions [45,50,88]. Recently, as noted earlier, there have been calls for alternate theoretical explanations in the technology adoption literature (see $[ 2 , 4 , 5 , 7 5 , 8 8 ] )$ . Our research answers this call. We examine the IS continuance intentions decisions from a value appreciation perspective. This perspective proposes that users’ contin uance intentions are their rational status quo preference, which is a conscious decision-making outcome. Users take their value appreciation of the status quo as the reference state for comparing the gain, loss, and costs of alternatives. The high and positive difference between the value of status quo and the values of alternatives determines users’ status quo preference to continue using the technology. These findings deepen our understanding of users’ status quo preference, its antecedents, and its effects on continuance intentions. In addition, as status quo preference is the outcome of users’ conscious and rational decisions, our findings enrich the conventional wisdom of reasoned action and planned behavior for explaining how users make rational decisions and form intentions.

Third, taken together, the findings from this work are generalizable to a variety of contexts. The context for the first study was consumer use of a commonly used technology—i.e., a smartphone. The context of the second study was employees dealing with a new system implemented in an organization. Prior research (e.g., [34,91]) has called for paying special attention to the context by arguing that, in some cases, context is important in ultimately establishing generalizability and/or finding boundary conditions. That our findings varied somewhat between the consumer and organizational settings suggests that there may be po tential for further investigation and possible contextualization of the model.

Finally, our findings not only advance our understanding of contin uance intentions, but also expand the conventional wisdom of contin uance intentions with insights through the integration of habit and trust into a holistic model. ## 5.2. Practical implications

Our work provides insights for practitioners. First, our findings inform users that continuance intentions are rational and economic decisions that save money, reduce effort, and maximize the value of prior investment in the technology. This decision is based on the un spoken outcome of a comparative value assessment of the technology and its available alternatives. There is a value equilibrium or optimal point at which the value of technology equals that of alternatives. The value balance point is also affected by time because, as time goes on, the value of new alternatives increases, whereas the value of the incumbent technology decreases. For certain technologies (e.g., smartphones), time may become the most important factor in comparative value assessment. According to our findings (i.e., mean values in study 1) and the pace of current smartphone advancements, the time point for smartphones is expected to be two-and-a-half years.

Second, the factors of users’ trust and habit had positive effects on users’ assessment of status quo preference that delay the time frame of switching to alternatives. Our findings show evidence of this effect such that many users used their smartphones for three or more years. For these users, they can enjoy a longer period of use, extend value appre ciation, and gain better return from the initial procurement investment. Trust and habit may also result in users ignoring new product launches and refusing to change their technology. Users may be unable to enjoy the latest fashionable technologies, but this could be compensated by the gains from the core determinants of status quo preference and the statu quo itself. For the suppliers of new technologies, these factors may reduce user demands for the purchase of new technologies, slow the product development life cycle, and provide time and opportunities to innovate radical features, functionalities, and even new technologies. Clearly, it is not easy to persuade users to make changes, but they do make changes. Their changes or upgrade decisions depend on whether the new alternatives can provide relatively greater advantage, radical features, and high value compared to users’ incumbent technologies. It should also be noted that the extent of change can vary greatly from transformational to incremental [103] and can play a role. Therefore, smartphone vendors need to take a customer-oriented approach, care fully plan their product series, precisely time the users’ momentum, and provide new technologies just in time to meet user demands.

Finally, our findings indicate that continuance intentions are not purely a decision of users. They may be considered supplier-managed user intentions. In fact, suppliers can impact users’ trust and habit through cultivating users’ knowledge, capability, and skills with useroriented training support. Such support can reduce users’ trial-and error learning processes, reduce their time and effort invested in the technology, and reduce users’ technology-specific competence. With support from the supplier, users can quickly develop their habits, and in line with our findings, users’ trust can be engendered. Eventually, as users feel less dependent on the incumbent technology, their apprecia tion for the value of the status quo will not be too high. This low status quo preference may increase users’ intentions of switching to a new technology. In organizational contexts, support, especially peer support, may be vital to breaking away from the status quo (e.g., [76,77]).

## 5.3. Limitations and future research directions

We note some key limitations of our work. First, our research focuses on the overall technology rather than a collection of specific feature sets. Jasperson et al. [36] proposed a conceptual framework to argue that, in the post-adoption stage, most users use a narrow band of features and grow comfortable with these features. In the context of our first study, one might argue that when a new smartphone offers some key new features, users might be motivated to switch. Although our work is consistent with a vast body of research in technology adoption including UTAUT/UTAUT2, future research should consider studying adoption at the feature level as the basis for testing the model presented in this research. Second, customer commitment is conceptualized as affective commitment, normative commitment, and continuance commitment [3], but we only focused on continuance commitment. Future research should examine the effect of affective commitment and normative commitment on continuance intentions. Third, we did not consider the purchase price of smartphones in our first study, though this is alleviated to some extent due to our inclusion of price value. The price could be an important factor especially for low-income users. Low-income users may consider a smartphone purchase to be a significant cost. This could in crease their perceived value of the incumbent technology and reduce their motivation to adopt a new technology. We limited our analyses to iPhone and Samsung users only (study 1) because the price for their products was comparable. Future research could include user’s income and the original purchase price explicitly in the research model. Finally we limited our research to two international brands in the first study. Future research could include domestic smartphone samples to compare and contrast users’ continuance intentions tied to different brands, identify consumers' preferences to international and domestic brands and search for the reasons behind their preferences. The generalizability of our findings should be interpreted with consideration to the research context. It would be useful for future work to replicate this study in other contexts to test the models’ generalizability of our findings.

## 6. Conclusions

This work advanced our extant understanding of IS continuance in tentions. We control for the predictors of UTAUT2 and leveraged pros pect theory to present users’ status quo preferences as new predictors of continuance intentions. We presented a holistic model that integrated status quo preferences with the conventional knowledge on IS contin uance use—i.e., users’ trust and habit. We found support for our model.

We found that the users’ status quo preferences were strong and sig nificant determinants of continuance intentions. Trust and habit were predictors of users’ status quo preferences. Our findings suggest that users’ continuance intentions are rational, conscious decisions such that high, positive difference between the value of status quo and the value of alternatives, along with the commitment to status quo, determines users’ continuance intentions.

## Appendix B: Key findings from the pilot study

We conducted a pilot study among smartphone users in Hong Kong, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen. The goal of the pilot was primarily to test the reliability and validity of our scales, especially the new scales. More details for these measures are presented in conjunction with study 1.

In each of the four cities, Hong Kong, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen, 500 paper questionnaires each were distributed to evenly stratified smartphone users: university students, company and institutional staff, managerial or administrative staff, and professionals. Completed surveys were returned within two months of distribution. We received 1.558 responses for a response rate of about 78%. Of this sample, about 42% were women average age was about 34 years (SD = 11.19 years), and almost 80% had a college degree. We dropped 52 incomplete responses. To investigate whether some significant differences existed between the survey samples, we randomly chose fifteen observable variables and four demographic variables from the samples and ran ANOVA tests for the observable variables and $\chi ^ { 2 }$ tests for the demographical variables. The results confirmed that there were no significant differences among the samples. Therefore, we combined the four samples together as the empirical dataset for further analysis. Our dataset had 538 iPhone users, 648 Samsung users, and 372 other brand smartphone users. Our analysis then focused on the respondent of iPhone and Samsung users due to the comparable sample sizes and these being international brands. This resulted in a revised sample size of 1,186.

All the items had Cronbach alpha values greater than 0.70, thus supporting reliability. All items had high loadings (≥ 0.71) on their own constructs and low cross-loadings, thus suggesting convergent and discriminant validity. The high factor loadings (≥ 0.71) and the high AVE (≥ 0.50) values support convergent validity for all scales. √AVE values of all scales were higher than the correlations between the construct and other constructs, thus supporting discriminant validity. Thus, the scales were reliable and valid—and gave us confidence to proceed with the main studies.
