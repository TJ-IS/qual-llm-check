# Intention to use smartwatch health applications: A regulatory fit and locus of control perspective

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Mobile health Smartwatch health apps Regulatory focus Motivation Adoption

## A B S T R A C T

Smartwatch applications are growing in popularity, but little is known about why people are motivated to use different smartwatch health apps. Drawing on regulatory focus theory, we propose that the fit between in dividuals’ regulatory focus (i.e., primarily prevention or primarily promotion) and the regulatory properties of smartwatch health apps (i.e., primarily promotion apps or primarily prevention apps) helps to explain in dividuals’ motivations related to intentions to use smartwatch health apps. Further, we show that individuals internal health locus of control strengthens the effect of regulatory fit. This study contributes by demonstrating how regulatory focus and locus of control influence intentions to use smartwatch health apps.

## Introduction

In the mobile health application (app) market, smartwatch health apps are gaining in popularity [1]. A smartwatch is a wrist-worn, net worked watch with various sensors and a small touch screen [1,2]. Smartwatch apps are unique in that they offer focused affordances, given the limited screen size and form factor, but also in that they have access to sophisticated and granular sensing capabilities (e.g., motion, heart rate, etc.). In this study, we focus specifically on mobile health apps offered via smartwatches.

Mobile health apps offered via smartwatches can provide convenient tools for either promoting healthy behavior or preventing adverse health outcomes. Smartwatch mobile health apps can help in addressing some of the underlying problems with the current healthcare system, including high costs and low accessibility of healthcare services, by empowering people to better manage their own health regardless of time and place [3–5]. For instance, one benefit of such smartwatch health apps is that they provide users better convenience for some applications (e.g., engaging in fitness activities or monitoring heart rhythms) than smartphone health apps because they are operated on the wrist of users. Additionally, both the breadth and accuracy of what can be measured on a smartwatch keeps improving, enabling real-time monitoring of many physiological measures [6]. The current generation of smartwatches even includes sensing capabilities for electrocardiograms (ECGs) and blood pressure. Thus, with the recent advancement of smartwatches, mobile health applications are evolving into user-centered tools that not only help users to engage in health promoting behaviors (e.g., exercise) but also to self-monitor and prevent more serious health conditions in a cost-efficient manner [6,7]. Accordingly, the number of smartwatch health apps is growing in the application market, and people are more frequently using smartwatch health applications to help guide activities associated with health and well-being [8].

Despite the increasing popularity of smartwatch health apps, how ever, few studies have investigated the factors that motivate the inten tion to use smartwatch health apps. While previous research has investigated the intention to use information technologies (IT) [9–11, 12], this body of work has focused on individuals’ general perceptions toward IT (e.g., usefulness of IT), offering a somewhat limited theoret ical explanation of why individuals with different motivations are heterogeneously motivated to use a particular type of IT. Individuals have different motivational needs, and smartwatch health apps are designed to fulfill specific and heterogenous needs of individuals, such as pro moting physical fitness or preventing adverse health outcomes. Thus, the degree to which a particular type of smartwatch health app appeals to different individuals may vary. Additionally, from the practical standpoint of guiding developers and marketers of smartwatch health apps, previous IS models (e.g., UTAUT) [10,11] do not provide sufficient guidance as to which type of app individuals will find most appealing.

The aim of this study is to explain why individuals with different motivational characteristics are inclined to use different types of smartwatch health applications. We focus on the pre-usage stage within IT use theory [12], with the goal of understanding usage intentions in this emerging domain of IT use. Although individuals’ perceptions to ward IT in the “pre-use” phase may be modified by experience [12], intention to use in the “pre-use” phase is a critical factor that positively influences “post-use” user acceptance [13] and correlates with post-usage intention [14]. In addition, in the context of smartwatch health applications, pre-usage intentions may influence app downloads. Accordingly, understanding the mechanisms that shape the intention to use smartwatch health applications in the “pre-use” phase is important. Our approach is consistent with research on adoption and diffusion of innovations [15], particularly with models that seek to describe and explain early adoption [16–18]. While knowledge of the entire diffusion process is ideal, when innovations are emerging, understanding what drives pre-usage intentions is vital, as without such knowledge critical mass and full diffusion may be stymied by negative perceptions.

Given that individuals’ motivations guide their behavior [19] and that technologies have specific properties designed to fulfill specific individuals’ motivational needs [20,21], we argue that a good fit be tween individuals’ motivational characteristics and the properties of a smartwatch health app will motivate individuals to want to use the app. Also, given that smartwatch health apps rely on self-management, we suggest that individuals’ motivational strength toward engagement in self-health management strengthens the effect of this fit.

As a first step toward explaining the role of fit in smartwatch health app usage intentions, we draw on regulatory focus theory. Regulatory focus theory suggests that individuals can have two distinct motiva tional orientations: promotion focus and prevention focus [22,23]. Promotion and prevention focus are separate and distinct constructs and thus may vary independently. However, most individuals tend to be oriented primarily toward either a promotion focus or a prevention focus. A promotion focus drives an individual’s need for growth and development. Therefore, people with high promotion focus tend to pursue a desired end state. In comparison, a prevention focus drives an individual’s need for safety and security. Therefore, people with high prevention focus tend to pursue avoidance of losses [23–26]. Prior research has consistently shown that regulatory focus affects the choices individuals make through “regulatory fit” [27,28]. Specifically, when people experience “regulatory fit,” which is a match between their regulatory focus and the means used to approach a goal [29], they experience a feeling of rightness and this is transferred to their assess ment of a chosen object, increasing its perceived value [28,30].

Second, to exploit the concept of regulatory fit between the regula tory focus of a potential app user and the nature of the app itself, we categorize smartwatch health apps as having primarily a health pro motion focus (called promotion apps) or a negative health outcome prevention focus (called prevention apps). This regulatory categoriza tion is based on the types of outcomes to be expected from using such apps and the required goal pursuit strategies needed to use the apps. For example, a workout app can be categorized as a promotion type app, which helps users gain physical strength (i.e., health promotion outcome) by engaging in a workout (i.e., health promotion strategy). We acknowledge that a workout app could also be classified as a prevention app, especially if it aids in increasing physical activity with the goal of reducing the potential for chronic disease. However, in this study, as explained later when we discuss our experimental design, we explicitly positioned the workout app as being designed for promoting strength and endurance. This approach was designed to create regulatory fit fo those with a promotion focus and to avoid any possible confound due to confusion over whether the workout app was primarily promotion or prevention oriented. In comparison, a heart monitoring app can be classified as a prevention app, which helps users prevent adverse health outcomes (e.g., heart attacks and strokes) associated with heart ar rhythmias (i.e., negative health outcome prevention focus) by moni toring their heart rhythm (i.e., prevention strategy). While we do not claim that these two categories are exhaustive or mutually exclusive, we argue that our categorization has critical implications in practice because promotion apps (e.g., exercise and fitness apps) make up the largest portion of the wellness management category of the digital health app market [8], and prevention apps best reflect the direction of the evolution of smartwatches. Additionally, promoting healthy be haviors and preventing adverse health outcomes are two main purposes of healthcare management that have been used as a frame in many previous healthcare research studies [31–35]. While it is already known that the “regulatory fit” experienced by an individual while using products and services may vary depending on product/service charac teristics, the impact of the association between regulatory focus and the type of product/service on individuals’ intention to use has not yet been examined. In this study, we suggest that the influence of regulatory focus on intention to use a particular type of smartwatch health app depends upon regulatory fit.

Third, we argue that individuals’ motivational strength toward engagement in self-health management is represented by their internal health locus of control (I-HLOC). I-HLOC refers to a person’s tendency to attribute health status to their behaviors rather than to external factors beyond their control [36,37]. Prior research suggests that people with high I-HLOC invest more in their health than people with low I-HLOC [37,38]. Thus, I-HLOC is an important indicator of an individual’s motivational strength toward engaging in healthy behavior and may moderate the impact of regulatory focus on the intention to use mobile health apps. More specifically, we theorize that I-HLOC moderates the impact of promotion focus on intention to use a promotion app, and that it also moderates the impact of prevention focus on intention to use a prevention app.

In summary, this study suggests that motivation for use of smart watch health apps starts with a fit between the regulatory focus of the app (promotion or prevention) and the regulatory focus of the user (promotion or prevention) and is moderated by I-HLOC. Motivated by this line of thinking, this study seeks to answer the following research questions:

RQ1: Does the fit between smartwatch health apps (promotion and prevention-oriented apps) and an individual’s regulatory focus affect the intention to use these apps?

RQ2: How does an individual’s I-HLOC affect the relationship be tween their regulatory focus and their intention to use different types of smartwatch health apps (i.e., promotion and prevention-oriented apps)?

To answer these research questions, we conduct a within-subjects, scenario-based experiment using a crossover design [39] in which the application type (promotion app or prevention app) is manipulated. This study measures subjects’ intention to use a smartwatch health app as the dependent variable of interest. As in the context of this study, when the behavior is volitional and the individual has the information to shape his or her behavioral intention, behavioral intention is a good predictor for the individual’s future behavior [40,41]. In addition, current smart watch users are early adopters with unique personal characteristics [42]. Therefore, at this point in the adoption process, when smart watches have not yet been widely adopted, it is appropriate to focus on intention to use rather than actual behavior.

## Theoretical background

This study is anchored in the IS use literature [43] and particularly in the “pre-use” phase of IS use [12]. Previous IS use literature has investigated factors that influence IS use at both the “pre-use” and “post-use” phases. In the domain of “pre-use,” individuals’ general perceptions toward IT, such as usefulness, ease of use, performance expectations, and enjoyment, have been suggested as determinants of IS adoption [10,44,45], whereas, in the “post-use” domain, the confirma tion of these perceptions has been proposed as the key predictor of satisfaction and continuance intention [12,45]. Although individuals perceptions toward IT in the “pre-use” phase may be modified by experience [12], the previous literature consistently shows that in dividuals’ perceptions in the “pre-use” phase are positively associated with “post-use” perceptions [46], and that the attitude toward IT in the “pre-use” phase has a positive effect on the “post-use” attitude [12,47]. In addition, previous research demonstrates that intention to use in the “pre-use” phase positively influences “post-use” user acceptance [13], and pre-usage intention correlates with post-usage intention [14]. Therefore, individuals’ perceptions toward IT and their intention to use it in the “pre-use” phase are important not only in the pre-use adoption of IT but also in the post-use adoption and continuance intention. In addition, in the context of mobile application, such as smartwatch health applications, pre-consumption perceptions are important because pre-consumption perception can influence app downloads, which are essential in creating network effects. There are many stories of in novations failing due to incorrectly assessing market needs or prefer ences (e.g., Google Glass), which could also occur in the smartwatch health app domain if insufficient attention is given to pre-usage per ceptions. Accordingly, the previous mobile application literature has investigated factors influencing intention to use (or download/install) mobile applications in the “pre-use” phase [48–50].

For an emerging IS use market such as smartwatch apps, many consumers have yet to use and experience such new forms of use. Of particular importance in this context is understanding what would motivate use of apps that may impact an individual’s health. While we know that general mechanisms such as ease-of-use and usefulness are important considerations [10,11], such mechanisms do not provide nuanced insights into what might motivate specific types of users (i.e., varying by regulatory focus and I-HLOC, in our case) to consider using specific types of apps (i.e., varying by regulatory focus design, in our case), particularly when the apps are delivered on a new device (i.e., a smartwatch). Therefore, our research design focuses on motivational fit between specific types of potential users and specific types of smart watch health apps.

## Smartwatch health apps and motivators for the use of such apps

Unlike smartphones, smartwatches can monitor physiological mea sures in real time with various sensors, and the accuracy of monitoring is improving with time [6]. While previous generations of smartwatches were able to measure pulse, step count, and estimate calories burned, the current generation of smartwatches can even measure blood pres sure and produce an ECG. As smartwatches continue to become more advanced, people may be able to use them to complement face-to-face visits with healthcare professionals. Indeed, the technology holds the promise of allowing people to monitor their health conditions in a cost-efficient manner $[ 6 , 7 ]$ . For example, today’s smartwatches can enable people to monitor themselves for cardiac arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation [6]. Therefore, with the advancement of smartwatches, smartwatch health apps are evolving into user-centered tools for pre venting adverse health outcomes, raising questions regarding how in dividual differences and prevention-focused characteristics of smartwatch health apps influence intention to use such apps. Addi tionally, since the smartwatch health apps work on the user’s wrist, they can provide more convenience for some applications (e.g., engaging in fitness activities or monitoring heart rhythms) than smartphone health apps. For example, when a person does sit-ups while following the guidance of a workout app that visually represents the exercise along with a countdown timer, wrist-worn smartwatches make it easier to follow the displayed exercise and check the timer as compared to using a smartphone (see Fig. 1).

Despite the advantages and increasing popularity of smartwatch health apps, only a few studies have examined the factors that motivate individuals to use them [6,51]. While motivators for the use of IT have been extensively studied [9] by drawing on well-recognized theories such as the technology acceptance model (TAM) [44], the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [52], and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) [10,11], the focus has been on the general perception of users regarding the target IT (e.g., ease of use and use fulness), environmental factors (e.g., social influences), and facilitating conditions as antecedents of intention to use IT [53]. However, these previous research studies on intention to use IT focusing on the afore mentioned theories cannot explain why individuals with different mo tivations may be more or less inclined to want to use particular types of smartwatch health apps. Smartwatch health apps rely on self-management [54,55] and have their own unique orientations (e.g., promoting healthy behaviors or preventing adverse health outcomes). Since individuals have different motivational needs and every smart watch health app is designed to fulfill specific motivational needs of individuals, the extent to which individuals intend to use a certain app will vary from person to person depending upon the fit between an in dividual’s motivational need and the designed purpose of each app (e.g., fitness app or heart monitoring app). In this study, we advance a new model that incorporates the motivational characteristics of individuals as well as the designed characteristics of smartwatch health apps to explain why an individual with specific motivational characteristics is inclined to want to use certain types of smartwatch health apps.

## Regulatory focus

Regulatory focus theory (Higgins 1997) suggests that individuals have two self-regulatory orientations: promotion focus and prevention focus. Promotion focus is driven by the need for growth and develop ment, whereas prevention focus is driven by the need for safety and security [24, 25]. Therefore, promotion-focused individuals pursue desired end states (e.g., pursuit of gains) and are sensitive to the pres ence and absence of positive outcomes. In contrast. prevention focused individuals prioritize avoiding undesired end states (e.g., avoidance of losses) and therefore are sensitive to the presence and absence of negative outcomes [26,30,56]. Prior research has demonstrated that regulatory focus influences individuals’ perceptions and attitudes, and ultimately their decision-making [24,27,29,57]. For instance, the use of mobile health (mHealth) apps has been shown to be conditioned on whether or not an individual is trying to prevent chronic disease advancement or promote health behavior when perceived vulnerability to chronic disease is low [29]. Thus, we know that regulatory focus plays a significant role in the use of mHealth apps offered on smartphones, but we do not vet know if similar motivations impact smartwatch health applications.

Regulatory focus influences individuals’ attitudes and behaviors through “regulatory fit.” Regulatory fit is a match between an in dividual’s regulatory focus and the way he or she pursues a goal (i.e., goal pursuit strategy). As a consequence of experiencing regulatory fit, people develop a positive attitude and engage more strongly in what they are doing [28,30]. Promotion focus, which is driven by the need for growth and leads to the pursuit of gains, fits better with approach strategies striving toward gains; in comparison, prevention focus, which is driven by the need for safety and leads to the avoidance of losses, fits better with avoidance strategies guarding against losses [26]. Prior research has shown that people feel positive emotion and are more persuaded and motivated when their goal pursuit strategies fit their regulatory focus [58–60].

Prior research has shown that individuals assign different impor tance to alternatives depending on the fit between the choice alterna tives and their regulatory focus [28,61]. Further, Higgins et al. [62] demonstrated that regulatory fit increases the perceived value of objects. When it comes to product choice. individuals with a promotion focus show more interest in a product’s comfort-oriented features, whereas individuals with a prevention focus show more interest in a product’s safety-oriented aspects [63]. Similarly, Bettman and Sujan [64] demonstrated that individuals’ preferences for a product with creativity features or a product with reliability features depend on which features better fit their regulatory focus. It has also been shown that consumers more positively evaluate advertisements when the advertisement fits their regulatory focus [63].

In the health context, research has shown that people exhibit greater engagement in health-related behavior such as fruit and vegetable intake when they receive tailored messages that fit their regulatory focus [65]. Further, people perceive health-related information as more valid and easier to process when the information fits their regulatory focus [66].

As noted earlier, the impact of the association between regulatory focus and the type of product/service on individuals’ intention to use has not yet been examined. Given that different products and services have their own promotion or prevention characteristics [67], smart watch health apps can be categorized into promotion-oriented apps and prevention-oriented apps based on the outcomes associated with using the apps (i.e., promotion outcome vs. prevention outcome) and the required goal pursuit strategies (i.e., pursuit of gains vs. avoidance of losses) needed to use the apps. Following this approach, we explore how regulatory focus as a motivational factor affects individuals’ intention to use promotion- vs. prevention-oriented smartwatch health apps. We propose that differences in individuals’ regulatory focus orientations give rise to differences in use intentions toward different types of smartwatch health apps depending upon the fit between the individual’s regulatory focus and the type of app being considered.

## Internal health locus of contro

Given that smartwatch health apps rely on self-management [54,55], we need to examine how individuals’ motivational attitude toward engagement in self-health management influences intention to use smartwatch health apps. Locus of control reflects an individual’s belief about the degree to which outcomes in life are determined by his or her behavior [38,55]. People with a high internal locus of control believe that the outcomes in life stem mostly from their behavior; in compari son, people with an external locus of control believe that outcomes stem mostly from external factors beyond their control [38,68].

I-HLOC refers to a person’s tendency to attribute their health status to their own behaviors [36,37]. People with high I-HLOC believe that “they themselves have control over the status of their physical health” [30, p.171]. Previous research on I-HLOC has focused on the relation ship between this construct and healthy behaviors. For example, Steptoe and Wardle [69] demonstrated that high I-HLOC is associated with in dividuals exhibiting healthier behaviors such as exercise, salt avoidance, and eating fiber. Additionally, Nafradi et al. [70] showed that high I-HLOC promotes medication adherence. Researchers in this area have argued that people with high I-HLOC invest more in their health because they expect a higher return on investment and get more pleasure and satisfaction out of engaging in healthy behaviors than people with low I-HLOC [37,38]. Even though many studies found significant relation ships between I-HLOC and healthy behaviors, the significance of these links has been inconsistent across studies, and the strength of these links vary considerably among individuals [37,55,71]. In a review study that examined the relationship between I-HLOC and healthy behaviors, Cheng et al. [37] demonstrated that the I-HLOC-healthy diet relationship is stronger for the samples containing more women and argued that this result might be due to women’s negative attitude toward foods containing less nutritional value such as snacks. Thus, it is plausible that previous inconsistent results for the relationships between I-HLOC and healthy behaviors could be explained by individuals having varying degrees of motivational preference toward healthy behaviors.

## Model development and hypotheses

In this section, we present our research model and hypothese (Fig. 2).

As described earlier, promotion apps are those in which the out comes of using apps satisfy the need for growth and require users to engage in an approach strategy (i.e., the pursuit of gains). In compari son, prevention apps are those in which the outcomes of using the apps satisfy the need for safety, and the apps require users to engage in avoidance strategies (i.e., avoidance of losses). For this study, we chose to examine a workout app as being representative of promotion apps in that it can help users gain physical strength (i.e., promotion outcome) by engaging in a workout (i.e., approach strategy). We chose a heart monitoring app as being representative of prevention apps in that it can help users prevent heart complications and strokes (i.e., prevention outcome) by monitoring their heart rhythm (i.e., avoidance strategy). While we acknowledge that depending upon its functionality and use a workout app could also be framed as having a prevention orientation, we explicitly positioned the workout app described in our experiment as being designed for promoting strength and endurance. This was done to ensure that subjects in our experiment who had more of a promotion focus would perceive the workout app to have a greater regulatory fit than the heart monitoring app. Table 1 presents descriptions for both the promotion app and the prevention app we chose to study.

The descriptions of the apps used in this study were based upon actual descriptions of similar apps available in the market. For the workout app, we based our description on the “StrongLifts” app. For the heart monitoring app, we based our description on the “Apple Heart Study” app. We chose these two apps because the “StrongLifts” app best reflected the characteristics of a promotion app, and the “Apple Heart Study” app best reflected the characteristics of a prevention app. Furthermore, we wanted to model our descriptions based on reputable apps (e.g., those with a high average rating) that were gender neutral, and these two apps met these criteria.

We theorize that due to regulatory fit, differences in individuals regulatory focus will affect their intentions to use different types of smartwatch health apps. Specifically, we hypothesize that a promotion focus positively influences intention to use a promotion app, and a prevention focus positively influences intention to use a prevention app. According to prior studies, individuals selectively pay attention to in formation that is congruent with their regulatory focus [30,59]. More over, when people experience regulatory fit, their attitude toward a product or service becomes more positive and motivation to use it is enhanced [30,60]. This is because people will perceive the product or service as being more useful when they find the outcomes associated with it and the goal pursuit strategies required to use it to be consistent with their regulatory focus. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Individuals with a promotion focus are more likely to have a higher intention to use promotion apps.

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Individuals with a prevention focus are more likely to have a higher intention to use prevention apps.

We further theorize that I-HLOC strengthens the impacts of regula tory focus on the intention to use smartwatch health apps. Specifically, I-HLOC should (1) positively moderate the impact of promotion focus on promotion apps and (2) positively moderate the impact of prevention focus on prevention apps. According to previous studies, people with high I-HLOC invest more in their health than people with low I-HLOC because they expect a higher return on investment and get more plea sure and satisfaction from engaging in healthy behaviors than people with low I-HLOC [37,38]. In addition, a previous review article sug gested that the positive influence of I-HLOC on healthy behaviors in creases as individuals perceive greater value associated with healthy behaviors [37]. As suggested in this study, the value that people perceive to be associated with smartwatch health apps may depend on the degree of fit between their regulatory focus and the type of smart watch health app. When there is regulatory fit, we expect that I-HLOC will strengthen the relationship between regulatory focus and intention to use a smartwatch health app. Thus, we advance the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): I-HLOC moderates the effect of a promotion focus on the intention to use a promotion app, such that the positive effect of a promotion focus on the intention to use a promotion app is stronger when I-HLOC is high than when I-HLOC is low.

Hypothesis 4 (H4): I-HLOC moderates the effect of a prevention focus on the intention to use a prevention app, such that the positive effect of a prevention focus on the intention to use a prevention app is stronger when I-HLOC is high than when I-HLOC is low.

## Method

## Experimental design and participants

We conducted a within-subjects experiment using a crossover design [39] in which the application type (promotion app vs. prevention app) was manipulated. Participants were randomly assigned to read either the descriptions of a workout app (i.e., a promotion app) or a heart monitoring app (i.e., a prevention app). Participants then answered a set of measurement items about their intention to use the app, after which participants read the description of the other app and answered ques tions about their intention to use it. The experiment was conducted via Amazon Mechanical Turk, a web-based crowdsourcing service. A total of 98 subjects participated in the study and were compensated 80 cents each for doing so. Sixty-eight usable responses were obtained; thirty responses were excluded from further analysis due to manipulation and attention check failures, which we determined based on manipulation check and attention check questions embedded in the measurement instrument. The average age of participants was 41.7 years, 53% of the participants were male (n=36), and 47% were female (n=32).

## Procedure

The sequence of tasks involved in the experiment is described in Fig. 3, and the entire protocol for the experiment is presented in Appendix A. First, participants were asked to read a set of instructions and a brief introduction to smartwatch health apps. Participants were asked to assume they already owned a smartwatch, always wear this smartwatch, and have full access to all app features on the smartwatch at any time. Next, participants were given information about both the workout app and the heart monitoring app and asked about their in tentions to use each of these apps. The order in which participants received information about the two different apps was randomized. Next, participants were asked to answer a set of questions which included manipulation checks, as well as measures for regulatory focus, I-HLOC, and health anxiety (which served as a control variable). Finally, participants were asked to provide demographic information.

## Construct measures

Our dependent variable, intention to use the workout app and the heart monitoring app, was measured using three items that were adapted from Venkatesh et al. [10]. To measure regulatory focus, an eighteen-item measure (i.e., nine for promotion focus, nine for prevention focus) from Lockwood et al. [59] was used. Additionally, a five-item measure of I-HLOC and a five-item measure of health anxiety were adopted from Snell et al. [36].

## Control variables

We included three control variables: age, gender, and health anxiety. Age and gender were adopted as control variables because previous research studies demonstrated that age and gender influence mobile health adoption [72–74]. Health anxiety was included as a control var iable because it is potentially associated with both regulatory focus and intention to use mobile health apps. Prior research has demonstrated that health anxiety influences healthcare utilization such as visiting a doctor [75]. Also, health anxiety can interact with regulatory focus in influencing individuals' health-related attitudes and behaviors [76].

## Manipulation check

To assess the effectiveness of the application type (promotion or prevention) manipulation, we examined whether participants correctly answered a manipulation check question that prompted them to select two smartwatch apps that they were introduced to from a list of five, including workout app, activity tracking app, heart monitoring app, mental health app, and daily yoga app. Participants who correctly selected both the workout app and the heart monitoring app were included in subsequent data analysis.

## Order effect

We examined whether the order in which participants received in formation about the two different apps influenced their intention to use a workout app or a heart monitoring app. A paired samples t-test showed no statistical differences in use intention between the two groups of participants who received information about the two smartwatch health apps in a different order.

## Common method bias assessment

To evaluate common method bias, we first conducted Harmon’s single factor test [78,79]. As a result of the test, six factors with eigen values greater than one were produced. The first extracted factor accounted for 29.5% of the variance in the data; thus, common method bias is unlikely to be a significant issue in our data because the first extracted factor did not explain most of the variance in our data. Additionally, following the procedure suggested by Podsakoff et al. [78], we assessed the measurement model by adding a common method construct. When we did so, we found that the item loadings, correla tions, and covariances remained stable between the measurement models with and without common method construct. The average dif ference of item loadings between the measurement models with and without a common method construct was .038, and the values of all correlations and covariances were not changed. Therefore, common method bias does not appear to be a significant concern in our data.

## Testing of hypotheses

To test our hypotheses, we used hierarchical OLS regression. Hierarchical regression partitions the variance of the dependent variable based on a set of independent variables which are added incrementally to the regression model. As a result of the Breusch–Pagan test (see Ta bles 3 and 4), which examines whether the variance of errors from a regression depends on the values of the independent variables, our data showed heteroskedasticity; thus, we used robust standard errors, which enables valid statistical inference in the presence of heteroskedasticity [80]. Heteroskedasticity does not bias coefficient estimates and does not influence the interpretation of r-squared statistics in OLS regression [80]. In this study, the statistical inferences using robust standard errors and OLS standard errors were consistent. which lends further robustness to our findings.

First, the effect of a promotion focus on individuals’ intention to use the workout app (H1) was examined. As shown in Model 2w in Table 3, promotion focus had a significant positive effect on the intention to use the workout app $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 7 , ~ t = 2 . 5 6 , ~ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , thus supporting H1. In other words, individuals with a higher promotion focus showed a higher intention to use the workout app than individuals with a lower pro motion focus. In Model 1w, age, gender, health anxiety, and I-HLOC explain 21.1% of the variance in intention to use the workout app. When promotion focus and prevention focus are added (i.e., Model 2w) to Model 1w. they add 9.8% $( \Delta R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 9 8 , F ( 2 , 6 1 ) = 4 . 3 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ to the variance explained. The unique contribution of promotion focus in model 2w is 8.9% $( \Delta R ^ { 2 } { = } 0 . 0 8 9 , F ( 1 , 6 1 ) { = } 7 . 8 2 , p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , which means promotion focus explains 8.9% more variance in intention to use the workout app over and above the variance explained by age, gender, health anxiety, I-HLOC, and prevention focus.

Next, the effect of a prevention focus on individuals’ intention to use the heart monitoring app (H2) was examined. As shown in Model 2h in Table 4, the results indicate that prevention focus had a significant and positive effect on the intention to use the heart monitoring app (β=.345, $t { = } 2 . 6 8 , \ p { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ , supporting H2. In other words, individuals with a higher prevention focus showed a higher intention to use the heart monitoring app than people with lower prevention focus. In Model 1h, age, gender, health anxiety, and I-HLOC explain 6.8% of the variance in intention to use the heart monitoring app. When promotion focus and prevention focus are added (i.e., Model 2h), they add 17.3% $( \varDelta R ^ { 2 } =$ $0 . 1 7 3 , F ( 2 , 6 1 ) = 1 0 . 1 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ to the variance explained. In the post hoc analysis, we examined relationships we were unable to hypothesize based on existing theory, but that could contribute to the extension of theory and deepen our understanding of how individual differences influence intention to use smartwatch health apps. First, we tested the moderating effect of I-HLOC on the relationship between prevention focus and intention to use the workout app. As shown in Model 4w in Table 3, I-HLOC had a significant negative moderating effect on the relationship between prevention focus and intention to use the workout app $( \beta { = } { - } 0 . 1 5 9 , \ t { = } { - } 2 . 4 2 , \ p { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ . Specifically, a high I HLOC weakened the relationship between prevention focus and inten tion to use the workout app. In the hierarchical regression analysis, the interaction term (i.e., Prevention × I-HLOC) explains an additional 3.4% $( \Delta R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 3 4 , F ( 1 , 6 1 ) = 5 . 8 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ of the variance in intention to use the workout app over and above the variance explained by Model 2w.

Fig. 4 illustrates the interactions between I-HLOC and promotion focus and between I-HLOC and prevention focus that were observed. We conducted a simple slope analysis to test whether the slopes are signif icantly different from zero. The results demonstrated that promotion focus had a significant positive influence on intention to use the workout app when I-HLOC is high (1 standard deviation above mean) (β=0.539, t=4.39, p<0.001). However, when I-HLOC is low (1 standard deviation below mean), the influence of promotion focus on intention to use the workout app was not significant (β=0.112, t=1.06). This result implies that the degree of fit between promotion focus and a promotion app has a higher impact on intention to use a promotion app when people have a higher tendency to attribute health status to their behavior.

Additionally, the results from our simple slopes analysis indicate that a prevention focus did not significantly influence intention to use the workout app both when I-HLOC is high (β=-0.170, t=-1.21) and when I-HLOC is low (β=0.152, t=1.10). Our findings show, however, that prevention focus can have a differential impact on the intention to use a promotion app depending on the magnitude of I-HLOC, such that pre vention focus has a positive influence on the intention to use a promo tion app when I-HLOC is low and a negative influence when I-HLOC is high.

Next, we tested the moderating effect of I-HLOC on the relationship between promotion focus and intention to use the heart monitoring app. As shown in Model 3h in Table 4, I-HLOC had no significant moderating effect on the relationship between promotion focus and intention to use the heart monitoring app (β=0.005, t=0.04, p=0.97).

Lastly, we tested the impact of regulatory focus that is incongruent with the type of smartwatch health app on the intention to use smart watch health apps (i.e., the effect of prevention focus on the intention to use a promotion app, and the effect of promotion focus on the intention to use a prevention app). As shown in Model 2w in Table 3, prevention focus did not influence intention to use the workout app (β=-0.03, t=- 0.24). However, as shown in Model 2h in Table 4, promotion focus had a significant positive effect on intention to use the heart monitoring app (β=0.408, t=3.67, p<0.01).

## Discussion

In our experiment, we focus on the pre-usage stage of IS use theory [12] and demonstrate that the fit between smartwatch health apps (promotion app and prevention app) and an individual’s regulatory focus explains some of the heterogenous motivations for the intended use of these apps. We also show that the effect of this fit-on intention to use a promotion app is strengthened by an individual’s motivational strength toward engaging in health behavior (i.e., I-HLOC). Also, we find that I-HLOC weakens the effect of prevention focus on intention to use a promotion app.

However, contrary to our expectation, I-HLOC did not moderate the relationship between prevention focus and intention to use the heart monitoring app. This result could be due to the low power to detect an interaction effect between prevention focus and I-HLOC (i.e., power: .227). However, this result could be due to the low effort of using the heart monitoring app. Users’ intention to use mobile health apps is influenced by the costs and the burdens associated with using such apps [5]. Given that I-HLOC is one of the indicators that represent motiva tional readiness to engage in healthy behavior [38] and that motiva tional readiness is critical when a behavior requires considerable effort (e.g., physical exercise) [81], I-HLOC may influence intention to use an app only when the use of the app requires a high amount of effort. When smartwatch health apps do not require users to invest a lot of effort to use them (e.g., just wearing the smartwatch and waiting for the alert from the app), factors such as motivational readiness and I-HLOC may not have much influence on intention to use the app, as individuals with both low and high I-HLOC can overcome whatever small barriers exist to using the app. In other words, there may be an effort threshold that must be overcome before we see much of an effect associated with I-HLOC and the choice to engage in healthy behavior.

In a post hoc analysis, we examined the impact of promotion focus on intention to use a prevention app, and the impact of prevention focus on intention to use a promotion app. As noted earlier, while prevention focus did not influence intention to use the workout app (i.e., promotion app), promotion focus had a significant positive impact on intention to use the heart monitoring App (i.e., prevention app). These results may be due to the different level of effort associated with using the workout app and the heart monitoring app. Specifically, the users must spend more effort to engage with the workout app (i.e., they must perform a workout) than the heart monitoring app, which merely requires users to wear the smartwatch and wait for a possible alert from the app. De terminants of intention to use products include both the benefits from using it and the cost of using it [82]. Our post hoc analysis suggests that individuals with high promotion focus might find more promotion type of benefit from the heart monitoring app (i.e., prevention app) than individuals with a low promotion focus, and their perceived benefit from using the heart monitoring app might exceed the low “cost” to use it. However, individuals with a high prevention focus might not exhibit higher intention to use the workout app (i.e., promotion app) than in dividuals with low prevention focus because the “cost” of using the app is higher than the perceived benefits from using the app.

## Theoretical implications

This study makes meaningful contributions to several research streams. First, this study contributes to regulatory focus literature by examining the relationships between regulatory focus and product and service characteristics. Even though different products and services have promotion or prevention characteristics [67], few previous studies on regulatory focus have examined how these different product and service designs differentially appeal to individuals’ particular regulatory focus. This study categorizes smartwatch health apps into two catego ries—promotion and prevention—depending on the outcomes associ ated with using apps and the required goal pursuit strategies needed to use them. These two categories have differential appeal depending upon an individual’s regulatory focus and this is reflected in their intentions to use each type of smartwatch health app.

Second, this study contributes to mobile health literature by demonstrating how individual difference factors, such as regulatory focus and I-HLOC, influence the intention to use smartwatch health apps. Even though mobile health is more individual-centered and relies on self-management [54,55], little is known about how individual dif ference factors influence the intended use of different types of smartwatch mobile health apps. This study demonstrates two indepen dent constructs (promotion focus and prevention focus) and one moderating construct (I-HLOC) as individual difference factors that in fluence the intention to use mobile health apps.

Third, this study contributes to I-HLOC literature by examining the interaction effect of I-HLOC and individuals’ motivational factors on the intention to use smartwatch apps. Previous I-HLOC research mostly focused on the correlated relationships between I-HLOC and intention to adopt healthy behaviors; however, the significance of these links was inconsistent across studies and the strength of these links varies considerably among individuals, who might have different attitudes and motivations toward health behaviors [37,71]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that examines the interaction effect of IHLOC and individuals’ motivational factors on the intention to engage in health-related behavior. Specifically, this study empirically shows how I-HLOC interacts with promotion focus and prevention focus in influencing the intention to use promotion apps.

Fourth, this study contributes to the IS use literature by examining why individuals with different motivational orientations consider using certain types of apps in the “pre-use” phase of IS use [12]. Even though previous research studies on the intention to use IS have investigated how individuals’ general perceptions toward IS (e.g., ease of use and usefulness) influence their use intention [10,11], these studies do not provide nuanced insights into how the motivational fit between specific types of potential users and specific types of IS influence potential users use intention, especially in the “pre-use” phase of IS use. This study demonstrates how individuals with different regulatory focus are motivated to use smartwatch health apps that vary by regulatory focus design, offering theoretical explanation of why individuals with different motivations are heterogeneously motivated to use a particular type of IS in the “pre-use” phase of IS use.

## Practical implications

The findings of this study can be translated into practice by providing health practitioners with insights on how to design health promotion programs using smartwatch health apps. Health practitioners may be able to consider individuals’ inherent motivational orientations, such as promotion focus and prevention focus and I-HLOC to provide them with more effective health promotion programs. Also, this study provides marketers of smartwatch health apps with practical implications on how to promote their apps based upon a consideration of individuals’ regu latory focus and level of I-HLOC. Our results suggest that the marketing of a promotion type of app should target promotion-oriented individual but that the marketing of a prevention type of app can target both promotion and prevention-oriented individuals. Additionally, despite the fact that regulatory focus is viewed as a trait, regulatory focus can be manipulated for a short time [62]. Therefore, marketers of smartwatch health apps may be able to prime [83] their potential customers in order to temporarily increase customers’ intention to use their apps.

## Limitations and directions for future research

First, measuring intentions represents a limitation and additional research is needed to confirm whether the results we obtained translate into actual behaviors. Further research is needed to examine the postuse perceptions of users to understand how attitudes and behaviors associated with the use of smart health apps change with experience. Also, while we instructed study participants to assume that they already owned a smartwatch and wore it all of the time, we did not measure participants’ actual experience with smartwatches, and it is possible that some of our participants had such experience.

Second, given the nature of our experimental design, we were not able to examine post-usage intentions. One direction for future research would be to examine post-usage intention and see if the result pattern that we obtained with pre-usage intention is replicable in the post-usage phase.

Third, for the purposes of designing an experiment with clear ma nipulations, it was necessary to focus on single-purpose apps and to position them as being clearly promotion oriented or clearly prevention oriented. To ensure that our experiment had ecological validity, we searched smartwatch heart monitoring and workout apps on the Sam sung Galaxy watch app store and found more than 50 single-purpose apps that could be classified as dominantly having either a promotion or prevention focus. Specifically, 65% (13 out of 20) of heart monitoring apps and 87% (39 out of 45) of workout apps were single-purpose apps. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that some smartwatch apps are multi purpose and include both prevention- and promotion-oriented features or aspects. One limitation of our research is that we cannot fully extrapolate our findings to such apps. Further research, conducted at the feature level, would be needed to determine if regulatory focus could be applicable at a more granular level than what we examined, which was focused on the app level.

Fourth, even though our choices for a promotion app (i.e., the workout app) and a prevention app (i.e., the heart monitoring app) reflect the apps’ characteristics (i.e., outcomes associated with using apps and the required goal pursuit strategies in order to use them) that appeal to individuals’ particular regulatory focus, we did not explicitly consider the effort that would be related to use such apps in our experiment. Given that both benefits and costs influence the adoption of products and services [82] and that the costs and user burden for using apps negatively affect users’ intention to use mobile health apps [5], future research needs to examine how cost-related factors influence the relationships between regulatory focus and the intention to use smart watch health apps.

As discussed in the previous section, I-HLOC did not moderate the relationship between prevention focus and intention to use the heart monitoring app, and this result might be due to the extremely low effort related to the use of heart monitoring app. We propose that there is an effort threshold that must be overcome before we see much of an effect of I-HLOC on the choice to engage in healthy behavior. Future research needs to empirically test whether I-HLOC influences healthy behaviors only when healthy behaviors require high effort or “cost.”

## Conclusion

Despite the increasing popularity of smartwatch health apps that rely on self-management [54,55], few studies have investigated the influence of individual difference factors on intention to use smartwatch health apps. This study examined how individuals’ inherent motivational ori entations and internal beliefs regarding their ability to control their health have differential influence on their intention to use a promotion app versus a prevention app. We hope that this study leads to additional research on the impact of fit between individual difference factors and characteristics of mobile health apps on the use of such apps.
