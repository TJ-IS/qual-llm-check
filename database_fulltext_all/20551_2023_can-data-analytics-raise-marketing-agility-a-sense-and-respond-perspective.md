# Can data analytics raise marketing agility?-A sense-and-respond perspective

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Data analytics orientation Market knowledge specificity Marketing agility Senseand-respond perspective

## A B S T R A C T

Data analytics (DA) is a growing technique to generate knowledge of and to raise responsiveness to marketplace, yet relatively few studies clarified its framework and to examine its effects on marketing actions. This study builds a DA framework based on sense-and-respond perspective and practices and accordingly examines their impact on marketing agility and the mediating effects of market knowledge specificity. We conduct in-depth interviews and surveys to measure the new framework. Results from 198 observations confirm the new frame work and indicate that the DA constructs have positive but different effects on marketing agility and the mediation of market knowledge specificity.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Background

Data analytics (also known as data-intensive analytics and big data analytics) are playing a critical and growing role in diversified academic sectors, including economics, political studies, manufacturing [1], governmental sectors [2,3], and, of course, marketing and business fields [4–6], but the field is not brand new. Past efforts in mathematics, statistics, information science, sentimental analytics, and social metrics have focused on data analysis techniques that have also been used in both the strategic marketing and the consumer behavior literature [7–9].

## 1.2. Theoretical development

## 1.2.1. Sense-and-respond model

Data analytics have encompassed growing and flourishing issues during the past decade, yet there are still some research gaps in the current literature. Academics, practitioners, and administrative sectors have begun to invest in resources for data analytics in recent years, with many fields actually showing particular achievements. Studies typically have focused on exploring analytical techniques such as data mining [2, 10], statistical analysis [4], and data visualization [11]. While past ef forts on data analytics seem to be diversified, many studies are partial to certain facets, the whole picture of data analytics is still neglected, and relatively few have employed a solid and well-developed theoretical basis to portray how firms collect, analyze, and use data from a sys tematic and full viewpoint. Such portrayals may include the contents of data analytics, classification of analytical behaviors, and proper archi tecture or frameworks to clarify data analytics efforts. Related issues have also received attention from academia and practitioners, and both public and private sectors are extensively applying data analytics as well. It is, therefore, appropriate to construct a solid architecture that portrays a firm’s behavior when conducting data analytics, denoted as data analytics orientation, with a solid theoretical basis. The first research gap in recent data analytics literature is, hence, a lack of a solid theo retical basis to construct the architecture of a firm’s data analytics orientation model to clarify the contents and behaviors of data analytics conducts.

The rise of recent data analytics practices is due to growing pressure and challenges originating from the dynamic changes in the business environment, which force organizations to be starved for any sense of the changes that might help them respond to the environment accord ingly [12]; i.e., sense-and-respond actions. Orlandi et al. [12] treat sense-and-respond ability as an organizational capability to encounter external changes and discontinuities to create and sustain competitive advantages for success in the dynamic market environment. Two capa bilities, sensing and responding, therein play critical and particular roles. Sensing includes scanning, learning, and interpretive activity [13], probing customer needs, understanding latent demand, and assessing supplier and competitor responses [14]. Responding means that, once potential challenges and/or opportunities are sensed, the business organizations must mobilize their existing resources, processes, or services to match future market conditions [14,15].

Contemporary technological development offers a new tool for sensing and responding; i.e., data analytics. In practice, data analytics use and process internal and external data as information and knowl edge to scan and understand the current and potential conditions of the marketplace. The processed outcome may provide market intelligence regarding potential risks and opportunities. Recent literature indeed clarifies data analytics in a sense-and-respond format. Chan [16] treats data analytics as a key to create competitive advantages and to reflect a complex and rapidly changing market. Feki et al. [17] underscore the potential role of data analytics in the transformation of supply chains to create organizational values. Kitchens et al. [18] and Hajli et al. [19] clarify the role of data analytics in addressing customers’ changing needs for new product success. Araz et al. [20] illuminate the functions of data analytics in operational risk management.

Previous literature shows that one major function and utility of data analytics to business organizations is to rapidly perceive and sense sig nals and intelligence in the environment to properly and agilely respond to conditions and changes in the marketplace. The sense-and-respond perspective thus could be treated as a potential theoretical basis for exploring data analytics-related issues. This study employs it as the basic architecture to construct a firm’s data analytics orientation model and to accordingly clarify the data analytics contents and behaviors.

## 1.2.2. Purpose of conducting data analytics

Data analytics do not make sense unless accompanied by particular purposes. Current studies have extended data analytics issues to diverse fields, including innovation [9], value creation and co-creation [6,21, 22], new product success [23], and information technology (IT) trans formation [6]. Some efforts have paid off by examining the effects of data analytics’ capabilities on firm performance [24], incremental and radical innovation with the information governance theory [25], and dynamic and marketing capabilities from a dynamic capability perspective [26]. Data analytics are also treated as new solutions to analyze market data, to support strategic decision-making for destina tion management of tourism services [27], and to raise decision quality [6]. Data analytics seem to be relevant to many issues and contribute to a broad range of fields, yet the issues covered in the current literature are quite divergent based on their own perspective. It is still necessary, by rooting into the essence of data analytics, to clarify and state the pur poses and reasons why business organizations conduct data analytics, as well as the functions or benefits of data analytics to business organiza tions. The second gap of current studies is a lack of comprehension of what data analytics work for business organizations.

To bridge the gaps in studies, the current literature indicates that their related strand tends to link the sense-and-respond perspective to clarify agility of business organizations. Roberts and Grover [28] use sense-and-respond to investigate the effects of data analytics on firms customer agility and competitive activity, whereas Mathiassen and Vainio [29] and Schmidt and Mathiassen [30] explain the relationship of agility and organizational dynamic capability of small firms. In partic ular, Seo and La Paz [31] provide a six-element cyclical process of pursuing organizational agility based on a sense-and-respond ideology (exhibited in Fig. 1.(a)). Agile organizations are alert to sensing and perceiving incoming market signals and then need to process the signals by responding to the market information to recognize its possible im pacts. During the process, the organizations require a realignment of resources and learning experiences from the changing conditions to build their competencies and consequently organizational agility.

Seo and La Paz [31] state that, in detail, sensing and responding abilities determine the conduct of processing through agility-building. Sensing is the ability to perceive and detect changes in the environ ment. As a first step of agility formation, business organizations endeavor to understand signals in both their (1) internal and (2) external environments to sense changes and trends that impact them. Another factor that determines the ways of processing is responding ability, which means organizational (1) pro-action and/or (2) reaction to signals or changes in the marketplace. Once the signals have been sensed and processed, agile organizations must quickly formulate and execute appropriate responses to the marketplace.

Seo and La Paz [31] put forward a generic model for explaining the formation procedure of organizational agility through IT. Considering data analytics are a particular processed outcome of IT, this study en deavors to specify Seo and La Paz’s [31] model for the application of data analytics. Once the essence of data analytics is processed, the types of data analytics can be classified into some types by contextual de terminations. Following Seo and La Paz’s [31] model, the data and in formation that business organizations employ to sense and perceive signals in the environment might be those collected and accumulated internally from the organizations, such as internal databases and past transactional records, while they also could be those externally gathered from social media and Internet websites. As for the responding factor, organizations’ purposes of data analytics can be classified as a reactive approach, which works to interpret market conditions based on past information, and a proactive approach, which attempts to prepare a response to market conditions prior to the changes. To fill the first gap in the literature, this study thus employs the sense-and-respond perspec tive (i.e., (1) internal/external sources, as well as (2) reactive/proactive purposes) as the theoretical basis to construct the architecture of a firm’s data analytics orientation model to clarify the contents and behaviors of data analytics.

## 1.2.3. The analytics-knowledge-agility framework

Based on the appropriate sensing sources and responding purposes, the acquired data and signals are processed to generate organizational competency, including market information, intelligence, and knowl edge. With a resource combination of alignment and learning activities, business organizations can build agility in the market. This study, therefore, aims to follow Seo and La Paz’s [31] framework to further develop the data analytics orientation model with internal/external and reactive/proactive dichotomies. We, thus, build a data analytics → knowledge → agility framework as a basis to clarify the functions of data analytics conducts (exhibited in Fig. 1(b)).

Seo and La Paz’s [31] framework in practice is applicable for data analytics research. For business organizations, data analytics provide meaningful information from multifarious and disorganized data re sources $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ sensing/perception in Seo and La Paz’s model). Some recent studies have started to focus on data analytics issues related to mar keting, advertising, and other market topics $[ 2 4 , 2 5 , 3 2 ]$ . Considering the business organizational context, data analytics should be based on the demand to understand the market situation, such as competition, customer preferences, environment trends, and supply chain conditions, to respond to the current situation and changes in the market $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ responding). One primary task of data analytics resides in data processing a large quantity of immediately and diversified information assets (i.e., processing) for firms to strengthen decision-making, idea exploration, and process optimization [33]. Under pressure from drastic changes in the marketplace, the recent literature endeavors to investigate how firms perceive changes to respond to them accordingly; i.e., marketing agility [34].

The past literature has put forth efforts to clarify the determinants of marketing agility, such as the ability to use IT and to perceive the environment [31] and the conducts of strategic forecasting [34], all of which can be executed with the help of data analytics. Some recent studies have clarified the effects of data analytics on raising organiza tional and marketing agility [3,35,36]. Ghasemaghaei et al. [37] even employ the dynamic capability theory to link data analytics (IT capa bility) with firm agility (dynamic capability). We, thus, follow Seo and La Paz’s [31] framework to explore the effects of data analytics on marketing agility and derive the first research question of this study: Is the effort of data analytics able to raise the marketing agility of an organization?

## 1.2.4. Specific knowledge and marketing agility

During the process of data analytics to generate marketing agility, however, some potential factors may be considered as a critical moderating mechanism. In the process of Seo and La Paz’s [31] framework, the formation of competency is the critical mechanism be tween processing and generation of agility. In the framework, compe tency refers to knowledge and experience about the market and to processes for how to do things internally and with external partners. Benefiting from the development of IT, visual communication, mobile devices, as well as the popularity of social media in the Internet world, business organizations can easily gather a large amount of data flow through information platforms and databases [38], allowing them to analyze the collected data to generate useful knowledge for marketing efforts $[ 4 , 1 0 , 2 7 ]$

All firms in practice have their own internal database, but only some of them can process and transform the data to build up effective knowledge related to the marketplace [39,40]. In addition, data ac quired from external sources, such as social media and Internet websites, are open and available to the public and not exactly able to be trans formed and internalized as knowledge with any competitive advantage specifically to the firms. Hence, only those firms that are able to analyze data from internal and external sources to build specific market knowledge can raise their competencies to generate agility; specific knowledge to the firms should be a critical mediating factor for gener ating marketing agility [33]. We accordingly build up the second research question: Is the effort of data analytics able to build specific market knowledge that raises the marketing agility of a firm?

Based on the previous research questions, this study builds a data analytics → knowledge specificity → marketing agility causality that matches Srivastava et al.’s [41] framework. They link the resource-based view with marketing and propose a framework to explain how a market-based resource is a form of leverage to process organizational capability so that it creates or sustains competitive ad vantages. Bridging it with this study, a market-based resource (data) is leveraged (through data analytical conducts) as market-based assets, which can then be divided into relational and intellectual assets (specific knowledge) by focusing on their particular strategic purposes. Organi zations may process those market-based assets to generate organiza tional capability (marketing agility) and, hence, produce competitive advantages. Thus, data analytics and specific knowledge are tactic-level issues, while marketing agility is a strategic-level issue in this study. Srivastava et al.’s [41] framework is seen as a proper theoretical basis for the causality.

In response to the previous research questions, we first comprehend the contents and behaviors of data analytics in marketing practice to construct a data analytics orientation model based on the sense-andrespond perspective and Seo and La Paz’s [31] model. We then explore how data analytics of a firm can help raise its marketing agility through specific market knowledge.

## 2. Literature review and inferences of the hypotheses

## 2.1. Data analytics’ orientation

Following the IT trends, many resources and efforts from diversified subjects have been employed for the development and utilization of data analytics [4,11]. Recent achievements point out that the concern regarding data analytics tends to concentrate on specific areas for particular purposes, such as the linkages between service innovation and value creation [9,21], business value and performance [22], virtual factory [1], and new product success [23]. From the perspective of domain ontology, the results of these studies can only be treated as particular events or sets of data analytics. Because scant literature has constructed an integrated ontology of data analytics, this study shall construct the architecture of data analytics’ orientation based on the demand and practices of marketing.

This study takes a sense-and-respond perspective and sets (1) inter nal/external sources and (2) reactive/proactive purposes dichotomies as the architecture to construct the model of data analytics’ orientation. The two dichotomies match Parise et al.’s [42] dimensions regarding data analytics. Published in a semi-academic periodical, Parise et al. [42] present a framework for big data strategies to understand the analytical techniques used by firms based on their strategies. The framework is formed by two dimensions. The first dimension is the type of data; i.e., transactional versus non-transactional data. Firms use transactional data by collecting existing, structured data internally and employ non-transactional data from external sources outside of their firms. The data are typically unstructured and non-owned (e.g., social media data). The second dimension is the objective of business organi zations: measurement versus experiment. Organizations are concerned about data that can be directly measured or data that they own. They want to employ data not owned and unstructured for the purpose of their own experiments. On this basis, Parise et al. [42] built a four-construct framework: performance management, data exploration, network observation, and decision science.

Parise et al.’s [42] framework offers an insightful and potential construction, but, though it is formed with a practical and conventional start, it still lacks a solid theoretical consideration for further academic development. Nevertheless, it is still a referential framework for the development of data analytics’ orientation because of the following reasons. Here, we can bridge the two sense-and-respond dichotomies with Parise et al.’s [42] dimensions.

The first point covers the two major types of data: transactional and non-transactional. A firm may employ data and information from diversified sources. The major types in practice, however, are data collected and structurally documented internal to the firms (i.e., internal sources), as well as the unstructured data obtained from partners and existing and potential customers external to the firms (i.e., external source). For data analytics’ practices, the former may be stored from internal past transactions, deal records, and market research and surveys (i.e., transactional) and named as firm-documented data; the latter can be collected through external data sources not from previous internal transactional records (i.e., non-transactional), but generated by stake holders in the marketplace from social media and online platforms and named as market-generated data [42]. The internal versus external di chotomies of sense-and-respond are consistent to related theoretical frameworks, such as Zack’s [43] knowledge strategy dimension regarding whether primary knowledge sources originate internally versus externally for an organization, as well as Moorman’s [44] inter nal versus external orientation of organizational information processes. We consider making a linkage of data analytics with marketing agility.

This study defines marketing agility as a firm’s capabilities regarding interactions with customers, utilization of resources from external partners, and the harmony of internal conducts. We accordingly classify marketing agility into three elements: customer, partnering, and oper ational agility ([34,41]; details are provided in a later description). Taking customer agility as an example, marketers may raise agility regarding customer responses by realizing their purchase behaviors, analyzing past transactions and purchase records documented by the firms (internal sources) [43,44], as well as exploring the emergent customer demand and potential market development by looking at customer-generated responses and comments published on social media gathered from the marketplace (external sources) [42,43].

After integrating the internal versus external sources of the senseand-respond perspective with the relevant constructs of Parise et al. [42], Zack [43], and Moorman [44] and considering the explanation on marketing agility, we thus develop the first dimension of the data ana lytics’ orientation model, which regards the data sources as firm-docu mented versus market-generated data (reflecting internal versus external sources, respectively). This refers to data being properly developed and structured by the firms or that generated by existing and potential customers, competitors, and other stakeholders in the marketplace.

The other dichotomy of the sense-and-respond perspective (i.e., reactive/proactive purposes) can also be identical to the second di chotomy of Parise et al. [42]; i.e., measuremental/ experimental data, as dimensions and classifications of past theoretical bases. Ghani et al. [45] classify analytical techniques for big data and social media into four purposes: descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analytics. Based on Zack [43], Moorman [44], and Parise et al. [42], measur emental data are properly measured, documented, and developed data that can be applied to track structured, routine, and existing tasks and activities of the organizations on an inductive basis. Moorman [44] regards such data as formal information utilization and governance [44]. Firms can also exploit such existing data to provide insights and describe consumption patterns, trends, and incidents that have happened in the past or present (descriptive analytics), which can be treated as knowledge exploitation of Zack’s [43] knowledge strategy and can help realize and clarify the reasons for the current conditions and changes in the marketplace (diagnostic analytics) to react to the contexts in the marketplace [46].

Experimental data, in contrast, mean unstructured data or data not owned by the organizations for the proactive purpose of making exper iments and for self-challenging actions to encounter new and future conditions on a deductive basis. Firms need to proactively gather such data through Moorman’s [44] informal data acquisition and trans mission rather than just use current data. These proactive analytics may include forecasting techniques and statistical analysis that give new insights for the future (predictive analytics), which is identical to Zack’s [43] knowledge exploration. They may also be simulation and optimi zation analysis and decision analysis techniques that explore a support mechanism to obtain solutions and improvement ways for future development (prescriptive analytics) [45,46]. The reactive versus pro active dichotomy of the sense-and-respond perspective also corresponds to Zack’s [43] exploitation versus exploration classification and Moor man’s [44] formal governance versus informal governance. This study thus proposes to converge the previous classifications with Parise’s [42] when considering the purposes of data analytics and thus sets the second dimension of the data analytics of the framework as reactive versus proactive purposes; i.e., descriptive and diagnostic purpose versus pre dictive and prescriptive purpose.

This study places (1) market-generated versus firm-documented data and (2) reactive versus proactive purposes as the dimensions of the scope and accordingly constructs a model for data analytics’ orientation with four constructs: outcome assessment, opportunity seeking, network observation, and experimental appraisal (exhibited in Table 1 and Fig. 2). Note that these four are developed as constructs (rather than types/typologies) to explain and measure the orientation of the four facets of organizational data analytics’ behaviors, and they are not mutually exclusive in practice for firms. Detailed descriptions of the constructs are provided in the following sections.

## 2.1.1. Outcome assessment

Outcome assessment means that a firm exploits its internal and structured data regarding its current marketing conducts to assess its own organizational performance as the basis for future improvement and modification. Similar concepts and terms include performance management [42], business value [22], and performance enhancement [9]. In marketing practices, firms may settle on a management intelli gence system and database [10] to elaborately analyze their data and to comprehend past financial, production [40], and market performances of each market segment [5,42]. They may also conduct in-depth analysis through their self-collected primary data regarding customers’ purchase records and personal information/demographics to understand their target market niches and their relative market contributions to firm performance [47]. They may apply online analytical processing [42] to analyze the data from daily transactions and operations to find rules and business models for their firms [22]. They may deliberately appraise the marketing effectiveness of advertising campaigns and sales promotions [32.40]. Previous studies on data analytics have emploved internal data to clarify the current outcome as well as to predict market trends as the basis for further managerial efforts, including education, training, and marketing [4].

Even much earlier than the rise of information systems, firm man agement has realized how to improve its organization’s effectiveness by clarifying the association between managerial conducts and financial and market performances. Thus, outcome assessment is not a new issue. The critical point of outcome assessment is to concentrate on clarifying current performance and the effectiveness of organizations by analyzing existing internal data [42]. The data set thus tends to be highly struc tured, and the methods and techniques employed are simplified and straight, such as simple judgment, descriptive statistical analysis, and forecasts based on the trend of the statistics [22]. Hence, outcome assessment focuses on the internal part of organizations to improve and refine their current outcome.

## 2.1.2. Opportunity seeking

Opportunity seeking refers to the extent to which a firm analyzes its internal and structured data source collected from the current market and organizational conditions to explore potential marketing issues and opportunities. Some terms in past studies are close in meaning to op portunity seeking, such as data exploration [42] and data mining. Marketers may explain and predict purchase behaviors, judge de terminants of consumers’ decisions, and clarify the impacts of marketing efforts [9,47], such as product/service features, promotion activities influence on market performance, and the development of competitive intelligence techniques [32]. Prior to the concept of data analytics, business organizations in the past few decades have explored customer behaviors and responses in the marketplace by analyzing daily records and buyers’ basic demographics. The results from opportunity seeking could help induce the firms to seek potential, workable, and alternative opportunities in the marketplace, some of which were not even considered or of much concern in the past. Hence, opportunity seeking may motivate aggressive managerial conducts and marketing involve ment in the marketplace.

To some extent, opportunity seeking relies on analytical techniques and tools, including multivariate statistical techniques, structured query language, and forecasting techniques, to interpret and predict the future development and trends of the marketplace [11,40]. Therefore, firms execute opportunity-seeking actions primarily based on data sources organized internally through aggressive intent and experimental pur poses to explore new and emergent knowledge, opportunities, and trends [4]. Hence, opportunity seeking helps firms to recognize the reasons and antecedents of organizational performance and effective ness, to investigate the critical determinants of customer behaviors, and to have in-depth and extensive understandings about the marketplace and their internal operations.

## 2.1.3. Network observation

Network observation refers to data analytics for when a firm employs external and unstructured data collected from outside to exploit more indepth and to analyze its current context for the purpose of observing and monitoring market factors that it has not been concerned about nor dabbled in before. Relevant concepts include social monitoring, listening, analytics [9], and network analytics [48]. In recent practices, firms have delicately analyzed messages, texts, dialogues, comments, and even customer complaints on their websites, social media (e.g., Twitter and Facebook) [4,11], and communities of practices. They have also attempted to clarify the explicit or implicit meanings through keyword analysis and customer relationship management analysis [40] to accurately mirror the conditions and perspectives of external stake holders, particularly those of customers and the media sector. Network observation is, hence, able to reflect diversified responses from the marketplace, such as customers’ viewpoints, perceptions, preferences, commitment, word of mouth, and media contacts to the firms and their market efforts [47]. Some alternative approaches include using IT to collect data from mobile devices and disseminating market information to explore the differences in customer responses between the market segments by analyzing demographic and behavioral profiles [40].

Firms with a higher extent of network observation in general employ social media as well as social media marketing. They require particular expertise and techniques, including textual analysis, sematic analysis, social analysis, A/B test (also known as split-run testing), network an alytics, and social indicators [35]. They may also employ customer-related analytical techniques such as advertising analytics [49], retail analytics, and path-to-purchase analytics [40]. Firms that are concerned about network observation place emphasis on the market performances of their products and services but pay more attention to data outside their organization to clarify the marketing performance from diversified sources. Such data analytics based on social platforms could help them comprehend the consistency or gaps between the in ternal conducts of their firms and the external responses from customers in the marketplace.

## 2.1.4. Experimental appraisal

Experimental appraisal refers to the extent to which a firm elabo rately plans and settles on a data analytics’ mechanism to collect un structured, non-routine data from external data sources to appraise potential market opportunities with experimental and innovative intentions [42]. Some studies in the past, such as Grover et al.’s [9] stra tegic transformer, Ren et al.’s [22] business transformation, and Shamim et al.’s [50] big data decision-making, are close to experimental appraisal in their concepts. To encounter an emergent market context and competition, firms are forced to upgrade products/services and to modify their marketing decisions and potential alternatives. Experi mental appraisal is thus helpful as a reference for decision-making. In practice, firms provide platforms to collect customers’ opinions and ideas regarding product/service development through their online/off line channels. They may also gather data from the marketplace as the basis for marketing efforts with an open mind [49]. In addition, in stitutes in both public and private sectors build mechanisms to conduct market surveys directly from the target market segments or audiences, such as online voting systems, which are beneficial to have a realistic understanding of market development [10]. Some firms work to communicate and respond directly with their existing and potential customers through the Internet and social media platforms [4,47].

Firms with a high degree of experimental appraisal rely on their capability of data acquisition and analysis, including a solid basis on IT and social platforms, professional skills of statistical/multivariate anal ysis, social analytics and indicators, modeling [1], and even crowd sourcing [42]. These capabilities require sufficient professionals and data analytics’ infrastructure. They seek challenging, experimental opinions from outsiders and attack new opportunities with innovative intentions to improve their existing marketing conduct and to face the emergent business environment [40].

## 2.2. Marketing agility

Agility is an emergent concern in both practical and academic fields. Past studies refer to agility as a set of processes when an organization senses changes in its internal and external environments, responds effectively, timely, and cost-efficiently, and learns from its past experi ences to improve its competencies [31,34,51,52]. Based on these con cepts, agility consists of concepts, including environment detection, recombination of assets, knowledge and relationships, and responsive ness to the changes. Recent concerns about agility are due to the dra matic changes inside and outside of firms, which are challenging traditional principles, such as standardization and mass production, because of their weak responsiveness to the environment. Firms must cautiously detect opportunities and threats in the environment, seek a proper niche with competitive advantages, and agilely adjust their resource combination to respond to new trends and environmental sit uations [53].

Earlier studies tend to treat agility as an organization-level factor with a generalized perspective; i.e., organizational agility. Because the marketing practice is located in the downstream of the value chain and directly faces dramatic and changing market conditions, such a mar keting practice needs the most and can reflect the best organizational agility in the marketplace. This study thus looks to concentrate on the agility issue regarding customers, competitors and competitions, and organizational capability in the marketplace. To make a closer linkage to marketing by referring to the definitions in D’Aveni [51], Goldman et al. [52], and Sambamurthy et al. [34], our study defines marketing agility as an organization’s capability to detect competitive opportunities in the marketplace and to assemble the requisite marketing assets, knowledge, and internal and external relationships with speed and responsiveness to match customers’ demand.

The perspectives of existing studies regarding the antecedents and contents of marketing agility have still not converged. Several studies have linked up with information and communications technology [37], but their empirical results point to a relationship between the two that may be either positive or negative [53,54]. As for the contents of agility, earlier literature tends to treat it as a single-construct issue with some later studies building it with multiple elements. Lu and Ramamurthy [55], for example, set up organizational agility with market-capitalizing agility and operational adjustment agility, which are attributed to both external orientation and internal orientation, whereas some studies pay attention to customer-related agility [56,57]. Considering the previous definition, the marketing agility of an organization reflects its capabil ities of understanding customers, partnerships with peripheral partners, and marketing-related conducts. These objects are identical to Samba murthy et al.’s [34] classification of agility: customer, partnering, and operational agilities. Sambamurthy et al. [34] shape the agility with these three types by reconceptualizing digital options and IT, which are highly relevant to the major issue of this study – data analytics. Customer and partnering agility forms from external sources, while operational agility is generated internally from the organizations. Accordingly, this study follows Sambamurthy et al. [34] and defines marketing agility as a firm’s capabilities regarding interactions with customers, utilization of resources from external partners, and the har mony of internal conducts. We thus build marketing agility with three elements – customer, partnering, and operational agilities – and describe their details in the following sections.

## 2.2.1. Customer agility

Roberts and Grover [28] define customer agility as the extent to which a firm senses and responds quickly to customer-based opportu nities for innovation and competitive actions. To comprehend customer behaviors and usage contexts, firms should be able to perceive or detect internal and external market signals and instantly convert them into actionable knowledge, particularly those granted from their customers [31]. Kohli and Jaworski [58] argue that customer agility is beneficial to understand the voices of the customers, obtain market intelligence, and detect market opportunities through planned competitive actions. Lu and Ramamurthy [55] denote it as market-capitalizing agility, which is defined as a firm’s ability to quickly respond to and capitalize on changes through continuously monitoring and quickly improving pro duct/service to address customers’ needs. Following Sambamurthy et al. [34], we define customer agility as the capability to detect and respond to customer-related market signals to explore and exploit opportunities for innovation and competitive action moves.

Sambamurthy et al.’s [34] previous definition signifies that customer agility is developed based on both exploitative and exploratory capa bilities for knowledge about customer context and market opportunity. Thus, firms rely on data analytics for both exploitative and exploratory purposes. Past studies have looked into market and marketing research on customer behaviors and usage context [28]. By analyzing their own internal data, for example, firms can investigate the contribution of each market segment and predict future market development trends. They can also clarify the association of, and the heterogeneity between, cus tomers’ demographic factors [47]. Thus, the firms’ degree of outcome assessment and opportunity seeking can positively raise their customer agility.

As for data analytics with exploratory purposes, firms with higher degrees of network observation and experimental appraisal often collect market signals by employing social media platforms, mobile devices, and online customer communications [47,35], and target to raise product development success and customer agility by online reviews and social analytics [35]. In practice, Nambisan [59] contends that customers could help raise a firm’s responsiveness to match customer demand through several ways, including submitting innovative ideas for products, collaboratively designing and developing products with cus tomers, testing new products or making product trials in the initial stage of product development, and giving advice for product modification and adjustment prior to official product announcement. Thus, these non-transactional, exploratory data analytics could be used to raise customer agility [28].

The previous inference signifies that four types of data analytics have positive effects on the customer agility of a firm. Thus, we build the first hypotheses of this study.

H1a. : The degree of a firm’s outcome assessment positively impacts its customer agility.

H1b. : The degree of a firm’s opportunity seeking positively impacts its customer agility.

H1c. : The degree of a firm’s network observation positively impacts its customer agility.

H1d. : The degree of a firm’s experimental appraisal positively impacts its customer agility.

## 2.2.2. Partnering agility

To detect and agilely respond to changes in the environment, firms not only encounter challenges and rivalry in the marketplace on their own but also collaborate to disseminate, converge, and generate knowledge for agility with some critical partners. This is named as partnering agility. Dyer and Singh [60] note a firm’s capability to detect and use market opportunities through ways such as outsourcing, work division, and collaboration with critical partners. Zheng et al. [61] denote it as collective agility. Integrating Venkatraman and Henderson [62], Lu and Ramamurthy [55], and previous viewpoints, we define partnering agility as a firm’s capability to leverage the assets, knowledge, and competencies of partners, including suppliers, distributors, contract manufacturers, and providers, through outsourcing, collaboration, alliances, partnerships, and joint ventures, to detect and use opportunities in the marketplace.

Partnering agility helps firms to leverage their interorganizational relationship with their partners to collect market intelligence from multiple aspects, expertise, and geographical areas so that they can integrate them as intelligence about the marketplace. Their relational network is also the basis to converge knowledge and intellectual assets from diversified partners to reflect market conditions and develop marketing agility [63,64]. Partnering agility may be facilitated under collaboration and teamwork with their partners. It is, however, still deeply rooted from resources and knowledge that the focal firms possess.

Lin et al. [65] develop an industry-level knowledge management system based on practices in the Asia-Pacific region. They indicate that a certain proportion of knowledge activities in this area is not carried out by an individual business organization, but rather is conducted by related and supporting firms on the basis of demand and factors of the core firms in the industry [61]. Madhavan and Grover [66] argue that a T-shaped skill is essential for knowledge workers (named as hybrids) to possess, meaning that they should possess a rich breadth of knowledge (the horizontal stroke on the T) based on a specific knowledge focus (the vertical stroke). With the perspective of marketing agility, a firm needs to effectively converge diversified resources and knowledge obtained from related partners (the horizontal stroke) based on its own solid knowledge basis (the vertical stroke). The success of such convergence relies on effectively aggregating acquired data from the marketplace, conducting an interorganizational co-examination and confirmation of the data, and accurately integrating them with resources and knowledge of the partners as useful knowledge and intelligence to seek potential market opportunities. Hence, the knowledge resources of the focus firm are still critical for raising partnering agility.

Past literature has pointed out that partnering agility may be derived from both exploitative and exploratory efforts. From the practice of business collaborative relationships, firms with a higher extent of outcome assessment can employ the findings of data analytics to pre cisely point out potential problems that need solving with their partners, including examining specific sales channels that perform inferior, screening product items that have induced significant customer com plaints during the distribution process, and predicting market trends that may shift significantly both in the supply and customer sides. The findings of outcome assessment serve as a basis to instruct specific channel members, deal with customer dissatisfaction, solve bottlenecks in supplier and customer sides, and redesign objectives and routines for interorganizational collaborations [67]. Firms can use social analytics to clarify critical antecedents that determine market performance in dicators to concentrate marketing efforts and investment funds on them through cooperation with related and supportive colleagues. The prac tices may include adapting a promotion mix in the distribution channels according to the impact of each promotion activity and adjusting product designs and features based on customers’ reactions to the product items.

Regarding data analytics that use external, non-transactional data, high social analytic-oriented firms can assemble and analyze data from social platforms and accordingly disclose essential findings to relevant partners and make them modify their existing products. They can also transfer customers’ messages, complaints, and suggestions on current products and services submitted to the firms’ websites to their related partners for instant joint improvement [68,69]. Marketers with a high degree of experimental appraisal can directly collect existing and po tential customers’ attitudes, dialogues, and comments for emergent products/services from social platforms or acquire essential information from field surveys. They can then deliver the critical findings to related partners prior to their official product launch and mass production to make the necessary repositioning, redesigning, and production sched uling [69].

The previous review and inferences signify that all four types of data analytics positively impact the increase of partnering agility. We accordingly build the next hypotheses.

H2a. : The degree of a firm’s outcome assessment positively impacts its partnering agility.

H2b. : The degree of a firm’s opportunity seeking positively impacts its partnering agility.

H2c. : The degree of a firm’s network observation positively impact its partnering agility.

H2d. : The degree of a firm’s experimental appraisal positively impacts its partnering agility.

## 2.2.3. Operational agility

The core concept of organizational agility indicates that the rise and development of marketing agility rely not only on the quick detection of market opportunities and changes as well as collaborative adjustment with supportive partnership but also rely on speedy and accurate ad aptations during the marketing-related operational process. Such a process may include a flexible recombination of market resources and knowledge [67], a redesigning and reconfiguration of the operational process to transform or reorganize the current resources [68,70], and decreasing information asymmetry within organizations to team up their departments to encounter and respond to market contexts and opportunities collectively [71]. Hence, the internal operational process is also treated as one essential element of marketing agility and named as operational agility [55]. Based on Sambamurthy et al. [34], this study defines operational agility as the ability of a firm’s business processes to accomplish speed, accuracy, and cost economies when exploiting opportu nities for innovation and competitive action.

Sambamurthy et al. [34] advocate that the development of opera tional agility is due to speed, accuracy, and cost advantages of marketing actions, and that firms need to focus on the current situation of their operational process. Studies have presented that operational agility mainly responds to reactions in the marketplace, and workable efforts still reactively focus on the current situations of existing products and services to make corresponding adjustments. Thus, the efforts of oper ational agility need more reactivity to current market conditions rather than any proactivity of new market changes and opportunities. For example, Wang and Tarn’s [72] empirical survey about the association of organizational tasks and manufacturing competitive priorities reveals that efforts to improve operational cost, accuracy, and speed are highly reactive but lowly exploratory and proactive manufacturing activities. Therefore, operational agility mainly concentrates on reactive actions under the current market context and opportunities, but the relevance of proactive actions is relatively low, such that data analytics’ tasks are mainly based on reactive purposes; i.e., outcome assessment and network observation.

Firms with a high level of outcome assessment in practice tend to interpret market performance from data analytics to clarify the opera tional and transactional processes that need to be improved [22], particularly focusing on the customer behaviors of market segments with weaker performance, lower satisfaction, or lower economic contribution. They also aim at weakening market segments based on analyzing the historical records of the primary targets for process improvement. Firms with a higher extent of network observations analyze consumer opinions, complaints, and mutual communications as the basis of further process adjustment and improvement [9].

After reviewing previous studies, the literature appears to signify that the two types of reactivity-based data analytical techniques could positively impact the development of operational agility. We thus accordingly build the next tranche of hypotheses.

H3a. : The degree of a firm’s outcome assessment positively impacts its operational agility.

H3b. : The degree of a firm’s network observation positively impacts its operational agility.

## 2.3. Market knowledge specificity

In this study, we treat market knowledge specificity as the essential mediator in the association between data analytics and marketing agility. De Luca and Atuahene-Gima [73] and Galunic and Rodan [74] define knowledge specificity as the extent to which market knowledge is specific to the firm’s environment. Knowledge specificity covers the outcomes of knowledge generated by an in-depth analysis of market-related strategy, products, and customer behaviors to encounter the needs of specific market segments and to face actions of specific competitors [73]. Such knowledge is often highly organized, needs the involvement of experienced members, and thus requires particular organizational routines to satisfy market demand. Therefore, specific knowledge is often time-sensitive in nature, satisfies demand from the specific market segments, and decreases the possibility and willingness of competitors’ imitation.

## 2.4. Data analytics and market knowledge specificity

Based on characteristics and sources, data used for outcome assess ment and opportunity seeking are tailor-made for particular marketing purposes and actions [22,47] and collected based on specific requirements or contexts for firms’ market-related tasks [4,10]. The data format is often properly prepared for ease of analysis. Hence, it highly matches the specificity of the market situation.

Both network observation and experimental appraisal are conducted for a firm’s particular needs to understand market situations and customer behaviors by collecting and analyzing external data. Specif ically, firms collect the necessary data from social media with particular purposes and viewpoints to the market [4,10], focus the responses and expressions of the customers by viewing messages and comments on social media [35], invite potential ideas online to achieve their experi mental and innovative goals [4,10], and build a sophisticated mecha nism to obtain opinions from the market. The collected data are open to the public and not exclusive to counterparts in the marketplace such as potential competitors, while the analytics are still conducted based on the specific demand of the marketers. Following the previous inference, we build a separate hypothesis regarding the impact of data analytics on market knowledge specificity.

H4. : The extent of a firm’s data analytics’ orientation positively im pacts market knowledge specificity.

## 2.5. Moderation of market knowledge specificity

## 2.5.1. Data analytics, market knowledge specificity, and customer agility

Customer agility of a firm relies highly upon its understanding of existing and potential customers in the marketplace, making the degree of market knowledge specificity a critical factor. Knowledge specificity is reflected in the extent to which a firm can dominate over a specific knowledge domain, including contexts of each market segment [8], customer preferences, and competitor actions. Firms should identify market signals in organizations both internally and externally and accordingly instantly convert them into attainable knowledge, particu larly those from their customers [31].

Chatfield and Raddick [3] treat customer agility as a dynamic capability in sensing and responding to market conditions. They study the relationship of data analytics and customer agility in the govern mental sector from a value creation perspective. With a practical perspective of data analytics, the identification of such market signals is conducted based on firms’ accumulated experiences regarding market-related information, as well as their capability to internalize the historical market evolution as their specific knowledge [75]. The behavioral scope of customers is quite wide and diversified and may include demographics, household factors, consumer preferences, decision-making, and even the purchase, selection, and response to a firm’s marketing action [11,47]. Such a wide and diversified behavioral scope increases the distance between the firms and their customers and may be detrimental [75]. Hence, firms have to specify knowledge to their customers by focusing on the behavioral scope of the target market segments to handle diversified customer conditions.

Past studies regarding knowledge management have argued that the specificity of organizational knowledge provides a good basis to develop an internal knowledge society [76]. Such a type of society is embedded in the social system within the organizations and provides a platform for members to recognize external market signals. Specific organizational knowledge not only increases the tacit understanding of members to judge the market situations but also raises difficulty for competitors to interpret them, both of which are helpful to make a specific response to customer conditions in the marketplace [28]. Clark et al. [77] argue that a solid market-related information infrastructure is helpful to enhance the expertise and intellectual specificity of employees. Thus, high knowledge specificity signifies that a firm can immediately drive the signals from the customer side, and it also helps obstruct competitors imitation and interpretation about similar signals [73,78], thereby improving its customer agility.

The previous review indicates that market knowledge specificity of a firm is an essential factor that determines whether data analytics lead to better customer agility. Here, we build the following hypothesis.

H5. : Market knowledge specificity of a firm positively mediates the association of data analytics with customer agility.

## 2.5.2. Data analytics, market knowledge specificity, and partnering agility

Partnering agility is based on the synergy generated from collabo ration between the focal firms and their cooperative partners to enhance the utility of knowledge, intelligent assets, and resources, as well as to quickly respond to market changes and emergent opportunities. Hence, partnering agility is determined by synergic knowledge resources of both parties; i.e., the focal firms and the partners, allowing the focal firms to encounter a complicated and diversified interorganizational context [61]. Because both parties have their own internal organiza tional context and external networks, the focal firm’s market knowledge generated by data analytics may be incompatible to its partners [74]. During the collaboration, the focal firm still needs to analyze relevant data to obtain exclusive and specific market knowledge so that it can lead its partnership and raise the agility of its product demand. Because of extensive interdependence on operational procedures during the partnership, the knowledge may spill over, which not only induces a sudden risk of imitation and opportunism by partners but also reduces the willingness and efforts for a partnership between both parties. To dominate the partnership, the knowledge obtained from data analytics should be specific to the focal firms and causally ambiguous to the partners [11,61].

Past studies have tended to point out that knowledge specificity is an essential factor of a successful interorganizational collaboration for the focal firms [79]. Biggiero and Sammarra [80] and Wang and Tarn [72] emphasize that one major purpose in a collaborative relationship is to recombine domain knowledge to form specific knowledge. Partnering agility is the outcome of the focal firms’ collaboration with their external partners based on their demands to make up for any recent deficiency or to strengthen existing advantages. Based on the purposes of enhancing marketing agility, the primary value of the partnership is to provide complementary and synergetic knowledge resources to compensate or strengthen what the focal firms lack. The specific knowledge resource of the focal firm is thus an essential incentive to exchange and complement knowledge with the partners [81]. With a perspective of market knowledge, many data analytic efforts of the focal firms, such as applying past market experience on current marketing activities, clarifying critical market indicators by statistical analysis, and obtaining customer responses from social media, should be conducted on a solid basis of owned and specific knowledge to blend into collaboration and assistance from the partners [79,81]. If the focal firm acquires new market knowledge from data analytics that is inconsistent or heteroge neous with existing knowledge domains, then it needs to collaborate with the partners to encounter possible new conditions beforehand, particularly when the data are obtained from social media and platforms [64].

The previous review signifies that the conducts of data analytics should generate specific market knowledge for the basis of determining partnering agility. We, hence, build the following hypothesis.

H6. : Market knowledge specificity of a firm positively mediates the association of data analytics with partnering agility.

## 2.5.3. Data analytics, market knowledge specificity, and operational agility

Subject to organizational and relationship embeddedness, the oper ational process of a firm is organizational specific [3]. The core concept of operational agility is to quickly adjust market-related operational processes and agilely reorganize specific resources and knowledge to respond to market changes [67], to reduce/eliminate information asymmetry and communication gaps across departments of organiza tions, and to collaboratively employ market knowledge and resources specific to the organization that help it correctly face environmental changes and market opportunities [34,71]. Chatfield and Raddick [3] advocate that data analytics can discover the routines embedded in critical administrative processes in governmental sectors to create public value with citizens. Hence, interorganizational knowledge to an internal specific context is a core element for operational agility.

The efforts of outcome assessment and opportunity seeking in prac tice circumscribe and confirm the improvement scope of operational procedures by examining market performance and its antecedents and especially by clarifying those market segments or marketing operations that exhibit weaker performance, poorer customer loyalty, and lower satisfaction. They may also set operational objectives for exploring po tential and declining segments based on historical data and market forecasting [34]. Because data of outcome assessment originate from inside the organizations, outcomes of data analytics help generate market knowledge with higher specificity. Clark et al. [77] argue that information infrastructure, which is properly configured for particular organizational demand, is able to acquire data for detailed analysis, thus raising the expertise and knowledge specificity of the members. Praha lad and Bettis [82] point out that specific organizational knowledge evokes in-depth interdisciplinary interaction and cooperation across units of organizations, which lead to specific ideas for new pro ducts/services to encounter new demands. Past literature has indicated that operational agility relies on flexible knowledge transfer and sharing with units in organizations, thus highly relating to the organizations ability to create specific knowledge [68,83,84].

The primary utility of network observation is to employ social ana lytics’ techniques or social network tools, to obtain customer responses and opinions directly from the market, to clarify current market obsta cles, managerial problems, and potential opportunities, and to learn more about specific customers and market segments. The core effect is to help firms deepen their knowledge and understanding of the market place. Thus, knowledge specificity to the market plays a fundamental role in operational tasks such as resource reallocation, process improvement, education and training redesign, and target market modification [29]. This study thus infers that market knowledge speci ficity of a firm plays a mediating role in the association between data analytics and operational agility and builds the next hypothesis.

H7. : Market knowledge specificity of a firm positively mediates the association of data analytics with operational agility.

## 3.1. Research framework

Following the previous inference and setup of hypotheses, this study consists of three constructs: data analytics, marketing agility, and mar ket knowledge specificity. The research framework of further efforts is exhibited in Fig. 3.

## 3.2. Research method

## 3.2.1. Steps of the empirical research

The empirical part consists of two studies. Because the major construct of this study (i.e., data analytics’ orientation) is a growing issue with divergent viewpoints, we thus construct a new model based on Zack’s [43] dichotomies. It is essential to appraise the fitness of the new framework and to circumscribe the content of each data analytics construct. Therefore, we conduct a pilot study, Study I, with an in-depth interview to verify the new model. Based on the results of Study I, we further conduct three empirical surveys to measure and assess the new model and to examine the previous hypotheses; i.e., Study II.

## 3.2.2. Study I: an in-depth interview

This study conducts an exploratory in-depth interview to build up a basis for further scale measurement and the empirical surveys. Among seven practitioners invited as the interviewees, four are marketing managers in service industries, including two in charge of social media and advertising in a convenience store chain and transportation services, respectively; one is in charge of sales management in an express delivery service; and one is the chief marketing executive of a franchise restau rant system. The other three interviewees are information managers: one is in charge of data analytics of an e-commerce platform, one is in charge of social media analytics for an Internet celebrity agent, and one is the chief information officer of marketing research services. All seven interviewees conduct data analytics in their daily tasks and encounter such changing conditions of customers and competitors in the market place that can be treated as representatives for this in-depth interview.

The interview was conducted one-on-one (one interviewer and one interviewee). Prior to the formal interview, we designed three interview questions and passed them on to the interviewees. The questions include: (1) What analytical efforts are conducted in your company corresponding to each of the constructs? (2) What contents of data are required in your company corresponding to each of the constructs? (3) What are the pros and cons for the utilization of each data analytics construct in your company? To help the interviewees realize the research purpose and issues, we also attached two data analytics readings [4,40] accompanying the questions.

All contents of the interviews were recorded and then decoded jointly by two experts, who are business professors with doctorate de grees and professional majors in marketing and information management. They both have offered educational courses and carried out research projects of data and social media analytics during the past three years. Each item was rated on a three-point scale for the degree that the items fit the meanings of the particular constructs (1=strongly agree, 2=agree, and 3=disagree). The two experts conducted content analysis independently. They decided to set both interviewees rating the items as 1 (strongly agree) as the acceptance threshold of item fit and set them ≥2 items for each construct as the lower limits of the item amounts. Table 2 summarizes the results. The items’ descriptors of marketing efforts for data analytics are selected as the basis for the item descriptors for further scale measurement.

3.2.3.2. The formal survey. To examine the hypotheses, we then employ the scales to conduct the formal study. The detailed item descriptors are exhibited in Table 4. Here, we describe the sampling method, sample profiles, and data analysis techniques as follows.

3.2.3.3. Sampling and the samples. Because this study concerns mar keting agility and data analytics issues, we select the observations based on the next considerations. First, observations directly encounter market conditions rather than firms that just solely conduct manufacturing ac tivities. Second, observations have internal solid information infra structure and systems to conduct data analytics’ efforts. Third, observations employ market knowledge as the basis to make market related decisions and conduct marketing efforts rather than just con tract manufacturing. Fourth, we are able to obtain enough samples with solid sampling frames and a sufficient amount of observations.

Based on previous considerations, this study selects service in dustries as the sample for the empirical survey. Many manufacturers do not directly face market changes, whereas some contract manufacturers may just meet a small quantity of customers. It is not critical for them to engage in delicate market agility and data analytics. Relative to manufacturing sectors, service industry providers encounter a more dynamic and turbulent market environment and directly meet customer demand and rival competition. The significant scale of their customers forces them to employ data analytics to agilely respond to the market. Thus, this study aims to set service industries as the target of the empirical survey.

From the previous considerations, this study builds the sample population from the Top 500 Service Enterprises and Top 100 Financial Enterprises Ranking in Taiwan surveyed by Common Wealth Magazine, which is one of the most trustworthy industrial rankings in Taiwan. Taking into account the acquaintance and relevance to marketing agility and data analytics, this study sets three types of professional positions as the targets: (1) the CEOs, (2) the executives in charge of marketing, and (3) the executives in charge of information management. This study employs source separation sampling to avoid single-source bias induced from common method variance [86]. The three types of samples are assigned to respond to respective items: either one of their type (1) CEOs or type (2) marketing executives in the same firm is asked to respond to items of marketing agility and market knowledge specificity. The t-test results indicate that the mean differences of these items between types (1) and (2) are all insignificant, while type (3) information executive are assigned to answer the item descriptors of data analytics.

All 600 service providers were surveyed by mailing sampling. The questionnaires were mailed to the respondents with a description mentioning their names and job titles on the envelopes. After a 45-day period of data collection (from March to May 2017), 202 observations were returned. After deleting 4 unusable ones, this study gathered 198 effective respondents (33% rate of return).

3.2.3.4. Statistical analysis techniques. The study employs multiple regression models to test the hypotheses; those with mediating effects (i. e., H1b–H4b) are examined with Baron and Kenny’s [87] technique. The results are exhibited in Tables 6–9. Models 1–4 present the direct asso ciation of data analytics and market knowledge specificity (i.e., H4); models 5, 12, and 19 list the impacts of knowledge specificity on mar keting agility; models 6, 8, 10, and 11 list the explanations of data an alytics on customer agility (H1a–H1d); models 13, 15, 17, and 18 show the effects of data analytics on partnering agility (H2a–H2d); models 20 and 22 list the impacts of data analytics on operational agility (H3a–Hb); and models 8, 10, 15, 17, and 21 present the mediation of market knowledge specificity in the relationship between data analytics and marketing agility (H5–H7). Finally, we use WarpPLS 7.0 to conduct SEM analysis to examine the overall research framework, including the construct validity and common method bias [88].

This study employs WarpPLS because of the following reasons. Similarly to LISREL, SEM, and PLS, WarpPLS can implement compositeand factor-based algorithms as well as analyze the indirect and total effects of mediating relationship. One other necessity of using WarpPLS is for the examination of common method bias. Kock [88] indicates that neither of both elements of a traditional confirmatory factor analysis (i. e., convergent and discriminant validity tests) can be effective in iden tifying common method bias, whereas WarpPLS offers an effective alternative. Classically, collinearity is defined as a predictor–predictor pattern in multiple regression models. In case of two or more predictors measuring the same construct or facet, they are defined as being collinear; this definition is restricted to vertical collinearity. Lateral collinearity, in contrast, is defined as a predictor–criterion case, whereby a predictor variable measures the same construct or a facet as a variable to which it points in a model, which is the criterion variable in the predictor–criterion relationship [88].

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Data analytics and market knowledge specificity

To examine the mediating effects of market knowledge specificity, we should first confirm the positive impacts of data analytics on market knowledge specificity based on Baron and Kenny’s [87] method. As listed in Table $^ { 6 , }$ their results are unexpectedly partially not supported by the empirical survey. The β coefficients of the four data analytics elements (i.e., outcome assessment, opportunity seeking, network observation, and experimental appraisal) are.34, .33, .10, and .07, respectively: only the former two types reach the p < 0.05 statistical significance, signifying that only part of H4 is supported empirically. Such results may be because the data for network observation and experimental appraisal are collected from external sources but do not exclusively belong to the firms and may not be exactly obtained, designed, stored, and owned by the firms, meaning that the data are not specific to them [77]. These data are open, but not confidential, to all customers and competitors, and, hence, it is also hard to block imitation from competitors [73,78]. Based on Baron and Kenny [8], such results signify that the efforts of network observation and experimental appraisal fail to generate knowledge specificity, and subsequently they cannot mediate the degree of marketing agility. Therefore, the continuing statistical analysis regarding H5–H7 is unable to process the mediating effects for network observation and experimental appraisal.

## 4.2. Data analytics, market knowledge specificity, and customer agility

Regression analysis results in Table 7 list the effects of data analytics on customer agility and the mediating effects from market knowledge specificity, indicating that the coefficients of outcome assessment, op portunity seeking, and network observation are 0.22, 0.26, and 0.07, respectively, all of which reach $p < 0 . 0 5$ significance. However, the coefficient of experimental appraisal on customer agility is 0.03, which fails to reach a statistical significance. The results mean that H1a, H1b, and H1c are supported by empirical evidence, while H1d is not statis tically supported.

As for the mediating effects, the coefficients of the mediator and market knowledge specificity are statistically significant at 0.24 (for outcome assessment) and 0.21 (for opportunity seeking). Both β co efficients decrease after adding the mediation of market knowledge specificity (0.22 to 0.14 and 0.26 to 0.19, respectively) with lower sta tistical significances, signifying partial mediating effects. In the positive effects of outcome assessment and opportunity seeking on customer agility, the results reveal that market knowledge specificity plays a mediating effect; H5 thus receives partial support from empirical evidence.

## 4.3. Data analytics, market knowledge specificity, and partnering agility

The results are listed in Table 8, indicating that the regression co efficients of the four constructs are .43, .39, .18, and .17, respectively, all of which are significant below the $p < 0 . 0 5$ level and thus have positive impacts on partnering agility. The results denote that H2a–H2d are supported.

As for the mediating effects, the β coefficients of market knowledge specificity are statistically significant at 0.39 and 0.36 for the mediation of outcome assessment and opportunity seeking, respectively. Both co efficients drop after adding the mediation (0.43 to 0.30 and 0.39 to 0.26, respectively) with lower and still statistical significances, indicating partial mediating effects. The results represent the positive impacts of outcome assessment and opportunity seeking on partnering agility, and market knowledge specificity plays a mediating role. H6 thus receives partial empirical support.

Comparing the scales of coefficients of Tables 7 and 8, we find some interesting differences between the two. All β coefficients for the direct impact of data analytics on partnering agility are larger than those on customer agility (0.03 to 0.26 and 0.17 to .43). The mediating effects of market knowledge specificity on partnering agility are all higher than those on customer agility (0.36 to 0.39 vs. 0.21 to 0.24). The decreased scales of coefficients after adding the moderation in the models of partnering agility are all larger than those of customer agility. Previous evidence indicates that the direct and mediating impacts of data ana lytics and knowledge specificity on partnering agility are more signifi cant than those on customer agility.

## 4.4. Data analytics, market knowledge specificity, and operational agility

The evidence in Table 9 indicates that regression coefficients of outcome assessment and network observation are .22 and .24, respec tively. The statistics of path analysis, moreover, imply that four data ana lytics’ constructs offer a partial explanation on knowledge specificity and the three marketing agility elements. Three of the four data analytics’ constructs (except for opportunity seeking) can practically explain three marketing agility elements (H1a, H2a, H2c, H2d, and H3b are supported), particularly the highest effects on partner agility.

The results also support that knowledge specificity positively medi ates the previous significant effects in the relationship of outcome assessment and two elements of marketing agility: customer and partne agility. The results also support that knowledge specificity generated from data analytics positively impacts the three marketing agility ele ments (the coefficients are .307 to .351). Thus, H5 and H6 are partially supported.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

## 5.1. General discussion

After the literature review, this study finds two major research gaps in data analytics-related studies: they lack (1) employing a solid theo retical basis to construct the architecture of a firm’s data analytics orientation model and fail (2) to comprehend which data analytics work for business organizations. To fill these gaps, this study follows a senseto-respond model, particularly Seo and La Paz’s [31] agility formation process, and accordingly sets generating knowledge specificity and raising organizational marketing agility as the critical purposes of a firm’s data analytics. This sets up a data analytics → knowledge speci ficity → marketing agility causality and aims to clarify the relationship of firms’ data analytic orientation and marketing agility, as well as the mediating effects of market knowledge specificity in the relationship. A four-element data analytics’ orientation model is developed by inter nal/external sources and reactive/proactive purposes’ dichotomies, which are also based on the sense-to-respond model.

This study takes the Top 500 Service Enterprises and Top 100 Financial Enterprises Rankings in Taiwan as the samples and selects their CEOs and executives in charge of marketing and information tasks as the target observations. The empirical survey obtains 198 observa tions with the following conclusions.

The evidence of regression analysis shows that the degree of all fou data analytics’ constructs can raise our understanding about customer behaviors and market changes so that firms can then enhance partnering and operational agilities. Only the two firm-documented constructs have positive effects on customer agility. Such results may derive from the sampling bias of data analytics and respondents’ invasion. Data for network observation and experimental appraisal may be generated by current clients, potential customers, and even the unknown public. The PLS results even indicate a negative impact of experimental assessment on customer agility. From a methodological viewpoint, the sampling of such data sources often lacks sufficient population and solid sampling frame, meaning that the data are only obtained with convenient, non probability sampling [90]. If we consider the respondents’ behaviors, then those observations providing messages, comments, and advice are not exactly qualified for the purpose of analytics. They may not be real current or potential target customers [49] or might even be intentional respondents such as competitors or malicious persons, particularly those efforts of experimental assessment, such that the firms could misread the data and thus misjudge the direction for further marketing decisions and actions.

Outcome assessment and opportunity seeking are also conducted with data collected from inside of the firms based on their particular requirements, organizational contexts, and market conditions. The data system and information infrastructure may be built, acquired, or self generated based on particular purposes such as customer facets, competitor responses, or marketing issues [77]. The analyzed data are formed as a knowledge base in secret and stored in documentary or interpersonal communication networks inside of the organizations [70]. The market knowledge they generate shows tacit causality and ambi guity and thus reduces the possibility of information leakage and limi tation of the competitors [28,75]. Such data analytics are conducted particularly on market conditions, and thus the data are relatively cor rect, specific, and tacit to the firms for raising marketing agility relative to that for network observation and experimental appraisal [73,78].

We also infer that market knowledge specificity is a mediator in the association between the four data analytics’ constructs and marketing agility. The results of regression analysis and PLS, however, point out that only the two firm-documented data analytics’ constructs confirm the mediation. The two market-generated types only show direct effect on partnering and operational agility, whereas they fail to generate knowledge specificity as to what we hypothesize. We infer that this occurs because of the foreignness of the data sources [49]. Data of network observation and experimental appraisal are gathered externally for the firms. Thus, they can acquire data on their demand directly from the marketplace, but the data are open to the public simultaneously [4, 47], which makes the data also available for competitors and makes them be aware of firms’ market intention after gathering the data. This could even increase the possibility of imitation and defensive actions such that the knowledge is not specific any more [90]. Past studies have pointed out that the market signal may be inconsistent with what the firms interpret from it [91]. Data collected from social media and websites may not match what the firms really need because of users inferior expression for the generated content or perceptual gaps between the respondents and the firms [90]. This reduces the effect of network observation and experimental appraisal on market knowledge speci ficity and thus obstructs its mediation on marketing agility.

Empirical evidence indicates that data analytics present worse effect on customer agility relative to those on partnering and operational agility. Partnering agility depends on firms’ collaboration to dissemi nate, converge, and generate knowledge with critical partners; opera tional agility relies on speedy and accurate adaptation on marketing related operational processes [55]. The critical concern for both is the internal resources of the organizations rather than facing sophisticated and dynamic external conditions in the marketplace, which is consistent with the results that firm-documented data analytics perform better on marketing agility than those of market-generated ones. This result seem to convey that data analytics only partially work for marketing agility. Firm-documented data are sourced from historical market transactions inside of the organization, which are easy to link past market experi ences with internal operational procedures to adapt for the future and also contribute to sharing and distributing the information to partners to collaboratively detect and comprehend current market conditions. Customer agility, however, involves a broad domain of market factors, such as culture, demographics, consumer behavior, and their reaction to marketing actions, making the firms need a higher variety of external data to cover diversified facets regarding customers and also reducing the effects. This finding seems to echo why data analytics-related literature focuses on the variety of the data [9,90].

The results indicate that the degrees of network observations and experimental appraisals fail to generate significant market knowledge specificity, and, hence, we cease to clarify any further mediation on marketing agility like what the hypotheses (H5–H7) infer. The other examination of mediating effects, however, supports the hypotheses that market knowledge specificity indeed plays a mediating role in the cases of outcome assessment and opportunity seeking. From the details of the β coefficients, mediating effects on partnering and operational agilitie are larger than on customer agility. The coefficients in Tables 7–10 also signify that knowledge specificity has higher direct effects on partnering and operational agilities than that on customer agility. The previous results consistently confirm the impact of market knowledge specificity on raising marketing agility.

This study also offers an approach to classify the observations into three types of data analytics’ orientation with cluster analysis: data af fluents, internal searchers, and external utilizers. ANOVA results indi cate that, relative to those emphasizing internal or external data sources (i.e., internal searchers and external utilizers), those firms that comprehensively conduct data analytics involving all the four constructs (i.e., data affluents) consistently perform the best on marketing agility. Such evidence signifies that the four data analytics’ constructs might be complementary to each other rather than exclusive or incompatible. According to the numbers, 55% are classified into this cluster, signifying that most firms that conduct data analytics tend to exert effort and re sources on the four constructs in a full manner.

## 5.2. Marketing/managerial contribution

This empirical study confirms the better effects of outcome assess ment and opportunity seeking on market knowledge specificity and marketing agility than network observation and experimental appraisal, denoting that it is relatively worth investing more in the two firmdocumented data analytics. We infer that such effects come from the features of the two data analytics, such as their interiority, tactness, and causal ambiguity [28,75]. To ensure their effects, the firms should measure or collect performance indicators based on specific marketing objectives, build tailor-made databases and information infrastructure rooted in internal resources and organizational embeddedness [22,47], sophisticatedly analyze the internal data based on their demand to comprehend particular customer segments, competitor reactions, and marketing issues [73,78], and confidentially store the generated knowledge within documentary or interpersonal communication net works inside of their organization.

The evidence supports the hypotheses that network observation and experimental appraisal have positive direct influences on marketing agility; their effects on market knowledge specificity, however, are not statistically supported. Such results signify that data collection and database building for these two market-generated analytics need more consideration for organizational specificity. For instance, firms should search for messages on social platforms rooted in their own particular resources and product/service items with a competitive edge, involve intensive competitive perspectives and awareness in network observation, and provide exclusive and advantageous potential alternatives for experimental appraisal [4,47]. Collecting social messages unsystemati cally and broadly could just make the analytical efforts go in vain. To ensure data correctness and avoid inaccurate and malicious messages from external data sources, firms need more data auditing and detection to improve data quality from external sources. Some recently developed tools and techniques, such as data cleaning and verification, could be critical for data quality and analytical practices in the near future [9,90]. Another reason may be the short history of application. Data analytics are less of a newly developed issue lately than statistics and performance evaluation. Most firms may not have sufficient analytics for social platforms, particularly small- and medium-size enterprises. To strengthen a firm’s capability, it is very essential to develop social analytical talents or bring in relevant techniques and equipment for recent requirements.

Finally, empirical results indicate that, among the three elements of marketing agility, data analytics exhibit the worst effects on customer agility. This study infers that it may be because customer-related factors are diversified and complicated, and, hence, raising customer agility needs a higher extent of data variety for analytical efforts. Data variety plays a major role in past studies regarding data analytics [9,90]. Firms must put forth efforts to understand more customer-related indicators, such as their satisfaction, repurchase behaviors, and customer referrals and membership, and not just those internal indicators regarding finance, profitability, and productivity. Firms need to collect in-depth data regarding customer demographics and behavioral factors to comprehend their existing and potential customers and accordingly conduct detailed and elaborate data analytics to clarify the causality of these factors on customer behaviors. As for the data gathered from social media and open platforms, firms need to obtain and clarify such external data and market intelligence in a broader and more complete manner. Except for their behavioral and demographic factors and decision con siderations, they still need to pay more attention to in-depth and tacit messages, including feelings, emotional reactions, involvement, prefer ences, and complaints. Moreover, they should be more sensible to the customers’ responses to marketing actions and to their competitors to strengthen market knowledge.

## 5.3. Theoretical contributions and future research directions

Based on the literature review, model construction, and empirical efforts, we provide some theoretical and academic contributions for the marketing field. This study follows the sense-and-respond perspective to build a framework based on Parise et al.’s [42] classification, Zack’s [43] dichotomies, and Moorman’s [44] dimensions and thus classifies data analytics’ orientation as outcome assessments, opportunity seeking, network observations, and experimental appraisals. The SEM examination of model goodness of fit confirms its construction, and the empirical evidence linking the four constructs with knowledge speci ficity and marketing agility also supports explanation of the new framework. This study, hence, contributes to building a novel and po tential sense-and-respond perspective to circumscribe and explore organizational data analytics’ conducts for further marketing academic research.

Except for the previous four elements, this study develops the framework based on two features of data analytics as the dichotomies – i.e., firm-documented versus market-generated and reactive versus proactive purposes – which have been supported by the measurement assessment and the empirical evidence. The development is rooted in the practices and characteristics of data analytics while corresponding to relevant theoretical bases [43,44]. Such a framework not only could supplement the gap between the data analytics’ practices and academic conducts, but also update the classical viewpoints regarding knowledge management strategy [43] and market information process [44] to be closer to the era of data analytics. In addition, this study involves data collected from social media and online platforms (i.e., market-generated) as the dichotomy, which may offer progress for the framework to keep up with the recent trend of data analytics for mar keting usages.

Data analytics are a prominent and growing subject in recent years. The real effect of data analytics, however, is still of concern to the marketing and business academic community. This study conducts an empirical investigation by linking data analytics with marketing agility and market knowledge specificity, and the results initially verify the impacts. Thus, this study advances the effects of data analytics on marketing actions.

The results herein indicate the individual effects of each data ana lytics’ construct – that is, firm-documented analytics perform better than market-generated ones. This study accordingly advocates that each data analytics’ construct may have heterogeneous contributions to marketing actions and provides explanation and research directions for the future.

Based on efforts of the previous empirical study, we provide some opportunities for future research. This study follows the sense-andrespond perspective as well as Seo and La Paz’s [31] framework to clarify the association of data analytics, knowledge specificity, and organizational marketing agility. The regression analysis results (Tables 7–9) from the empirical survey indicate 0.03–0.12 R-square values for the explanation of market-generated data, while the co efficients of firm-documented data are higher and range from 0.07 to 0.37. From a closer look at the WarpPLS results for the overall model (Table 9), the average R-square is up to 0.300, and there is a sufficient R-square contribution ratio of 0.95, which is near the ideal standard of 1.00. It signifies that the model has a moderate explanation level; there are, however, still other factors to explain agility with data analytics issues and knowledge specificity. Therefore, future efforts could follow this data analytics → knowledge specificity → marketing agility cau sality to involve sufficient factors to comprehend the explanation on organizational agility.

This study sets up a framework based on a sense-and-respond perspective, Parise et al.’s [42] classification, Zack’s [43] dichotomies, and Moorman’s [44] dimensions and thus builds, measures, and assesses a four-construct model of organizational data analytics’ orientation and initially confirms its explanation on marketing efforts. Hence, the newly developed framework seems feasible and applicable for application and replication in future research.

This study also employs firm-documented versus market-generated and reactive versus proactive purposes as the dimensions for the theo retical basis of the data analytic framework. The framework is not only empirically supported, but each dimension could potentially help with more specific progress. For instance, firm-documented data can further be classified into specific types, such as deal records, market research data, and historical market documents, among others; while the marketgenerated content could be provided by customers, producers, pro viders, and potential users [40]. As for reactive versus proactive di chotomy, the descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive analytics are still emergent issues in marketing fields and need more clarification by academic efforts [45]. There is still solid space for further development of data analytics dimensions.

We have presented the previous model with two dichotomies. Data analytics are a growing academic and practical field involving much diversified techniques, tools, and applications. It is reasonable that we may classify data analytics’ conducts with alternative dimensions or perspectives. For instance, Grover et al. [9] employ symbolic versus functional dichotomies with a value creation perspective for model construction, which can be followed to reexamine the related issues.

The empirical results of this study exhibit that the two marketgenerated data analytics have worse explanation than the firmdocumented ones. Both network observations and experimental ap praisals are newer and growing analytical techniques relative to outcome assessments and opportunity seeking. Thus, one still needs to reconfirm whether and why their effects are weaker. Potential directions include conducting replicatory studies for reconfirmation and focusing on the side effects from the causes of foreignness, malice, and prob lematic sampling. These new data analytics’ techniques and tools are worth more consideration and verification.

## Statement of Intended Contribution

Data analytics is playing a growing role in marketing and related fields, including academic and practical sectors. Nevertheless, most studies are partial to some particular analytical facets, while the whole picture and the effectiveness of data analytics are still neglected. It is thus a worthy and potential issue to construct a solid architecture to portray a firm’s behavior when conducting data analytics, named data analytics orientation, and to clarify the impact of data analytics on marketing efforts. Under pressure from drastic changes in the market place, the recent literature endeavors to investigate how firms perceive changes and comprehend market conditions to respond to them accordingly, i.e., marketing agility. After the literature review, this study finds two major research gaps in data analytics-related studies: they lack (1) employing a solid theoretical basis to construct the architecture of a firm’s data analytics orientation model and fail (2) to comprehend which data analytics work for the business organizations.

To respond to them, we comprehend the contents and behaviors of data analytics in marketing practice to construct a data analytics orientation model based on sense-and-respond perspective, Seo and La Paz’s (2008) model, Parise et al.’s (2012) classification, Zack’s (1999) dichotomies, and Moorman’s (1995) dimensions, and thus classify data analytics orientation as outcome assessments, opportunity seeking, network observations, and experimental appraisals. Based on the model, this study then attempts to explore how data analytics of a firm can help raise its marketing agility through specific market knowledge. Hence, they lead to the following research questions:

What are the contents and behaviors of a firm’s data analytics conducts? How can we construct the architecture of a data analytics orientation model for a marketing purpose?

Is the effort of data analytics able to raise the marketing agility of an organization?

Is the effort of data analytics able to build specific market knowledge that raises the marketing agility of a firm?

The results indicate the individual, but dissimilar, effects of each data analytics construct: firm-documented analytics perform better than market-generated ones. This study, hence, contributes to building a novel perspective to circumscribe and explore data analytics conducts for further marketing academic research, and accordingly advocates that each data analytics construct may have heterogeneous contribu tions to marketing actions.
