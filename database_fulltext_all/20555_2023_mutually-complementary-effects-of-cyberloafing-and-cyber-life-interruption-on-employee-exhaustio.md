# Mutually complementary effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on employee exhaustion

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cyberloafing Cyber-life-interruption Exhaustion Personal demands Personal resources Information and communication technology

## A B S T R A C T

Currently, people are accustomed to cyberloafing, or nonwork-related cyber activities during working hours, and cyber-life-interruption, or work-related cyber activities during nonworking hours. Job or personal demands no longer rely on either work- or nonwork-related domain resources. We propose a dynamic demands-resources model regarding cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption to elucidate their direct and interaction effects on work and nonwork exhaustion. By identifying these dynamics, this study can enable both employees and em plovers to utilize these activities for better management of emplovee experiences in the technology-enabled flexible working environment, which, in turn, would bring about significant implications for employee job performance.

## 1. Introduction

Information and communication technology (ICT) has fundamen tally changed the relationship between work and life. People are always connected to the internet, and the ubiquity of mobile devices has blurred the boundaries between work and life. Employee switching between work and life has become frequent and rapid. It is no longer the case that employees work only during their office hours and completely enjoy their personal lives outside of office hours. Rather, people seem to be constantly switching back and forth between personal and work roles at both home and work. At the same time, such switches also mean that ICT has brought great flexibility and freedom. Particularly, in the pandemic situation, the traditional notion of a physical space where people go to work together is inappropriate. Indeed, many high-tech companies have already relied on employees who are located worldwide and may never meet face to face. Given growing ICT-enabled flexible working, it is important to understand how cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption affect employees’ work and nonwork exhaustion, which is linked to their work productivity and quality of life.

Cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption have marked the changing work environment and work style of employees in the digital age. Cyberloafing refers to nonwork-related cyber activities during working hours, whereas cyber-life-interruption refers to work-related cyber ac tivities during nonworking hours (Table 1). They have received wide spread attention. First, regarding cyberloafing, some prior research has emphasized its dark side of stealing job resources for personal demands [e.g., 1–3], but others have regarded it as a means to refresh employees minds or fulfill tasks for families and friends [e.g., 4–6]. Second, regarding cyber-life-interruption, some prior research has pinpointed its dark side of draining personal resources for job demands [e.g., 7–9], but others have viewed it as a means to achieve employees’ ambitions or complete tasks for their colleagues and organizations [e.g., 10–12].

Prior literature has separately examined the impact of cyberloafing or cyber-life-interruption on employee work- and nonwork-related outcomes, such as employees’ work and nonwork exhaustion. Work and nonwork exhaustion have prominent values in academic research because they are the most commonly discussed feature in organizational research and are found to have the most consistent relationships with job performance [13–15]. It is also recognized as one of the critical outcomes in the IT work environment [16]. Investigating the variations in work and nonwork exhaustion triggered by cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption can enhance the understanding of employee perceptions and outcomes. Since most employees are engaged in both activities in their daily lives, the lack of nuanced understanding of the interaction effect of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption can lead to misguided managerial actions and policymaking in the ICT-enabled flexible working environment.

Although cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption are similar in terms of their nature and medium, they play two completely different roles in employee daily lives. Both act as double-edged swords. Cyber loafing, which relies on work resources to fulfill personal demands, may aggravate work exhaustion but alleviate nonwork exhaustion, whereas cyber-life-interruption, which relies on personal resources to fulfill job demands, may aggravate nonwork exhaustion but alleviate work exhaustion. As employees engage in cyberloafing and cyber-life interruption, job and personal demands no longer rely on either work or nonwork domain resources. Cyberloafing may reduce the nonwork exhaustion triggered by cyber-life-interruption, while cyber-lifeinterruption may reduce the work exhaustion triggered by cyberloaf ing. Both work and nonwork exhaustion may be minimized as a result. In other words, their advantage may complement another’s weakness. Since very few studies have investigated their interaction effect, the following research question has been posed as the basis of this study: “Could cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption complement each other? And how?” In other words, do cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption exert beneficial (adverse) effects on employees’ work and nonwork exhaus tion? How do their individual beneficial effects moderate each other’s adverse effects to alleviate work and nonwork exhaustion?

This study aims to explore the direct and interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on employee exhaustion that emerge from work and nonwork domains. Grounded in the job demandsresources theory [17], this study proposes a new dynamic demands-resources model with regard to cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption. Particularly, it draws on different perspectives of the conservation of resources theory [18] to explain the underlying psychological process of the complementary effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on work and nonwork exhaustion.

Using a quantitative survey of 751 employees, this study attempts to elucidate the behaviors and perceptions of general employees who play vital roles in the economy. The results provide support for the suggestion that the mutually complementary effect of cyberloafing and cyber-life interruption could mitigate nonwork exhaustion.

This study contributes to both the theoretical and practical levels. Theoretically, this study extends the job demands-resources theory from the work domain to the nonwork domain by proposing a new dynamic demands-resources model with regard to cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption. Drawing upon the conservation of resources theory, this study further illuminates the underlying psychological process behind the complementary effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on employees’ work and nonwork exhaustion. This study extends the literature in which cyberloafing and cyber-life- interruption have been examined separately and fragmentedly. The results show that cyber loafing and cyber-life-interruption individually had a direct effect on intensifying both work and nonwork exhaustion. Nevertheless, when cyber-life-interruption and cyberloafing interact, they change the strength of the original relationship between cyber-life-interruption and nonwork exhaustion. The results also demonstrate that when cyber loafing and cyber-life-interruption were high, both work and nonwork exhaustion could be minimized

Practically, our empirical results contribute to various levels. The findings enable employers and employees to utilize cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption in the ICT-enabled flexible working environ ment. It will arouse awareness of the complementary effect between cyberloafing and cyber-life- interruption and provide employers with useful insights. Employers, hence, will be inspired to revisit ICT-related policies to achieve the proper balance that benefits both employers and employees. This study can also enable IT practitioners, such as software engineers, to understand the strengths and threats of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption and provide fit-for-purpose applications and even interventions to maximize (minimize) the positive (negative) im pacts on employee experiences. These practical IT solutions may, in turn, further convince employers and employees to accept and realize the flexibility and freedom of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption.

## 2. Theoretical development

In this section, first, work and nonwork exhaustion is defined. Next, the job demands-resources theory is introduced to explore the potential demands-resources dynamics across work and nonwork domains induced by cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption. Then, the hypoth eses regarding the individual direct effects of the activities on work and nonwork exhaustion are discussed. Furthermore, the conservation of resources theory is used to explain the hypotheses regarding the inter action effects of the activities.

## 2.1. Work and nonwork exhaustion

According to the job demands-resources theory, exhaustion is one of the symptoms of strain caused by job demands and leads to poor job performance [17]. Halbesleben and Bowler [13] noted that employees exhaustion has become almost the most commonly discussed feature in organizational research because it is a primary indicator of burnout. It has been found to have the most consistent relationships with outcome variables, such as job performance [13–15]. It is also recognized as one of the critical outcomes in the IT work environment [16]. Therefore, investigating the variations in employees’ exhaustion triggered by spe cific factors, e.g., cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption in this study, can enhance the understanding of employee perceptions and outcomes.

Exhaustion can be divided into two types, namely, work exhaustion and nonwork exhaustion, depending on the source of strain. Work exhaustion is defined as “the depletion of one’s emotional and mental energy by work demands” [19]. Job demands that outweigh the avail able resources lead to work exhaustion [18]. Likewise, if employees find that their resources are not sufficient to fulfill their personal demands, they suffer from nonwork exhaustion [20,21], which is “the depletion of one’s emotional and mental energy by nonwork demands” [19].

## 2.2. Job demands-resources theory

The job demands-resources theory, introduced by Bakker et al. [17], is a well-established theory that illustrates the demands-resources dy namics in a work domain. Its basic assumption is that job characteristics can be considered either job demands or job resources. Job demands refer to the aspect of a job that consumes employee mental or physical energy. It is also proven to be a unique predictor of strain, e.g., work exhaustion, that hinders employee job performance. An example of job demands can be a task assigned by supervisor or an enquiry of a client that takes employees’ time and effort to fulfill. Job resources, on the other hand, refer to the aspect of a job that can help employees to achieve work goal or personal development, cope with job demands, and, hence, reduce strain. It can be time or automation tools that are used for work-related purposes and address job demands. The job demands-resources model has further extended to suggest that if job resources are not sufficient, employees may utilize personal resources, for example, self-efficacy and optimism, to soothe the relationship between job demands and strain [22]. Other than job demands, the prior literature has also reckoned that the personal characteristics of employees, such as emotional instability and ambitions, impose personal demands on themselves [23].

While the job demands-resources theory confines its focus to only the work domain, we attempt to extend the theory by integrating the liter ature on the nonwork domain. Derks et al. [24] noted that smartphone use in the workplace, which allows employees to keep connected with their family and friends, extends personal demands to the work domain and causes employees to use workplace resources to deal with those personal demands. This is in line with the concept of cyberloafing in this study, which describes nonwork-related cyber activities during working hours. Demerouti [25] has also recognized that digitalization, which enables flexible work schedules and locations even outside of the working hours and workplace, are the resources that help employees reduce job demands. This is similar to the concept of cyber-life-interruption in this study, which describes work-related cyber activities during nonworking hours outside of the office. With the aid of ICT, employees are able and expected to deal with both personal and job demands anytime and anywhere. As employees engage in cyberloafing and cyber-life- interruption, the dynamics of demands and resources will no longer be limited within one single domain.

There are clues in the recent literature that demands-resources dy namics across work and nonwork domains exist and affect employee experiences in both domains. For example, diminishing personal au tonomy can accomplish professional commitment [11]; employees work engagement is energized by their nonwork-related activities of the previous day [26]; the positive effect of work engagement can spill over to nurture happy dual-earning couples [27]; knowledge professionals who collaborate remotely with globally distributed partners are more likely to suffer from work–life conflict that leads to high turnover intention and poor job performance [28]; and workaholism leads to work–family conflicts [29]. They implicitly mentioned that the situa tional or social resources obtained from one domain can lead to positive outcomes in the opposite domain, or the demands of one domain can lead to negative problems in the opposite domain. However, the dy namics of demands and resources across work and nonwork domains induced by cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption have rarely been mentioned in the literature. Identifying these dynamics will shed light on employees’ perceptions of the activities, thereby enabling both em ployees and employers to utilize the activities for better outcomes in the ICT- enabled flexible working environment.

## 2.3. Demands and resources in the work domain

## 2.3.1. Cyberloafing – personal demands in the work domain

Lim [30] defined cyberloafing as the voluntary use of company internet access by employees for nonwork-related purposes during their office hours at their office. Because of the ubiquity and versatility of smartphones and tablets, cyberloafing is no longer desk-bound or limited to company internet access. Therefore, cyberloafing as defined in this study is the voluntary use of ICT by employees for nonwork related cyber activities during working hours.

Mobile devices allow employees to stay connected with family and friends and further complete nonwork-related tasks for them during working hours or at office [4,24,31]. This is not only because employees want to be available for their family and friends during working hours [24] but also due to some personal tasks or obligations that cannot be completed during nonworking hours [4]. Cyberloafing is another loafing behavior that allows employees to detach from work [32–34]. Em ployers criticize it as deviant behavior that abuses work time and workplace resources and therefore hinders organizational performance [35,36]. Employees perform cyberloafing to retaliate against unfavor able outcomes, such as work boredom or overload, unfair rewards, and lack of control over their work environment [2,37]. The literature has proven that the use of smartphones may become habitual and interfere with work [38].

Cyberloafing can distract users to the point that they have difficulty returning to the normal workflow [39] and negatively influence their job performance [40]; hence, it should be controlled [41].

Based on the previous literature, cyberloafing is a nonwork-related activity that consumes job resources to fulfill personal demands. Per sonal demands can be a request for the help of a family member, a gathering to mingle with friends, or idle time to refresh employees stressed minds [e.g., 4,33]; job resources can be the time or automation tools that are supposed to be used for work-related purposes [e.g., 17, 25]. Despite the motivation, the nature of cyberloafing is in line with the definition of “demands” in the job demands-resources theory. In the case of cyberloafing, it is the personal demands that occur on the wrong side – the work domain. This leads to extended demands-resources dynamics – using job resources to address personal demands. As employees are criticized for their misuse of job resources and/or unfinished job de mands accumulate due to cyberloafing, employee work exhaustion in creases. In this connection, this study postulates that cyberloafing aggravates work exhaustion.

H1a: Cyberloafing increases work exhaustion.

## 2.3.2. Cyber-life-interruption – personal resources in the work domain

Cyber-life-interruption is defined as the interruption of one’s family or personal life by working during nonworking hours through ICT. Previous literature has argued that by engaging in cyber-lifeinterruption, employees can bring job demands into the nonwork domain and use personal resources to fulfill job demands even after working hours [10]. They can stay connected with their jobs while away from the office and finish their work without being physically present at their workplaces [12,42]. Cyber-life- interruption can be considered an opportunity to show off their abilities and commitment to their orga nizations [9]. Employees can allocate their personal resources, such as time and energy, to complete unfinished or more work tasks, and hence, their work exhaustion can be alleviated [19].

Overall, cyber-life-interruption consumes personal resources to fulfill job demands. Job demands can be a task or a request from a colleague, supervisor, or client, an online meeting with partners of different time zones, or acquisition of job-related knowledge [e.g., 9,

19]; personal resources can be the time, energy, or even social connec tion that employees can solicit and are supposed to be spent on nonwork-related purposes [e.g., 19,43]. While the source of cyber-life-interruption resources is a nonwork domain (i.e., personal resources), cyber-life-interruption can be regarded as the additional “resources” in a work domain based on the job demands-resources theory. This implies extended demands-resources dynamics – using personal resources to address job demands. According to the job demands-resources theory, as job demands are fulfilled by cyber-life-interruption, their work exhaustion can be reduced. Hence, this study postulates that cyber-life-interruption alleviates work exhaustion.

## H1b: Cyber-life-interruption decreases work exhaustion.

## 2.3.3. Compensation perspective of the conservation of resources theory

As prior literature has rarely discussed cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption together, their potential complementary relationship ha yet to be noticed. The job demands-resources theory can help predict employees’ exhaustion and behavioral reactions to their asymmetric cyber activities but cannot explain the psychological process [22]. Thus, in the following section, the conservation of resources theory is utilized to elucidate the psychological mechanisms underlying the complemen tary effect of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption.

Under the conservation of resource theory, resources are defined as the objects, personal characteristics, conditions, or energies that are valuable to a person or act as a means for a person to attain the resources [18]. Although Hobfoll [18] has not clearly defined demands, he ha noted that demands are something to be coped with adequate resources; otherwise, stress will arise, and stress usually leads to employees exhaustion. In fact, the resources and demands of the conservation of resource theory conceptually align with the resources and demands in the job demands-resources theory. Only the job demands-resources theory focuses on the resources and demands that are specifically related to the work domain.

Moreover, the conservation of resources theory suggests that when an individual’s resources are threatened or drained by heavy demands from one source, he or she will reallocate other available resources or acquire new resources from other sources to replenish the drained re sources and fulfill the demands [18]. Referring to the “compensation” perspective of the conservation of resources theory, this study proposes that, as engaging in cyberloafing, employees can also perform cyber-life-interruption to utilize their personal resources to replenish their job resources and fulfill their remaining job demands flexibly.

Employees are expected to use workplace resources for work-related purposes and perform work-related activities during working hours. Because of cyberloafing, they cannot allocate sufficient time and energy to fulfill their job demands or feel the tension of engaging in deviant behavior [2,30]. Because of unfinished job demands or the pressures of performing deviant behaviors, work exhaustion emerges. To alleviate the mental burdens or secure employment, employees will need to reallocate personal resources and continue working beyond working hours. Employees engaged in cyberloafing regard cyber-life-interruption as an opportunity to compensate for the work domain, alleviating work exhaustion. Thus, this study postulates that cyber-life-interruption can mitigate the increased work exhaustion caused by cyberloafing.

H1c: Cyber-life-interruption moderates the relationship between cyber loafing and work exhaustion such that the adverse effect of cyberloafing on work exhaustion becomes weaker as cyber-life- interruption is higher.

## 2.4. Demands and resources in the nonwork domain

## 2.4.1. Cyber-life-interruption – job demands in the nonwork domain

Most previous literature has argued that by allowing constant con nectivity to work, cyber-life-interruption can bring work stress into the home [7]. It is also referred to as an electronic leash, as it ties employees to work during nonworking hours [44]. Employees are supposed to relax physically at home and detach psychologically from work after the workday and restore their energy for the next day of work [45].

However, cyber-life-interruption causes employees to drain personal resources from the nonwork domain to fulfill job demands [19]. Because an employee’s energy and time resources are limited, he or she may experience greater conflict between the work and nonwork domains, causing emotional issues [46].

With cyber-life-interruption, employees can fulfill their job demands using their personal resources. It is similar to the definition of the additional “demands” in a nonwork domain based on the job demandsresources theory. Contrasting cyberloafing, cyber-life-interruption is the job demand that occurs in the nonwork domain. This leads to extended demands-resources dynamics in another way – using personal resources to address job demands. As a result, employees who engage in cyber-lifeinterruption do not have sufficient time and energy to fulfill their per sonal demands because they utilize their personal resources to address job demands. From the theoretical lens of the job demands-resources theory, as the personal demands that cannot be performed due to cyber-life-interruption accumulate, employee nonwork exhaustion rises. In this connection, this study postulates that cyber-life-interruption ag gravates nonwork exhaustion.

## H2a: Cyber-life-interruption increases nonwork exhaustion.

## 2.4.2. Cyberloafing – job resources in the nonwork domain

By engaging in cyberloafing, employees complete nonwork tasks for family and friends during working hours using their mobile devices [4, 24,31]. Cyberloafing enables employees to virtually transit from the work domain to the nonwork domain to address personal demands [47]. If organizations offer flexibility to employees in this regard, work–family enrichment can be achieved [48,49]. Some literature has suggested reasonable cyberloafing allowances rather than banning such activities to achieve better organizational outcomes [5,50]. Others have mentioned that employees regard cyberloafing as a means of coping with stress and refreshing their minds [32]. Allowing cyberloafing and workplace internet leisure browsing in the workplace for 12% of the total work time can restore the mental resources of employees [51].

With the aid of ICT, employees can deal with their personal demands when they are present in the work domain during working hours by performing cyberloafing. While the source of cyberloafing resources is a work domain (i.e., job resources), cyberloafing can be regarded as the additional “resources” in a nonwork domain based on the job demandsresources theory. This implies extended demands-resources dynamics – using job resources to address personal demands. In other words, em ployees who engage in cyberloafing provide work time and energy to fulfill their personal demands during working hours or in their office. From the theoretical lens of the job demands-resources theory, as per sonal demands are fulfilled, their nonwork exhaustion can be reduced. Hence, this study postulates that cyberloafing alleviates nonwork exhaustion.

## H2b: Cyberloafing decreases nonwork exhaustion.

## 2.4.3. Justice perspective of the conservation of resources theory

The conservation of resources theory suggests that employees expect reasonable allowances to be provided to compensate for the resources drained by any unjust treatments of their organizations for the sake of justice [52]. Some literature on cyber-life-interruption has concluded that employees regard cyber- life-interruption as unjust treatment because their nonwork resources are drained involuntarily; hence, em ployers are expected to do something to restore justice [9,53]. Referring to the “justice” perspective of the conservation of resources theory, this study proposes that employees engaging in cyber-life- interruption perceive cyberloafing as a reasonable allowance that not only compensates for their loss of personal resources but also restores justice following unjust cyber-life-interruption.

Although most supervisors treat cyberloafing as a counterproductive behavior that should be controlled or even strictly prohibited [e.g., 2,4], cyberloafing can address nonwork exhaustion caused by cyber-life-interruption. For example, if employees feel they deserve a break for cyberloafing during working hours and if the organization formally allows them to do so, they can fulfill their personal demands that have not been performed due to cyber-life-interruption [50,54], alleviating their nonwork exhaustion. Thus, this study postulates that cyberloafing can mitigate the increased nonwork exhaustion caused by cyber-life-interruption.

H2c: Cyberloafing moderates the relationship between cyber-lifeinterruption and nonwork exhaustion, such that the adverse effect of cyber-life-interruption on nonwork exhaustion becomes weaker as cyberloafing is higher.

## 3. Methodology

In view of the cross-domains demands-resources dynamics in the ICT-enabled flexible working environment, this study aims to explore the direct and interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life- inter ruption on employee exhaustion that emerge from work and nonwork domains. It posits that cyberloafing individually increases work exhaustion but decreases nonwork exhaustion, while cyber-life- inter ruption increases nonwork exhaustion but decreases work exhaustion. In addition, this study further hypothesizes that the interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption collectively reduce the work exhaustion and nonwork exhaustion. The subsequent sections summa rize the data collection and analysis strategies of this study.

## 3.1. Samples and procedures

The data utilized in this study were collected through a quantitative survey. This study targeted a wide spectrum of employees across China, which is one of the economies that records the highest world smart phone penetration rate [55]. General employees from different in dustries with or without management duties from Hong Kong, Macau, Mainland China, and Taiwan were invited to participate. Although employees of these regions are in different economic systems, what they share is that their economies are transforming from labor-intensive to knowledge-intensive and their work, and most importantly, their life styles are fundamentally changed because of the rapid development of ICT [56]. This study, relying on the empirical results of a quantitative survey among a large population of employees, is expected to reflect the behaviors and perceptions of general employees who play vital roles in the economy.

Invitations were sent to trade unions and their associate unions in the targeted regions in an attempt to approach employees of diverse back grounds. The major trade unions in different regions included the Chi nese Federation of Labor, Federaçao ˜ das Associaçoes ˜ dos Oper´arios de Macau, the Hong Kong Federation of Trade Unions, and the PRC Federation of Trade Unions and their associate unions.

Corporate members and/or individual members of professional bodies and commercial associations, e.g., Hong Kong Institute of Mar keting, the Sustainable Fashion Business Consortium, and the Chinese General Chamber of Commerce, were also invited. Over 10,000 poten tial respondents were successfully reached.

Invitations with a URL to access the online questionnaire were sent to potential respondents by email. The URL did not have any identifiers that could reveal the origins or identities of the participants. Participa tion was voluntary and anonymous. The participants were required to provide full written informed consent before the survey began. A gift coupon (amounted the equivalent of HK\$100) was offered to all par ticipants as an incentive. All data were used solely for the analysis in this study and treated confidentially. The captured email addresses of the participants were only for the distribution of the gift coupons and erased after use.

In total, 1178 records were captured, and 751 valid records were finally obtained. Of the 751 valid responses, 70% were female, and 30% were male. Fifty-three percent of the respondents were Hong Kong residents, while residents of Mainland China and Taiwan each comprised 22% of the sample. More than 70% of the participants were aged between 21 and 35 years, with 1–15 years of full-time work experience. They were mainly frontline employees, i.e., 68%, in the import/export, wholesale, and retail trades. Only 22% of the re spondents were middle management staff, while senior executives and professionals comprised less than 10% of the sample.

## 3.2. Measurements

There were 18 questions in the questionnaire, which took less than 15 min for participants to answer. All measurements were adopted from previous literature. Most of the items were measured on seven-point Likert-type scales. Because the study was conducted primarily among Chinese employees, the measurement items were translated from the original English into traditional Chinese by bilingual individuals using the back-translation method. By replacing the local terminologies of Mainland China and Taiwan, two additional Chinese versions were developed.

Appendix A provides a sample questionnaire including the list of measurement items and the nominal values of the categorical variables. The cyber-life-interruption measurement was adopted from that of nonwork-time life interruptions by Chen and Karahanna [19]; cyber loafing behavior from Khansa [57]; work and nonwork exhaustion from Chen and Karahanna [19].

Various control variables were adopted for the dependent variables according to the known predictors suggested in prior literature. De mographic data that were adopted as control variables in previous research, namely, gender, age, and education level, were controlled in this study [58–60].

Occupational ranking, job nature, tenure, marital status, number of dependents, financial burden, and health condition were controlled because they were believed to have potential effects on the dependent variables [19–21]. Among these variables, the number of dependents, which would affect employees’ perception and experience of work, was defined as the number of people who relied on the respondents for financial support but not necessarily their children [61]. Considering the diverse background of the participants, their industries and residence areas, which were believed to have potential effects on all the dependent variables, were also controlled.

## 5. Discussion and implication

Based on the theoretical foundation, this study examines the direct and interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on work and nonwork exhaustion. While the job demands-resources theory targets the demands-resources dynamics in the work domain, this study proposes a new dynamic demands-resources model and investigates the demands-resources dynamics across work and nonwork domains induced by cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption. Furthermore, this study draws on different perspectives of the conservation of resources theory to explain the underlying psychological mechanisms behind the complementary effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption, which has yet to be noticed in the previous literature.

Three of the hypotheses (H1a, H2a, and H2c) were supported. Another two hypotheses (H1b and H2b) were not supported because their statistically significant results were opposite-than-expected. One hypothesis (H1c) was not supported due to the insignificant result (see Fig. 4). The unsupported hypotheses may provide opportunities for future research.

Attention should be given to the diverse effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption both individually and jointly. Cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption were found to be significantly and positively correlated with both work and nonwork exhaustion, respectively (H1a and H2a). These results echo the previous literature that has primarily emphasized the adverse effects of cyberloafing and cyber- lifeinterruption.

On the other hand, when acting alone, neither cyberloafing nor cyber-life-interruption had the potential to alleviate nonwork or work exhaustion, respectively (H1b and H2b). This part of the results con tradicts the literature that has suggested their potential bright side in regard to fulfilling demands of the opposite domain and alleviating exhaustion in that domain. It can be explained that employees actually feel bad to engage in illegitimate cyberloafing rather than feel good in handling personal demands [2,4,36]. Likewise, they are stressed out by their personal lives being interrupted by job demands rather than glad to fulfill job demands $[ 7 , 9 , 7 4 ]$ . These perceptions of emplovees are often affected by the reactions of their families, friends, and working partners to their behaviors [43]. The theory of planned behavior [75] can also help interpret these psychological implications. Although cyber loafing can help address personal demands, it is actually a disturbance that creates work exhaustion and extends nonwork exhaustion to the work domain. For cyber-life-interruption, employees are distressed by the job demands that they are obliged to accomplish during non-working hours, despite that they have already disconnected from work. Although cyber-life-interruption can help fulfill the job demands, it is indeed an intruder that creates nonwork exhaustion and extends work exhaustion to the nonwork domain. As a result, when acting alone, cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption increase both work and nonwork exhaustion.

This study further investigated the interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on work exhaustion (H1c) and nonwork exhaustion (H2c). Employees are accustomed to engage in cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption activities in reality. Both the activities are caused by the ubiquity of ICT and are similar in terms of nature and medium. Each can be used as a resource to satisfy the demands in the opposite domain. The study results find that the interaction between cyber-life- interruption and cyberloafing changed the strength of the original relationship between cyber-life- interruption and nonwork exhaustion (H2c). In other words, cyberloafing mitigated the increased nonwork exhaustion caused by cyber-life-interruption. These results enriched our understanding of the development of the dark and bright sides of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption. Although cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption could not individually alleviate work and nonwork exhaustion, they did collectively reduce nonwork exhaustion based on the “justice” perspective of the conservation of resources the ory. These findings contribute to clarifying the mixed results of their effects in previous literature, in which cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption have not been considered on the same dimension. More specifically, this study could interpret the common circumstances that, after cyber-life- interruption, employees could engage in cyberloafing during working hours to satisfy their outstanding personal demands and ease their nonwork exhaustion. Conversely, after cyberloafing, em ployees might foresee the need to work beyond working hours to get their unfinished jobs done; thus, they would feel less nonwork exhaus tion if cyber-life-interruption occurs. The pioneering dynamic demandsresources model with regard to cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption was supported; the discovery of the complementary interaction effect of cyber-life-interruption and cyberloafing on nonwork exhaustion was confirmed.

However, the interaction between cyberloafing and cyber-life interruption could not significantly moderate the relationship between cyberloafing and work exhaustion (H1c). This indicates that although cyber-life-interruption makes employees continue working on job de mands during nonworking hours, the positive emotions following the accomplishment of job demands cannot offset the negative emotions created by long working hours or work overload. This finding echo those reported previously in the literature that cyber-life-interruption is not enough to alleviate high job pressure [9,53]. Its limited benefits accompanied by task accomplishment or professional commitment cannot override the disadvantages created from the mental burden of prolonged work time and role transition [11,19]. Our results are also supported by the work–family positive spillover mechanism [43], in which factors such as the relationships of employees with their families, working partners, and organizations and their value priorities may play important roles in developing the positive impacts of cyberloafing and cyber-life- interruption. The potential influences of these factors may mediate the direct effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on work and nonwork exhaustion or even moderate the effects of their interaction.

Separately, the slope test results demonstrate that high cyberloafing triggered the lowest work exhaustion when cyber-life-interruption was high (see Fig. 2) and high cyber-life-interruption triggered the lowest nonwork exhaustion when cyberloafing was high (see Fig. 3). These results prove that higher levels of cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption enable employees to realize a high degree of flexibility. Employees can achieve a win–win situation, where the demands from both work and nonwork domains can be completed, with both work and nonwork exhaustion minimized. This finding is in line with previous literature on the work–family interface, which has stated that boundarycrossing activities enable employees to allocate their resources and handle job and personal demands flexibly in accordance with their needs and preferences, thereby enriching their work–family lives [48,49].

Other literature has suggested that employees in segmentation oriented organizations experience less work–home conflict than em ployees in integration-oriented organizations $[ 7 6 , 7 7 ]$ . In contrast, our results demonstrate that work and nonwork exhaustion were not the lowest if any of the switches were obstructed. When either cyberloafing or cyber-life-interruption was lower, work and nonwork exhaustion were higher than they were in the best scenario, in which both cyber loafing and cyber-life-interruption were higher. Additionally, nonwork exhaustion was maximized when cyberloafing and cyber-life- interrup tion were lower. These results imply that employees cannot be better off if they minimize cyberloafing and/or cyber-life-interruption activities. If employees cannot actualize their flexibility as they wish, a lose–lose situation will occur. Employees’ work and personal lives will be upset. For example, in an organization, if cyberloafing is not allowed but cyber-life-interruption occurs frequently, nonwork exhaustion will be maximized because the unjust treatment of cyber-life-interruption cannot be restored. Based on the conservation of resources theory [18], employees will subsequently avoid cyber- life-interruption. Both cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption will occur at lower levels. The demands- resources dynamics across different domains will be mini mized, and employees will not be able to fulfill work and personal de mands properly. Not only will nonwork exhaustion be maximized, employees will also suffer from work exhaustion. Heavy demands from both domains will accumulate, and both work and nonwork exhaustion will continue to increase. Employees will fall into a vicious cycle.

## 5.1. Theoretical contribution

Because of the ubiquity of ICT, it is no longer the case that employees work only during their office hours and completely enjoy their personal lives outside of office hours. This study furthered our understanding of the demands-resources dynamics across work and nonwork domains and the subsequent exhaustion triggered by cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption. Our results reveal the mental and behavioral changes of employees due to the direct and interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption. It expands upon research on employee demandsresources management, ICT, and behavioral intervention technologies.

First, this study extends the application of the job demands-resources theory [17] from only the work domain to the nonwork domain. Pre vious literature has examined cyberloafing and cyber-life- interruption separately, leading to some mixed results. Additionally, the cross-domain demands-resources dynamics induced by cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption have not been examined. This study proposes a new dynamic demands-resources model to reveal the dark and bright sides of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption holistically. We found that cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption could not individually alleviate work and nonwork exhaustion, but their interaction alleviated nonwork exhaustion. This sheds light on the complicated interaction between their potential bright and dark sides. Hence, further investi gation of their interacting effects became more important and interesting.

Second, this study revealed the underlying psychological process of the demands-resources dynamics in the context of ICT. It drew on the compensation and justice perspectives of the conservation of resources theory to explain the underlying psychological mechanisms of the complementary effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on work and nonwork exhaustion. It investigated to what extent they complement each other to help alleviate the work and nonwork exhaustion triggered by their individual effects. The study results sup port our suggestions of the justice perspective of the conservation of resources theory to explain that cyberloafing restores justice of unjust cyber-life-interruption and mitigates the adverse effects of cyber-lifeinterruption on nonwork exhaustion.

Third, previous studies have not considered whether an employee engaging in cyberloafing or cyber-life-interruption also engages in another activity. Thus, this study examined employee perceptions of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption from a new perspective, i.e., the potential mutually complementary effect of cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption on employee exhaustion. By considering the activities on the same dimension, this study elucidated the best and worst scenario and facilitated a comparison of outcomes under various circumstances. The results show that the interaction between cyberloafing and cyberlife-interruption changed the strength of the original relationship be tween cyber- life-interruption and nonwork exhaustion. Moreover, if the levels of cyber-life-interruption and cyberloafing are high, both work and nonwork exhaustion will be minimized. In contrast, when the level of cyberloafing is low and that of cyber-life-interruption is high, work exhaustion will be maximized, and when the levels of cyber-lifeinterruption and cyberloafing are both low, nonwork exhaustion will be maximized.

## 5.2. Practical implications

From a practical perspective, this study inspires employees’ and employers’ practical resource allocation strategies and contributes to the development of behavioral intervention technologies by providing empirical data and a theoretical understanding regarding human –technology interactions.

At the personal level, the results help employees realize the positive and negative impacts of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption, enabling them to appreciate, predict, and even productively utilize the levels of these activities. Employees in specific situations may consider taking different approaches strategically to achieve optimal outcomes. For example, when cyberloafing is allowed in an organization, em ployees will benefit from frequently being involved in cyber-lifeinterruption, thereby minimizing both work and nonwork exhaustion. In contrast, when cyberloafing is not allowed in an organization, it will be detrimental for employees to be frequently involved in cyber-lifeinterruption, as doing so would not minimize work exhaustion but would maximize nonwork exhaustion.

Moreover, the study findings increase employer and management awareness of the mutually complementary effect of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption. This understanding is particularly important when working from home becomes necessary during an extraordinary period, such as when COVID-19 locked down almost the entire world in 2020. Employers and management staff can carefully consider how to shape their ICT-related policies, infrastructure, and workflow to deal with any unexpected circumstances. They can establish monitoring and reward systems to properly monitor and motivate employees and facil itate their work without compromising their mental health while simultaneously preventing any work-from-home disadvantages.

At the public policy level, the findings can inspire governments to review the adequacy of existing laws and regulations, which is partic ularly important for protecting employee rights and benefits in this digital era. Our results show that simply prohibiting cyberloafing and/or cyber-life-interruption does not produce better outcomes in terms of employee exhaustion. Instead, flexible policies and practices that allow employees to manage their resources and demands can help produce favorable outcomes in both work and nonwork domains. In contrast to the strict and uniform rules of some economies or companies to forbid cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption, options such as formalizing employee needs for cyber breaks, recognizing employee efforts during nonworking hours in the maximum working hours and minimum wage scales, adjusting the right of employees to disconnect to an optimal level, and encouraging employers to establish reasonable organizational cyber policies are worthy of consideration.

Finally, having practical IT solutions available in the market may further convince employers and employees and facilitate them in taking advantage of the flexibility and freedom of cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption. Hence, this study may inspire software engineers to apply behavioral intervention technologies and develop applications for managing or even intervening in cyberloafing and cyber-life- interrup tion activities to maximize their positive impacts and simultaneously ensure employees’ work–life balance. A firm can provide decent relax ation and monitoring tools to manage cyberloafing behaviors to reenergize employees; employers and management staff can also consider establishing transparent and fair conversion systems so that employees’ nonworking-hour contributions and virtual absence from work can offset each other in terms of time and tangible rewards.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations that deserve attention in future research. First, the scope of this study was limited to demands, re sources, and work and nonwork exhaustion with the job demands resources theory. Work and nonwork exhaustion have prominent values in academic research because prior literature has recognized it as one of the critical outcomes in the IT work environment. They have also been well proven to negatively affect job performance and other workrelated outcomes. Hence, our results are crucial to predict job perfor mance and other work-related outcomes. However, work and nonwork exhaustion do not constitute the final stage of the job demands-resources model. Future research should include job performance and other out comes as dependent variables, maximize samples with diverse back ground, and adopt cluster analysis technique to explore the effects of the interaction between cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption.

Second, social desirability was considered a potential problem in this study because measures of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption were self-reported. Precautionary measures such as online surveys, anonym ity, neutralization of questionnaire presentation, and spreading in vitations through trade unions (instead of employers) were taken. It i still possible that respondents would underreport undesirable cyber loafing behaviors, overreport desirable cyber-life-interruption behav iors, or undermine their redundant situations or complaints [9,11,30,57, 78,79]. Future research should carefully consider the appropriate measures to avoid social desirability bias to further enhance the generalizability and robustness of the survey.

Third, this study did not address the space and time of exhaustion, although time and place may be important factors for differentiating work and nonwork exhaustion. In further research, the experience of employees in different situations could be considered, such that more precise predictions could be realized and fit-for-purpose management and remedial policies could be developed.

Fourth, cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption involve certain so cial connections of employees with their families, working partners, and organizations, which may affect the development of the positive/nega tive direct and interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption. Further research may differentiate the potential in fluences of social connections from other factors in the demandsresources dynamics induced by cyberloafing and cyber-lifeinterruption. Matched-pair research examining the effects of various social relationships of employees on demands-resources dynamics is worth exploring.

Fifth, cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption cover a wide range of activities that may have implications for work and nonwork exhaustion. Quantitative research cannot provide insight into the complicated be haviors and judgments of employees. In future research, the frequencies, intensities, times, and tasks involved in cyberloafing and cyber-life interruption could be examined to reveal their potential values and ef fects. Moreover, other methods such as focus groups, longitudinal studies, and experiments could be utilized to achieve a deeper under standing of employee perceptions.

Finally, this study pioneers the examination of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption on the same dimension. It is necessary to contrast and demonstrate the potential beneficial and adverse effects of cyber loafing and cyber-life-interruption individually and jointly. This study, hence, opted to break down and analyze their relationships path-by-path [72]. However, this strategy may undermine some organic development of the dynamic demands-resources mechanism. Some marginally sig nificant/insignificant results may become different if hypotheses are analyzed jointly. Future research may, based on this initial framework, adopt an integrated approach to drill into the interaction effects of cyberloafing and cyber-life-interruption.
