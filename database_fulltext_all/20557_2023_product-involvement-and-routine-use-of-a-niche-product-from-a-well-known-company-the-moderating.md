## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Niche product Product involvement Routine use Well-known companies

## A B S T R A C T

Contemporary well-known companies are making cross-disciplinary efforts to promote niche products outside the scope of their core businesses. However, little is known about the routine use of these niche products. Based on the theory of involvement, we proposed a research model and conducted an online survey regarding Apple Pay to validate the hypotheses. Several insights were obtained through empirical analysis. Specifically, (1) routine use can be affected by product involvement, which in turn is positively influenced by ideal selfcongruence and social influence, and (2) the effects of ideal self-congruence and social influence on product involvement are moderated by gender.

## 1. Introduction

To further improve their business ecosystems and meet consumers various needs for a wide spectrum of products, many contemporary well-known companies are eager to provide consumers with a one-stop experience. This may lead them to make cross-disciplinary efforts to promote new products that were not previously part of their core busi ness, and sometimes these new products may even pull the focal com panies to a totally new industry. For instance, Apple Inc., a company that specializes in consumer electronics, began offering a new mobile pay ment service, Apple Pay, in 2016. Considering that many fields are dominated by massive and highly competitive businesses (e.g., Volks wagen and Tesla for Google Car; PayPal and Alipay for Apple Pay), such non-core products promoted by well-known companies can often be considered niche products (i.e., personalized products that meet the needs of certain target consumers [1]). These new niche products are often tailored to the fans of the focal companies, that is, to consumers who already recognize their corporate culture and design philosophy (e. g., only users with certain iPhones can use Apple Pay). In practice, consumers can easily switch back from a niche product to a mass-market or popular alternative if the niche product does not fully meet their expectations after short-term use, which may prevent focal companies from expanding the niche services. Thus, it is imperative for the focal companies to understand how to leverage their famous brand to drive consumers to integrate niche products into daily life [2] (i.e., routine use), which will ensure product success [3] and greater long-term profits.

While the existing literature has greatly extended our understanding of routine use, some critical research gaps remain to be filled. First, in our context of payment systems, we generally have little knowledge of the contextualized antecedents for the routine use of a niche product Prior research on routine use mainly focuses on mass-market/popular products, such as enterprise systems [4] and mobile health services [3]. Although those identified antecedents may also be critical for the routine use of a niche product, these antecedents are not context-specific and thus do not help us further extend our understanding of the focal phenomenon [5]. Second, little is known regarding how individuals beliefs about a brand affect their routine use of the brand’s niche product. Theoretically, an individual’s use behavior can be directly shaped by their beliefs about the focal product [6]. Accordingly, it is meaningful to investigate how brand-level belief is transferred to product-level belief about a niche product, which in turn affects routine use. More importantly, beliefs regarding a niche product should repre sent its heterogeneity, and if the product is consistent with consumers inherent needs, values, and interests, consumers are then more likely to engage with the niche product than the competing mass-market prod ucts [1].

Third, while leveraging gender in marketing is a basic segmentation strategy [7], our understanding of the role of gender in brand-to-product belief transfer is quite limited. In general, males and females tend to think differently about the effects of brands and products and behave in different ways [8]. In particular, gender is likely to predict individuals long-term perception and use of some technologies [9], which is the case in our study. More importantly, gender can influence the process of belief formation [10]. Considering that niche products are developed to satisfy various individual needs, including gender in the research frame will add to the significance of our study.

Drawing upon the theory of involvement, we propose that routine use of a niche product can be directly affected by the user’s product-level belief, i.e., niche product involvement. Further scrutinizing the research context, we identify brand-level beliefs, i.e., brand loyalty, ideal self congruence, and social influence, as three critical brand-level anteced ents that can predict product involvement. Moreover, we employ social role theory to explain how gender moderates the effects of these three antecedents. To validate the research model, an online survey was conducted, and most of the hypotheses were supported.

Several theoretical implications can be drawn from this study. First, this study is among the first to investigate routine use of niche product from well-known brands; hence, it contributes to the literature on routine use. Second, this study can extend involvement theory by revealing the moderating effect of gender in our context. Third, our study can stimulate scholars to further explore the phenomena associ ated with niche products from well-known companies. This study also offers significant practical implications based on its empirical findings.

The rest of this article is organized as follows. First, we briefly review the existing literature on routine use, niche product, theory of involve ment, and social role theory. Next, we present our research model to predict routine use of the niche product, followed by our research method and data analysis. Finally, we discuss our results, as well as their theoretical and practical implications.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Routine use and niche products

In the information systems literature (as shown in Appendix 1), several theoretical perspectives (e.g., technology acceptance model [11], elaboration likelihood model [3], motivation theory [4], and commitment theory [12]) have been employed to interpret routine use of different types of information technologies. It is argued that routine use derives from either the focal technology’s instrumentality for users (e.g., it supports employees’ work tasks) [4] or users’ consciousness or habit because of long-term use [4]. Routine use is theoretically different from other use behavior-relevant concepts. For instance, Bhattacherjee et al. found that after the initial learning process (initiation, adoption, adaptation, and acceptance) [13], the use of the focal technology be comes a normal activity in users’ daily life. Besides, while routine use can to some extent be seen as continuance, it further underlines the conscious and automatic mechanisms associated with use (i.e., in dividuals often consciously and automatically engage in routine use) [14].

We observe that extant research on routine use exclusively focuses on mainstream or mass-market products/technologies [15,16], with attention rarely paid to routine use of niche products. Theoretically, this differentiation is significant because niche products are totally different from their mass-market counterparts. Niche products often have more personalized features tailored to the needs or characteristics of some specific customers than mass-market products, which aim to satisfy the needs of the majority of consumers [1]. In fact, the most significant hallmark of niche products is that they have unique features that can match particular groups of consumers [17]. Several studies have been done to investigate the phenomena relevant to niche products. For instance, niche products have a long-tail effect in satisfying the demands of some small groups of customers; accordingly, they enable their focal companies to obtain price premium [18]. Oh et al. further suggested that the more a consumer knows about the features that fit her/his unique needs, the more likely she/he will choose niche products [19].

Because of these differences, it is believed that routine use of niche products cannot be well explained by well-established technology acceptance theories, which were originally employed to interpret adoption and use of mass-market products. In addition, in our context, the focal niche products are provided by well-known companies with famous brands, which adds to the complexity of studying routine use, particularly given that the focal company is relatively weak in the niche product field, where several sophisticated competitors already existed.

Theoretically, individuals’ adoption of a certain product can be directly affected by their beliefs about it [6]. In our context, we aim to investigate how a well-known company leverages its brand to drive individuals to engage in routine use a niche product. Brand-level belief can affect product-level belief [20]; hence, we further propose that be liefs about a niche product can likewise be shaped by beliefs about the well-known brand. That is, there exists a belief transfer (i.e., brand-to-product belief transfer) mechanism for interpreting routine use of the focal niche product. To further examine this belief transfer pro cess, we involve gender in the theoretical frame as gender can shape belief formation [21].

Accordingly, we develop the following theoretical framework (see Fig. 1). We propose that beliefs about the well-known brand can shape individuals’ beliefs about the focal niche product, which in turn affects individuals’ routine use of niche product. Besides, it is further proposed that the brand-to-product belief transfer varies between males and females.

## 2.2. Theory of involvement

We employ an involvement perspective to specify brand-to-product belief transfer in this study. In contrast to mass-market products, which aim to satisfy the shared needs or values of a large group of customers, niche products are expected to satisfy customers’ inherent needs and values that can differentiate them from others [1]. Theoret ically, product involvement refers to the degree of commitment on the part of a consumer’s inherent needs, values, and behavioral response to a certain product [22], and it is theoretically related to the importance and interestingness of the focal products [23]. Prior research has sug gested that product involvement can predict consumers’ long-term be haviors and beliefs regarding the focal product, such as repurchase decisions [24], product satisfaction [25], and repeated behavior (e.g., intention to return [4, 26]). High product involvement, in particular, can increase the possibility of purchase because of the consumer’s perception that the product is relevant to their inherent values and needs [27]. More importantly, product involvement further underlines prod uct significance, deriving from the extent to which the focal product can satisfy one customer’s inherent needs and interests [28, 29], which is consistent with the personalized features of niche products. To this end, involvement can capture the belief that derives from the heterogeneity of the niche product. An involvement perspective is thus considered appropriate for interpreting the routine use of a niche product.

According to the theory of involvement, four factors contribute to product involvement: cultural ideologies, cultural intermediaries, life themes and proiects, and social networks [23]. The two macroenvironmental factors, i.e., cultural ideologies and cultural in termediaries, can produce a consumption context conducive to product involvement [30]. Cultural ideology refers to the shared values and meanings associated with some objects in culture [31], while cultural intermediaries are the conduits that can transfer the meanings in a certain culture into specific meanings in consumption [32]. Life themes and projects relate to consumers’ essential, everyday concerns that they want to be addressed and to their identities [33]. Life themes and pro jects often derive from consumers’ personal experiences and social in teractions, and they can thus be employed to illuminate inherent beliefs regarding products or brands [30]. Theoretically, social network refers to a consumer’s social connections with others, and it is a medium for social influence regarding product or brand evaluations [4].

As shown in the theoretical frame, we attempt to contextualize the antecedents of product involvement as brand-level constructs. It is argued that cultural ideology is at a higher theoretical level and that its effect on product involvement is likely to be mediated by cultural in termediaries because cultural intermediaries can provide consumers with an instrument to interpret the specific meaning of product and brand in a cultural ideology [23]. For the sake of simplicity, we remove cultural ideology from the research frame because it is theoretically distant from consumers’ brand – or product-specific beliefs.

Altogether, it is proposed that routine use of one niche product from a well-known company/brand is affected by product involvement, which in turn can be predicted by three types of brand-level antecedents, i.e., expected beliefs regarding cultural intermediaries, life themes and projects, and social networks. To further validate the appropriateness of the proposed research model for this study, it is necessary to identify some substantial and specific antecedents. Accordingly, based on the theoretical meanings of the three types of antecedents and further scrutinizing the specific characteristics of the niche product in our research context, we identify brand loyalty, ideal self-congruence, and social influence as three critical brand-level antecedents for product involvement. In the next section, we will provide a detailed explanation of the selection of these three specific factors and how they affect routine use.

## 2.3. Social role theory

According to social role theory, males and females are likely to think and behave in different ways [34]. In this study, three relevant gender differences may help us further understand the role of gender in forming product involvement. First, while females care more about the relational nature of products, males are more likely to underscore convenience and perceived usefulness of the products [35, 36]. Second, in technology adoption literature, it is suggested men are more likely to be motived by intrinsic motivation [37]. Toward this end, it is argued that males may choose to further value the focal brand that is consistent with their own value system [38]. Third, females are inherently more social-experiential, with greater interpersonal warmth, and they are more susceptible to others’ opinions and advice, while males’ assess ments of products are oriented to themselves rather than others [7].

As shown in the next section, these differences have distinct impli cations for product involvement. More specifically, gender is proposed to moderate the relationships between the three independent variables (i.e., brand loyalty, ideal self-congruence, and social influence) and involvement in our context.

## 3.1. Product involvement and routine use

According to involvement theory, consumers with high involvement usually hold strong beliefs about the focal product, and the stronger beliefs consumers hold about a product, the more irreplaceable it is for them [22]. Accordingly, consumers with strong beliefs are more likely to be committed to the product [39]. Previous research has found that product involvement can affect consumers’ decision-making process and, accordingly, the frequency of product use [40]. Theoretically, product involvement indicates that consumers believe the focal product is consistent with and can satisfy their inherent values and needs [27]. That is, consumers with high product involvement are more likely to value their connection with the focal products [11] and, accordingly, are more likely to engage in using it despite its competitive alternatives. This is particularly meaningful in our context where the focal niche product is surrounded by powerful competitors. It is argued that high-level product involvement can drive consumers to underline the desired inherent values embedded within the niche product; thus, they integrate the niche product into daily use, despite its relative weaknesses and disadvantages compared with existing powerful competitors. ## 3.2. Antecedents for product involvement

We employ brand loyalty to represent expected brand-level belief related to cultural intermediaries in our context. According to Coulter et al., a cultural intermediary refers to a social entity (e.g., manufac turers, advertisers, and retailers) that provides a communicative link between consumers’ interpreted brand meanings and public’s shared understanding of relevant brands [23]. In our context, one focal well-known company plays the role of intermediary because it interprets the meaning of the brand, and it is eager to lock in customers to make them engage in consuming more products under the brand. Theoreti cally, brand loyalty, which is a critical antecedent of consumer behavior in the competitive era [41], refers to a consumer’s deeply held commitment to repurchase or repatronize the products of a specific brand despite changing situations [42]. It represents consumers’ psy chological connection and behavioral tendency regarding the brand, i. $\mathrm { e } _ { \cdot , }$ consumers with high brand loyalty tend to consistently choose a specific brand and reject others [43]. To this end, brand loyalty can to a large extent represent the role of intermediaries (i.e., the focal well-known companies in this study). More importantly, in this study, the focal niche product’s utility may not be as competitive as its existing powerful rivals; hence, it may thus rely even more on consumers’ loyalty to the focal brand.

It is argued that brand loyalty can directly affect product involve ment. As previously discussed, brand-level belief can affect productlevel belief. Accordingly, consumers with high-level brand loyalty are more likely to be involved with the products under the brand [44]. It is suggested that brand loyalty may result in a kind of “halo effect” [45], i. e., global evaluation influences one’s response to other attributes and the impression of one attribute dominates the impression of other in dependent attributes. That is, loyal consumers tend to recognize the niche product provided by the focal brand regardless of its weaknesses and faults. Accordingly, customers with high brand loyalty may auto matically choose to believe that the products of the focal brand are important and meaningful for them. On this basis, we hypothesize:

## H2. Brand loyalty is positively related to product involvement.

In this study, ideal self-congruence is employed to represent con sumers’ life themes and projects. As mentioned previously, life themes and projects are related to one’s life experience and can also reflect one’s inner world [23]. Niche products can better meet one’s personal needs and show their tastes are different from others than mass-market products [1]. In particular, the personalized characteristics of niche products prompt consumers to identify with the culture and ideas behind the brand, thereby connecting to their inner world. Generally, the more the focal brand can meet the customer’s ideals, the more the customer is willing to perceive the products under the brand as signifi cant and meaningful. Accordingly in this study, we choose a construct that can properly reflect individuals’ inner value, i.e., ideal self-congruence. Ideal self-congruence refers to an individual’s perceived match between brand value and what he/she would like to be, which is related to one’s life experience [46]. By investigating and reflecting on life themes and projects that are relevant to the self, an individual comes to know more about their inherent needs, as well as how to associate products with the self. From the consumer perspective and in our context, life themes and projects refer to individuals’ general tastes in products and the identities they want to claim [17]. Theoreti cally, ideal self-congruence implies that a consumer compares her/hi own self-concept with the image of a brand [47]. The self-concept rep resents consumers’ personal needs, and thus, this comparison reflects a consumer’s taste and her/his identity regarding the focal brand/com pany [48]. To this end, it is appropriate to conceptualize ideal self-congruence as a factor that can represent life themes and projects.

It is proposed that ideal self-congruence regarding the focal brand can positively affect product involvement. A consumer with high ideal self-congruence tends to underline the value of a focal brand that cor responds to what she/he is like [49]. When a consumer believes the value of the focal brand fits her/his ideal self, she/he underscores the value of the products under this brand [50]. In our context, the con sumers of niche products are eager to claim their value and distinction, which to some extent can be represented by the identity derived from the focal brand. ## H3. Ideal self-congruence is positively related to product involvement.

Social influence is employed to represent the influence of social network in our study. In a social environment, one’s views can be influenced by the emotions, opinions, and behaviors of others, i.e., in dividuals will behave and think based on their interpretation of infor mation they obtain from their social networks [52]. Thus, if a niche product is widely supported by one’s social surroundings, she/he may easily recognize its value. Theoretically, social network depicts the so cial surroundings that can influence one’s product consumption [30]. That is, other social actors interact with the focal consumer and form the defined social network. It is argued that social influence from the sur rounding social actors (such as one’s family members, friends, and other connections) may stimulate the focal consumer to think and behave as expected [53], i.e., recognizing the focal brand in our context. In this study, these social actors can be either critical or general. On the one hand, a consumer’s critical social actors $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } }$ , family members, friends, and colleagues) frequently give local feedback about consumption [54]. As a result, the consumer receives abundant information about the focal brand. which in turn may stimulate her/him to behave and think in certain ways regarding the niche product. On the other hand, general social actors, such as both online and offline merchants, are also critical for niche product involvement, as these social actors may provide feedback to the consumer on a daily basis, ultimately affecting the focal customer’s attitude or belief toward the niche product.

We argue that social influence (from both critical and general social actors) can positively affect niche product involvement. On the one hand, critical social actors who support the focal brand can facilitate an individual’s involvement with the niche product as theoretically in dividuals tend to think and behave based on the influence of their crit ical social actors [48]. If their critical social actors support a well-known brand, an individual is under social pressure to choose that brand’s products, which stimulates her/him to preferentially consider or further investigate the focal niche product. In this case, an individual is more likely to find the niche product to have unique values that fit her/his inherent needs. On the other hand, general social actors who support the focal brand can also stimulate an individual to be involved with the niche product. In our context, general social actors actually determine the utility of the products under the brand. If general social actors support a well-known brand, it is beneficial for an individual to choose products under that brand; otherwise, their utility would be reduced. In this case. to ensure her/his utility. an individual is likely to selectively underline the unique values of the niche product, which can potentially contribute to product involvement. ## 3.3. Gender as moderator

First, according to social role theory, males are more concerned about the functional value of products, whereas females care more about the relational nature [35]. For women, social interaction and intangible attributes act as the main components of their brand loyalty. For men, brand loyalty mainly derives from the convenience and perceived use fulness of the products [55]. Moreover, as mentioned above, brand loyalty is strongly associated with the halo effect [45]. It has been found that the halo effect is more significant for females than males [56]. Thus, it is expected that brand loyalty has a more significant influence on the routine use of niche products for female consumers. Based on this, we hypothesize:

H5a. The relationship between brand loyalty and product involvement is stronger for females than for males.

Previous research suggests that males are more likely than females to underline informational cues that are interlinked with their value sys tem [38]. Regarding perceptions and behaviors toward information technologies, scholars specifically found that men are more sensitive to intrinsic motivations [37]. We thus regard that males are more con cerned about products related to meeting their values, which are rep resented by their ideal self-congruence. Meanwhile, when consumers want to demonstrate their self-concept, they may underline the value of a brand [50]. As such, it is believed that compared with females, males tend to value the brand more if the brand is consistent with their ideal self, which in turn leads males to be more involved with the niche product belonging to the focal brand. Hence, we hypothesize:

H5b. The relationship between ideal self-congruence and product involve ment is stronger for males than for females.

Social role theory underlines the importance of relational links, which can influence one’s behaviors, and such influence is particularly significant for women [7]. Generally, females are more socially depen dent than males. It is believed that females are more sensitive to support and consensus from social surroundings than males [57]. Accordingly, women are more likely to value the viewpoints of other social actors. That is, if other social actors expect the focal individual to take a positive attitude toward or to use a niche product, a female consumer is more likely to behave accordingly such that she/he will be more involved with the product. Based on these arguments, we hypothesize:

H5c. The relationship between social influence and product involvement is stronger for females than for males.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Samples

In the past decade, mobile payment, a type of payment application where a mobile device is used to initiate, authorize, and confirm an exchange of financial value in return for goods and services [58], has become extremely popular in China. To further support mobile payment in its business ecosystem and further “lock in” its customers in China, Apple launched Apple Pay in February 2016. Since then, Apple Pay has been supported by some retailers and online platforms in China.<sup>2</sup> Similar strategic moves were adopted by other smart mobile device providers; for instance, Samsung promoted Samsung Pay, while Huawei launched Huawei Pay.

However, before 2016, the mobile payment market in China was quite mature and competitive. It was estimated that the two leading providers, Alipay and TenPay, occupied more than 90% of the total mobile payment market in China [59], and almost all mobile payment users had already adopted one or both of the two applications. Apple Pay failed to even make the top ten mobile payment applications in 2017, with a negligible market share. Thus, applications such as Apple Pay and Huawei Pay were actually niche products because they are tailored to a limited number of customers. Apple Pay, in particular, would have a limited customer base as it is not available on any device that is not made and sold by Apple (e.g., Android and Windows devices). More over, Apple Pay adopts a simple design philosophy and only supports the payment function than other mobile payment services, which support multiple functions (e.g., chatting, gaming, loans, etc.). This actually further limits the usage of Apple Pay in China. It is believed that Apple Pay should be more attractive to loyal consumers of Apple who favor its corporate culture and design philosophy. However, in practice, many users may try it one or two times, but most end up abandoning it [60]. It is thus imperative for Apple to understand how to attract users to inte grate Apple Pay into their daily payment-related activities, i.e., to develop their routine use.

To validate the research model, we targeted the users of the iPhone that can support the Apple Pay application. Specifically, we launched an online survey on the website www.wjx.cn, which is a leading profes sional online survey platform with more than 100,000 users. Most re searchers in China have used this platform to collect data in their research. It supports two modes of questionnaire administration, namely, a fee-based service and free self-directed data collection. The free self-directed data collection approach was chosen because (1) the authors can well get access to the mainstream iPhone users (i.e., 18–50 years old young and middle-aged people), and (2) the snowball method in this mode is effective enough for finding valid respondents. After generating a link to the questionnaire, we distributed it to our friends, colleagues, and relatives, and they further distributed it to more re spondents. By doing this, we can potentially collect a sufficient number of questionnaires to validate the research model.

We set the maximum time duration to answer each questionnaire as 20 min and the minimum as 4 min. If a given respondent took more than 20 min or less time than 4 min to finish the questionnaire, it was doubted that the questionnaire was not carefully answered (e.g., all answers were the same). Accordingly, relevant questionnaire was considered invalid and discarded. To exclude invalid respondents, we also set two screening questions, i.e., “Do you use an iPhone?”, and “Your smartphone can support the use of Apple Pay?”

In total, 286 respondents completed the survey between April 2017 and May 2017. After removing the respondents whose smartphones were not the iPhone that can support Apple Pay and whose answers were excessively consistent, we obtained 198 valid samples for data analysis. A time point during the study period was selected to divide the 198 valid samples into two equal groups, i.e., 99 respondents before and after the time point. We carried out t-tests to evaluate the differences between the means of the two groups on age, gender, and level of education [61]. No significant differences in terms of means were found between them. This indicates that our results are not likely to be threatened by nonresponse bias. As shown in Table 1, of the 198 participants, 53.5% are females, and most of the respondents belong to the 18–50 age group (94.0%). More than 90% of respondents have a bachelor’s degree, and more than 90% have been using mobile payment for >2 years. ## 5. Results

## 5.1. Measurement model

We employed partial least squares (PLS), a second-generation tech nique with the ability to model relationships among multiple predictors and criterion variables [67], to assess the validity of constructs (by estimating loadings) and the relationships among variables. The mea surement model was assessed by the full sample and each subgroup (male/female) separately. First, construct reliability was assessed by using Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and individual item loadings. As shown in Table 2, all the values for composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha were greater than the acceptable threshold of 0.60 [68], thus indicating good construct reliability [69]. Second, a confir matory factor analysis was conducted to assess convergent and discriminant validity. As shown in Table 2, all values of AVE were higher than the threshold of 0.5, supporting good convergent validity [70].

Two approaches were adopted to assess discriminant validity. First, discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the square root of AVE for each construct and correlations of that construct with other con structs, or we examined whether the item loadings on their own con structs were greater than the loadings on other constructs. The results in Table 2 show that most of the constructs achieved a good discriminant validity. To further assess discriminant validity, we measured the item loadings on their own constructs, and the results are shown in Appen dix 3. All the item loadings were greater than the threshold of 0.60 [67], which indicate an acceptable convergent validity [71]. Furthermore, Table 2 also shows the descriptive statistics, including mean values, standard deviations, and correlations of all the variables. Because the constructs show high correlation in Table 2, within- and between-construct VIF values were calculated to test multicollinearity [69]. As shown in Appendix 4, all of the within- and between-construct VIF values were among 1 and 3, satisfying an acceptable threshold of 5 [72]. Thus, collinearity was not likely to be a great concern in this study.

As all collected data are self-reported, we assessed for common method biases through statistical analyses. First, we used Harman’s onefactor test [73]. the first-factor loading is 40.9%, which indicates low common method biases [74]. Second, following Liang et al. [75], we incorporated into the PLS model a common method factor that included the indicators of all the principal constructs and accordingly calculated the variance of each indicator, substantively explained by the principal construct and by the method. As shown in Appendix $^ { 5 , }$ the average of substantive factor loadings was 0.781, while that of method-based variance was almost 0. The difference between these two loadings was significant. In addition, most method-based factor loadings were not significant. Thus, we conclude that the method has a negligible effect on our study.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

The results support five of the seven hypotheses, and we accordingly obtain some insightful findings. First, while prior research on routine use does not differentiate mass-market products from niche products, this study further points out that routine use of niche products is affected by consumers’ product involvement. This finding suggests that routine use of niche products mainly derives from consumers’ deep psycholog ical feelings or beliefs about the focal products, which has not been well identified in prior literature [3].

Second, contrary to our expectations. brand lovalty has no significant effect on product involvement. This is inconsistent with previous research that found that consumers with higher brand loyalty are more likely to prefer the brand’s products [44]. One possible explanation is that consumers may hold positive beliefs about a focal brand’s niche products because of the halo effect, yet at the same time they may also evaluate the products more objectively by comparing them with other alternatives. In our specific case, the competing alternatives have already been well adopted by most users. It is thus possible that Apple

In addition, contrary to our expectations, there is no significant difference between females and males in terms of brand loyalty. One possible explanation would be that females in our context cannot well evaluate the relational nature of the product because of its very limited appli cation opportunities, and thus they do not significantly differentiate from male users.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

One important theoretical implication of this study is that it is among the first to attempt to investigate routine use of niche products from well-known companies. Prior research on routine use tends to treat the focal products exclusively as mass-market. thus failing to differentiate mass-market products from niche products [3]. Drawing upon involve ment theory, our study suggests that the routine use of niche products is theoretically different from that of mass-market products. That is, routine use of niche products is not an outcome of a simple cognitive process like routine use of mass-market products; instead, it originates with a deeper psychological state (i.e., niche product involvement in this study). Toward this end, our study opens a new window for future research by suggesting that to gain a more solid understanding of routine use of niche products, scholars need to integrate the theories and factors that reflect individuals’ deep psychological states into the research frame.

Second, by further considering the nested relationship between a well-known brand and its niche product (i.e., the niche product belongs to the well-known brand), we propose a brand-to-product belief transfer theoretical frame. Accordingly, based on involvement theory, we develop a research model to depict how brand-level antecedents (i.e., brand loyalty, self-congruence, and social influence) affect niche prod uct involvement, which in turn impacts routine use of niche product. Our findings reveal that brand-to-product belief transfer does exist in our context, suggesting an insightful path for researchers to investigate the focal phenomenon.

Third, the moderating effects of gender revealed in this article extend our understanding of involvement theory. While existing literature on product involvement mainly highlights either its antecedents or out comes (e.g., [60, 61]), we have little knowledge of the role of gender in the product involvement formation process. Our findings reveal that the influences of brand-level beliefs on product involvement are contingent upon gender differences. To this end, it would be meaningful to include gender in theoretical analyses related to product involvement.

## 6.3. Practical implications

This study also provides several practical implications for product providers. First, our research findings suggest that well-known com panies should take several measures to enhance the routine use of their cross-disciplinary products. At the stage of system design, the company should take advantage of its own core brand culture. This involves establishing a cohesive corporate culture and integrating its own brand concept into product design so that every consumer has a good experi ence and perceives the corporate culture when using its products.

Second, we suggest well-known companies take measures to identify how consumers see the focal brand and what they expect from it. For instance. customers can be invited to tell their stories with the well. known brand, which may help the focal company figure out how to better meet customers’ desires and demands. A well-designed brand image, one that is consistent with customers’ inherent needs and values, would also stimulate consumers to be involved with the focal niche product despite its weaknesses and disadvantages.

Third, focal companies should pay extra attention to marketing strategies when offering niche products. For instance, they may increase their brand awareness and image through a wider scale of advertisement $( \mathbf { e . g . }$ , with celebrity endorsements) or public welfare activities $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ by donating money or providing COVID-19 relief). Considering the signif icant role of social influence, these well-known companies can also invite their fans to disseminate the information about a niche product.

Fourth, focal well-known companies need to employ gender-based tactics to increase product involvement. On the one hand, results show that the relationship between ideal self-congruence and product involvement is stronger for males than for females, indicating that males’ product involvement perception is more likely to be shaped by ideal self-congruence. Accordingly, focal companies need to take rele vant actions to make the values conveyed by the brand are more attractive to male customers. For instance, some famous male business leaders can be invited to promote the brand so as to stimulate males to further value the focal brand. However, for female customers, consid ering the effectiveness of advertisement, it is not that necessary to invest many resources (i.e., not as many as the amount for males) in conveying consistent values. On the other hand, it is revealed that the relationship between social influence and product involvement is stronger for females than for males, we suggest the focal company put more effort into forming a social context to make female customers surrounded by the band. For instance, those who recommend the brand to female cus tomers will be given more rewards than those who recommend the brand to male customers. By doing this, female customers will be more likely to be involved with the focal niche product, which in turn can predict their routine use.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

Despite the helpful implications above, several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, while prior research suggests that many factors may influence product involvement, our study only focuses on three of them: brand loyalty, ideal self-congruence, and social influence. Addi tionally, we do not consider the impact of government regulation or socioeconomic factors. The research model may also benefit from including payment method as a construct because traditional cash- and card-based payments are still used. Further research may include these additional relevant factors to gain a more comprehensive understanding of routine use. Second, this study was only conducted in China. It would be interesting to study routine use in other countries by taking into ac count culturally specific factors to improve the generalizability of the results. Even in the Chinese context also, the use of Apple Pay is highly dependent upon whether retailers have the supporting infrastructure. To this end, future research may focus on some specific retailers that do support Apple Pay to further investigate the focal phenomenon. Third, although it is valid to justify the research model, the cross-sectional research design may prevent us from obtaining a finer-grained under standing of the focal phenomenon. Because the whole market is dy namic, future research may benefit from a longitudinal design, which can offer a more detailed understanding of how routine use of niche products is shaped in a changing context. Finally, although companies must promote the routine use of their niche products, several factors may inhibit individual consumers’ product adoption and routine use. It would, therefore, be meaningful for future research to further

investigate these factors.
