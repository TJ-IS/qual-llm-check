# Does additional audio really work? A study on users’ cognitive behavior with audio-visual dual-channel in panoramic digital museum

Xiaoyang He , Dan Wu

<sup>a</sup> School of Information Management, Wuhan University, Luojia Hill, 430072, Wuhan, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Panoramic roaming Digital museum Audio-visual channe Cognitive load Eye-tracking

## A B S T R A C T

Multi-channel human-computer interaction offers a new perspective for optimization research in the digital museum, though there is no empirical evidence on how these factors affect cognition. This paper conducted a controlled experiment using the audio guide as a breakthrough point, aiming to determine the cognitive changes before and after the audio-visual dual-channel interaction. The results demonstrate that adding the auditory channel brought more cognitive load but not better cognitive outcomes. This paper evaluated the reasons behind it and noted that designing channels based solely on technical feasibility is undesirable, with suggestions pro posed for museum practitioners and platform developers.

## 1. Introduction

Panoramic roaming technology is an important part of Web 3D technology [1]. Also known as 360-degree video technology [2], virtual reality based on spherical video [3], etc., it aggregates various multi media elements (such as audio and video) based on real images to achieve vivid scenes and real experiences [4]. Due to its low cost [5], short period [6], and excellent presentation effect [2], it has become increasingly popular among educators and researchers in recent years [7]. It has been successfully used in medical education [8], language learning [9], science [10], and other fields. To a certain extent, the application and promotion of panoramic technology in digital museums have solved a series of objective problems such as physical distance, poor weather, maintenance or closure, etc. With the increasing popu larity of mobile terminals and the normal prevention and control of COVID-19, this tour mode can achieve efficient interaction between online visitors and offline exhibitions [11] and improve the quality of the exhibition and user experience so that its practical significance is self-evident. Several panoramic digital museums have been built based on large national entity museums, such as the British Museum, the Louvre Museum, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the Palace Museum [12].

The International Council of Museums (ICOM) clearly states that museums acquire, conserve, research, communicate and exhibit the tangible and intangible heritage of humanity and its environment for education, study, and enjoyment [13]. Therefore, in addition to enhancing exhibitions and reconstructing cultural heritage, scholars are highly concerned about the important role of digital museums in edu cation [14–16]. The theme of International Museum Day 2022 is "The Power of Museum", which includes innovating on digitization and accessibility, noting that digital innovation can make museums more accessible and engaging, helping visitors understand complex and subtle concepts [17]. Many studies have been conducted to support the posi tive contribution of technologies and media forms such as virtual reality (VR) to museum learning [18–20]. However, the involvement of emerging technologies and multimedia forms does not always promote learning. Makransky et al. [21] found that highly immersive VR tech nology leads to increased demand for working memory processing and reduces knowledge acquisition compared to traditional media. Cao et al. [22] pointed out that the impact of virtual interaction on learning effi ciency was not significant.

In order to relieve the pressure caused by the high sense of immer sion on users, virtual museums generally provide an audio guide, background music, and other functions, presenting information through both audio and visual channels. At the same time, people strongly de mand the multi-sensory experience of museums. Therefore, museums should try to develop and utilize sensory forms other than vision [23]. The theoretical basis of this initiative can be traced back to the Multi-channel Hypothesis put forward by Allport [24] in 1972. He confirmed relieving the pressure of the visual channel by developing and utilizing the auditory channel is an efficient and feasible choice. Mayer et al. [25] have conducted more than 200 experiments and proposed the

Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML) based on the Dual-channel Hypothesis, which has become an important theoretical basis for designing multimedia teaching materials. Subsequently, scholars have continued to explore the impact of the application of audio-visual dual channels on user cognition, but the conclusions vary widely. The function of audio-visual dual channels may be different under different boundary conditions.

Previous research on panoramic digital museums has mainly focused on the technical aspect, and a few user studies have focused on behav ioral performance, experience, and satisfaction, ignoring user cognition. Monsell [26] pointed out that a system that does not take users cognitive ability as the starting point is of no value because use cognition is the basis of all user behaviors. Opening up multi-channel i the leading way to alleviate the visual pressure caused by the high im mersion of virtual museums. In the past, researchers have made many explorations in the integration of virtual reality technology and multi-channel. This study is user-centered, extending cognitive research to the emerging scene of the construction of digital museum information technology to provide a more comprehensive perspective for digita museum research. At the methodological level. this study creatively introduced the Personal Meaning Map (PMM), combined with main stream cognitive load measurement methods (subiective scale. knowl edge test, and eye movement experiment), to avoid the erasure of helpful information under traditional methods and expand the mea surement dimension of learning effect, establishing a comprehensive cognitive load measurement system and providing targeted method reference for cognitive research in the field of cultural heritage. In addition, the Multimedia Learning Theory was put forward 20 year back, and more than the relevant principles are needed to guide the design of multimedia learning materials under the current technica conditions. During this period, the relevant research is also scattered in different fields, with little mutual verification and less involvement in the humanities. In the face of theoretical lag and field gap, this research creatively applies CTML to the field of digital cultural heritage, which expands the application scenario and scope of CTML, thus enriching and updating the theory. The audio guide is an important research object of physical museum research. However, relevant research in virtual mu seums could be more extensive. The audio guide is expected to become a powerful supplement to visual presentation, but it may also aggravate information overload. According to the author's investigation. the existing panoramic digital museum is relatively free regarding whethe to set the audio guide and way how to set it. This function appears to be in the awkward situation of "existing for existence". In terms of guiding practice, research on the effect of the auditory channel in digital mu seums is still in its infancy. The current study provides empirical evi dence for the judgment of the educational value of auditory channels in digital museums. Information overload has become a fundamenta challenge in the field of human-computer interaction. This paper ex plores it from the perspective of multi-channel, which is of great sig nificance to the construction of virtual museums.

Both CLT and CTML believe that rational exploitation of other channels effectively reduces the pressure of the visual channel. On this basis, some designers believed that in scenes with high visual impact, such as panoramic roaming systems, adding the auditory channel can balance the visual pressure and avoid information overload, thus giving users a more relaxed cognitive experience. But there is no convincing supporting data to reveal how and why the user’s cognitive load changes, so we propose the first question:

Q1: How does the user’s cognitive load change before and after the interaction between the visual and auditory channels?

Most designers of panoramic digital museums believe that adding multiple channels will enable the full display of exhibits and make the system more functional and visible. They regard the Multi-channel Theory as an essential supporting concept. Coupled with the fact that the importance of audio guides in physical museums has been repeatedly proven with a long history [27], the designers take it for granted that transplanting this feature into panoramic roaming systems will also work well [28], which is not technically challenging to achieve. So they take the audio guide as the primary way to open up the auditory channel. However, does the inclusion of an auditory channel get better results? One of the most critical functions of museums, especially in the field of education, will open up the auditory channel to improve learning. Accordingly, we propose the second question:

Q2: Does the addition of auditory channel help improve users learning?

Since eye tracking data is an intuitive reflection of the cognitive process, many previous studies have verified the relationship between eye movement and cognitive load. This study aims to determine which eye movement features of the cognitive process are associated with better learning outcomes under different audio conditions. That is, what behaviors lead to better outcomes? This study would provide first-hand supporting data for optimizing the design of panoramic digital museums and help to probe the reasons for dynamic changes in learning outcomes more profoundly and precisely. Therefore, we propose the third question:

Q3: What visual features lead to better learning of users under different audio conditions?

Therefore, this study takes the audio guide function of the panoramic roaming system of digital museums as the starting point and takes the "Panoramic Palace Museum" as the experimental platform to conduct controlled experiments in a laboratory environment. Combined with the consumption and final results of the cognitive process, the measurement of cognitive behavior is converged into cognitive load and learning ef fect, the difference between knowledge acquisition and cognitive load with or without an audio guide is analyzed, and the impact of the addition of auditory channel on users’ cognition is tested, and method how to improve cognitive performance while controlling the level of cognitive load is explored; it is an important objective of this study. Our results demonstrate that adding the auditory channel brings more cognitive load but not a better learning effect, which implies that the current combination form of the audio-visual channel is unreasonable. On this basis, the current paper makes suggestions for optimizing the audio guide function to help museums to improve the information transfer link and unleash the value of audio-visual dual channels interaction.

After the introduction, the second part presents the theoretical background and reviews the related research. The third section puts forward the research hypotheses. Part IV describes the research design, including experimental design, data collection, and measurement. Part V analyzes the data and reports the results. Part VI discusses our find ings, provides theoretical and practical implications, and discusses the limitations. Our conclusions are obtained in the last part.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Cognitive load theory (CLT)

CLT was proposed by Sweller [29] in 1988. He defined cognitive load as the total amount of mental activity imposed on an individual’s cognitive system during a specific task and considered the user’s cognition as resource consumption. Subsequent researchers have refined the theory [30–32]. According to CLT, the elements that constitute human cognitive structure include long-term memory and working memory [31]. New information can be accumulated in long-term memory after first being processed in working memory [32].

CLT assumes that any learning task imposes three types of cognitive loads on working memory: intrinsic cognitive load, extraneous cognitive load, and germane cognitive load [33,34]. Intrinsic cognitive load is caused by the intrinsic difficulty and complexity of the learning task and depends on learners’ prior knowledge [35]. Extraneous cognitive load is due to inappropriate instructional design, so poorly designed instruc tional materials should be reduced to the maximum extent possible [36]. Germane cognitive load is the degree of mental effort that learners put into constructing schemas when associating information from long-term memory with new information. The residual capacity of intrinsic and extraneous load plays a role in increasing or decreasing extraneous cognitive load [36].

However, the capacity of working memory is limited. When pro cessing demands exceed working memory capacity, cognitive behavior becomes inefficient or ineffective [6], and learning will be hindered [37]. Therefore, unnecessary load in working memory should be reduced [38]. In the field of instructional design, the main recommen dations based on CLT are to provide material that is consistent with learners’ prior knowledge (reduce intrinsic load), avoid unnecessary and confusing information, stimulate processes leading to rich and profound knowledge (avoid extraneous load) [34], and stimulate learners to process the material more deeply (increase germane load), thereby increasing learning [21]. Working memory capacity can be best utilized by presenting more different information or using dual channels (visual and audio cues) [39] and managing the interactivity or complexity of materials [32].

In earlier studies, the cognitive load was mainly determined through indirect approaches, such as task time or error rate [40,41]. Nowadays, the primary measurements of cognitive load include three categories: dual-task method [42], physiological measurement [43,44], and sub jective scoring [45,46].

## 2.2. Cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML)

Following earlier findings, Mayer [47] proposed the Cognitive The ory of Multimedia Learning (CTML), which proposes the underlying processes in the learner’s mind during meaningful learning. Dual-channel and limited capacity are important premises of the theory [47]. Dual-channel points to the Multi-channel Hypothesis proposed by Allport [24], which states that information management has two different channels: visual and auditory. Studies have confirmed that different channels occupy different psychological resources [24], which provides a theoretical basis for the realization of multi-channel inter action. Limited capacity is based on CLT, and Sweller et al. [40] have proven through empirical research that following the instructions of CLT, working memory can reduce the load in multimedia learning.

In the practical environment, the bandwidth load of the visual channel is much larger than that of other channels. The cognitive load can be significantly reduced if mental resources are reasonably allocated among channels. Some studies have reported that the auditory is the most important source of cognitive information other than visual, ac counting for up to 20% [48], and multimedia learning environments provide strong conditions for simultaneous support of learning by multi-channel.

Multimedia can be characterized as the simultaneous presentation of verbal and pictorial information [49]. The presentation of instructional materials in a multimedia format is usually justified by invoking the Multimedia Principle [50]. As multimedia becomes broader, multimedia learning gets associated with acquiring or constructing knowledge by processing combinations of language and pictures of varying complexity [47]. Numerous educational studies used CLT [40] to explain the po tential benefits of using multimedia to improve learning. CTML explains that the brain does not interpret information in a mutually exclusive way from multimedia presentations based on text, images, and hearing. Instead, these elements are dynamically selected and arranged to generate logical mental structures based on each individual [51]. Dag hestani et al. [52] considered students knowledge constructors who actively select and construct verbal, visual, and perceptual information or knowledge in their way.

However, not all multimedia presentations are considered equally effective. According to Mayer [53], instructional materials should be based on knowledge about how the brain works and how people process information. Therefore, evidence-based theories should be proposed to guide the construction of multimedia instructional information. Mayer and his colleagues contributed to the formulation of 15 principles of multimedia instructional design that are based on more than 200 experimental research studies on how people learn from words and graphics [25]. Among them, the Modality Principle [54] states that upon presentation in the form of an audio explanation, the text will be pro cessed in the auditory channel so that the visual channel focuses on the processing of animated information and avoids overloading. The Redundancy Principle [55] points out that the depth of students learning through "animation + voice explanation" is better than through multimedia materials in the form of "animation + voice explanation + screen text explanation" and that adding on-screen text explanation to the existing audio explanation hinders comprehension. Both these principles are closely related to this study.

Nowadays, especially with the rapid development of multimedia technologies, these multimedia principles have been extended to various learning environments, such as computer-based learning environments [56], virtual learning environments [57], or augmented learning envi ronments [58]. The study of virtual multimedia learning principles in learning environments is vital for future research to reveal the current status and gaps of multimedia learning principles in different learning environments and to expand their boundaries.

## 2.3. Research on how the addition of an auditory channel affects learning

The manner how to use diverse information technologies to enrich the functions of digital museums and expand the bandwidth of infor mation transmission has been the subject of researchers in various countries. Early studies focused on content architecture [59], applica tion programming [60], core technologies [61] of virtual digital heri tage, and theoretical guidance that needed to be improved [62]. After a certain level of technology accumulation, scholars have launched an evaluation to explore technology usability [63] and evaluate the ad vantages and disadvantages from the user perspective [62,64]. The core position of users gradually came to the fore.

Presenting information through audio-visual dual-channel is the primary way to relieve visual pressure in the actual construction of virtual museums. Several studies have demonstrated that the applica tion of auditory channels in multimedia may have various advantages for learning [18,21,65,66], but some studies have failed to prove posi tive results $[ 6 7 , 6 8 ]$ . Scheiter et al. [69] pointed out that the reason is that learners must integrate information expressed in different ways, which is challenging. Multimedia learning can represent unique learning opportunities but may also challenge learners’ working mem ory ability, resulting in cognitive overload [40].

Audio-visual dual-channel interaction may affect learning in different fields, multimedia forms, and speech features. Therefore, it is necessary to explore how adding an auditory channel in panoramic digital museums affects the distribution of audience psychological re sources and learning effects. Based on CLT [29] and Makransky et al. [21], the current study ar gues that audio-visual dual-channel interaction affects users’ cognitive load. The panoramic roaming system provides lush picture information, and the delivery of the tour information through the auditory channel will effectively share the visual stress brought about by the high level of visual immersion. The user uses the auditory channel to process the audio guide and the visual channel to focus on the screen image, and the two channels work simultaneously. Highly relevant auditory material can reduce distraction and avoid overloading the visual channel. Accordingly, Hypothesis 1 is proposed:

H1: The user’s cognitive load will be reduced after realizing audiovisual dual-channel interaction

Based on CTML [47] and other related findings [18,65,66], this study suggests that adding an auditory channel to the panoramic digital museum would facilitate user learning. Compared with using the visual channel only, opening up the auditory channel can make the learning material presented in a more concrete and perceptible form during the learning process and relieve the pressure of the visual channel. At the same time, the audio guide can provide an experience closer to the real tour scenario, reducing the distraction caused by viewing the navigation text when the user observes the screen in formation, resulting in a better immersive learning experience. When the visual load is shared, working memory can be better utilized, thus enabling users to devote more energy to understanding and transforming the learning material, successfully building schemas, and thus achieving better learning results. Accordingly, Hypothesis 2 is proposed.

## H2: Adding an auditory channel will improve learning.

Based on previous studies measuring the relationship between eyemovement features and cognitive load, this study believes that users eye-movement characteristics can reflect their learning process and that visual features differ before and after dual-channel interaction. In this study, eight eye movements that were validated by numerous researchers in other fields with consistent findings were selected for collection and analysis, including pupil diameter [70], blink rate [71], blink interval [72], total fixation count and fixation count within Areas of Interest (AOD) [73], total fixation duration and fixation duration within AOI [73,74], and task completion time [40]. Blink rate is nega tively correlated with cognitive load, and the other metrics positively correlate with cognitive load. Accordingly, Hypothesis 3 is proposed:

H3a: The larger the pupil diameter, the better the learning effect.

H3b: The lower the blink rate, the better the learning effect.

H3c: The greater the blink interval, the better the learning effect.

H3d: The higher the total fixation count, the better the learning effect.

H3e: The higher the fixation count within AOI, the better the learning effect.

H3f: The longer the total fixation duration, the better the learning effect.

H3g: The longer the fixation duration within AOI, the better the learning effect.

H3h: The longer the task completion time, the better the learning effect.

## 4. Research design

## 4.1. Measurement

This experiment aimed to assess the subjects’ learning effects and cognitive load both before and after the audio-visual dual-channel interaction. The learning effects were measured based on a knowledge test and PMM. Cognitive load was measured from eye-tracking and the WP scale, and the attributes of the variables were assessed by learning effect.

## 4.1.1. Measurement of learning effect

This study used a combination of subjective and objective methods to measure the learning effect, specifically the "Knowledge Test + PMM". The knowledge test could directly quantify the changes in the subjects cognitive status, so two sets of questions were incorporated into the questionnaire. The knowledge test in the pre-questionnaire consisted of 16 multiple-choice questions, 15 of which were related to the experi mental stimulus material (10 on the Taihe Palace and 5 on the Kowloon Wall), and examined the mastery of the task scenario. One question was misleading and not related to the material. The content of the items in the post-test questionnaire was consistent with the pre-test, and the questions and options were not arranged in the same order. Due to the specificity of the museum learning space, the study introduced the PMM as a valid measurement tool for informal learning environments to complement the knowledge test [75]. We evaluated the impact of this learning on subjective perceptions, attitudes, and emotional awareness by comparing graphical changes in respondent feedback before and after learning [56]. Participants were allowed to describe their thoughts using words, phrases, pictures, etc., in any order.

## 4.1.2. Measurement of cognitive load

Cognitive load measures include subjective, task performance, and physiological measures applicable to different contexts and load levels. There is no "perfect" method. Among them, the long-established sub jective measures are simple and easy to use and have the advantages of high surface validity, no task interference, and uniform dimensionality. It is the method of choice for most scholars. Therefore, this study is also based on the subjective measurement method. Considering that this method also has defects, such as subjective perception evaluation, this study used the other two methods for reference so that multiple evalu ation indicators would support each other and achieve the goal of approaching the actual cognitive load as much as possible.

## (1) Subjective measurement—the WP scale

Through experimental testing and data analysis, Sun [76] and other researchers believe that the WP scale is the ideal measurement tool in multimedia learning under medium and low-difficulty tasks. The re sources of each dimension in the scale are more in line with the process of this experiment. Therefore, the WP scale which was located in the post-test questionnaire was selected as a subjective measurement tool and modified according to the actual situation of this study. After excluding the irrelevant dimension of language output resources, the experimental scale contains seven psychological resources. After each task is completed, participants need to score the use of these seven psychological resources between 0 and 10 points according to their subjective feelings. The sum of the seven scores is the total cognitive load generated by the subjects in this task. Table 2 shows each dimen sion of the scale and its definition. The reliability and validity test shows the α coefficient of 0.663, indicating the acceptability of the sample reliability. The KMO value is 0.793, indicating that the scale has good validity and meets the experimental requirements.

## (1) Task performance measurement—learning effect

It is generally believed that when other variables are controlled, better learning results indicate lower intrinsic and extraneous cognitive burdens that hinder learning and higher germane cognitive burdens that promote learning. Based on the measurement results of the learning effect in the following sections, we made an attribute judgment on the change of cognitive load after adding the auditory channel.

## (1) Physiological measurement—eye tracking

Over the feasibility of the experimental environment and equipment, this study collected eye movement data. The main equipment used in the eye tracking experiment is the aSee eye tracker developed by 7Invensun Company. The hardware device has a 60 Hz / 120 Hz binocular sampling rate, and the eye movement data is processed by Core Studio software. In addition, since mobile technology has fully penetrated daily life and the educational environment, the panoramic digital museum is committed to improving the services provided on mobile terminals. Therefore, users in this study conducted experiments on an iPad Air3 mobile device. The device’s display size is 10.5 inches, and the resolution is 2224 \* 1668. The device has a high sales volume due to its cost performance, so it is representative. With moderate screen size, it ensures full appreciation of the 360-degree panorama and accurate eye-tracking data to be obtained. In this experiment, eight specific eye movements were selected for collection and analysis.

## 4.2.2. Task

In each scenario, the task was to enhance understanding of the theme landscape by visiting the theme site and obtaining information on his torical background, practical functions, and architectural design. In order to avoid the distortion of participants’ behavior caused by a toolong or too-short tour time, the pre-experiment determined that the tour time of TP was 10 min and that of KW was 5 min. When the remaining time was less than 1 min, the participants were prompted by the host. The task could be terminated when they were unwilling to continue to watch or had finished the tour.

## 4.2.3. Procedure

The experiment was carried out in a professional laboratory. The process is shown in Fig. 3. In the preparation stage, the host needed to know about the eye condition of the participants and replace them if they did not meet the requirements. Then the host explained the experimental process to the participants, guided them to master the essential operation of the task platform, and explained the specialized knowledge involved in the questionnaire. The participants could ask questions all the time. After confirming their understanding of the content above, they were asked to fill in the pre-test questionnaire on a mobile phone.

The host placed an iPad Air3 in front of the participants to obtain eye-movement data. The participants were well equipped with headmounted eye trackers and adjusted their posture and distance under the guidance of the host, as shown in Fig. 1, to achieve the best acqui sition. Then, the eye tracker was calibrated using the three-point method until satisfactory results were obtained. Fig. 2 is a screenshot of the main trial console, which reflects the task scene in users’ initial view and al lows observation of users’ gaze point movement. After passing the calibration, the formal task began.

The formal experiment was divided into two parts (two task scenarios). After each part was completed, the participant filled in a post-test questionnaire on mobile devices after 20 s of rest. After filling out the questionnaire, they could rest for about 3 min. After completing all the eye movement experiments, the host conducted interviews on user behaviors and opinions. The experiment was accomplished after getting their answer. Each part of the experiment took an average of 15 min, and it took participants about 40 min to complete the entire experiment.

## 4.2.4. Participants

In this experiment, the samples were recruited by online question naire, and each participant was paid 50 yuan after completing the tasks. All of them have signed the informed consent. After excluding invalid data, a total of 60 valid participants were recruited, covering un dergraduates, masters, and doctors from all five departments in the university. The demographic characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 3. They ranged in age from 18 to 28 years and were less familiar with the panoramic digital museum. None of them had expe rience using the "The Panoramic Palace" WeChat applet before.

From the perspective of self-perception, according to the interview, 35(58%) out of 60 valid participants thought that they had better learning when audio was available, and the reasons can be summarized as the following two points:

(2) More memorable experience. Participants felt that the presence of the audio guide forced them to focus on the unclear and incomprehensible parts, and they would try to make it obvious by reading the text or watching the scenes in detail to have a deeper impression. Another 25 (42%) of the valid participants believed that they learned better without the audio guide. They noted that the audio guide became a distraction and even led to negative emotions such as anxiety due to the overly specialized vocabu lary, pacing that did not match personal travel habits, and discomfort with the tone of the audio guide. In addition, this group of participants tended to learn through self-exploration, believing that they would miss the details with an audio guide and could not handle the stimulation of the two channels at the same time.

Our conclusion is that although users’ subjective perception of audio navigation was mixed, the addition of an auditory channel did not help to improve the learning effect. Audio navigation did not work or even had a negative effect on user learning. Especially when the information contained in the scene was limited, the audio guide became a kind of interference. Combined with the results in Section 5.1 above, the addi tion of auditory modality brought more cognitive load rather than a better learning effect, indicating that the new cognitive load was the extraneous cognitive load.

## 6. Discussion

In this section, we summarize the findings, explore the reasons behind them, and discuss the contributions of our work to theory and practice. We also reflect on the limitations of this study.

## 6.1. Research findings

At this point, we can answer the three questions raised at the beginning of the paper. First off, the adoption of the audio-visual dualchannel interaction led to an increase in the user’s overall cognitive load. Second, adding an audio channel had no effect or even a negative effect on user learning. Additionally, the audio guide was annoying, whereas it was simpler to develop schemas that supported learning when more focus was placed on the scene itself and the commentary text without audio. Our conclusion appears to go contrary to accepted wis dom, and the current dual-channel effort has not produced the intended results

## 6.1.1. Reason discussion

Overall, the addition of the auditory channel brought more cognitive load while not working or counteracted the learning effect; it suggests that the new cognitive load was extraneous cognitive load caused by the unreasonable combination of audio-visual channels. We believe the reasons are as follows. First of all, human cognitive resources are limited [37]. The addition of the audio guide mobilized the otherwise idle auditory channel, which was bound to occupy part of the working memory. At the same time, the data illustrate that the opening of the auditory channel did not relieve the visual pressure. Thus, the extra neous cognitive load was paid so that the remaining cognitive capacity decreased, leading to the corresponding reduction of germane cognitive load [36,88]. In other words, the learners’ mental effort in the process of constructing schemas when associating information from long-term memory with additional information was insufficient, and therefore learning became less effective. Subsequently, according to the Redun dancy Principle [55], we believe that users had to invest additional cognitive resources in linking the content of the audio guide with the commentary text rather than linking the tour information with the scene itself as we expected, which also involved the conversion of signals from different channels, curtailing the cognitive resources for comprehension even more [40]. This is corroborated by the data on the relationship between eye-movement features and learning effects in the absence of the audio guide. In addition, schema construction takes time, and learners process information in sufficient time [89], so the matching degree between the speed of the audio guide and information reception and processing by learners directly affects the learning effects.

Accordingly, the speed of digesting auditory information needs to syn chronize with the pace of the visual tour in order to establish a better experience. Data show that the pace of the current audio guide did not match the pace of digesting information and touring for most partici pants, and users lacked time to think about the auditory and visual contents. The audio even interrupted thinking. Furthermore, in accor dance with Attention Filtering Theory, the brain would not pay attention to information if it were deemed to be a distracting factor, preventing it from being processed [88]. Therefore, once the learner had to discon nect from or actively ignore the audio guide, the auditory channel would no longer be an effective channel for information transfer. More seri ously, once the speech content was disconnected from the graphical content, the memory effect in the presence of audio would be 5–11% worse than in the absence of audio according to the Irrelevant Sound Effect [90], and the audio guide would then negatively affect learning. Nevertheless, this inapplicability would not endure indefinitely because some users might be attracted back to the speech rhythm after a break from the guide, or they might alternate between these two states constantly.

## 6.1.2. Conclusion comparison

Thus, this study offers proof of the value of audio-visual channels in panoramic roaming systems for museum learning and education. The results suggest that if the goal is only to facilitate learning, there is no need to open an auditory channel for guidance, which supports Mayer’s research on the Redundancy Principle. According to Mayer et al. [47], situations, where the content of the audio narration is identical to the on-screen text, are called redundant presentations. They argue that redundancy should be eliminated by cutting out unnecessary and re petitive important material. When irrelevant voices are excluded, users can get better transfer and memory [67]. Notably, Mayer et al. [55] and most of the previous studies demonstrated redundancy effects by comparing learning effects in narrative and narrative plus screen text conditions, while this paper compares screen text and narrative plus screen text. In contrast to the current study, Makransky et al. [21] rejected the applicability of the Redundancy Principle in an immersive virtual reality experiment setting more similar to the present study. They claimed that the acquisition of biological knowledge is unaffected by different presentation methods. Fang et al. [65] emphasized that experimentally relevant audio information in the field of computer sci ence has been confirmed to help improve user performance and that information overlap reduces distraction. Based on a ubiquitous English listening learning environment, Chang [89] argued that a dual-channel presentation model could connect speech and text, thus improving learning. Thus, not all learning domains, learning modes, or scenarios can benefit from the concepts of multimedia learning. Applying these theories and principles without considering the above factors is impre cise and even counterproductive, and the scope and conditions of their application require continuous improvement by researchers. Therefore, this research is a meaningful exploration of CTML in the field of pano rama roaming systems in museums.

In addition, this study found that commentary text has a significant impact on the learning effect of virtual museums. Data show that small scenes are more sensitive to the addition of an audio guide, and the negative effect of audio is more significant. Because when the total amount of information is limited, what users need is to expand the amount of information in the scene for their choice rather than repeating the existing information. Commentary text is one of the most important sources of information for users to learn in virtual museums. Data show that the degree of emphasis on the explanation words is positively correlated with the learning effect, which also provides evidence for the necessity of strengthening the content construction of the explanation words. As Bard [91] said, "People try to make museums communicate information more effectively, but rarely re-examine the communication itself." For the investigation of the interaction between museums and the public, researchers mostly evaluate from the perspective of effectiveness and a few from a production standpoint. Therefore, this study provides empirical evidence for the starting point of the qualitative research of Zhu et al. [92], arguing that the transformation of museum communi cation methods begins with the production of texts, and changes the role of popular science explanations from the one-way propaganda "knowl edge promotion" and "word machine" to "knowledge mediation", putting forward the framework and opening up negotiations. It is also in line with the value orientation of Lee et al. [93], that is, museum practi tioners and platform developers should pay attention to the educational and entertaining elements in virtual reality, and it is very important to provide accurate and rich information in a novel and attractive way.

## 6.2. Contributions

## 6.2.1. Theoretical implications

This study has made contributions to human-computer interaction research in the following aspects.

First, we extended the cognitive behavior research to the construc tion of digital museum emerging technologies and analyzed the learning effect and cognitive load of users in a panoramic roaming system. Pre vious studies have mostly focused on technology, while the results of this study elucidate that technological feasibility is not equivalent to the success of numerical construction. Whether new technologies can bring better cognitive results needs experimental verification and data sup port. This provides an important user perspective for research in the field of digital museums and extends cognitive research to new scenes.

Second, this study discussed some principles in CTML, mainly trying to test the applicability of the Redundancy Principle. It was observed that in the panoramic roaming system in the field of cultural heritage, it is not feasible to organize audio-visual explanations by simple reading. The research conclusion is in agreement with the proposition of Mayer et al. [55], which provides data support for the existence of the redun dancy effect in panoramic digital museums. Application scenarios and scope of this theory are expanded, and CTML is enriched with the development of technology. However, it is highlighted that a small part of the participants never or seldom looked at the text explanation in the course of the experiment. They explained in the interview. "When the text content was consistent with the voice, I closed the explanation menu immediately because it was unnecessary to parallel audio-visual dualchannel in the process of transmitting the same information." Some participants explained that they checked text explanations only because they wondered how long the speech would last. Listening without reading the text is interpreted as the Modality Principle, which is also a way that Mayer [54] found to reduce cognitive load. Therefore, this part of the behavior is an interactive combination of the redundancy effect and modality effect. Although this part of the data is too small to affect the experimental results and needs further elaboration.

In addition, this study established a comprehensive and targeted cognitive load measurement system by combining the WP scale, knowledge test, PMM, and eye tracking. Knowledge test has remained a common strategy used to evaluate the learning effect in previous studies. Although this test is simple to use in other learning fields, it ignores the particularity of the scene of museum learning. Therefore, this study introduced PMM rooted in the museum into the measurement system to avoid effective information being erased or ignored due to methodo logical reasons, which significantly expands the measurement dimen sion of the learning effect and provides a method reference for the research on cognitive load in the field of museums in the future. Moreover, this study creatively combined eye movement data and subjective scale data for analysis, corresponding the process character istics with the consequences, which made the analysis results more comprehensive and provided data support for improving the system. At the same time, it is also a profitable attempt to apply eye tracking in a panoramic roaming system.

## 6.2.2. Practical implications

This study has many practical contributions and implications. It ex amines the impact of learning materials in different channels on users cognition so as to provide a basis for museum managers and designers of panoramic roaming systems to understand users and improve services. As more and more museums turn their attention to digital technologies, such as panoramic tours, the applicability of previous multimedia learning material design principles should be re-examined. The current and other related studies suggest that a technology-centered approach to channel design is incorrect and that simple stacking of channels should not be expected to lead to better learning and an easier cognitive experience. Technical feasibility is only one aspect of platform con struction, and attention should also be paid to how to combine the unique advantages of technology with original materials and design with users as the center.

While data analysis and user interviews confirmed that learning is better and the learning experience is easier without voice guidance. When asked if it was necessary to open and optimize auditory pathways in the Panorama Digital Museum, 50 participants (90%) indicated the need for optimized audio-visual dual-channel interaction for a better experience, more comprehensive information reception, and wider crowd targeting. Based on the above results, this study puts forward the following suggestions for museum practitioners and platform de velopers: (1) Improve the actual quality of the commentary content. The museum should invest in a professional and creative research team to dig deep into the cultural connotation and provide materials for the setting of the guide content. Under the trend of the integration of li braries, museums, and archives, the use of linked data can realize the effective use and sharing of collective resources. In terms of expression, it will shorten the distance between users and the exhibits by marking professional vocabulary and telling stories, which will cause moderate emotional resonance. (2) Improve the overall level of the audio channel. In terms of quality, a more intimate interaction is constituted by providing a clearer sound and a more human interpretation. In terms of presentation, the power of personalized setting and rhythm control should be transferred to the user by setting adjustable speed, optional diverse voice packs, and a visible progress bar so as to promote under standing and thinking. (3) Improve the matching degree of audio-visual channels. According to the results of the eye-tracking experiment, users focus on the correspondence between the guide information and the actual scene, which will also lead to better learning and understanding Therefore, audio-visual matching can be facilitated by segmentation and tagging of the explained text [25]. (4) Improve the sensitivity and vividness of touching operation. The results show that the current but ton has a certain delay, causing confusion in users unfamiliar with the platform. Further, due to the lack of volume of the buttons, the touch operation is not vivid, so the user is not sure whether the system has received instructions, and the continuous click also interferes with the normal tour. Attention should be paid to these operational problems so that more psychological resources can be invested in real learning and creation so as to improve the quality and efficiency of tours [21].

## 6.3. Limitations and future directions

Initially, due to objective factors such as the experimental site, the participants recruited for this experiment are all university students and more participants were needed to verify the characteristics of other age groups. At the same time, the proportion of female participants was 85%. Although the test results reported that the data under different genders did not have statistical differences, this may lead to some fea tures being magnified and others being erased, thus affecting the comprehensiveness and generalization of the conclusions. Therefore, it is still necessary to balance the gender proportion of the sample.

Subsequently, the participants in this study had a low level of a priori knowledge of the learning material and were all beginners. Currently, many studies in the field of user cognition have confirmed that users at different levels (e.g., beginners, skilled, experts, etc.) exhibit different cognitive characteristics. Therefore, a comparative analysis of users with different levels of knowledge in different domains represents one of the directions for further research. In addition, it was the first time that all participants used this experimental platform. Although they were introduced to the platform and instructed to familiarize themselves with the operation before starting, some of them still got lost in other sce narios or unexpectedly quit due to unfamiliarity based on the feedback, which may have some impact on the participants’ emotions and lead to slightly higher feedback on the operational resources in the WP scale than reality. Meanwhile, due to the limitation of objective conditions, this experiment was carried out in the laboratory scenario, which is different from the actual application scenario of the platform. The uniqueness of the above experimental environment may have had an impact on the results.

In addition, the psychological impact of the experimental design on the participants should be considered. Considering the practical reason that each person looked at two scenes and experienced two audio stimuli, although such an experience facilitated them to add some sub jective contrast, this could have an impact on their perception (ampli fying the difference).

A total of 60 effective participants were recruited, and the mean value of the test efficacy of the conclusions was 0.694, which is not a satisfactory result. Therefore, the sample size should be expanded if conditions permit, and the count of tasks should be reduced to avoid the psychological impact on the subjects so as to ensure the reliability and generalization of the conclusions. In addition, in the process of completing the task, they would inevitably speculate about the purpose of the experiment and consciously pay attention to the questions mentioned in the pre-test to obtain higher scores, thus affecting their own behavior and causing distortion of the experimental data. In order to balance this situation, some topics that could only be understood by observing the details of the scene were set in the knowledge test, and the PMM was also employed to measure the all-around performance of learning. However, it is still difficult to measure the difference caused by the psychological suggestion.

Moreover, the combination form and setting scheme of the dual channel deserves further exploration. In order to strictly control the variables, the audio guide was looped before the end of the tour in the group with auditory access. As some participants mentioned, too many loops could create annoyance and even make the audio guide perceived as a distraction. According to previous studies, negative emotions may affect users’ cognitive behavior. Therefore, how to minimize the influ ence of such emotions on the experimental results through the experi mental design is also a question to be taken into account in the ensuing research. In the future, more controlled groups can be aggregated in terms of the controllability of the playback rhythm of speech, timbre, and speech rate to propose a proven combination to further improve and supplement the results of this study.

## 7. Conclusion

A controlled experiment was conducted to explore user cognition under the audio-visual dual-channel of the panoramic digital museum. Overall, adding auditory channels did not result in better learning while bringing more cognitive load, suggesting that channel design should not adopt a technology-centered approach. Conversely, it is premature to assume that an additional auditory channel is unnecessary. Opening up the auditory channel is still a potential design direction if designers take user-centered approaches, enhance content building, focus on facili tating knowledge acquisition, and reduce unnecessary cognitive load. Therefore, how to unlock the value of audio-visual dual-channel inter action remains an urgent issue for panoramic digital museum designers and researchers to address in the future studies.
