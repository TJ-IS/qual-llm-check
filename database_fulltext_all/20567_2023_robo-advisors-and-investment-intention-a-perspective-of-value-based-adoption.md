# Robo-advisors and investment intention: A perspective of value-based adoption

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Financial robo-advisor Uncertainty reduction strategies Perceived value Perceived risk Perceived benefit Investment intention

## A B S T R A C T

Financial robo-advisors have been widely used to assist individuals in their investment decisions, making it important to reduce uncertainties in the assistance process. Existing empirical studies rarely explore uncertainty reduction strategies and their implications on users’ investment intentions in the context of financial roboadvisors: our study attempts to address this gap. We construct a model to explain how uncertainty reduction strategies affect users’ investment intention in using financial robo-advisors. By collecting and analyzing a sample of 307 financial robo-advisor users, we find that algorithmic interpretability, structural assurance, and interactivity as uncertainty reduction strategies are positively related to users’ investment intention through the value-based adoption mechanism. Our research extends the value-based adoption model and uncertainty reduction theory in the financial robo-advisor context. We provide insights to financial robo-advisor service providers about focusing on improving algorithmic transparency, third-party assurance, and interactivity of financial robo-advisors to enhance perceived value and investment intention

## 1. Introduction

This research is partially motivated by the adoption of financial roboadvisors in the financial industry. Using J.P. Morgan Automated Investing, a typical financial robo-advisor, as an example, the financial robo-advisors of J.P. Morgan provide appropriate portfolio for each investor. Based on users’ information, financial robo-advisors auto matically match the client’s specific investment objectives, timeframe, and risk criteria with various risk-return investment portfolios. The financial robo-advisors can adjust and rebalance the portfolio by reacting to financial market changes. A similar application of financial robo-advisors can be found in Vanguard Group, Morgan Stanley, and BlackRock. Since their introduction, financial robo-advisors have been developing rapidly and receiving continuous attention from the finan cial industry. In 2022, the assets under the management of financial robo-advisors in the U.S. financial market are expected to reach \$1660 billion and grow by 16.1% annually [1]. The strong growth of financial robo-advisors has become a central topic in the fintech field [2].

Financial robo-advisors refer to a service for providing online private asset management advice that is automated and algorithmic without the need for human intervention [2,3]. Financial robo-advisors are characterized by broad investment types, low capital threshold, less investment knowledge requirements, low service fees, and strong real-time responsiveness to the market [3,4]. Different from the tradi tional way of obtaining investment advice from human advisors, financial robo-advisors use algorithms to quickly filter data and simulate asset portfolios to provide personalized investment advice services in a disintermediated and efficient manner [3,4].

We find mainly two research streams in the previous research on financial robo-advisors: Algorithmic optimization and studies on user behavior. Regarding algorithm optimization, scholars have designed algorithms for optimal portfolios of asset classes based on investment objectives and risk preferences [3,4]. Users can interact with financial robo-advisors, consult the latest news, and also generate behaviors that compare financial robo-advisors with human advisors in terms of pro fessionalism, perceived trust, and performance expectations [2,5,6]. In addition, researchers have explored the adoption behavior of investors for financial robo-advisors and the post-investment adjustment of their use of financial advisors [2]. The perspective of factors influencing user behavior contains algorithm features, platform characteristics, and user characteristics. Existing studies have explored the impact of algorithmic features such as fairness, accountability, transparency, and recommendation quality [7–9]. In terms of platform characteristics, the previous literature has compared the impact of certain features of financial robo-advisors on investors. For example, financial robo-advisors that have conversational features are more likely to in crease the likelihood that investors will follow portfolio recommenda tions and improve positive investor attitudes toward financial services firms [6]; in addition, the naming or non-naming of financial robo-advisors affects the investors’ assessment of their capabilities [10]. In terms of user characteristics, the users’ previous investment experi ence, income level, age, investment knowledge, and investment moti vation are influential in the intention to use financial robo-advisors [2, 11].

Although scholars have analyzed the factors that influence the cli ents’ use of financial robo-advisors, we find two gaps in user behavior research related to financial robo-advisors. First, financial robo-advisors aim to help investors make optimal asset allocations by considering risks and benefits tradeoffs [12,13]. Risks and benefits are factors that cannot be ignored in investing. However, the existing literature has not considered a mechanism that integrates perceived risk and perceived benefit into a system to examine how it affects clients’ investment in tentions. Second, uncertainty avoidance is an important problem in personal financial investments [12]. However, the existing financial robo-advisors literature rarely systematically examines which techno logical improvements mitigate uncertainties in investing contexts from the consumer experience perspective.

It makes sense to address the two aforementioned research gaps because the perceived risk influences consumer decisions and the perceived benefit provides the primary motivation for consumer de cisions [14]. Thus, how consumers perceive the risks and benefits of engaging financial robo-advisors has a decisive impact on whether consumers use them. In addition, the shift in the investment approach from human advice to AI-automated and algorithmic investment ex poses investors to possible technology-level uncertainty. Therefore, it is critical to consider reducing technology-level uncertainty. This study constructs a structural equation model that not only integrates risks and benefits to address these two gaps but also considers reducing uncer tainty from the technology level. Specifically, our research addresses the following questions:

RQ 1: What is the mechanism that influences users to adopt financial robo-advisors for investment?

RQ 2: How do algorithmic interpretability, structural assurance, and interactivity as uncertainty reduction strategies affect the mechanics of using financial robo-advisors for investing?

To answer the research questions, we developed a conceptual research model for this study according to the value-based adoption model proposed by Kim et al. [12], which explores the effects of un certainty reduction strategies on investment intention using the value-based adoption mechanism. In collaboration with an online data provider, we collected data from actual users of financial robo-advisors to examine the model. The findings showed that uncertainty reduction strategies are effective in improving investors’ perceptions of the value of financial robo-advisors, which in turn influences investors’ invest ment intentions.

Our research makes the following contributions. First, we extend the value-based adoption model by identifying the perceived risks and perceived benefits in the AI-assisted financial investment context. Sec ond, this study develops uncertainty reduction theory in the context of using financial robo-advisors. Third, we investigate the investment intention of users in the context of AI-assisted financial investment by capturing the essence of return pursuit and risk avoidance in financial investment.

Finally, this paper provides valuable insights for practice in the financial industry. This study proposes that financial institutions enhance their focus on client-perceived value, for example, by improving privacy protection regulations to reduce perceived risks, and by exploring client-perceived benefits in multiple dimensions. In addi tion, we recommend improving the algorithmic interpretability, struc tural assurance, and interactivity of financial robo-advisor systems to boost the business performance of financial firms.

## 2. Theory background

## 2.1. Value-based adoption model

Kim et al. [12] integrate findings from technology adoption and value studies to propose a value-based adoption model. This model holds that individuals measure the perceived value of a technology by evaluating the perceived sacrifices and perceived benefits of using it. The perceived sacrifices are the costs and efforts that individuals spend to adopt technology, such as time, transaction costs, and technicality [13,15,16]. The perceived benefits are the expected advantages of technology, including technological competence and economic and emotional benefits [12,13,15]. The perceived value is the correlated belief in the perceived benefits and perceived sacrifices and the overall comparison of the benefits customers receive with the sacrifices they make [17,18]. Kim et al. [12] believe that the technology adoption model is limited in explaining the adoption intentions of individuals who are both technology users and consumers and that rational con sumers consider value maximization when purchasing and using infor mation and communication technologies. The value-based adoption model emphasizes the technology adoption intentions of individual users and identifies the perceived value of information and communi cation technologies as the principal determinant of consumers’ adoption decisions; other beliefs also influence their adoption intentions mediated by the perceived value.

The value-based adoption model brings a new perspective to un derstanding consumers’ adoption of information and communication technologies. For example, Lin et al. [16] use the value-based adoption model to examine the continuance intention of internet protocol tele vision and show that the perceived net value, as a function of perceived sacrifice and perceived benefit, is a significant predictor of satisfaction and continuance intention. Liang et al. [15] use the value-based adop tion model to examine consumers’ intentions to adopt sharing platforms. These studies incorporate cost–benefit analysis in value-based adoption mechanisms, explore the factors that influence value perceptions, and explain how value perceptions affect information and communication technology adoption.

Inspired by previous research, this study applies the value-based adoption model to the context of investing with financial roboadvisors, explores the technical features that affect consumers perceived value, analyzes investors’ perceived risks and perceived benefits, and explains how perceived value affects investors’ investment intention to use financial robo-advisors.

It is appropriate to use this model in the context of this study for two reasons: (1) Financial robo-advisors are an emerging online investment technology that is rapidly evolving, and clients also consider the perceived value before applying it to their investments. For example, Puustinen et al. [11] developed a perceived investment value scale and argued that the value of various investments is included in transaction-based benefits and sacrifices. (2) The value-based adoption model was developed to explain consumer technology adoption behavior, which is consistent with the purpose of our study, namely, to explore the users’ behavioral intentions to use financial robo-advisors for investment purposes.

The process of assessing the perceived value of a product is a context based subjective and relative experience in which the product is judged as a benefit and/or sacrifice [11]. Value is perceived based on if the perceived benefit outweighs the perceived sacrifice $[ 1 7 , 1 9 ]$ . The higher the perceived value, the greater the consumer’s willingness to purchase [20]. Similarly, investors will decide whether to invest using financial robo-advisors based on their perceived value.

To identify the customers’ perceived value regarding financial roboadvisors, we measured the perceived value based on the definition of perceived value in terms of benefits and sacrifices. Regarding sacrifice, it can be divided into monetary and non-monetary sacrifices. For example, the price of a product/service is a monetary sacrifice for the consumer [21], whereas non-monetary sacrifices include transaction costs, time costs, psychological costs, and search costs [16,18]. As argued by the value-based adoption model, there is uncertainty about the sacrifices and benefits when adopting new technologies, especially for the highly sensitive topic of online investments. Uncertainty is one of the most fundamental properties of perceived risk [22]. Therefore, to better characterize the model, our study uses perceived risk in conjunction with investment characteristics to measure the likelihood of sacrifice in using financial robo-advisors. In other words, our study uses a value-based adoption mechanism to explain clients’ investment de cisions and measures the perceived value of financial robo-advisors in terms of perceived benefits and perceived risks.

## 2.1.1. Perceived benefit

Previous research has defined perceived benefits as the extent to which consumers perceive themselves to be better through these transactions. These benefits can include time savings, cost reductions, and increased choice [14]. Perceived benefits play an important role in individual behavioral intentions because consumers evaluate products or services based on perceived benefits [23,24]. Similarly, investors will decide whether to invest with financial robo-advisors based on their perceived benefits.

Consumers evaluate perceived benefits in multiple aspects. Previous studies have shown that factors such as product attributes, product category, time pressure, product intake, product quality, and reputation significantly affect the consumers’ assessment of perceived product benefits [19,24,25]. For example, in food consumption, customers assess perceived benefits based on attributes and perceived health benefits from the food [24,25]. In online flash sales, perceived product value is influenced by time pressure and product involvement [19].

Using financial robo-advisors provides many benefits, such as con venience, time savings, 24/7 availability, low fees, and real-time stock monitoring [2]. In choosing perceived benefits, this study follows two principles. The first principle relates to the antecedents in the model that affect the perceived value, namely algorithmic interpretability, struc tural assurance, and interactivity. The second principle is consideration of the practical situation of the user’s investment purpose. Considering these two principles, we include perceived financial benefit in the model for two reasons.

First, the antecedents in this study that affect consumers’ perceived values relate to the technological features of financial robo-advisors. The benefits of financial robo-advisors, such as time savings, 24/7 avail ability, and real-time monitoring, are general features of AI technology that are less related to algorithmic interpretability, structural assurance, and interactivity and are observed in other AI technologies, such as smart floor sweepers. Second, financial benefit is always a strong motivation that influences investment decisions for investors [11,15]. Thus, it is necessary to include perceived financial benefit in the model. In this study, financial benefit is reflected in both the cost expenditure and return on investment. Considering the cost expenditures, perceived fees have a significant negative impact on perceived benefits [12]. The low fee of financial robo-advisors provides financial savings to investors. On the other hand, uncertainty reduction strategies (that is, algorithmic interpretability, structural assurance, and interactivity) provide infor mation and contextual support for assessing perceived investment returns. Therefore, this study incorporates perceived financial benefit into the research model.

## 2.1.2. Perceived risk

Perceived risk is what consumers perceive as the uncertainty and adverse consequences of purchasing a product or service [22,26]. When faced with new technologies, consumers may have a negative view because of the cost or risk [12]. Perceived risk can reduce the perceived value of e-commerce, especially because financial robo-advisors contain algorithms and automated recommendation processes, which make in vestors more sensitive to errors [12,27]. Therefore, it is particularly critical to guard against the risks associated with financial robo-advisors.

As a multidimensional construct of potentially uncertain negative results, perceived risk can include related losses in the financial, per formance, physical, psychological, and decision-making fields [14,28]. Scholars have applied perceived risk to various research contexts and identified multiple dimensions of risk. Online shopping risks are typi cally divided into financial risk, performance risk, and privacy risk [28], whereas Bhatnagar et al. [29] identify product category risk and financial risk as the dominant risks of online shopping. Farivar et al. [30] classify the risks in online communities as commerce risk, privacy risk, and social risk. Although the perceived risk is divided into multiple di mensions, the psychological schema that shapes perceived risk is similar and has a similar impact on consumer behavior.

Based on the research context of this study, we define perceived risk as the perceived uncertainty and potential loss in the process of using services. Meanwhile, this study adheres to the multidimensional view of risk proposed by previous scholars, which is based on the possibility of investment loss and the characteristics of highly sensitive information required for online personalized recommendations. Therefore, we divide risk into two dimensions: Financial risk and privacy risk.

Financial risk is defined as the possibility of monetary loss associated with the purchase of a product, which is negatively related to perceived value [31]. Perceived financial risk is a significant type of perceived risk when it comes to the personal decision of using a new product and service [29]. In this research, we define perceived financial risk as the risk of loss perceived by investors because of uncertainty in investment benefits. Privacy risk reflects the extent to which information privacy may be compromised and lost when users have no control over col lecting and processing their personal information by companies [32,33]. Information privacy is an important ethical issue, with significant im plications for the risks associated with modern information and communication technologies [34].

## 2.2. Uncertainty reduction theory and financial robo-advisor

## 2.2.1. Uncertainty reduction theory

Uncertainty reduction theory, which was originally applied to interpersonal interactions, posits that, when confronted with strangers, individuals are primarily concerned with uncertainty and increase the predictability of their partners’ behavior in the interaction through information-seeking strategies [35,36]. When the perceived uncertainty exceeds the individual’s tolerance level, the individual’s level of infor mation seeking and information processing increases [37]. At this point, interactants can reduce the suspicion of interaction partners by seeking information or switching to less risky alternatives [16,38].

Subsequent scholars broke through the scope of interpersonal communication and have applied uncertainty reduction theory to various communication environments. For example, Brashers [39] ex tends uncertainty management theory, which is based on uncertainty reduction theory, by making it suitable for interpersonal communication in organizational settings. With the popularity of computers, individuals engage in various information-seeking behaviors in a computer-mediated environment to reduce uncertainty [40]. For example, dating site participants collect information about potential daters online [41]. Researchers in the information systems field have extended uncertainty reduction theory to the context of human inter action with technology platforms [38,42]. For example, using uncer tainty reduction theory to study how to manage the uncertainty of citizens’ use of e-government services [42], examining the effect of uncertainty reduction strategies on the consumers’ willingness to apply the virtual world to the workplace [38]. Similar to the previous situa tion, investors experience some uncertainty in their use of financial robo-advisors, and the uncertainty reduction theory can be a useful perspective for examining these uncertainties.

Individuals typically reduce uncertainty and increase predictability in communication through three information-seeking strategies: Active, passive, and interactive [16,41]. Active strategies involve subjects actively seeking information about the object of interaction by observing and interpreting the communicative environment through various channels [38,41]. In passive strategies, the subjects rely pri marily on information provided by reliable third parties to identify the interaction environment [38]. Interactive strategy is individuals inter acting directly with partners to obtain information that reduces uncer tainty [16,41]. In this study, we consider financial robo-advisors service providers as the subject of uncertainty reduction behavior imple mentation and seek uncertainty reduction measures at the level of financial robo-advisors technology features.

## 2.2.2. Uncertainties in financial robo-advisors

Considering uncertainty reduction theory, we define uncertainty in our study as a cognitive state regarding technology that fluctuates based on the discrepancy between the expected information and the quality of the information obtained [43]. The process of clients using financial robo-advisors for investment is also a process of human-computer interaction. In this process, algorithmic fairness, algorithmic interpret ability, information transparency, structural assurance, interaction mode, and other factors affect the clients’ attitudes toward financial robo-advisors [7,9,38]. Specifically, the uncertainties in clients’ in vestments of using financial robo-advisors can be understood in the following ways.

First, financial robo-advisors have failed to meet the customers’ ex pectations in terms of algorithmic interpretability and information transparency. Being more transparent and interpretable are important trends for the future development of financial robo-advisors. Previous studies have shown that financial robo-advisors have some core issues to address in terms of service quality, such as algorithmic transparency and balancing information asymmetry [44]. Transparency means that the recommending agent can explain to the user why he or she recommends some items [9]. Increased interpretability can help consumers recognize situational normality and greatly increase the overall acceptance of interactive decision aids [45,46]. In recent years, the emergence of financial robo-advisors has increased investor expectations for algo rithmic transparency [42]. However, there is no industry standard for the level of transparency required, and some firms have no details related to how financial robo-advisors make investment recommenda tions. In addition, two financial robo-advisor firms have been penalized by the Securities and Exchange Commission for providing false infor mation to customers. Thus, over time, information asymmetry will inevitably lead to client skepticism, which further increases client ex pectations of algorithmic interpretability.

Second, there are risks associated with the human-computer inter action process based on financial robo-advisors. Information privacy is one example. The financial robo-advisor is a service that provides algorithm-based automated wealth management advice [2]. This also means that customers need to submit some sensitive information, such as bank card information, investment history, and personal preferences, to the financial robo-advisors for the service to work smoothly. Infor mation privacy is one of the biggest barriers in the developing e-com merce and has also been identified as the key to its success [32]. Customers’ concerns about the privacy of their information can prevent them from adopting financial robo-advisors for their investments. In addition, the interaction environment and process can influence the investors' perception and understanding of the potential risks of finan cial robo-advisors [47]. Individuals prefer human-to-human communi cation when faced with both manual and algorithmic advice, which is also known as algorithm aversion [27]. Especially for highly sensitive topics such as investing, shifting sensitive communication processes (such as financial consulting) from manual to IT-based communication conflicts with human perceptions and expectations [2]. Therefore, the presence of uncertainty perception during interactions with financial robo-advisors is a matter of concern.

Finally, the lack of structural assurance also amplifies the risk of uncertainty when using financial robo-advisors [14]. Structural assur ance relies on various forms of third-party information to increase the safety and security of the service and transaction [38,48]. A lack of structural assurance increases uncertainty in investments and transactions.

First, we consider improving algorithmic interpretability as an active strategy. Regulations emphasize algorithmic transparency and require explanations [8]. Moreover, algorithmic transparency is a subjective concept, how transparency and interpretability are judged lies in the user’s disposition and can even be reconstructed according to the user’s perception [5]. Second, structural assurance is the presence of various structures or elements in the environment, such as regulations, guarantees, and promises, that are passively relied on by individuals to assess whether the environment is safe and secure [48,49]. ## 3.1. Uncertainty reduction strategies and investors’ perceived risks

Algorithm interpretability refers to the degree of human under standing of the given black-box model or AI decisions [8]. In the field of artificial intelligence, how to promote better understanding and appre ciation of decision aids for users is an important research question [50, 52]. Individuals are loss averse [53]. Therefore, users engage in more information-seeking behavior to reduce risks when adopting new tech nology investments [22]. Algorithmic interpretability can meet the users’ needs for information search and refined cognition. To take full advantage of algorithmic tools, we must allow users to observe and understand the behavior of the algorithm, which is a key requirement for financial robo-advisors [8].

Based on the definition of perceived financial risk in this study that it is due to perceived benefit uncertainty, we argue that the more ambig uous the information searched for investors, the stronger the perceived benefit uncertainty and the stronger the perceived financial risk. The interpretive information provided to investors by financial robo advisors will satisfy the investors’ needs for financial knowledge and understanding of algorithms. Therefore, we consider that increasing the interpretability of algorithms in financial robo-advisors can help in vestors reduce their exposure to perceived financial risk. Thereby, Hy pothesis 1a is:

H1a: The degree of interpretability of financial robo-advisor algorithms is negatively related to perceived financial risks.

Algorithmic interpretability is the ability of a system to provide ex planations in human-understandable language [54]. Accurate pre dictions, descriptions, and relevance are inevitable requirements for interpretable systems to achieve decision assistance [8]. A proper explanation can improve the overall acceptance of the recommender system, make the system more friendly and easy to use, and give users a sense of reassurance [7,8]. When people understand how an algorithm system works, they are more likely to use the system correctly and trust designers and developers [7]. In contrast, if the algorithm is highly complex and not understood by humans, the results of the algorithm will not be accepted [7,27]. Therefore, it can be considered that if more investors understand the algorithmic recommendation process, the more likely they will trust the financial robo-advisor. Previous research has shown that trust can reduce perceived privacy risks and increase the likelihood of revealing private information [55]. Thereby, we propose Hypothesis 1b:

H1b: The degree of interpretability of financial robo-advisor algorithms is negatively related to perceived privacy risks.

Structural assurance refers to structures such as guarantees, regula tions, third-party assurances, promises, or other procedures that in dividuals perceive as promoting the safety of computer-based operations to avoid loss or reduce uncertainty [38,48]. On the interface, clues related to structural assurances include “contact us” clickable icons, regulations, encryption, seals of approval, explicit privacy policy state ments, and third-party vouchers. [38,50]. Consumers perceive a safe online environment through structural assurances, which play an important role in reducing the risks that may arise in transactions [56]. When conducting online transactions, consumers are hesitant because of supplier uncertainty and personal information risks, which will hinder the application of e-commerce [48]. In this case, adding a structural guarantee to the website, such as a trusted third party, can increase the people’s trust in the website, thereby promoting secure interactions between the two parties [48,57].

Research by Srivastava and Chandra [38] has shown that structural assurance is closely related to passive strategies, in which individuals rely on third-party information to reduce uncertainty and risk. Some online transaction sites (e.g., Alipay, PayPal) introduce third-party escrow services to provide buyers with transaction guarantees and reduce the risk of financial loss for consumers [58]. In addition, effective institutional mechanisms can guarantee the safe conduct of financial transactions [50]. Our study argues that various forms of structured assurances can provide customers with a secure transaction environ ment and reduce the consumers’ perceptions of financial risk. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 2a:

H2a: The structural assurance from the financial robo-advisor is nega tively related to perceived financial risks.

Companies are increasingly relying on “user portraits” to locate po tential customers and make personalized recommendations, but the resulting privacy issues can also inhibit e-commerce transactions [59]. A lack of financial security and the likelihood of hacking increases the risk of leaking the consumers’ credit card information [60]. Structural assurance can reduce the consumers’ concerns about privacy risks, which in turn can have an impact on consumer behavior [59,61]. For example, data seal provider TRUSTe claims that its online sales increased by 29% using its privacy seal [57]. In addition, customer privacy is also safeguarded by providing institutional mechanisms, credit card guarantees, clear privacy policies, and online banks using third-party digital certificates [38,57]. Therefore, the use of structured assurances will help reduce the privacy risks that investors perceive when using financial robo-advisors. Based on this elaboration, we pro pose Hypothesis 2b:

H2b: The structural assurance from the financial robo-advisor is nega tively related to perceived privacy risks.

The financial robo-advisor is a digital interface that guides investors through a fully automated investment advisory process [5]. Because the first generation of financial robo-advisors used formal language, the interface lacked social cues and anthropomorphic features, which made it difficult for consumers to reduce uncertainty and build trust [57,50]. After the emergence of chatbots, the second generation of interactive financial robo-advisors also emerged.

Interactive uncertainty reduction strategies are strategies where individuals interact directly with information sources and obtain in formation from each other to reduce uncertainty, rather than relying solely on active and passive strategies [38]. Easily interactive financial robo-advisors are characterized by ease of navigation, controllability, structural consistency, and fault tolerance [6,12]. Previous studies have shown that as an uncertainty reduction strategy, direct interaction with technology-based platform information sources can alter the users’ trust, assessment of firms, and finance-related decisions [6,38]. Therefore, financial robo-advisors with good interactivity increase the trust of in vestors, better enable users to perceive risk controllability, increase fault tolerance, and reduce the investors’ perceived financial and privacy risks. Thereby, we propose hypotheses 3a and 3b:

H3a: Interactivity of financial robo-advisors is negatively related to perceived financial risks

H3b: Interactivity of financial robo-advisors is negatively related to perceived privacy risks.

## 3.2. Perceived risk and investment intention

Perceived risk, a factor that inhibits the consumers’ purchase behavior, is associated with loss of expectations, future uncertainty, and disruption of perceived benefits. Perceived risk has been shown to affect consumer behaviors, including information acquisition and processing, and brand loyalty, while impairing product evaluation (e.g., perceived usefulness) and adoption [22,37].

In the e-service domain, perceived risk has been identified as a major barrier to consumer acceptance [47]. Especially regarding highly sen sitive topics, personal aversion to losses makes investors less likely to use technologies with high perceived risks [2,53]. This also includes the need for financial robo-advisors to build long-term relationships with investors, which can complicate the consumers’ decisions [47]. Studies have found that perceived financial risk has a significant impact on consumer loyalty and product purchase intentions [26,29]. Concerns about privacy risk can trigger consumers’ privacy protection responses, including misrepresentation, negative word of mouth, complaints, and stopping patronage [32]. Therefore, in the case of financial robo-advisor services, we argue that perceived risks (financial risk and privacy risk) affect the investors’ intention to use financial robo-advisors for invest ment purposes. As a result, we propose the following hypotheses:

H4a: Perceived financial risk is negatively related to the investment intention of using financial robo-advisors.

H4b: Perceived privacy risk is negatively related to the investment intention of using financial robo-advisors.

3.3. Uncertainty reduction strategies and investors’ perceived financial benefit

## 4. Method and results

To test the research model in Fig. 2, we used a questionnaire survey method to identify and appropriately adjust items in the available literature by using a seven-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Measurement items of the model are shown in Table A1. The scale of algorithm interpretability was adapted from Kohler ¨ et al. Our survey used the Credamo platform (www.credamo.com) to distribute electronic questionnaires. Credamo is a professional research platform used by many research institutes and companies to collect data. Its data quality is recognized by global research institutions. For the representative population for our research, we recruited participants from across China and set a criterion that participants must use financial robo-advisors (for example, MICAI) to ensure they had relevant

experience.

We collected 367 responses from the Credamo platform and used three methods to filter the questionnaires. First, we emphasized in the questionnaire introduction that the respondents had to have experience using financial robo-advisors, and we designed questions to exclude those who had no experience and who were not serious about their answers. Second, each participant was allowed to take the survey only once; we removed duplicate questionnaires. Third, the platform assisted in screening the sample. Finally, we obtained 307 valid questionnaires from 367 answers from respondents. Among the 307 survey re spondents, males accounted for 49.8% of the responses, whereas females accounted for 50.2%. The majority of respondents who have experi enced financial robo-advisors are between the ages of 18 and 40 years, accounting for 90.9% of the total respondents. ## 4.1. Measurement model test

We analyzed the survey data using Partial Least Squares, performed through SmartPLS 3.3.3 software. Partial Least Squares is suitable for analyzing models with latent variables. Many studies in the information system field use Partial Least Squares and find it to be an effective method of analysis [38]. Following the two-step approach advocated by Anderson and Gerbing [75], we first evaluated the measurement model and then examined the relationship between latent variables in the structural model. To test the measurement model, we tested content validity, convergent validity, and discriminant validity.

To ensure the content validity of the measurement model, we reviewed the relevant literature. After the initial completion of the questionnaire, professors and doctoral students conducted a review test and then revised and improved it. A pre-test independent of the re spondents was then conducted. After the pre-test, respondents completed the questionnaire, in which they were asked if they had any questions about the contents of the questionnaire. The results of the pretest questionnaire were excluded from the main sample to test three types of validity.

Before testing the convergent validity, we tested the reliability of the scale. As shown in Table 1, Cronbach’s alpha value of the corresponding items of each latent variable is higher than 0.70, indicating that the scale has good reliability and high reliability.

Convergent validity shows the correlation of indicators in a construct. To test for convergent validity, we used three metrics: Factor loadings, average variance extracted, and composite reliability. As shown in Table 1, the factor-loading values are all above 0.70, indicating that the item loading is good and has a strong correlation with the corresponding construct. The composite reliability values are also higher than the proposed threshold level of 0.70 [76]. The average variance extracted values of the constructs ranged from 0.61 to 0.72, which satisfies the threshold of average variance extracted values greater than 0.50 for constructs [77].

In terms of discriminant validity testing, we used two techniques. First, we tested the cross-loading values. As shown in Table A3, the cross-loadings are poor on non-corresponding constructs and well on corresponding constructs, indicating that the data have good discrimi nant validity. Second, we used the Fornell-Larcker criterion and compared these construct correlations with the square root of average variance extracted [77]. As shown in Table $\mathrm { A 4 , }$ , the square root of construct average variance extracted is greater than the correlations with other constructs. In other words, the diagonal data is greater than the off-diagonal data, again demonstrating discriminant validity. In addition, we checked for multicollinearity issues by calculating the variance inflation factor. As shown in Table 1, the variance inflation factor ranges from 1.29 to 2.20, which is less than the threshold of 5, and the correlations between variables are all below 0.80 [78]. Thus, we can rule out multicollinearity problems.

## 5.2. Discussion

This study explores how uncertainty reduction strategies affect in vestment intentions. Specifically, we introduce uncertainty reduction theory into the financial robo-advisors context, propose scenarioappropriate uncertainty reduction strategies, and investigate the ef fects of perceived risk and perceived benefit on individual investment decisions using the value-based adoption mechanism. A total of 307 valid data supported our model.

## 5.2.1. Theoretical implications

First, our study extends the value-based adoption model by identi fying perceived risk and perceived benefit in the context of AI-assisted financial investments. In the investment context, risks and benefits are the primary concerns of investors [15,80]. Users who employ financial robo-advisors are also investors, so weighing the risks and benefits of technology adoption is necessary [12]. However, the existing research on financial robo-advisors does not contain any studies that examine technology acceptance from the perspective of perceived value. In this study, we apply a value-based adoption mechanism to the financial robo-advisors scenario by capturing the contextual characteristics of financial robo-advisors and determining the utility of perceived risk and perceived benefit in influencing users’ investment intentions. Our study shows that financial risk, privacy risk, and financial benefit have a sig nificant effect on investment intentions. Privacy risk is the most influ ential factor. Privacy is an important topic for information systems research, and it affects not only individuals’ attitudes but also their acceptance of technology [81]. Financial robo-advisors provide in vestors with accurate and personalized investment plans based on their multidimensional data [2]. Previous studies have ignored users’ privacy concerns in scenarios involving financial robo-advisors. Our study in corporates privacy risks into the value-based adoption model and pro vides a new perspective to explain users’ engagement of financial robo-advisors for investing.

Second, our study introduces a new theoretical perspective, uncer tainty reduction, to explore the factors that influence users’ perceived value and investment intentions and to better understand financial robo advisors. Uncertainty reduction theory has been applied to the context of interacting with platforms to reduce uncertainties in the use of technology [38,42]. When users interact with financial robo-advisors, uncertainty arises because of insufficient technology perceptions or informational differences [43]. Thus, it is important to consider AI-related factors to provide a stable environment for investors. How ever, empirical studies have not explored users’ investment intentions from the perspective of reducing technological uncertainty. Based on uncertainty reduction theory, this study seeks to identify uncertainty reduction strategies in light of three types of information-seeking stra tegies: Active, passive, and interactive. This study identifies three stra tegies for algorithmic interpretability, structural assurance, and interactivity in this new context. These strategies reflect the contextual characteristics of AI-assisted financial investments. Our findings spe cifically analyze these uncertainty reduction strategies in the context of using financial robo-advisors. The empirical results suggest that it is feasible for service providers to use uncertainty reduction theory to reduce uncertainties in personal experience technologies.

Third, this study examines users’ investment intentions in the context of AI-assisted financial investment by capturing the essence of financial investment, which is that investors are expecting risk reduction and focusing on investment returns. Existing studies have not suffi ciently researched investor adoption, use, and interaction with financial robo-advisors [2]. In considering the context-specific elements, we integrate the characteristics of financial robo-advisors into the uncer tainty reduction and risk–benefit framework. This integration bridges the gap in the lack of research on financial robo-advisor adoption from a technology experience perspective. Furthermore, most existing studies on financial robo-advisors have been summarized in terms of their positive attributes, such as expertise, automation, low service costs, and wide audience [2–4]. The benefits, risks, and black boxes that AI brings to humans go hand in hand. Our study validates the context-specific variables theoretically and empirically, both positively and negatively.

## 5.2.2. Practical implications

This study provides new insights into the practices of the financial industry. First, our research indicates that users evaluate whether to invest with financial robo-advisors based on perceived value, which enlightens financial firms of the need to focus on improving users perceived value. The study shows that users measure perceived value by considering perceived risks and perceived benefits and that risk aversion activates investment intentions more than perceived benefits. Therefore, financial institutions must focus more on risk avoidance in AI in vestments, especially the avoidance of privacy risks. Financial in stitutions can explore viable practices to reduce the perceived risks and increase the perceived value of users in the following ways:

(1) Raise awareness of the ethical and legal issues that arise from algorithmic-automated decision-making using individuals’ sen sitive data and inform users of the purposes for which sensitive data are collected, to improve algorithmic interpretability and transparency.

(2) Develop and improve privacy protection regulations and pro visions, create a comprehensive process for privacy protection management and supervision before, during, and after invest ment, actively create a safe investment environment for in vestors, and enhance structural assurance.

(3) Develop systematic publicity and education activities for users to ground the concept of protecting clients’ rights in every aspect of connecting with clients. For example, the user privacy policy should always be prominently placed in the interface of financial robo-advisors for easy access by investors.

Second, our research suggests effective ways to reduce technology uncertainties and boost the business performance of financial firms that provide AI investment services. Service providers can innovate financial robo-advisors by focusing on enhancing algorithmic interpretability, structural assurance, and interactivity through using the following practices:

(1) Breaking the algorithmic black box, improving algorithmic interpretability, ensuring information transparency and clarity of the recommendation process, and explaining to users the ratio nale for recommending investment portfolios to enhance the support of algorithmic models for actual business.

(2) Increasing structural assurance by establishing authentication services and providing digital certificates and third-party gua rantees during the fund payment process. In addition, financial institutions should improve proactive communication with reg ulators and third-party social institutions. For example, financial institutions should regularly report information related to their efforts to protect user privacy, including providing selfassessments and supporting materials to regulators and cooper ating with regulators to execute various tasks.

(3) Increasing the information exchange with users and creating intelligent financial assistants or AI virtual customer service for financial robo-advisors so they can dock to the internal business systems of enterprises, cover various financial service scenarios, such as intelligent recommendations and intelligent queries, and provide consulting services to customers efficiently and effec tively. In addition, service providers should provide smooth user feedback channels, establish an automated complaint manage ment mechanism, deploy machine learning and natural language processing to understand the nature of complaints and related trends, and determine the factors that affect individuals’ use of financial robo-advisors for investment.

Third, our research captures the essence of user investment, which is that it is not enough to simply reduce risks, but also to focus on expected benefits. The perceived benefits examined in this study give financial firms a more detailed understanding and more space for manipulation in attracting clients. Existing studies related to financial robo-advisors have mentioned perceived benefits less frequently. Previous research defines perceived value in terms of user needs and classifies it into economic value (such as monetary savings, efficiency), functional value, emotional value (such as emotions and experience), and social value [17]. This allows service providers to offer interactive interfaces to users from multidimensional value levels using computer-mediated financial robo-advisors in a personalized manner to meet their needs for perceived benefits. This also provides insights and shortcuts for users to better experience and accept financial robo-advisors for investment purposes.

## 6. Conclusion

This study explores the effects of uncertainty reduction strategies on investment intentions and the underlying action mechanism in financial robo-advisor scenarios. To this end, we place the perceived value in AI investment scenarios, explaining the impacts of algorithmic interpret ability, structural assurance, and interactivity as uncertainty reduction strategies on users’ investment intentions using the value-based adop tion mechanism. Specifically, we test the impact of uncertainty reduc tion strategies on users’ perceived financial risk, perceived privacy risk, and perceived financial benefit, and find that these perceptual variables play key roles in investment intentions, thus contributing to the existing value-based adoption model. The results of this study contribute to the theoretical understanding of users’ investment behavior in the scenario of using financial robo-advisors and provide practical guidance to reduce the uncertainty of the usage process, such as providing digital certificates and third-party guarantees in the transaction process.

We admit several limitations to this paper. First, some potential external factors have not been examined in our study, including users economic conditions, personality characteristics, and regulatory changes. In terms of economic conditions, for financial institutions, financial robo-advisors offer significant advantages with improved accessibility, ease of use, and cost reduction, making it appropriate for low-net-worth clients to use them for investment purposes. However, high-net-worth clients have considerable transaction sizes and generate significant profits for financial institutions, so in this case, the focus should be on private banking, supplemented by recommendations from financial robo-advisors, to maintain high returns. Considering personal characteristics, introverts find it challenging to interact with people and may prefer financial robo-advisors, whereas extroverts tend to interact socially and may prefer human advice. Considering the regulatory environment, a more ambitious regulatory environment provides better protection for investors, which promotes investment intention [82]. Therefore, future research could explore how these factors influence users’ investment intentions to use financial robo-advisors.

Second, this study focuses only on the common features of financial robo-advisors and does not distinguish between different types of financial robo-advisors. Financial robo-advisors include a stand-alone app on cell phones and an intelligent advisory function embedded in bank apps. Future research could distinguish between these two types and examine the impact of types of financial robo-advisors on in dividuals’ investment intentions.
