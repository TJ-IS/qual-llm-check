# How does artificial intelligence affect the environmental performance of organizations? The role of green innovation and green culture

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Artificial intelligence use Green innovation Green culture Environmental performance Agribusiness

## A B S T R A C T

Despite the importance of artificial intelligence (AI) in improving environmental performance, the mechanisms and boundary conditions through AI use affects environmental performance remain unclear. Using a sample of Chinese agricultural firms, the empirical results verify the positive impact of AI use on environmental perfor mance via green innovation of product and process. Moreover, our findings reveal that green culture positively moderates the impact of AI use on green product innovation, and it strengthens the positive mediation effect of green product innovation on the relationship between AI use and environmental performance. This study pro: vides insights and enriches the existing understanding of AI-enable green innovation, thus making practical implications for firms to achieve sustainable development.

## 1. Introduction

Environmental problems are important factors that are considered in investment decision making, product development, production, and operation [1,2]. Most entrepreneurs are aware that environmental protection practices play an important role in minimizing operational expenses, enhancing the corporate image, and gaining a sustainable competitive advantage [3,4]. Environmental protection has become a prerequisite for enterprises to augment their competitive advantages and achieve sustainable development, and it is an indispensable part of forward-looking corporate governance [5]. In recent years, firms have faced major environmental problems, such as global warming, resource depletion, waste emissions, and pollution intensification [6]. In terms of practical applications, many firms have used artificial intelligence in the field of environmental pollution control and have achieved success. For instance, in response to environmental problems, Microsoft and Alibaba have introduced technical solutions to mitigate global environmental degradation, enabling intelligent awareness and control of pollution sources at the organizational level. Long-term environmental perfor mance under conditions of optimization of production inputs depends on the implementation of sustainable and adaptive digital technologies and initiatives, to ensure the efficient use of green resources in the un foreseeable future [7]. The use of artificial intelligence is considered an effective way for firms to solve dynamic environmental problems characterized by uncertainty, complexity, and interactions [8]. In contrast to other digital technologies, artificial intelligence enables computer systems to simulate intelligent human behavior, disrupting the way firms traditionally operate and innovate [9]. Artificial intelli gence can help organizations with environmental monitoring and con trol, extracting useful environmental information and insights through machine learning and algorithms, building predictive models to identify potential environmental risks, and using expert systems to support or ganizations with environmental decision making [10]. While artificial intelligence has shown great potential for solving environmental prob lems, most firms lack sufficient knowledge about this emerging tech nology, and the current level of artificial intelligence use in organizations remains low [11]. Therefore, understanding how artificial intelligence can be effectively leveraged to improve environmental performance is critical for achieving firm sustainability.

Scholars have begun to focus on how artificial intelligence use affects environmental sustainability and have provided valuable insights that theoretically demonstrated the potential associations between artificial intelligence use and environmental performance, but the findings are paradoxical——positive and negative [12]. In concrete terms, some researchers have shown that artificial intelligence applied to environ mental forecasting and decision support can have a positive effect on firms’ environmental performance [13]. However, as artificial intelli gence models become more complex, their application generates a carbon footprint and aggravates firms’ environmental pollution [12]. The reason for the divergence of opinions may be that the mechanisms and boundary conditions through which artificial intelligence affects firms’ environmental performance remain ambiguous. This research argues that the association between artificial intelligence use and firms environmental performance is contingent upon the mediating role of green innovation. Green innovation refers to the development of envi ronmentally friendly products and processes that include the use of green raw material and ecological product design to reduce material usage, pollutant emissions, and consumption of energy resources, such as water and electricity [14]. Green innovation can help organizations advance artificial intelligence deployment, catalyze the knowledge coupling of product development and process management, and unleash the full potential of sustainability, thereby enhancing competitiveness in the business environment [15]. While researchers have emphasized the importance of green innovation for digital transformation and sustain ability, there is limited empirical research exploring how artificial in telligence initiatives influence environmental performance through green innovation [16]. Therefore, to unlock the black box between artificial intelligence and environmental performance, there is an urgent need to explore the mediating role of green innovation.

In addition, previous literature on the boundary conditions that artificial intelligence use influences firm performance has primarily focused on environmental dynamism [17–19], green digital learning orientation [20], and organizational flexibility [21], while neglecting the moderating effect of green culture. Green culture is a corporate culture associated with sustainable strategies, which refers to a collec tion of values, symbols, assumptions, and organizational policies reflecting an obligation or desire to operate as an environmentally friendly organization [22]. It blends workflows with green initiatives in a digital context and guides the environmental decision-making process, directly or indirectly influencing firms’ overall environmental efforts [23]. Companies with a strong green culture are well-positioned to harness artificial intelligence for promoting environmentally sustainable changes and to attain the utmost value in green innovation. A culture that lacks support for green initiatives may trigger a situation where firms adopt a reactive rather than proactive stance in addressing envi ronmental issues, leading to lower efficiency in digital innovation and exacerbation of environmental problems [23]. But as far as we know, there is still a lack of research on the role of green culture in enhancing the impact of artificial intelligence on green innovation and ultimately on environmental performance. Considering the importance of green culture and its impact on organizational activities, this study identifies green culture as a boundary condition for artificial intelligence-driven environmental performance mechanisms. This helps us to clarify how green culture moderates the relationship between artificial intelligence use and green innovation, which in turn affects firms’ environmental performance.

To address the inadequacies of previous research, this study uses dynamic capability theory as a lens and proposes a theoretical model that examines the influence of artificial intelligence use on firms’ envi ronmental performance, revealing the mediating role of green innova tion and the moderating role of green culture. The main contributions of this study are threefold. First, it shows the intrinsic mechanism by which artificial intelligence use acts on environmental performance and spec ifies the mediating paths of the green innovation of product and process, thus finding a new explanation for the relationship between artificial intelligence and environmental performance. Second, we find that green culture positively moderates the impact of artificial intelligence use on green product innovation, and it strengthens the positive mediation ef fect of green product innovation on the relationship between artificial intelligence use and environmental performance, thereby providing a nuanced understanding of boundary conditions (i.e., green culture) under which artificial intelligence use exerts its influence on environ mental performance. Third, this study complements the adaptability of dynamic capability theory. It elucidates how firms can transition from lower-order dynamic capabilities (i.e., artificial intelligence use) to higher-order dynamic capabilities (i.e., green innovation) in the sce narios of green culture, which helps us understand the process by which dynamic capabilities function in specific contexts.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

## 2.1. Dynamic capability theory

Teece [24] argued that organizations need to develop the capability of timely responding to market changes, updating their resource struc tures, and reconfiguring internal competencies in order to create new opportunities and gain a competitive advantage in environmental challenges. According to dynamic capability theory, the key to achieving sustainable strategic success for environmentally oriented firms lies in the coordination of value creation and value capture [25]. Firms engage in exploratory or developmental organizational change to reconfigure their core resources and capabilities and are committed to the pursuit of green competitive advantage and environmental governance effects. In the context of environmental sustainability, firms’ environmental per formance is recognized as a salient factor in attaining business competitive advantage [26]. When executing objective management, organizations need to set environmental performance as a priority. As organizations are challenged by data and information overload, it seems not sufficient to develop only common capabilities belonging to man agement, operations and governance [27]. A dynamic capability perspective can help us identify the driving factors to meet stakeholders demand for a cleaner and greener business environment [28].

The dynamic capabilities theory emphasizes the imperative for or ganizations to cultivate dynamic capabilities that enable them to effectively identify, interpret, and learn from vast quantities of data, with the investment and deployment of novel digital technology ini tiatives to rapidly respond to market changes and competitive pressures [29]. Artificial intelligence use can help organizations maximize the utility of the capabilities of sensing, grasping, and reconfiguring in un predictable environments, providing an optional technological capa bility for organizations to balance market response and internal coordination [30]. While artificial intelligence use (a low-order dynamic capability) provides a certain degree of digital flexibility for firms to address market turbulence, there is still a gap in shaping sustained environmental performance advantages, necessitating the cultivation of specific high-level dynamic capabilities [25]. Green innovation repre sents an organizational transformation that spans hierarchical levels and functional areas, effectively integrating internal and external green as sets and other core resources [31]. It motivates firms to promote exploratory innovation to achieve long-term goals, aligning with the characteristics of high-order dynamic capabilities [14]. Green innova tion is necessary to achieve a balance between value creation and cap ture activities and can be seen as an indispensable dynamic capability and medium for improving environmental performance in the digital transformation process [4]. Hence, the effective artificial intelligence deployment accelerates the formation of firms’ green innovation, enabling the continuous capture and creation of green value [32]. This shows the transformation of firms’ low-order dynamic capabilities into high-order ones, which leads to favorable environmental outcomes [33]. In summary, the dynamic capability theory provides a suitable analyt ical framework for this study to explain how artificial intelligence use influences green innovation and subsequent environmental performance.

## 2.2. Artificial intelligence use

Currently, there is no academic consensus on the concept and connotation of artificial intelligence [11]. It is defined as the science of training machines to act like humans, both by collecting and processing large amounts of data and by using various techniques to recognize patterns [34]. Some scholars argue that artificial intelligence is a collection of technology, mathematics, and statistical models and a large number of different types of data, supported by a growing number of applications that attempt to model or surpass the functions of human intelligence. In the information systems (IS) discipline, artificial intel ligence refers to the capabilities of robots to imitate the cognitive pat terns of the human brain and execute anthropomorphic instructions, including perceiving, acquiring knowledge, interacting with the external environment to make decisions, and even demonstrating crea tivity [35]. Others believe that artificial intelligence technology can be understood through the three principles of combination, recursiveness, and phenomena [36]. Combination refers to significantly improving business performance by combining technology with specific functions, such as decision-making tasks and business problems [37]. Recursive ness refers to the improvement of one technique in the technology network of artificial intelligence, which can cause conflicts with other technology design goals [38]. Phenomena means that artificial intelli gence is centered on data-driven learning, in which trained predictive models enable data-driven products and services to learn adaptively and adjust themselves based on feedback data [39]. Integrating both un derlying technical and functional perspectives, we consider previous approaches to define technology use in the literature and describe artificial intelligence use as the ability to correctly interpret and learn from external data, flexibly apply the acquired content, and consciously perform intelligent behaviors, with the aim of achieving specific tasks and goals [40–42].

Previous studies on the impact of artificial intelligence use on firms have focused on the areas of sustainability [9], sales forecasting [43], customer development [44], social responsibility [45], supplier man agement [46], and social media usage [47]. Firms can use artificial in telligence algorithms to consider the interactions between market dynamics and the demand side and to identify organizational decision priorities and policy environments for incorporation into strategic business planning. These initiatives not only optimize production pro cess arrangements but also avoid unnecessary energy consumption, thus helping firms reduce production costs and enhance their competitive advantages [48]. Therefore, artificial intelligence will revolutionize the business environment and how firms are established and managed on a scale far greater than that in the digital era. This change, coupled with exponential internet growth, will affect the way companies operate, the way they sell their products or services, and the way they manage their strategies.

## 2.3. Green innovation

A considerable body of literature has provided definitions of green innovation as the adoption of environmentally friendly materials to reduce negative externalities throughout the product lifecycle or the implementation of green technologies and processes to conserve energy, minimize waste, and decrease pollution, thereby achieving sustainabil ity [49,50]. Building upon this established definition, this study present novel insights into green innovation. Green innovation refers to the development of environmentally friendly products and processes that include the use of green raw material and ecological product design to reduce material usage, pollutant emissions, and consumption of energy resources, such as water and electricity [14]. Firms seek to align envi ronmental and innovation objectives across various stages of product or process, including design, development, manufacturing, promotion, and recycling. By assessing the extent of responsiveness to consumers’ green demands during product development and supporting cross-functional collaboration to drive green transformation in production processes, firms aim to enhance the flexibility and efficiency of executing their green strategies, ultimately bolstering environmental performance and sustaining green competitiveness [51]. Green innovation can be further categorized into two essential constructs: green product innovation and green process innovation [14,52]. Green product innovation refers to the use of nontoxic compounds or biodegradable materials in the pro duction process while changing or modifying product design to reduce the impact on environments and improve energy efficiency [14]. Green process innovation aims to reduce energy consumption in the produc tion process or the recycling process, which include reducing waste gas and wastewater emissions, decreasing water consumption, improving resource and energy efficiency, and switching from fossil fuels to bio energy [14,53,81]

Green product innovation and green process innovation play a crucial role in creating sustainable green value for businesses and meeting environmental legality and industry green standards [54]. However, they differ in emphasis and impact mechanisms. Differenti ating these two types of green innovation is necessary because their strategies and technological directions lead to different weights and priorities in the consideration of innovation activities and decision models for businesses. The purpose of green product innovation is to reduce adverse environmental impacts throughout the process from design to realization of product value, with a design concept that places greater emphasis on environmental assessments of the product lifecycle and consumer green trust than on the general product [55]. Green product innovation requires businesses to focus on environmental protection and energy conservation in areas such as product design, raw material procurement, production processes, and packaging, aiming for transparency of green information on products and achieving green value co-creation with consumer participation [56]. On the other hand, green process innovation focuses more on resource utilization efficiency and environmental pollution during the manufacturing process, with real-time monitoring of energy consumption and emissions, and real izing green and digital process management [57]. Compared to con ventional production processes, green process value creation driven by artificial intelligence enables businesses to integrate digital processes and green concepts to promote continuous innovation practices and reshape firms’ green value chain [58]. In addition, with the significantly increased awareness of sustainable development, firms can use artificial intelligence to manage the scope and speed of environmental issue response and adhere to environmental commitments [7,59]. Therefore, environmentally oriented firms are able to accelerate green knowledge sharing and process adjustments within their organizations, providing green products to the market and enhancing firms’ image [51].

## 2.4. Green culture

Organizational culture is a vital component of enterprise manage ment theory, serving a pivotal role in shaping the value orientation and strategic decision making [60]. It forms the bedrock for nurturing and advancing the core competitiveness of organizations [61]. Green culture is ingrained within organizational structure and influences the strategic trajectory of firms and further impacts decisions related to environ mental behavior and the implementation of green development prac tices, thereby affecting organizations’ sustainable development [62]. Green culture is defined as a collection of values, symbols, assumptions, and organizational policies reflecting an obligation or desire to operate as an environmentally friendly organization [22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that organizations fostering a strong green culture are more likely to adopt proactive environmental strategies and attain su perior environmental performance [22,63]. Consequently, many firms are prioritizing green development as a key strategy for gaining a competitive edge. Prior literature has indicated that a strong green culture can drive the adoption of green management practices within an organization and ultimately enhance the sustainability performance [64]. Importantly, in the digital age, the profound integration of green culture and digital transformation empowers organizations to enhance resource efficiency and develop sustainable products and services, thereby responding to the increasing demand for environmental con servation [63].

## 2.5. Environmental performance

Environmental performance refers to production performance that considers environmental factors [65]. Several indicators can be used to assess environmental performance; some of the most common are con trolling resource use, detecting emissions, and minimizing waste gen eration [66]. In terms of the relationship between firms and the environment, environmental performance exhibits the magnitude in which firms’ production operations influence the environment and as sesses the effectiveness and efficiency by which firms implement envi ronmental governance for environmental protection and sustainable development [67]. There is a consensus among scholars that environ mental performance evaluation should be used to measure sustainability in organizational production processes [68]. Environmental governance determines how firms define, manage, and implement natural resource goals and priorities, as well as how the impacts of employee behavior and decision rules (formal vs. informal) are considered in this process. Nishant et al. [13] argued that artificial intelligence provides firms with opportunities to effectively perceive, make decisions, and perform comprehensive planning that not only reduces the intensity of using energy, water, and land in their production processes but also enable and promotes effective environmental governance at a higher level.

Existing research has shown that environmental performance de pends on the type of main materials and auxiliaries used, energy com ponents, and product development technology, as well as the environmental impact generated by a firm’s operational and adminis trative processes [69]. Green innovation is related to the topic of envi ronmental engagement in corporate governance and helps firms reallocate resources and improve environmental performance [70]. Based on dynamic capability theory, our research helps managers un derstand how artificial intelligence can be used in business practices to achieve dynamic capability upgrades through green innovation, thus resulting in superior environmental performance.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis

## 3.1. The mediating role of green product innovation

In recent years, research on environmental issues has received a lot of attention from managers and academics. In order to demonstrate corporate social responsibility in environmental protection, firms inte grate greening concepts into their product branding, service develop ment, and customer relationship management [71,72]. Firms gain prominent green marketing feedback through intelligence-driven green product development, outperforming their competitors in terms of environmental performance [73,74]. Specifically, artificial intelligence facilitates the integration of green resources and knowledge within firms to develop environmentally friendly products that comply with green standards and custom traceable product eco-labels through big data analytics and intelligent model predictions of consumer data [75]. Firms combine artificial intelligence applications and other ancillary facilities to collect and track carbon emissions and other pollution throughout the lifecycle of green products, which helps firms implement information flow management to make green information about the production and use process transparent, and gain consumers’ green trust in the firms [55,76].

Furthermore, some scholars have indicated that firms’ green product innovation significantly influences environmental performance [77,78]. Firms that excel in green product innovation can better conduct product eco-design as well as green processing of solid wastes and emissions from the production process, which minimizes environmental impacts of business activities, meets market green needs, and achieves environ mental performance higher than the industry average [31]. This not only expands the green marketing business module and gives new products green perceived value, but also alleviates consumers’ concerns about the environment and transforms external pressure into improved firms’ environmental performance [79]. Overall, when firms fully leverage artificial intelligence technologies, they can rapidly respond to consumer preferences for green products and drive the implementation of green product innovation through predictive insights and solutions, thereby reducing resource wastage and emission pollution throughout the product lifecycle from design to recycling. Accordingly, we hy pothesize the following proposition:

Hypothesis 1. (H1): Green product innovation mediates the impact of artificial intelligence use on environmental performance.

## 3.2. The mediating role of green process innovation

Green process innovation integrates the dual concepts of green norms and innovation drive for firms’ production processes and plays an important role in digital environmental governance [80]. Unlike tradi tional process management that favors economic benefits assessment, green process innovation pays more attention to the weight of envi ronmental protection in the target design [57]. Some scholars showed that firms with a comparative advantage in artificial intelligence can better green design and algorithmically optimize their production pro cess [9]. Firms’ artificial intelligence deployment exhibits characteris tics of self-learning, self-adaptation, and self-action in response to external environmental changes, reconstructing the green manufacturing process and aligning current business and digital tech nology modules. thereby adapting to the complexity and comprehensiveness of organizational operations [15]. To achieve green process innovation, top management actively supports investments in artificial intelligence applications within the firms and develops end-to-end intelligent monitoring systems [32,81]. This measure enables early warning of pollution at the production source, as well as utilizing dy namic perception and scientific decision-making capabilities to accu rately identify and record production information such as energy consumption and material waste [4]. Artificial intelligence use can provide firms with solutions to upgrade production processes, optimize medium-term pollution control procedures, and improve the efficiency of green process innovation [15]

In addition, previous research has indicated that when considering how to improve environmental performance, firms need to emphasize the standards and governance methods of green process and incorporate ecological sustainability principles into their operational strategies [82, 83]. Green process innovation can help firms promote the sharing of environmental knowledge and pollution control experience at various stages and improve pollution treatment technology and environmental governance effectiveness, ultimately innovating traditional firms’ pro duction models [80]. Firms that implement green process innovation leverage digital technology advantages to plan optimal production combinations of existing products, accurately allocate production fac tors, and utilize renewable resources, aiming to reduce material waste, energy consumption, and pollution emissions, as well as to lower pro duction costs and achieve competitive environmental performance [49]. In summary, green process innovation is an appropriate medium to help organizations structure their artificial intelligence programs for optimal environmental performance outcomes.

Hypothesis 2. (H2): Green process innovation mediates the effect of artificial intelligence use on environmental performance.

## 3.3. The moderating role of green culture

Previous literature has shown that organizational culture is an important boundary condition for employing digital technologies to develop dynamic capability [84]. Green innovation is an indispensable dynamic capability that can enhance sustainability and contribute to reducing environmental burdens [31]. Green culture influences employee and management perceptions and attitudes towards envi ronmental sustainability, which helps organizations implement environmentally friendly changes in their operations [62]. In this sense, green culture may exert a positive moderating effect on the linkage between artificial intelligence use and green product and process innovation.

A strong green culture is pivotal in shaping the influence of artificial intelligence on green product innovation. First, a strong green culture provides organizational guidance by directing attention towards envi ronmental and sustainability concerns. Organizations can harness arti ficial intelligence to swiftly and efficiently amass and analyze extensive environmental and market data [34]. This will help in obtaining in-depth insights into consumer preferences and market trend, thereby offering valuable inputs for enhancing product innovation [75]. Second, green culture also serves as a driving force, encouraging employees to adopt environmentally conscious approaches to innovation [62], thereby elevating the upper limit of green product innovation. Specif ically, under the auspices of the green culture concept, employees can leverage artificial intelligence to further refine material selection and energy utilization within the product innovation process, with the objective of minimizing resource wastage and reducing environmental impacts [37,85]. This optimization extends to the design and manufacturing processes of green products. Finally, operating within the framework of a strong green culture, organizations can employ artificial intelligence to monitor and optimize the green product innovation. This entails ensuring that the production and delivery of products conform to environmental standards [49,86], thereby advancing the sustainability and transparency of green product innovation. Hence, we propose that:

Hypothesis 3a. (H3a): Green culture positively moderates the rela tionship between artificial intelligence use and green product innovation.

Organizations with a strong green culture will integrate environ mental protection as a central tenet of their strategic framework. Such green culture significantly influences the strategic deployment of arti ficial intelligence, leading to the reduction of resource wastage, enhanced operational efficiency, and a more substantial role in sup porting advancements in green process innovation [14]. First, green culture advocates data-driven environmental decisions, within which the utilization of artificial intelligence bestows a range of multidimen sional advantages. It enables organizations to leverage the wealth of data generated by their operational processes to yield insightful and effective decisions [35], thereby facilitating the refinement of their green processes. Second, green process innovation, though often entailing higher implementation costs, has consistently demonstrated superior efficacy when compared to alternative green practices [49]. A strong green culture serves as a compelling driver for organizations to embrace artificial intelligence usage in the realm of process innovation experimentation and monitoring. Artificial intelligence emerges as a pivotal instrument in this context, facilitating simulations, experiments, and real-time monitoring of operational processes, consequently aiding in the prediction and oversight of the outcomes of green process en hancements [36,87]. This strategic alignment not only yields cost sav ings but also elevates the upper limits of green process innovation. Therefore, we propose that:

Hypothesis 3b. (H3b): Green culture positively moderates the rela tionship between artificial intelligence use and green process innovation.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Questionnaire design

The survey comprised basic details on agricultural businesses and items pertaining to four variables, which were gauged by adopting a seven-item Likert scale. The measurements were all derived from existing studies and adapted to the setting of an agribusiness to suit the needs of this investigation (refer to Appendix Table A1). Artificial in telligence use was modified from Bag et al. [21], while green product innovation and green process innovation were adapted from Yuan and Cao [14]. Green culture was measured by seven items adapted from Umrani et al. [22]. Environmental performance was assessed using a scale adjusted from Huang and Li [4]. To guarantee the accuracy of concepts and the validity of the scale, this study used forward–backward translation techniques. The Chinese translation of the scale was completed by two translators who were fluent in both Chinese and En glish, and the wording of the measurement was fine-tuned to ensure accuracy according to the Chinese cultural background and Chinese language conventions. Two graduate students translated the Chinese questionnaire into English separately and deliberated on disparities until they arrived at a satisfactory consensus. Subsequently, a pretest questionnaire was administered to 20 agribusiness managers using artificial intelligence. A formal questionnaire was ultimately developed after the scale was revised based on the feedback received, resulting in more accurate content.

## 4.2. Collection of the sample’s data

This study used a questionnaire to collect the data of the sample. It considered three factors in selecting Chinese agricultural firms as research subjects. First, as important parts of emerging agribusiness entities, agricultural firms are the foundation for the steady develop ment of China’s national economy and have great significance in pro moting agricultural and rural modernization and rural revitalization. Especially against the backdrop of the COVID-19 pandemic, guaran teeing national food security and maintaining the adequate provision of essential agricultural products are of the utmost importance, and agri cultural firms have made indispensable contributions in this regard. Second, while China’s agricultural firms are growing rapidly, they face many obstacles, such as weak independent research and development capabilities, short value chains for core technologies, low resource input–output ratios, and deterioration of environmental quality. As these problems severely undermine the capabilities of Chinese agri businesses to respond to environmental problems and potential risks, adopting a sustainable development approach is urgently needed. Third, resource scarcity and environmental pollution have further strained the relationship between firms and the environment, and Chinese agri businesses have become aware of the crucial role of sustainability in business activities. Exploring the mechanisms and pathways of sus tainable development in Chinese agribusinesses can provide theoretical guidance and lessons for firms in developing countries and lessdeveloped regions.

The survey participants in our research were top or middle-level managers of Chinese agribusinesses because they had a more compre hensive understanding of their firms’ digital transformation initiatives, program implementation, resource allocation, and business benefits. First, we signed a collaboration arrangement with a renowned market research agency, which has a representative list and the contact infor mation of the target firms. Next, the survey agency sent an email con taining our questionnaire and a cover letter to the participants who met the study criteria. Ultimately, a total of 234 questionnaires were administered for two months, and four invalid responses were removed, resulting in 230 samples eligible for empirical examination. Table 1 shows the firms’ descriptive statistics. Most of the sample (68.3%) was composed of companies with fewer than 500 employees, while firms with more than 500 emplovees made up 31.7% of the sample. In terms of ownership structure, private enterprises accounted for 70.0% and non private enterprises accounted for 30.0%. Regarding the years of digital transformation, 66.1% of the agribusinesses reported more than 3 years of digital transformation, and 33.9% reported less than 3 years. Table 2 shows the characteristics of the respondents: 53.9% were male, 46.1% were female, and 85.2% were between 26 and 35 years old. The survey found that 95.7% of the participants had completed at least a bachelor’s degree, with 59.1% and 40.9% of the respondents serving as middlelevel and top managers, respectively.

## 5. Empirical tests and analysis results

This study leveraged partial least-squares path modeling (PLS-PM) to empirically examine the proposed research model, as the PLS estimator is the optimal method for estimating the composite model [88]. In recent decades, the PLS-PM method has gradually emerged as a mainstream estimation technique and has been widely applied in the field of information systems research [89]. By implementing bootstrap testing, this estimator is capable of evaluating the overall goodness of fit of the model. Moreover, PLS-PM can handle the empirical problems of three models (e.g., reflection models, causal formation models, and composite formation models), and we can further examine the research model’s fit and robustness using this analytical method. We used ADANCO 2 soft ware for the evaluation [90]. Because all the constructs of this model are theoretically derived from firm-made concepts, they were operational ized as composite constructs [89].

## 5.1. Common method bias test

Given that the data collection process utilized self-reporting ques tionnaires for sampling, our survey results may be subject to common method biases. To address this foreseeable concern, three measures were implemented in the survey design to minimize the potential for common method biases in this study. First, we provided a detailed introduction at the beginning of the questionnaire to explain the concept of artificial intelligence use, which assisted respondents in giving accurate answers.

Second, we randomized the order of measurement items in the ques tionnaire, preventing respondents from identifying causal relationships between constructs. Finally, we ensured the anonymity and confiden tiality of the information collected. Also, we used a marker variable technique to examine potential effects of common method biases results in order to demonstrate the validity and reliability of the data analysis [91]. As respondents’ age is theoretically unrelated to the key variables, this study chose respondents’ age as a marker variable. Then, we compared the difference between the two models before and after the marker variable was added. In Table $^ { 3 , }$ marker variable addition doe not influence the significance of the two model paths. The mean corre lation between the primary observed variables and the age of the re spondents was smaller than 0.100. This finding suggests the absence of common method bias in our statistical and evaluative procedures.

## 5.7. Endogeneity testing

This study used two-stage least squares (2SLS) and instrumenta variable methods to correct for any potential endogeneity. Several studies have indicated that firms tend to integrate artificial intelligence into their routine business procedures [95,96]. Drawing on the approach of selecting instrumental variables from existing research [97], we selected routine use of artificial intelligence as our instrumental vari able. According to the principles of instrumental variable selection, instrumental variables must meet the conditions of relevance and exo geneity. Thus, we have taken the following factors into account. First, routine use of artificial intelligence, the extent to which artificial intel ligence is continuously employed in regular business, influences the choices that organizations and employees make about artificial intelli gence for specific problem settlement and innovation, thus being asso ciated with artificial intelligence use. Second, routine use of artificial intelligence primarily addresses structured tasks within firms, whereas the process of improving firms’ environmental performance often in volves resolving unstructured problems. This suggests that routine use of artificial intelligence is not directly related to firms’ environmental performance. The first-stage estimation results of the 2SLS model show that the potential instrumental variable has a significant relationship with artificial intelligence use $( \beta ~ = ~ 0 . 4 7 7 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ but is not significantly related to firms’ environmental performance $( \beta = 0 . 0 3 0 , p$ $> 0 . 1 )$ . The first-stage F statistic is 51.58 (higher than the threshold of 10), and the weak instrumental variable test reports a Cragg–Donald Wald F statistic of 60.81 (higher than the critical value of 16.38 at the 10% significance level), both indicating the absence of weak instru mental variable issues. The second-stage estimation results reveal a positive correlation between artificial intelligence use and environ mental performance $( \beta = 0 . 6 0 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Therefore, endogeneity is not a major concern.

## 5.8. Robustness testing

This analysis used an alternative estimation approach to assess the three additional models, and these were also contrasted with the base line model. The first alternative model operationalized first-order structural green product innovation and green process innovation as second-order structural green innovation. All variables of the second alternative model were reflective rather than composite because subjectivity gave rise to the possibility that the construct specifications were inconsistent with those of some researchers. The third model to be tested characterized the four variables as reflective analysis objects on the basis of the first alternative method, combining the two steps of modifications indicated above. We conducted path significance analysis for the three alternative models proposed, and the results remained consistent with the research model. The evaluation data suggest that adjustments to variable specifications do not have a substantial effect on the analysis results. Furthermore, the empirical results (Appendix Table A2) demonstrate that the SRMR and ${ \tt d } _ { \tt U L S }$ of the alternative model fail and the overall estimated fits of the alternative models are not sta tistically better than those of the baseline model. Therefore, the model envisioned in this study proves to be correct. In conclusion, the test of robustness statistically enhances the reliability of the empirical results.

## 6. Discussion

This investigation sought to clarify the relationship between artificial intelligence use and firms’ environmental performance. Based on dy namic capability theory, our research illustrates the influence and mechanism of artificial intelligence use on firms’ environmental per formance, as mediated by green innovation. The empirical findings show that green product innovation and green process innovation serve as mediating roles in the process by which artificial intelligence use promotes firms’ environmental performance. We also find that green culture positively moderates the relationship between artificial intelli gence use and green product innovation. Moreover, we find that in or ganizations with a high level of green culture, the indirect effect of green product innovation on the relationship between artificial intelligence use and environmental performance is stronger compared to that of organizations with a low level of green culture. These results provide new evidence for a key research topic in the information systems com munity, namely the impact of artificial intelligence on environmental performance and the discovery of mediating mechanisms that influence these relationships.

First, we find that green product innovation mediates the impact of artificial intelligence use on firms’ environmental performance. The results indicate a significant indirect effect of green product innovation $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , supporting H1. Previous studies have preferred to adopt institutional theory, stakeholder theory, or resource-based view theory to illustrate how green product innovation emerges and develops under the influence of internal and external factors, ultimately improving firm performance [49,98,99]. However, this research has not adequately addressed the internal black box that exists between digital technology use and firms’ environmental performance. And this study uses the dynamic capability theory as a lens to explore the mediating mechanism through which firms use artificial intelligence to develop green products and respond to stakeholders’ demands to enhance environmental performance. According to the findings, the imple mentation of green product innovation presents a proactive solution that yields outstanding environmental performance for firms using artificial intelligence. This finding can be explained by the fact that green product innovation can help firms predict consumers’ green demand under the artificial intelligence model, monitor the adverse impact of products on the environment in real time, establish a green marketing feedback system, and improve firms’ environmental performance [57,77]. Consequently, our research findings contribute to a better understand ing of how green product innovation acts on the link between artificial intelligence use and environmental performance within firms.

Second, we reveal the mediating role of green process innovation in the relationship between artificial intelligence usage and environmental performance. The indirect effect of green process innovation is signifi cant $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , providing statistical support for H2. In recent years, scholars have increasingly recognized green process innovation as a crucial factor in enhancing operational efficiency and environmental governance for firms [81,82]. In the context of envi ronmental governance and sustainability, green process innovation is often considered as a mediating variable between antecedents and firm performance [101,102]. Moreover, some scholars have discussed the boundary conditions of the direct impact of green process innovation on environmental or financial performance [100]. However, very few studies have explored artificial intelligence use as a driving factor to promote firms’ green process innovation. In contrast to such IS research, this study elucidates how artificial intelligence use influences environ mental performance through the implementation of green process innovation within organizations, highlighting the vital mediating role of green process innovation. These findings reveal that green process innovation can assist firms using artificial intelligence in addressing internal coordination issues related to environmental governance, enabling them to standardize their production lines and manufacturing processes according to greener standards, and to learn approaches for digital transformation and green development. Thus, firms that use artificial intelligence facilitate green process innovation by aligning available resources and capabilities, thus leading to favorable outcomes to sustainable development.

Third, we reveal the moderating role of green culture in the linkage between artificial intelligence usage and green product innovation. The moderating effect of green culture is significant $( \beta = 0 . 1 0 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 ) ,$ providing statistical support for H3a. Furthermore, our analysis of moderated mediation indicates that high-level green culture strengthens the effect of artificial intelligence use on environmental performance via green product innovation. Compared to low and moderate levels of green culture, the indirect effect of green product innovation is larger at a high level of green culture. Our findings extend the research of Li and Lin [63], who demonstrate that green culture positively moderates the impact of digital transformation on environmental performance via green capability, and further highlight the critical role of green culture in the context of green development practices. Regarding the insignifi cant moderating effect on the impact of artificial intelligence use on green process innovation $( \beta = - 0 . 0 8 8 , n s ) _ { \scriptscriptstyle ; }$ , a possible explanation is that organizations are more likely to use artificial intelligence to support the design and production of green products to strengthen the green image of the organizations and meet environmental requirements of green orientation $[ 5 6 , 1 0 3 ]$ . Green culture is typically more prominently manifested at the product level due to its higher visibility [49]. Differing from green product innovation, green process innovation requires the systematic improvement of the whole operational and managerial pro cess and primarily concerns the technological and efficiency enhance ments within production processes [104]. The optimization and improvement of green process innovation often entail substantial costs and are time-consuming, while exhibiting return uncertainty. When utilizing artificial intelligence to enhance the green process innovation, organizations often give precedence to optimizing resource efficiency. Although these endeavors may incidentally lead to a reduction in environmental impacts, their principal motivation typically centers on economic advantages rather than environmental considerations [105]. Consequently, despite the importance of a green culture, in the context of artificial intelligence’s influence on green process innovation, resource efficiency and economic factors may take precedence over environmentally friendly orientation.

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions

First, theoretically speaking, the literature on artificial intelligence applied to sustainability covers different disciplines and fields and ad dresses environmental challenges. Nishant et al. [13] reviewed the application of artificial intelligence to energy conservation and renew able energy from 2015 to 2019, with primary emphasis on solving specific problems and providing technical solutions. However, few studies have leveraged IS theory to unpack the “black box” of artificial intelligence use in improving firms’ environmental performance, sug gesting that there may be as-yet-undiscovered mediating variables. Artificial intelligence is considered a promising and potentially disrup tive digital technology, and its impacts on environmental performance may occur directly or indirectly in various ways [106], which require the selection of an appropriate lens to elucidate the rationale. However, the internal mechanism involved remains unclear. In this study, we respond to the call for further investigating the potential mediating ef fects of green innovation in the context of technological capability development [50]. The findings indicate that the effect of artificial in telligence use on firms’ environmental performance is mediated by both green product innovation and green process innovation. This viewpoint offers a plausible explanation for the inconsistent findings on the effect of artificial intelligence on environmental governance in previous research. The current study provides insights into the underlying mechanism of how artificial intelligence use affects environmental performance and contributes to our understanding of the link between these two variables through the green innovation of product and process.

Second, green culture is one of the most crucial elements in obtaining environmental performance through use of the green approach [63, 107]. However, the moderating role of green culture in reinforcing the relationship between artificial intelligence and green innovation has rarely been discussed. Our study sheds new light on artificial intelli gence business value by identifying the moderating role of green culture in leveraging artificial intelligence to enhance environmental perfor mance through green product innovation. Specifically, green culture positively moderates the impact of artificial intelligence use on green product innovation. Moreover, the positive mediation effect of green product innovation on the relationship between artificial intelligence use and environmental performance is stronger in organizations with a high level of green culture than in organizations with a low level of green culture. The results elucidate that the impact of artificial intelli gence use is contingent upon the level of green culture, thereby high lighting the critical role of green culture as a boundary condition. Overall, our study contributes to IS research by offering a nuanced un derstanding of the boundary conditions (i.e., green culture) under which artificial intelligence use exerts its influence on environmental performance.

Third, the research complements the applicability of dynamic capability theory and leverages this perspective to illustrate how arti. ficial intelligence use, under the moderating effect of green culture, promotes firms’ environmental performance by conducting the green innovation of product and process. Despite previous studies that examined the influence of green innovation on environmental perfor mance [2], there has been limited consideration of the relationship be tween artificial intelligence use and green innovation, as well as discussion of boundary conditioning. The findings suggest that the use of artificial intelligence positively influences the green innovation of product and process (Table 5), implying that artificial intelligence use as a lower-order dynamic capability at the business level can facilitate firms’ green governance activities, sustainable business designs, and environmentally friendly production inputs to develop green innovation (i.e., a higher-order dynamic capability). It is worth noting that this digitally driven transformation of dynamic capabilities requires a cata lyst. Specifically, the impact of artificial intelligence use is contingent upon the level of green culture as a significant situational factor. This study identifies the boundary conditions that can help in further un derstanding how dynamic capabilities function in a particular context, thereby increasing the applicability of the theory.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

Our study has three practical implications for the managers of firms. First, to thrive in the digital age, firms need to leverage artificial intel ligence technologies to accelerate the shift from the traditional devel opment model to a digitally driven sustainable model. This requires managers to prioritize artificial intelligence technologies when dealing with the relationship between firms and environment, to develop arti ficial intelligence plans relevant to environmental sustainability, and to reduce business activities that have a negative impact on the environ ment [15]. In practical applications, firms such as Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Salesforce, and IBM are beginning to invest more in artificial intelligence technologies, providing infrastructure for artificial intelli gence services in the cloud and facilitating access to and use of cognitive technologies [108]. These firms use artificial intelligence technologies to make decisions that are consistent with sustainability goals, reshape structured and unstructured processes, and efficiently perform various greening tasks to identify and respond to real environmental problems and potential risks. Therefore, managers should prioritize the estab lishment of advanced digital platforms and highlight the cultivation of artificial intelligence managerial and technical talents within an orga nization. Furthermore, firms should enhance their abilities to perceive insights and interpret environmental information with the help of arti ficial intelligence, integrate internal and external green resources, and improve operation efficiency and business agility by creating new methods or improving existing ones in order to continuously improve their degrees of green innovation.

Second, as green innovation mediates the impact of artificial intel ligence use on firms’ environmental performance, managers need to focus on the development of green innovation of product and process. Green innovation can help firms reduce production costs, respond quickly to the environment, and meet the demands of stakeholders, with the aim of enhancing their competitiveness in volatile markets [109]. For instance, with increasing consumer concern about the environment, Mengniu, a Hong Kong-listed agribusiness, has begun to adopt the latest green development concepts, integrate environmental issues into its goals, practices, and strategies, and invest significant amounts of money, manpower, and resources in green innovation. Regarding the develop ment of green product innovation, organizations are suggested to pro actively invest in the research and development of both environmental technologies and artificial intelligence to predict customer demand for eco-friendly products within the market environment, thereby ascer taining the direction for innovative green product development to ensure alignment with market needs. Within the organizational frame work, companies should establish appropriate incentive structures aimed at encouraging outstanding contributions from employees involved in green product innovation, thereby stimulating their poten tial for innovation in this domain. In terms of interorganizational collaboration, organizations should reinforce partnerships with research institutions, environmental organizations, and technology providers to collectively drive green product innovation, with knowledge and resource sharing expediting the innovation process. Regarding the development of green process innovation, organizations are suggested to integrate artificial intelligence into the optimization of green process innovation to distill insights from the voluminous amount of data emanating from operational processes [13], thereby enabling the pin pointing of potential avenues for improvement. Moreover, it is imper ative to cultivate synergistic collaborations among disparate departments within an organization and promote cooperative relation ships between distinct entities to collectively propel the augmentation and optimization of green processes, consequently fostering the expe ditious enhancement of process efficiency and the reduction of resource wastage [63]. Finally, organizations should institute a proficient moni toring and assessment system to evaluate the environmental ramifica tions of green products or green processes, thereby enhancing the upper boundaries of green process innovation and green product innovation to improve environmental performance.

Third, the results indicate that green culture strengthens the effect of artificial intelligence on environmental performance via green product innovation. To effectively leverage the use of artificial intelligence in enhancing environmental performance, organizations should prioritize the cultivation of a strong green culture. Specifically, from a strategic vantage point, it is paramount for senior executives to prioritize envi ronmental sustainability as a central core objective and to seamlessly incorporate an eco-conscious orientation into the framework of their organizational strategies [107]. Furthermore, organizations ought to delineate explicit green development goals and take proactive measures to invest in and orchestrate the allocation of resources necessary for the execution of environmentally friendly practices. In this way, organiza tions can foster a strong green culture through continuous environ mentally friendly practices. From an operational vantage point, organizations should furnish their employees with training in the realm of green culture and environmentally friendly technologies. This is vital to enhance their awareness and skills, enabling better implementation of green strategies and policies within organizations. Furthermore, orga nizations should establish an enabling structure for internal green development, with the provision of incentives to reward employees for their active contributions to fostering a green culture and achieving green innovation.

## 6.3. Research limitations and future research

Because of the complexity of the research questions, this study has a few unavoidable limitations. First, we collected data from Chinese ag ribusinesses, which allowed us to control for cross-industry confounding but may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future research could incorporate samples from industrial manufacturing and internet com panies to ensure a more comprehensive understanding of the broader implications of artificial intelligence for environmental performance across multiple sectors. Second, although a range of effective measures and tests are available to reduce the bias introduced by subjective re ports, a secondary data analysis combined with causal inference could be a better approach to test the proposed relationship in future research. Finally, firms’ environmental performance may be affected by the external environment (e.g., carbon emission policies and competitive turbulence). Additional studies in the future are warranted to consider the role of the external environment and demonstrate how artificial intelligence affects organizations’ environmental performance under the influence of the external environment.
