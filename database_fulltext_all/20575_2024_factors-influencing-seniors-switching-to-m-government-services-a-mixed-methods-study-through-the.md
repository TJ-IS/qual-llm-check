# Factors influencing seniors’ switching to m-government services: A mixed-methods study through the lens of push-pull-mooring framework

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: M-government services Mixed-methods Seniors Switching intention Push-pull-mooring Urban-rural comparison

## A B S T R A C T

Based on the push-pull-mooring framework, this study applies mixed methods to examine the key factors affecting seniors' switching from offline to m-government services. A qualitative study was conducted to identify five factors of the push-pull-mooring framework, and a subsequent quantitative study used a hybrid SEM-ANN method to analyze data from 345 senior Chinese users. Results showed that transparency greatly impacts se niors’ satisfaction with m-government services. Perceived inefficiency significantly affects dissatisfaction with offline services, and inertia increases switching costs. However, perceived government investment does not significantly affect seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services. An urban-rural comparison yielded addi tional findings.

## 1. Introduction

Rapid developments in information technology and the popularity of smartphones, coupled with the impact of COVID-19, have pushed gov ernment sectors to increase the use of mobile technologies and devices (e.g., wireless networks, smartphones, tablet computers) to provide services to the public [1]. This phenomenon, called mobile government (m-government), can effectively improve communication efficiency, reduce operation costs, increase fairness and transparency, reach a wider population, and provide personalized services to users [2,3]. Currently, m-government has been launched in many countries around the world [4]. According to the United Nations E-Government Survey in 2020, the number of countries offering government services via mobile apps or short-message services has increased by 38 % since 2018 [5]. With the strong support of national and local governments in China, almost all provincial-level governments have established m-government apps, such as Zheliban, Suishenban, Beijingtong, Jinxinban, and Gan futong, to promote rapid information exchange within government agencies and provide citizens with a convenient way to obtain services [6].

Meanwhile, population aging is becoming a major concern for gov ernments worldwide [7]. According to a report published by the United Nations, senior people aged 60 and above account for approximately 11 % of the world’s population, and this proportion is expected to increase to 16.5 % by 2030 [5]. Compared with younger groups, seniors need more personalized support and assistance from the government because sensory input dulls over time, resulting in cognitive dysfunction and physiological impairment [7]. Obtaining government services, such as those for healthcare, medical insurance, and aging care, is important for senior citizens; however, in modern society, seniors cannot bypass on line portals [8]. Hence, when aging meets digitalization, how to help seniors integrate into a digital society and enjoy the convenience of digital life has become a very important issue for both government and society [7]. However, prior studies found that seniors’ use of new technologies is far below expectations [9,10]. There is a call for authors in the information system (IS) discipline to further investigate infor mation technology (IT) use among seniors [11], whose needs for digi talized government services should not be ignored [7,12,13].

China has the world’s largest senior population [6]. National statistics show that a total of 267 million people aged 60 or older lived in China by the end of 2021, accounting for 18.9 % of the total population [14]. With the accelerated growth of the senior population, Chinese local government agencies have taken seniors’ needs as an important consideration when developing m-government applications [6]. For instance, to allow seniors to better enjoy the convenience of digital life, Zheliban, the provincial m-government application developed by

Zhejiang Province, supports a dedicated “senior mode” with a special ized interface that can be activated with just one click. The senior-mode interface has an optimized visual design and is more concise and elder-friendly, with large fonts, high contrast, and minimalist interac tion to enhance readability for seniors. Also, to better meet senior citi zens’ daily needs, the senior mode emphasizes the display of high-frequency services in the lives of seniors, such as senior care ser vices, medical insurance payments, and preferential treatment certifi cation for seniors. Furthermore, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology in China released the “Special Action for Internet Application Adaptation to Aging and Barrier-Free Reconstruc tion.” The first batch of 217 websites and apps closely related to seniors lives have undergone an aging-friendly transformation, focusing on eight categories of high-frequency service scenarios involved in the daily lives of seniors [15]. In sum, the Chinese government has made many efforts to help seniors enter digital society.

In addition, as the COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated the overall popularization of digital technology throughout society, the digitally marginal group represented by seniors has actively or passively started a new digital life [16]. To limit the movement and gathering of people outside and effectively prevent the spread of viruses and the possibility of infection, many local governments strengthened online approval services and vigorously implemented no-contact services through m-government apps, applets, and government websites for the public to handle business and obtain government services without leaving home [6]. Although the pandemic impelled seniors to learn how to use m-government services and local governments have made many efforts to make m-government apps more friendly for seniors, less than half of senior netizens have switched to using the m-government services that they first adopted during the pandemic [17]. That is, more than half of them resist switching from offline services to m-government.

Switching is an interesting and critical topic that has not been well investigated thus far [18]. Switching between services refers to the ac tion whereby users cease or significantly reduce the use of their current service while replacing all or a substantial portion of it with an alter native service [19,20]. That is, when users are facing multiple services, they can end the current service and start using a new one or they can stay active through different services. Whether or not switching occurs depends on whether users are indeed more active via the new services and use previous services less frequently [21]. For instance, Hou and Shiau [19] discussed users’ switching between social network services in their study of users who have become less active on Facebook and more active on Instagram. According to Ye and Potter [22], switching inten tion is defined as a tendency to reduce the quantity or quality of a particular information technology over time. Based on prior studies, in the context of the coexistence of offline government service and m-government service, for this study, we define switching intention as seniors’ increased usage of m-government services and gradually decreased usage of offline government services. As a result, if users have high switching intentions, m-government services are more likely to be used than offline services.

Previous studies found a more prominent digital divide among se niors in terms of new technology skills and experience than among younger citizens [23], making them less willing to use mobile-based services in general. To ensure universally equitable access to govern ment services, Chinese governments provide offline windows (in par allel with and complementary to m-government services) for seniors to obtain government services [24]. Considering the coexistence of offline and m-government service channels, we aim to address the following important and understudied research question in this study: What fac tors enable (or inhibit) senior citizens to switch from offline to m-gov ernment services?

This study attempted to examine factors influencing seniors’ switch from offline to m-government services through the theoretical lens of push-pull-mooring (PPM) schema [25]. It posits that migration results from the combined effects of “push” and “mooring” forces from the origin and “pull” forces from the destination [26]. While pull factors attract individuals to a particular place or to use a particular service or product [27], push factors drive or force individuals to leave their place of origin [28] and mooring forces refer to personal and social factors that may prompt migrants to leave or stay at their origin [25]. Originating from human migration theory [29], PPM has been applied in studies that treated users as “residents” who use one channel and possibly migrate to another channel when it becomes a better alternative [21]. For instance, Yusfiarto et al. [30] adopted PPM as a framework to discuss users switching from cash to m-payments. In the current study, the m-gov ernment app and offline government service windows represent two service channels. When seniors switch between offline and m-govern ment, they analogically “migrate” between different channels. That is, their switching behavior can be treated as migration between different service channels; hence, a human migration model such as PPM is relevant for analyzing seniors’ switching between offline and m-gov ernment services. Indeed, PPM is one of the most broadly employed theoretical frameworks for understanding users’ service-switching be haviors [31]. It has been adapted to explore switching behaviors in IS with various technological artifacts, such as instant messaging services [32], blog services [33], mobile payments [34], and mobile shopping [35]. However, few studies in the IS literature have empirically exam ined the push, pull, and mooring factors that influence seniors’ switch ing to m-government.

To address this research gap, we employed a sequential mixed methods design, consisting of a qualitative study (Study 1) and a quantitative study (Study 2) to develop and validate a research model of the push, pull, and mooring factors affecting seniors’ switching to mgovernment. In Study 1, a semi-structured interview was used to identify the push, pull, and mooring factors for understanding seniors’ switching to m-government. After, in Study 2, a multi-analytical hybrid structural equation modeling-artificial neural network (SEM-ANN) method was used to empirically analyze how these factors influence seniors’ inten tion to switch to m-government services. Our findings contribute to theory and practice in several respects. First, we contribute to service switching research by incorporating contextual variables, such as perceived government investment, transparency of m-government ser vice, and inertia, into the PPM framework to analyze their effects on senior users’ switching intention toward m-government services. Second, a literature review shows that few studies have applied PPM to explore why users switch from offline to m-government services. There is even less research on seniors’ conversion behavior. Therefore, thi study expands the application of PPM to seniors’ m-government service context. Third, most previous studies generally regarded dissatisfaction, alternative attractiveness, and switching cost as push, pull, and mooring factors, respectively [21], leaving unclear the mechanisms through which push, pull, and mooring factors may affect seniors’ switching behavior. This study reveals the push, pull, and mooring mechanisms underlying seniors’ switch to m-government and uncovers the specific antecedent variables in each mechanism. Fourth, this study explores urban-rural differences in how some PPM factors (such as perceived government investment) affect seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services, yielding additional findings for extending PPM. Finally, as understanding the factors influencing seniors’ switching behavior is important for government agencies to improve their services, our find ings are expected to provide government agencies with a better under standing of seniors’ needs to improve and optimize service offerings.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Technology use among seniors

With accelerated population aging, governments have sought rele vant measures to prolong the active lives of seniors, allowing them to stay economically and socially independent. Technology is one such approach [36]. To gain insights into this issue, we collated the main studies related to technology use among seniors (see Appendix A). The existing literature on seniors’ technology use primarily focuses on two contexts. The first strand of work, which is more prevalent, revolves around the contexts of social network services [37,38]. For example, based on the technology acceptance model [39,40], Braun [41] explores the factors that influence the adoption of social networking websites among seniors through a questionnaire. These findings reveal that the frequency of internet use, perceived usefulness, and trust of social networking websites emerged as significant predictors of adoption, whereas ease of use and social pressures do not demonstrate significant effects. However, Pfeil et al. [42] argue that uncertainty regarding the identity and intentions of other users may cause individuals to be hesi tant to engage in online communities and participate in social media. Although initial adoption is a crucial first step toward achieving social network services success, actual success is demonstrated by continued usage rather than first-time use. Liu [43] investigated older adults continuance intention toward social network sites and found that satisfaction has the most significant effect on older adults’ continuance intention, followed by perceived usefulness, attitude, subject norm, and perceived behavioral control. Recently, Cao et al. [44] employed the expectation-confirmation model to discuss the interplay between cognitive and emotional factors during the formation of seniors’ inten tion to continue using social networking sites. The model identified several influential factors on continuance intention, ranked in descending order of effect: emotional attachment, perceived usefulness, satisfaction, confirmation of expectations, and perceived ease of use.

The second strand of work focuses on the specific context of healthcare technology $[ 2 4 , 4 5 , 4 6 ]$ . Prior researchers have reported that Internet use can improve seniors’ quality of life, mental health, and life satisfaction [47,48]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Banskota et al. [49] summarized various applications that address physical and cogni tive limitations and have the potential to improve seniors’ quality of life, especially during social distancing or self-quarantine. Despite the po tential benefits, there are challenges and barriers for seniors in adopting mobile health services. Guo et al. [50] investigated the enablers and the inhibitors of seniors’ mobile health service adoption behavior and spe cifically focused on two prevalent characteristics among seniors: tech nology anxiety and dispositional resistance to change. Deng et al. [24] provide valuable insights into the factors influencing the adoption of mobile health services among different demographic groups (e.g., senior and middle-aged citizens). Similarly, Zhao et al. [51] conducted a meta-analysis revealing specific moderating effects of age groups on the adoption of mobile health services and found that perceived ease of use, perceived vulnerability, and perceived severity are important factors impacting the use of mobile health services by middle-aged and senior citizens.

However, extant literature has rarely focused on the context of m government. One rare example is Talukder et al. [7], which explored the enablers and inhibitors affecting seniors’ willingness to adopt m-gov ernment in Bangladesh. The findings emphasize that performance ex pectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating condition, and social influence significantly influence seniors’ intention to adopt m-government ser vices. To gain a deeper understanding of users’ in-depth views and opinions on these issues, the authors also encourage adopting mixed-methods approaches consisting of both quantitative and quali tative studies. In addition, only a few studies have focused on seniors switching behavior. For instance, Nguyen et al. [52] and Lai and Wang [53] both discuss why seniors switch between digital media. As senior users worldwide increasingly use m-government services, it is crucial to understand their switching behavior from offline to m-government services.

## 2.2. M-government services and related studies

M-government is an extension or evolution of electronic government, whereby the transactions of matters and dissemination of information and services are conducted through mobile devices for public service delivery [8]. It provides citizens with customized, location-based, real-time information and services, allowing them access to govern ment services anytime, anywhere [54]. Its emergence has changed the government service delivery process [55], as citizens can access gov ernment services conveniently and quickly through their mobile phones instead of going to government agencies to complete forms and apply for services in person, which greatly saves time and effort.

Most studies of m-government services have focused on users adoption $[ 6 , 7 , 5 6 , 5 7 ]$ . Based primarily on the theory of planned behavior and supplemented by the mobile communication perspective, Hung et al. [58] suggest that communication efficiency and integrated communication service factors positively influence user adoption behavior in m-government service settings. Likewise, Abdelghaffar and Magdy [59] indicate that users’ willingness to use m-government in Egypt is strongly influenced by their perception of its usefulness. Furthermore, previous studies have provided evidence that the intention to adopt m-government depended on users’ expectation of social influ ence [60,61]. These findings support Wei et al’s. [62] argument that how significant others (e.g., family, friends, and colleagues) perceive m-government can significantly impact users’ perceptions about and adoption of m-government. In line with these conclusions, Ahmad and Khalid [8] reveal that trust and social influence are positively associated with users’ intentions to adopt m-government services.

In addition, a number of researchers have examined the postadoption use of m-government services [4,63,64]. Drawing upon ser vice science studies and the Chinese m-government context, Wang [63] confirms that perceived usefulness has a positive effect on the contin uous use of mobile government. Similarly, Wang et al. [64] declare that mobility, localizability, and personalization are positively associated with perceived value, which in turn is positively associated with users continuance intention in the m-government context. In addition, several studies have noted a focus on citizen satisfaction with m-government systems [63,65]. For example, by integrating the task-technology fit perspective and theory of procedural fairness, Chen et al. [2] developed a research model to demonstrate that seven mobile and wireless tech nology functions of m-Government increase the procedural fairness of governmental services, which, in turn, improve user satisfaction.

However, little attention has been paid to users’ switching behavior from offline to m-government services. Factors influencing users adoption and continued usage may not suffice to explain their switching behaviors, or the degrees of influence from these factors may differ [34, 66]. Although users’ switching behaviors have been investigated in various contexts such as social networking [67], blog services [33], mobile shopping [68], online auctions [69], e-commerce [35,70], and e-health care services [35], the unique characteristics of m-government services may challenge the applicability of the findings derived from other research settings. Furthermore, past studies have mainly focused on younger groups (e.g., middle-aged or students) who are healthier, more social, and more active in learning new things than seniors typi cally are [37]. Hence, earlier findings may not be applicable to explain the reasons for seniors’ switching behavior.

## 3. Mixed methods design

For this research, we employed a sequential mixed-methods design consisting of both qualitative and quantitative studies [71] for three reasons. First, scholars in the field of IS call for research pluralism, which corresponds with mixed methods [72,73]. Second, compared with a single research method, the mixed-methods approach provides richer insights by simultaneously addressing confirmatory and explanatory research questions [71,74], which is crucial for our research question including qualitative and quantitative research methods. The mixed-methods design is a relatively comprehensive approach that provides a global view [72,74]. Third, little research has described or explained factors influencing seniors’ switch from offline to m-government services through the theoretical lens of PPM. Unlike some theories that have fixed variables (such as the ease of use and usefulness of the technology acceptance model), PPM does not contain any fixed variables; therefore, researchers can examine key factors affecting migration behavior according to the research context and independently determine which variables best represent the push, pull, and mooring forces in a certain context [31,75]. Thus, we first con ducted a qualitative study to identify key factors of the PPM framework (Study 1), and then a subsequent quantitative study to verify our research model (Study 2). More specifically, in Study 1, a qualitative research method was used to form a systematic research model to investigate the antecedents of push, pull, and mooring effects. A hybrid SEM-ANN method was then applied in Study 2 to empirically analyze how those factors influence seniors’ intention to switch to m-govern ment services.

## 3.1. Study 1: qualitative study

Grounded theory, an evolving qualitative research method proposed by Glaser and Strauss in 1967, is based on a constructivist approach [76]. Coding in grounded theory is the process of analyzing the data, including open, axial, and selective coding [76]. In open coding, raw data is conceptualized and categorized through a line-by-line analysis. Axial coding seeks to understand categories in relationship to other categories and their subcategories, and further extricate the relation ships on which the axis of the category is focused. Selective coding is the final analysis of the relationship between the main categories, from which a theoretical framework is formed. Grounded theory is an exploration-oriented research method, which is especially suitable for exploring the “what” behind new phenomena and problems. Therefore, we consider grounded theory an appropriate method for investigating the factors that affect older adults’ switch to m-government services.

## 3.1.1. Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were used to collect data. A total of 21 senior users of Zheliban, the m-government App of Zhejiang province, were recruited as participants. According to the “2021 Provincial M-Government Services Ability Survey and Evaluation Report” published by the China Software Evaluation Center, Zhejiang Province received excellent scores in comprehensive assessments of provincial m-govern ment service capability; hence, this study examined Zheliban. Verifying that theoretical saturation has been reached is necessary to ensure the quality of the analysis results [77]. After analyzing 21 responses, it was found that additional data was becoming repetitive, indicating that theoretical saturation had been met; thus, further data collection was not conducted. The interviewees were labeled A01–A21; for example, A10 represents the 10th interviewee. Of the participants, 53.3 % were male, 46.7 % were female, and all were aged 60 to 75 years. Over 90 % had experience using Zheliban for more than one year, and 60 % of them had experience of more than two years.

Before the interviews, we first introduced the interview purpose to the interviewees and committed to maintaining the confidentiality of the interview data. Participants were then requested to answer openended questions, including “What are the advantages and disadvan tages of offline and m-government services?” “How do people around you feel about Zheliban?” “Are you aware of any efforts made by the government to improve the effectiveness of Zheliban?” and “Is it easy for you to learn to operate Zheliban?”

## 3.1.2. Data analysis

Fig. 1 offers a detailed description of the coding process for the qualitative data. First, open coding was conducted to identify phenom ena and categories. At this stage, the two research team members carefully read the interview text data line by line to extract the initial concepts. After concluding and refining them, 13 common codes were generated. Second, axial coding was devoted to establishing the rela tionship between common codes, from which the main categories were concluded. After careful analysis and discussion, the 13 common codes were classified into 5 main categories: perceived inefficiency, trans parency, subjective norms, perceived government investment, and inertia. Selective coding was done to obtain core categories from the main categories and construct theoretical models. This process yielded three themes (push factors, pull factors, and mooring factors) developed from five categories that may best describe the PPM framework.

Based on the qualitative data analysis, we identified (1) one pushmechanism antecedent, namely, perceived inefficiency, (2) three pull mechanism antecedents, comprising transparency, subjective norms, and perceived government investment, and (3) one mooring-mechanism antecedent, namely, inertia. The next section discusses how these factors influence seniors’ switching intention from offline to m-government services and are reported in Study 2 in detail.

## 3.2. Study 2: quantitative study

Based on previous literature and the findings of Study 1, we applied the PPM and constructed a research model to explain seniors’ switching from offline government to m-government services (Fig. 2). Specifically, push factors urge people to leave their original place of residence [78]. In this study, push factors mainly pertain to users’ dissatisfaction with the original offline government service and perceptions of its in efficiency $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ , the considerable time spent queueing in an offline of fice). Pull factors are mainly positive factors that influence people to travel or migrate to a new destination—that is, the new destination’s relevant attributes attract migrators [78]. In this study, pull factors are factors that encourage individuals to switch from offline to m-govern ment services. This study selected perceived government investment, transparency, and subjective norms as pull factors. Perceived govern ment investment refers to users’ evaluation of the local government’s investment in data integration, app development, and service process ing. Transparency means that individuals can apprise themselves of the materials needed to be prepared to conduct business and track business progress from anywhere, anytime, via smartphones. Previous studies indicated that the use of m-government services can largely curb cor ruption and improve the transparency of government affairs [54]. Further, other people’s support affects one’s usage behaviors [79], suggesting subjective norms as an important pull factor. Mooring refers to constraints in migration decisions, mainly time and switching costs [25]. Mooring factors lead people to decide against migration. In this study, switching costs and inertia were selected as mooring factors.

## 3.2.1. Push effect

According to PPM, dissatisfaction with the quality of life in the place of origin is an important factor prompting departure [25]. In terms of IT acceptance, users’ dissatisfaction with services positively affects their willingness to switch to other service providers in such domains as web browser services [22], blog services [80], and online games [81]. In the offline context, it has been found that users’ dissatisfaction with a paper-based work pattern is positively correlated with their intention to switch to online services [82]. Lack of satisfaction can lead to users ditching a product or service [22]. In this study, senior users who experienced negative feelings about offline government services (e.g., time spent queueing in an offline office or traveling to an offline office) are expected to be more likely to switch to m-government services. Thus, we hypothesize:

$H _ { I } { : }$ Seniors’ dissatisfaction with offline government services posi tively influences their switching intention to m-government services.

Perceived inefficiency is the degree to which people feel experiences such as inadequate content provision, wasted time, and other in conveniences are negative when using traditional methods [83]. Past research has defined perceived inefficiency as feelings of inadequacy and incompetence when performing tasks at work [84]. Governments worldwide have been moving away from face-to-face communication to a more digital approach since the late 1990s to increase efficiency, convenience, and flexibility in delivering services to their citizens and businesses [58]. As more online government channels are provided, inefficient face-to-face interactions are effectively reduced, alleviating citizen dissatisfaction with government services [85]. In this study, perceived inefficiency refers to the negative experiences seniors have when using offline government services, such as queuing, long waiting times, and physical exertion to conduct business [7]. Therefore, seniors perceive offline service processes as inefficient, which increases their dissatisfaction with offline government services. Thus, we hypothesize:

H : Perceived inefficiency positively influences seniors’ dissatis faction with offline government services.

## 3.2.2. Pull effect

Satisfaction is the user’s assessment of the experience of using a product or service [86]. M-government service satisfaction in this study refers to customer evaluations derived from differences between senior users’ expectations and their actual perceptions of the services provided by the m-government app. Satisfaction is widely accepted as a key driver of users’ willingness to continue to use a service and of their loyalty [86], and it plays a crucial role in user habit formation [57]. However, an exception is Ellis et al.’s [87] finding that satisfaction did not significantly affect computer usage intention when the participants were seniors. Based on the findings of most studies, we assume that when seniors are more satisfied with m-government services due to such fac tors as greater convenience and quick access to more information about public services through apps, their willingness to switch to m-govern ment services will be higher. Thus, we hypothesize:

H : Satisfaction with m-government services positively influence seniors’ switching intention to m-government services.

Perceived government investment refers to government support related to technology adoption and implementation, including policy support, financial investment, infrastructure investment, and coaching sessions [79]. Chong et al. [79] found that government support was important for consumers’ willingness to use online banking in Vietnam. Wu et al. [88] noted that greater investment in government service platforms increased people’s willingness to switch government services across platforms. Talukder et al. [7] verified that government invest ment is one of the critical factors increasing senior users’ use of m-government services. These investments (e.g., resources, knowledge, and support) can assist senior users in accessing m-government services more easily. Therefore, this study assumes when older people feel that the government attaches great importance to implementing m-govern ment services and invests considerable financial and material resources in infrastructure, education, and training, the higher their willingness is to switch to m-government services. Thus, we hypothesize:

$H _ { 4 } { \mathrm { : } }$ Perceived government investment positively influences seniors satisfaction with m-government services.

Transparency refers to how government agencies provide real-time information about citizens’ rights, laws and regulations, government services, and government decisions to the public through m-government apps [89]. Higher levels of information reliability (i.e., transparency) have a significant effect on citizens’ higher e-government satisfaction [90]. Consequently, in this study, transparency emphasizes the ability of m-government apps to systematically and simultaneously deliver rele vant information about the processes and outcomes of government services; the transparency of this information should increase users perceptions of the procedural fairness of government agencies’ services, which in turn affects users’ satisfaction with m-government services [2, 90]. When seniors use the m-government app, they can apprise them selves of the main processes and the materials they should prepare before doing business. They can track the progress of the business in real-time, and all these transparent service contents will improve their satisfaction with m-government services. Thus, we hypothesize:

H : Transparency positively influences seniors’ satisfaction with m government services.

Subjective norms refer to how individuals perceive that important people in their social circles support them in using new technologies [79]. Previous studies have shown that the reference group (e.g., family and friends) is an important source of trust [91,92]. People tend to obtain important information from their reference group when deter mining whether a service provider is trustworthy [93]. For senior users, Ellis et al. [87] found that the influence of subjective norms significantly affected seniors’ use of technology in Taiwan. Moreover, the opinions of friends, family, colleagues, and opinion leaders in the mass media can influence seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services [7]. In China, seniors usually live with and are cared for by their children, whose advice, attitudes, usage behaviors, and assistance can increase their satisfaction with m-government services. Thus, we hypothesize:

H : Subjective norms positively influence seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services

## 3.2.3. Mooring effect

Switching cost is the user’s perception of the cost of switching to a new service provider [82]. In this study, switching cost refers to the cost of switching from offline to m-government for seniors. Burnham et al. [94] indicated that costs include time and effort as well as money and psychological costs. Migration can be hindered by high switching costs [95,96], which means that people may abandon migration because of high costs [97]. Previous studies showed that switching costs can discourage people from switching to new services [32,81]. Yang and Lin [98] indicated that as most elderly users are retired, they might be more concerned about extra spending, such as purchasing mobile devices and subscribing to Internet access, leading them to continue using their current service and reducing their intention to try new social tools. Thus, if seniors’ perception is that using an m-government app is complicated and requires considerable time, effort, and money to adopt, their will ingness to use it will decrease. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H : Switching cost is negatively related to seniors’ intention to switch to m-government services.

Samuelson and Zeckhauser [99] describe inertia as the unconscious process of repeating the same behavior because of habitual attachment. According to Lin and Huang [100], inertia poses a barrier to utilizing new technologies, causing individuals to be less likely to attempt to use them successfully. Polites and Karahanna [101] found that inertia made users less receptive to new systems. Therefore, more opportunities (i.e., training and support) should be offered to senior users to allow them to more readily accept new technology [36]. In this study, seniors might have been accustomed to using offline government services. Some senior people may have no intention to switch to m-government services because they might not want to change and might experience feelings of anxiety over unfamiliar technology. Accordingly, we hypothesize as follows:

## H<sub>8</sub>: Inertia positively affects switching costs.

## 3.2.4. Control variable

Venkatesh et al.’s survey [102] of rural India found that among in dividual demographic characteristics, farmers’ gender, age, and income influenced the use of e-governance. Furthermore, Lallmahomed et al. [103] stated that to increase the usage of e-government services, people need high self-efficacy to meet the requirements of the intended func tions; increased usage is difficult to achieve if people exhibit low self-efficacy. Therefore, this study used gender, age, income, and self-efficacy as control variables.

## 4. Data collection and analysis

## 4.1. Measurement

The scale items measuring the constructs were developed from prior studies and adapted to the m-government context (Appendix B). Items measuring perceived inefficiency were drawn from the scale of Kim et al. [83]. The measurement of dissatisfaction with offline government ser vices was modified from Sun et al. [32]. Measurements of perceived government investment were adapted from Chong et al. [79]. The measurement of transparency was developed based on a study by Chen et al. [2]. Items measuring subjective norms were modified from Taylor and Todd [104]. Measurements of switching costs were adapted from Sun et al. [32]. Items measuring inertia were adapted from Zeng et al. [82]. The measurement of self-efficacy was developed from Compeau and Higgins [105]. Finally, items measuring switching intention were modified from Sun et al. [32]. A Likert-scale format with ratings from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7) was used to rate the items.

Since the respondents were Chinese users, the questionnaire was in Chinese; we used a back-translation method to complete the English –Chinese and Chinese–English translations [106]. As a result of this dual translation process [107], minor changes were made to the question naire to ensure that the meanings remained consistent during the translation process. To ensure clarity of instructions, content validity, and consistency of results, two scholars who are fluent in English and have a good understanding of m-government were invited to review the questionnaires in both languages. The final measurement items used in this study are shown in Appendix B.

## 4.2. Data collection

In this study, the respondents were senior users of the m-government app Zheliban in Zhejiang Province in China, which has taken the lead in digital government governance in China [108]. Zheliban has been ranked first in the country in terms of technical support services [37]. In 2016, the Zhejiang government, together with the help of the tech company Alibaba, initiated Zheliban, and by 2021, over 82 million registered users used the app, which integrates 87 government affairs applications created in different localities, as well as 1705 convenience applications [109]. Users of the app have been shown to require only 60 min on average to complete real estate registration, the fastest speed in the country [110].

A convenience sampling procedure was used to collect data. For better generalization, the potential respondents in this study were drawn from different target groups by gender, age, and geography. Thus, 12 research assistants were recruited and assigned to questionnaire administration and distribution. We conducted a face-to-face survey of seniors in parks, hospitals, community centers, and homes. After explaining the purpose and objectives of this study, respondents who were 60 years or older and had experience using m-government services were invited to join the study. Participants were informed that upon completion of the survey, they would receive a monetary reward. After they agreed to participate in the survey, m-government services were clearly defined and described to ensure that each participant understood the meaning during the survey. In order to strengthen respondents impression of the mobile government services, they were shown a video about two minutes long on mobile government services that demon strated various mobile government services such as bill payment and doctor appointments. After watching this video, the research assistant verified that each participant had no questions. In this way, we ensured that the participants responded with a solid understanding of m-gov ernment services, thus increasing the study’s internal validity. Re spondents completed the questionnaire on their own or data collectors provided aid on their behalf. A pilot test was carried out before the formal survey. The data was collected between January 10th and March 15th. 2022. After careful examination of the responses. 345 valid responses were kept for analysis after excluding respondents who gave the same answer to all questions. Of these valid respondents, 58 % were female and 42 % were male. All of them were over 60 years old. Approximately 51 % of the respondents lived in cities and the rest lived in rural areas. Most of them had middle school or high school-level education. Furthermore, 40 % of participants had a monthly income of less than 4000RMB (\$628). Please refer to Table 1 for details.

## 4.3. Common method variance

We used several statistical techniques to detect common method bias (CMB). First, following Kock and Lynn [111], the full collinearity approach was tested by calculating a single criterion with all latent variables in the model. A latent factor with a single indicator is created using randomly generated values for this single criterion. This approach is equivalent to the covariance-based SEM CMB test proposed by Lindell and Whitney [112]. When the model is run using partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) software, the variance inflation factors (VIF) values are inspected for CMB; when the VIF is greater than 3.3, the model might suffer from CMB [113]. In this study, as all the VIFs were below 3.3, CMB was not a concern. Second, following Bagozzi et al. [114], we examined the correlation matrix of the constructs in the measurement instrument and found no correlation greater than 0.9. Therefore, CMB is not a significant problem for this study.

## 5. Data analysis and results

## 5.1. Measurement model

PLS-SEM was applied to examine the measurement and structural models in this study for three reasons. First, covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) requires stricter assumptions about the normal distribution of the data than PLS-SEM [115]. This study employed convenience sampling to collect data, which may result in non-normal data distributions. Second, PLS-SEM is a better choice than CB-SEM when the research model is complex [116]. Based on PPM, this study proposes a complex research model, including nine variables and eight hypotheses, to explore the main factors influencing the seniors switching to m-government services. Third, PLS-SEM is better suited to exploratory research studies [117]. This study examined seniors switching intentions in the context of m-government by extending PPM to this new field. Therefore, SmartPLS 3 is considered an appropriate tool for this study.

We examined the reliability and validity of the construct measures before testing the hypotheses. The reliability of each construct was evaluated using Cronbach’s α, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE). Hair et al. [118] recommended that Cron bach’s α and CR be 0.7 or greater, while for AVE, 0.5 is considered the lowest acceptable level. Table 2 shows that all constructs had Cron bach’s α values greater than 0.75, CR values between 0.85 and 0.97, and AVE values greater than 0.66, indicating that they were all reliable.

Regarding discriminant validity, we found that the least square root of AVE was 0.81, which was greater than any correlation between any pair of constructs [119] (see Table 3), indicating a satisfactory level of discriminant validity. In addition. the Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio (HTMT) is another effective method for assessing discriminant validity [120], and, as shown in Table 4, the study’s maximum HTMT value was 0.751 (< 0.9), indicating satisfactory discriminant validity for all con structs. ## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Discussion of findings

Regarding push factors, we found that perceived inefficiency posi tively correlated with dissatisfaction with offline government services (supporting H2). When seniors perceive that conducting offline gov ernment services requires time and physical exertion such as queuing, they view government services negatively, echoing the findings of Kim et al. [83]. Furthermore, seniors’ dissatisfaction with offline government services positively influences their switching to m-government (sup porting H1), corroborating Zeng et al.’s findings [82].

Among pull factors, we discovered that transparency and subjective norms positively affect satisfaction with m-government services (sup porting H5 and H6). Perceived process transparency significantly en hances older adults’ satisfaction with m-government services (supporting H5), mirroring the findings of Chen et al. [2]. Furthermore, subjective norms positively affect their satisfaction (supporting H6). That is, support from friends or family members for m-government use as well as others’ recommendations affect seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services. Therefore, our results are consistent with those of Talukder et al. [7].

Regarding mooring factors, inertia positively and significantly af fects switching costs (supporting H8). In this study context, seniors have formed long-lasting inherent habits of conducting business offline and therefore find it unacceptable to relinquish their existing way of doing things by switching to the m-government app, which would also incur high switching costs (e.g., learning costs), echoing the results of Samuelson and Zeckhauser [99]. Furthermore, this study finds that conversion costs negatively affect willingness to convert (supporting H7), and the higher the conversion costs, the weaker the users’ will ingness to convert, which is consistent with earlier findings [32,35].

In this study, H4 was not supported. Among the factors that affected seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services, transparency was the greatest, followed by subjective norms. However, perceived government investment did not significantly increase user satisfaction with m-gov ernment; this led to a rejection of H4. This finding can be explained by two factors: (1) The effect of “perceived government investment” on mgovernment satisfaction may be necessary but insufficient compared to the other two antecedents. Users perceive that the government must invest in purchasing equipment, integrating data, and developing ap plications to implement m-government services, which are prerequisites for m-government implementation and generate user satisfaction. (2) It is also possible that there were moderating effects on the sample group. Research has indicated that rural and urban residents have different attitudes and levels of satisfaction with government services [125]. To test this hypothesis, the samples were divided into urban and rural (168) residents (see Figs. 4 and 5), and we compared path coefficients with the same statistical significance, following the method proposed by Keil et al. [126]. The effect of “perceived government investment” on “satisfaction with m-government” was not significant for older urban residents, but was significant for senior rural residents, confirming that the result of the insignificant effect of perceived government investment was influenced by residence.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

We found that transparency is the most important factor influencing seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services, further affecting their switching intention. Since the 1990s, China has vigorously developed egovernment [57]. In 2015, the level of informatization in Chinese society was significantly improved by the “Internet+” action plan [64]. Recently, local government services have increasingly facilitated public access to government services online through websites, apps, and WeChat mini-programs. This study’s results indicate that the openness and transparency of the content and process of government services can enhance users’ satisfaction. In the future, the government can use innovative technologies such as live streaming to interact with the public in real-time, explain policies online, and answer public inquiries, thereby improving its ability to solve issues.

Furthermore, support from family and friends can significantly in crease older citizens’ satisfaction with m-government use, as the family with whom seniors live daily has the most significant impact on their behavioral awareness. Family digital feedback gradually becomes an endogenous force to help seniors develop digital information compe tency. Family members play the most basic and direct role in enhancing seniors’ digital literacy, allowing seniors to gradually adapt to digital society through digital feedback from family members. Family digital feedback is thus the most direct and effective support for the smooth integration of older people into the information society. Face-to-face and hands-on instruction on smart product use by younger people not only helps seniors to receive knowledge proactively but also contributes to harmonious family relationships. Therefore, a family’s younger gener ation should pay attention to the needs of seniors to participate in and use the Internet promptly, and proactively help them to integrate into the information society.

Moreover, this study found urban-rural differences in perceived government input satisfaction with m-government services. Past studies indicated that e-government is well suited to serving urban residents because of cities’ high Internet coverage and low network access costs. In contrast, due to the high cost of IT infrastructure in rural areas, the high cost of network access and residents’ low computer literacy pose great challenges for governments to deliver services to rural or remote residents, which also exacerbates the urban-rural digital divide [37]. Past studies noted that the popularization of information and commu nication technology and Internet education in rural areas has enabled more rural residents to become IT literate, which can significantly in crease the acceptance of applications such as Internet banking and on line shopping [79]. In August 2022, China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued regulations on the precise monitoring of 5G network coverage in rural areas, requiring provinces (autonomous regions and municipalities) to complete 5G coverage in administrative villages in phases and reach a download rate of 100 Mbps, with Internet penetration rates in rural areas exceeding 60 %. Moreover, the cost of Internet access in rural areas has been significantly reduced. Most rural citizens can enjoy personal cell phones, fixed broadband, and Internet TV packages for only RMB 30 (\$4.1) to 40 (\$5.5) per month [127]. Therefore, our findings suggest that the government should further in crease investment in Internet infrastructure and enhance education on digital applications in rural areas, which will extend their use and in crease their satisfaction with m-government services and thus narrow the urban–rural digital divide.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations and offers directions for future study. First, we recruited a sample of seniors with m-government use experience from a single country, which may limit the generalization of the findings to other countries because of cultural differences [124]. Future research in other cultural settings can test whether the results are consistent with our findings and thereby ascertain the generalizability of our findings. Second, future research could examine other dependent variables. While we empirically investigated the key factors affecting seniors’ switching from offline to m-government services to determine the mechanism underlying the use of m-government services, much remains to be investigated regarding the mechanism. Unlike social media apps, mobile government apps are semi-mandatory apps for users. Government services are handled through mobile government service apps or offline government offices. Therefore, rather than dis cussing factors influencing users’ willingness to adopt mobile govern ment services, future research should pay more attention to discussing how to improve users’ in-depth or expanded use of m-government ser vices. Third, we found that senior people in urban or rural areas had different attitudes toward m-government. Future research can explore the reasons for the differences in using m-government services between urban and rural residents.

## 7. Conclusion

With the rapidly increasing number of seniors using m-government services, this study aimed to uncover the factors that impel seniors to switch from offline to m-government services. Based on the PPM framework, we argue that switching behavior is the result of a combination of factors involving push, pull, and mooring factors. Qualitative and quantitative methods were used in this study. We found that satisfaction with m-government services had the greatest impact on seniors’ intention to switch to m-government services, followed by dissatisfaction with offline government services while switching costs were found to have a significant negative impact. The transparency of government services had the greatest impact on seniors’ satisfaction with m-government services, while the perceived inefficiency of offline services enhanced senior users’ dissatisfaction with offline services. Inertia increases seniors’ switching costs. Moreover, this study found that urban-rural differences affected seniors’ satisfaction with m-gov ernment services. Our findings will afford government agencies a better understanding of seniors’ switching behaviors, improving the efficient and effective delivery of essential government services to them.
