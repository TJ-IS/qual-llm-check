# Spillover effects of interactions on user innovation: Evidence from a firm-hosted open innovation platform

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Open innovation platform User interaction User innovation Spillover effects

## A B S T R A C T

Firm-hosted open innovation platforms (OIPs) enable multidirectional communication and interaction among users, and they have become a vital IT artifact that absorbs external resources for enterprise innovation. A key challenge for OIPs is how to motivate users’ idea contributions. The support community appears as a Q&A section in a few OIPs, in which the special role of user behavior on individuals’ idea contribution is understudied. Based on a theoretical synthesis of innovation management studies, we propose hypotheses regarding the spillover effects of user interactions in a support community (i.e., help giving and help seeking) on individuals innovation performance (i.e., the quantity and quality of ideas). Using a dataset from a typical OIP, we employ two empirical studies to investigate the spillover effects of user interactions and their characteristics, including breadth and depth, on innovation performance. Our results confirm the existence of spillover effects and reveal a positive effect of interaction breadth and a nonlinear effect of interaction depth on innovation. Notably, the nonlinear relationship can be moderated by the interaction breadth. Our findings provide both theoretical and practical implications for user innovation and OIP operations.

## 1. Introduction

Since proposed by Chesbrough [1], open innovation has become an important business strategy for contemporary enterprises [2]. Advances in information technology have allowed companies to implement open innovation with their external product users by creating open innova tion platforms (OIP), where users can interact with each other online and propose ideas directly to the company [3]. User innovation has become an integral part of firm innovation, and companies in various fields have established OIPs for achieving their innovation goals. For example, P&G increased the percentage of external innovation from 15 % to 50 % through OIP, resulting in a 60% enhancement of its R&D capacity [4]. Likewise, LEGO has successfully implemented its OIP (i.e., Lego Ideas), and numerous ideas have been transformed into profitable business products [5]. However, not every OIP has achieved its designed goals. Prior research has found that many OIPs suffer from “Empty Bar Syndrome,” a metaphorical term depicting OIPs that, similarly to an empty bar, lack sufficient and sustained participation, thus hindering these platforms from realizing their intended goals [3,6]. For example, Midea, a well-known electrical appliance company in China, had to stop operating its OIP (open.midea.com), mainly due to the lack of sustain able and valuable idea contributions from the external participants. Therefore, how to maintain user engagement and encourage continued contribution of valuable ideas is one of the main challenges for OPI’s long-term success.

An essential part of OIP is an innovation community (IC) where innovative ideas are proposed by users and are collected and delivered directly to the company [7]. That is, IC serves as an idea collection section. Based on motivation and social interaction theories, prior research has investigated various incentive mechanisms used to moti. vate users’ idea contribution behaviors in the IC. For example, monetary and reputational rewards have been found to influence individuals’ idea contributions positively [8,9]; Chen et al. [10] revealed that user interaction, such as peer and firm feedback on ideas, affect users contribution levels in an OIP. However, not all OIPs build only an IC. Many companies, such as Dell, Salesforce, and Xiaomi, have cohosted an IC and a support community (SC) within the same OIP. A SC is a question and answer (Q&A) section, which is provided for users to help each other with questions regarding a company’s products and services [7]. While current research contributes to an in-depth understanding of individuals’ idea contribution behavior in IC, whether and how users behavior in SC influences their idea contribution behavior in IC is understudied.

Answering this question is important. As mentioned before, a SC is a Q&A section; thus, users participate in a SC in an interactive form of help giving/seeking. As an important form of social interaction, help giving/ seeking has been found to impact creativity by previous studies on organizational creativity [11–13]. Help giving describes activities where individuals assist the work of others, and help seeking represents ac tivities in which individuals seek the assistance of others [11]. Differ ently from user interaction in an IC, which is idea-focused and usually begins with the evaluation of ideas, help giving/seeking in a SC is problem-focused [14], and the interaction occurs when an individual obtains/gives an answer from/to others. Most users do not participate in SCs for the purpose of generating ideas; rather, they are there to seek help or help others. However, they may still be inspired to develop ideas in the process of Q&A interaction. For example, we observed an inter esting case in the OIP of Xiaomi,<sup>1</sup> named MIUI, which cohosts an IC and a SC. A Xiaomi phone user answered a question about the Xiaomi phone’s heat problem and communicated with others in the SC. After that, he actually raised the idea in the IC, suggesting that Xiaomi pro duced a metal magnetic suction heat-conducting case. Perhaps inspired by users’ questions or stimulated by others’ answers, the focal user in this case generated an idea that might not have appeared otherwise. This observation reveals that user interactions in SCs not only solve product usage problems, but also may have an unintended impact on users’ idea contribution in ICs.

How to promote user contribution in OIPs is a great challenge for both academics and practice. The emergence of SC may provide a new perspective. Scholars have also called for studies to better understand SCs’ role and its relationship to ICs in OIPs [7]. In addition, determining a SC’s influence on users’ idea contribution behavior can provide important platform design insights for OIPs. Thus, we are motivated to conduct this study and propose that user interaction in a SC might have unintended effects on their idea contributions in an IC.

To conceptualize such unintended results, we introduce the concept of spillover effects, which has been applied to diverse domains such as knowledge management, innovation management, and marketing. Spillover effects are generally characterized by involving two or more parties, possessing certain connecting channels and producing unin tended effects [15–17]. In our research context, an IC and a SC are two different sections in the same OIP, share the same group of platform users, and have interrelated topics. A SC is designed for users to submit and help each other to solve product problems. Furthermore, the interaction in SCs has the potential to stimulate individuals’ thinking, which might lead to idea generation. Thus, our research setting is consistent with the above characteristics of spillover effects. Clarifying the spillover effect of help giving/seeking in a SC on individuals’ idea contributions in an IC can refine the study of OIPs, highlight the important value of a SC, and provide design inspiration for firm-hosted OIPs. Thus, we believe it is reasonable and meaningful to use spillover effects as our research lens.

The current study aims to advance our understanding of whether and how participating in a SC produces spillover effects on individuals innovation performance in an IC. Specifically, we investigated how in dividuals’ help-giving and help-seeking activities in a SC affect the quantity and quality of their ideas proposed in an IC. To explore the research question, we interpreted the spillover effects as the accumu lation of information and the activation of knowledge based on a theo retical synthesis of innovation management studies. In the process of help-giving/seeking, individuals have the potential to process the stored and acquired knowledge, and establish novel linkages within it, thereby promoting the generation of new and high-quality ideas [3,18]. Meanwhile, the innovation management literature emphasizes that knowledge breadth influences individuals’ access to diverse informa tion, thus affecting the ability to generate new ideas [19,20]. A certain depth of knowledge, on the other hand, is considered necessary for idea generation, but may also induce cognitive fixation, which may result in individuals being trapped in a certain thinking frame and discouraging innovation [21,22]. Thus, we further explored the role of structural features of interaction, including breadth and depth, in influencing in dividuals’ idea contributions. In our research context, breadth refers to the number of different problem domains an individual engages in, and depth refers to the degree of an individual’s concentration on certain problem domains [23].

We utilized a large panel dataset from the OIP of Salesforce, a customer relationship management company, to test our hypotheses empirically. We first adopted a quasi-natural experiment to investigate how users’ innovation performance changes after participation in a SC. Then we conducted a fixed effects regression to further explore how the characteristics of interactions in a SC, in terms of breadth and depth, influence users’ innovation performance in an IC. The results indicate that an individual’s interaction in a SC inserts spillover effects not only on the quantity but also on the quality of ideas s/he proposes in a IC. We found that the interaction breadth is positively associated with the quantity and quality of ideas, while the relationship between the interaction depth and innovation performance presents an inverted Ushape. Interestingly, this inverted U-shaped relationship is further moderated by the interaction breadth. More specifically, the inverted Ushaped curve is flattened with broader interaction, but differences exist between help-giving and help-seeking behavior. In summary, this study presents novel findings on the spillover effects of interactions in a SC (help giving and help seeking) on users’ innovation performance in an IC in the context of OIPs.

Our study contributes to the open innovation literature by analyzing and examining the unique spillover effects of individuals’ participation in a SC (i.e., help giving and help seeking) on the quantity and quality of their idea generation in an IC. Our findings provide insights into the potential value of a SC in stimulating individuals’ idea contributions and extend previous work on the influence of different types of interactions on user innovation. In addition to confirming the spillover effects of participating in SCs, our study reveals that the breadth and depth of help giving/seeking have varying degrees of impact on idea contributions, thus complementing the innovation management literature on the relationship between social interaction and user innovation in the context of online communities. Furthermore, the results provide empirical evidence for a moderating effect of interaction breadth on the inverted U-shaped relationship between interaction depth and innova tion performance. Our findings, therefore, offer insights into ways to reduce cognitive fixation in idea contribution. Overall, our work reveals the unintended results of cohosting a SC and an IC in an OIP and pro vides valuable design implications for firm-hosted OIPs.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. User innovation in firm-hosted OIPs

In the last decade, OIPs have received increased recognition as a promising method for innovation and collaboration [24]. In particular, firms are encouraged to implement such platforms and open their innovation processes by involving both internal and external parties, such as employees and customers [25]. For example, Xiaomi claimed that 80 % of its system updates are based on user suggestions from the MIUI community. Such firm-hosted OIPs are important to enterprise innovation, as firms are able to fully control and manage the platform, monitor ideas immediately, and integrate feasible solutions into the development process of new products or services improvements [26]. Given these benefits, scholars are motivated to conduct research on

firm-hosted OIPs.

Existing studies on firm-hosted OIPs have focused on the dynamics related to user-level innovation and explored factors influencing ideator innovation in OIPs [27,28] because user contribution is the basis of the platforms’ operations. One stream of the extant literature has investi gated the motivational factors that drive users’ engagement with and idea contributions to host firms, which include self-interest-driven and prosocial-oriented factors in general [29]. The self-interest-driven mo tivations can be further divided into intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, which have different effects on users’ online contributions [30]. Spe cifically, intrinsic motivation refers to the inherent desire to do some thing [31], including inherent satisfaction or enjoyment of the action itself [32], the desire to feel competence and self-determination [33], and the willingness to help others [34]. In contrast, extrinsic motivation means that an act is performed because of existing external incentives [31], such as direct or indirect monetary compensation [35], reputation, and prestige [9,31]. Prosocial-oriented motivation mainly refers to the establishment or maintenance of interpersonal relationships through participation in online communities to gain belongingness, social iden tity, and reciprocity [36]. For instance, Qin et al. [37] found that user reciprocity, social identity, and altruistic motivation are conducive to the promotion of online users’ proactive contribution behavior in OIPs. Likewise, Dong et al.’s [29] research showed that users’ continuous contribution in virtual communities is affected by motives such as vir tual rewards, peer recognition and social learning.

Another stream of related literature has focused on platforms and explored how different OIP characteristics influence users’ performance. Using a dataset of 20 online innovation platforms, Balka et al. [38] demonstrated a positive correlation between platform openness (e.g., transparency, accessibility, replicability) and users’ contributions to innovative projects. Battistella and Ncmino [39] pointed out that network structure and visualization features of a platform could affect users’ idea contributions. In addition, the management style of a plat form has been shown to be significantly related to users’ willingness to participate [40]. For example, Chen et al. [10] found that timely feed back from the platform can positively affect users’ willingness to participate, which further influences their idea contributions. Recent studies have also investigated the influence of user interactions on users contributions, and this is overviewed in the next section.

## 2.2. User interactions in OIPs

The wide application of social media technologies enables multidi rectional communications and interactions among users, which influ ence creativity and innovation [23]. In the OIP context, diverse user interactions coexist and impact users’ idea contributions in different ways, such as facilitating knowledge diffusion and promoting collabo ration [25]. Recent studies have mainly focused on user interaction in the IC by studying how idea feedback from others influences in dividuals’ idea contributions. For example, Chan et al. [41] found that users’ peer-to-peer and peer-to-firm interactions can generate and share valuable knowledge and information, which in turn affects the likeli hood of generating ideas in OIPs. Using a large-scale dataset from Microsoft’s Idea platform, Yang and Han [42] investigated the rela tionship between users’ interactions and their innovative contributions and found that diverse user interactions around ideas promote active and critical thinking and contribute to idea postings. Table 1 provides a brief summary of recent studies about the effect of users’ interaction on their innovation performance. While these studies contribute to a deep understanding the effect of idea-focused interactions (i.e., feedback on ideas) on user innovation, the specific roles of user interaction in SCs (i. e., help seeking and help giving) have been neglected.

Help seeking/giving has been recognized as an influencing factor of creativity in the collective and organizational creativity literature [48]. It is a critical interaction pattern identified by Hargadon and Bechky [11] that can yield “moments of collective creativity.” Help seeking is activities in which individuals seek the assistance of others, while help giving is activities where individuals assist with the work of others [49]. Help seeking/giving can be manifested in diverse manners, such as providing support, sharing insights, and offering expertise [50]. It is considered to be related to the acquisition of information, the integra tion of ideas, and the reduction of uncertainty [13,14,51], which in turn benefits creativity. Specifically, it can bring individuals with diverse expertise in an organization together to address problems or develop new products, thus generating collective creativity [52]. Help see king/giving can provide psychological safety to individuals who are under time pressure [50], which has been found to be an inhibitor of creativity [52,53]. However, some scholars have pointed out negative effects of helping behavior on creativity. These studies found that helping activities may reduce the time and energy an individual devotes to creative tasks [54] and prevent helpers from questioning their own perspective on the problem, which may weaken creativity instead [13]. In summary, the organizational creativity literature generally agrees that help seeking and giving behaviors have a significant effect on creativity.

Although ideas are not exactly the same as crea tivity, scholars have pointed out that creativity is the generation of novel and useful ideas [52,53]. Thus, it is reasonable to study the relationship between help seeking/giving and the idea contribution of individuals in our context. As an important form of user interaction, help see king/giving commonly exists in an OIP regardless of whether it hosts a specific SC region. However, due to the lack of analysis of interaction content and the relatively small number of helping-related interactions, current research on user interaction in OIP has paid little attention to help seeking/giving. The classification results by machine learning showed that only 3% of the comments are helping-related. Help seeking/giving and feedback around ideas are essentially different. Help seeking/giving is initiated or offered by an individual specifically to solve a problem, while feedback can be pro vided without regard to any problem [14]. In other words, the essential difference between help seeking/giving and feedback is whether they are problem-oriented or not. Given the impact of help seeking/giving on creativity demonstrated in the organizational creativity literature, we assume that it also has significant effects on individuals’ idea contri butions in OIPs.

## 2.3. Spillover effects

Spillover effects are a kind of externality in general [56] and have specific meanings when applied to different fields of study (e.g., knowledge management, innovation management, or marketing). For example, knowledge spillovers are defined as the external benefits of knowledge generation, which are shared through certain channels by various parties other than the creator [15]. Research on knowledge spillover suggests that it facilitates knowledge diffusion among firms and promotes innovation and development [57]. In marketing, adver tising spillover effects describe the relationship between different pro ducts/services within the same brand. Research has shown that a company’s advertising one of its products can spill over and increase sales of other existing products of the same company [58]. Besides these spillover effects, other specific types of spillover such as technology spillover and economic spillover have also been investigated in previous studies. Recently, some information systems (IS) scholars have started to apply the perspective of spillover effects to their research [59,60]. Although there is no previous OIP research based on the spillover perspective, it has been applied by a few IS scholars in the study of online virtual communities. For example, in the context of knowledge exchange platforms, Wang et al. [59] explored the spillover effects of monetary incentives received in the paid section on individuals voluntary knowledge-sharing activity in the free section of the platform.

Based on the above definition and application of spillover effects in the relevant literature, we summarize the characteristics of the spillover effect in our research as involving two or more parties, possessing certain connecting channels, and producing unintended effects [15–17]. In our research context, some firms build a SC in their OIPs to provide a place for mutual assistance among members. However, the influence of such a design often goes far beyond the original intentions of its de signers [16]. Specifically, users in a SC not only get answers by seeking help and gain reputation and self-efficacy satisfaction by giving help, but also may enhance the sense of belonging to the firm-hosted OIPs, in crease generalized reciprocity within the OIP, and obtain needs and means information, which has proven to be crucial to creative idea generation [7,34,39]. As mentioned before, the SC and IC are two sec tions in the same OIP with a close connection. Meanwhile, in most cases, users participate in a SC for the purpose of solving a specific problem instead of creating ideas. However, the process of participation addi tionally increases their ability to generate novel and useful ideas. Clearly, it is consistent with the characteristics of the spillover effect we summarized above. We thus utilize the concept of spillover effects to analyze the impact of help seeking and help giving in a SC on in dividuals’ idea contributions in an IC.

## 3. Theoretical background and hypotheses development

## 3.1. Theoretical background

This paper draws on innovation management literature to explore how users’ interaction in a SC (i.e., help seeking and help giving) affect their idea generation in terms of quantity and quality in an IC. The literature emphasizes the acquisition and accumulation of information and suggests that two types of information are required for the gener ation of new and high-quality ideas [7]. The first is needs information, which refers to knowledge that is related to a problem, need, or op portunity. The second is means information, which refers to knowledge that is used to solve a problem, meet a need, or exploit an opportunity [7].

Some scholars point out that ideas cannot be created ex nihilo. Rather, they are created by utilizing previously stored knowledge, which needs to be activated first [18,61]. In our research, we propose that users' interaction in a SC represents the accumulation of both needs and means information and the activation of stored knowledge. Differently from interactions in an IC (e.g., peer/firm feedback), which is idea-focused and offers feedback information and preferences toward ideas, help seeking and help giving are both problem-focused [14], in which individuals do not have direct access to ideas. Instead, they obtain inspiration and accumulate information from the problems or the solu tions to the problems for their own possible future ideas. They also realize what kinds of difficulties product users are facing and what other scenarios the solutions may be applied to. Importantly, the original purpose to participate in a SC is to seek help and/or help others. Therefore, the accumulated information, activated knowledge, and further ideas one might get from participation in a SC are additional and unintended results. We therefore use the perspective of spillover effects to describe how the knowledge and information accumulated in SC in teractions influences idea contributions in an IC.

Individuals’ knowledge is usually organized as interrelated domains, and breadth and depth are used to describe the structure of the knowl edge domain [62]. The breadth attribute reflects the horizontal dimension and heterogeneity of knowledge, whereas the depth attribute describes the vertical dimension and uniqueness of knowledge [63]. Both the breadth and depth of knowledge domains can influence the generation of new ideas and may have different impacts [19]. On one hand, studies have emphasized that individuals are able to integrate ideas and generate new ones when they efficiently convert and recombine knowledge and information acquired from different domains [19]. For example, Rodan and Galunic [64] found that exposure to diverse knowledge and its recombination are related to innovation performance. Similarly, Hwang et al. [7] revealed that generalists with broad knowledge are more likely to create novel ideas. On the other hand, previous studies demonstrated that a certain depth of knowledge is required before one can produce a new idea, as it requires an indi vidual to have a deep understanding of the domain to reorganize the knowledge attributes [19]. Zahra and George [65] argued that knowl edge depth is critical and lack of sufficient expertise can lead to less feasible ideas and ultimate failure. In OIPs, with the intent to facilitate user experience and manage platform, ideas and questions are commonly classified into various topics based on their knowledge do mains. Users can interact with community members within a certain topic or across different kinds of topics. The interaction among members is considered to enable individuals with information advantages that can drive innovation [3,66]. In this study, we use interaction breadth and depth to describe the knowledge structure acquired by an individual. More specifically, interaction breadth refers to the number of different problem domains an individual engages with, and interaction depth refers to the degree of an individual’s concentration on certain problem domains [23].

In addition, the innovation management literature emphasizes that although knowledge depth contributes to innovation, it may also cause an individual to suffer from cognitive fixation. Cognitive fixation, also known as cognitive entrenchment [67], refers to solidified thinking that hinders various types of cognitive operations including recalling, solving problems, and generating new ideas [21]. It describes a tendency to fixate on a certain mode of thinking [7], which can hinder an individual from generating new ideas. Cognitive fixation can arise from both one’s own experience in solving problems and that of others [22]. It limits the heuristics used in the idea generation process, which can significantly decrease an individual’s creativity. Therefore, we assume that in dividuals who concentrate on a certain knowledge domain may suffer from cognitive fixation. Given the adverse effects of cognitive fixation, prior research has also investigated different ways to mitigate the negative effects of cognitive fixation. A possible way is to increase exposure to various information by interacting with diverse others [22], which describes interaction breadth in our research. Thus, to validate this approach, we further discussed the moderating effect of interaction breadth on the relationship between interaction depth and user innovation.

Fig. 1 presents our research model. First, we investigate whether users’ interactions in a SC affect their idea contributions in an IC. We propose H1a and H1b based on innovation management literature. Then we combine previous studies on knowledge depth/breadth and cogni tive fixation to discuss how the interaction structure (i.e., breadth and depth) in a SC influences individuals’ idea contributions in an IC and propose H2a/b and H3a/b. Further, we test the potential approach to mitigate cognitive fixation by adding moderating terms based on prior research and propose H4a/b regarding the moderating effect of inter action breadth. Notably, individuals’ idea contribution is discussed in two aspects, idea quantity and idea quality.

## 3.2.1. Effects of participating in interactions in SCs on user innovation

Differently from interactions in an IC, where users can engage in intentional exchange of knowledge with others through idea feedback, interactions in a SC (i.e., help giving and help seeking) are all problemfocused, and users access knowledge through unintended information exchange [68]. In the process of help seeking/giving, individuals are aware of the problems and needs that consumers encounter during product or service use, which can stimulate individuals to associate with product improvements and innovations in an IC [69]. Meanwhile, interaction in a SC provides individuals with diverse knowledge required for problem solving, since each question might be answered by multiple members with varying expertise. Moreover, current prevalent products are usually technology-based and contain complex functions. Comprehensive accumulated knowledge of customers’ needs and po tential solutions enables an individual to propose new ideas with higher quality $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , usefulness or feasibility) $[ 7 , 4 3 ]$ . Thus, individuals who participate in a SC are more likely to have access to diverse information needed for idea generation and produce higher-quality ideas by recombining and forging new associations among stored and acquired knowledge than those who do not participate in a SC. In addition, previous studies have pointed out that innovative ideas tend to emerge by accident during interactions [11]. We propose that by offering unin tended knowledge accumulation and idea inspiration in the collision of various problems and solutions, help giving and help seeking have the potential to increase the probability of such accidents. Our proposition is also supported by studies on role theory, which indicates that knowledge can spill over across multiple roles of an individual [70]. In our research context, individuals play the role of answerer or questioner in a SC and the role of ideator or commenter in an IC. The knowledge obtained by individuals as answerers or questioners may spill over into the role of ideators, which in turn affects individuals’ innovation performance.

In sum, this discussion suggests that problem-focused interaction in a SC can help accumulate diverse knowledge. By providing information about customers’ needs and opportunities to realize and recombine diverse means information, participating in a SC may have positive spillover effects on individuals’ idea outputs in terms of quantity and quality. Consequently, accumulated knowledge can be applied and combined with customers’ needs to produce more and higher-quality ideas. Therefore, we hypothesize

## 3.2.2. Effects of interaction breadth in SCs on user innovation

As discussed earlier, interaction breadth refers to the number of different problem domains an individual engages with. The innovation management literature has recognized the importance of accessing diverse knowledge for individuals’ innovation performance [69,70]. Interacting with diverse others offers individuals the potential to create novel links and associations among different types of knowledge and information acquired [3,73], which in turn can facilitate the generation of more and higher-quality ideas. Such stimulatory effects of diverse interactions on ideation exist both under traditional face-to-face dis cussion conditions and in online environments. For example, Nijstad and Stroebe [18] found that compared with control conditions, the contin uous information stimulation of relatively rich categories leads to more ideas being generated by individuals. Osborn [71] revealed that inter acting with diverse others can stimulate associations in memory that lead to higher-quality ideas. Moreover, recent research on idea creation and innovation processes has demonstrated that diverse peer in teractions have a positive effect on idea generation [42].

In our research context, help giving and help seeking in SCs involve different categories of knowledge domains, which represent different “thought worlds” [72]. Users voluntarily participate in diverse cate gories or focus on several categories based on their interests. A higher level of interaction breadth means that the focal user interacts with community members across multiple problem domains, which can be interpreted as being exposed to more diverse knowledge and expertise. The exposure provides them with more fresh angles for tackling prob lems and creating ideas [73]. We thus expect that participating in multiple categories of problem domains will provide users with the potential to modify, integrate, and recombine original knowledge components, thus promoting idea generation. Users who deal with a diverse set of problems could develop a better understanding of con sumer needs. They might apply this body of knowledge to idea gener ation and propose more useful and feasible ideas, which are important measures of high-quality ideas [7,43]. Hence, it is reasonable to suppose that broad interactions in a SC can produce positive spillover effects on individuals’ innovation performance in an IC. Following the above dis cussion. we hypothesize

H2a. Interaction (i.e., help-giving and help-seeking) breadth in a SC has a positive effect on the quantity of ideas users propose in an IC.

H2b. Interaction (i.e., help-giving and help-seeking) breadth in a SC has a positive effect on the quality of ideas users propose in an IC.

## 3.2.3. Effects of interaction depth in SCs on user innovation

Apart from breadth, depth is another important interaction feature, which is defined as the degree of individuals’ concentration on certain problem domains [23]. As mentioned earlier, idea generation is a pro duction process that activates and utilizes previously stored knowledge to generate ideas [18,74]. In the process of giving and seeking help, individuals who interact with others within a certain knowledge domain repeatedly receive homogeneous stimulation, which is considered beneficial for conceiving ideas quickly and deeply [75]. Additionally, concentrating on a certain domain helps users accumulate relevant in formation in more depth and build a deeper understanding of the actual problem, which contributes to developing feasible solutions and ideas. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that interaction depth is positively related to users’ innovation performance, including idea quantity and quality.

However, limited by the individual’s knowledge base and the ability to process massive information, the positive effect of interaction depth is not linear. It increases at a decreasing rate and has a maximum value that varies from person to person. Further, if an individual only interacts with others on problems within one or a few knowledge domains, he or she is continuously exposed to considerable homogeneous knowledge. Under this circumstance, cognitive fixation may occur as a manifestation of cognitive interference [75]. As a result, individuals are restricted from accessing diverse knowledge bases and questioning their own perspec tives on a problem, which reduces the possibility of continuous ideation and the chances of coming up with high-quality ideas.

Taken together, two latent functions exist. While the positive effect increases at a decreasing rate and eventually levels off, the negative effect rises as the interaction deepens. As a result, the two latent func tions are combined additively to explain an inverted U-shaped rela tionship, which reveals the “net effect” of interaction depth on users innovation performance. We thus propose that individuals with a moderate level of interaction in the SC outperform those with a high or low level of interaction. Therefore, we hypothesize

H3a. Interaction (i.e., help-giving and help-seeking) depth in a SC has an inverted U-shaped relationship with the quantity of ideas users pro pose in an IC.

H3b. Interaction (i.e., help-giving and help-seeking) depth in a SC has an inverted U-shaped relationship with the quality of ideas users pro pose in an IC.

## 3.2.4. Moderating role of interaction breadth

As mentioned, deep interaction may result in cognitive fixation, a tendency to focus on certain domains of knowledge, which can hinder individuals from thinking in an innovative way. Research in the cogni tive psychology literature suggests that interacting with diverse others may be one way to reduce cognitive fixation effects [22]. By interacting with diverse others, individuals can be exposed to diverse information and are more likely to redefine problems and to propose creative ideas [76,77]. In a SC of OIPs, help giving and help seeking across multiple problem categories enable individuals to exchange diverse information and stimulate individuals’ domain-relevant knowledge. The frequent category switching prevents users from being restricted to a certain knowledge base and thinking framework for solving problems. Following this line of reasoning, broad interaction in an SC, which represents the focal user’s engagement in a variety of problem domains, is supposed to mitigate the negative effects of cognitive fixation on an individual’s subsequent likelihood of generating ideas, resulting in a flattening of the curve. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H4a. Interaction breadth moderates the inverted U-shaped relation ship between interaction depth and the quantity of ideas users propose in an IC.

H4b. Interaction breadth moderates the inverted U-shaped relation ship between interaction depth and the quality of ideas users propose in an IC.

## 4. Research context and data

## 4.1. Research context

We conducted our research using data from the Trailblazer Com munity, the OIP of Salesforce, which was formally initiated in 2007 and claims to be the world’s first customer relationship management (CRM) platform. In the Trailblazer Community, registered users are allowed to post ideas, browse, comment and vote on other members’ ideas, and ask and answer questions. Notably, ideation- and question-related activities are carried out in different sections, IdeaExchange and Answers, repre senting the IC and SC respectively. Fig. 2 shows a screenshot of the Trailblazer Community. We believe that this OIP is a well-suited research setting for our research for the following reasons. First, the Trailblazer Community is a typical example of an OIP that cohosts an IC and a $s \mathrm { C } ,$ allowing us to investigate the spillover effects of user inter action in a SC on innovation performance in an IC. Second, after long term observation, we found that the platform has hundreds of weekly active users and thousands of posts, and all the activity records are accessible, containing details of ideas, comments, votes, answers, and questions. Such user interactive activities provide sufficient data for our empirical analysis. Third, users participate in the IC and SC voluntarily. Therefore, social influence is one of the main factors driving user engagement, in addition to personal factors, offering an opportunity to investigate social interactions without too much external interference.

We randomly collected 3162 users in the Trailblazer Community and crawled all the ideation- and question-related activity records from the dates they joined the platform to January 2020, with some of them participating in the SC and others not. A total of nearly 400,000 records were collected. To examine the fine-grained effect and ensure the observability of individuals’ idea contributions, we followed previous studies and constructed the panel data at the user–month level [29,78]. We conducted two studies to test our hypotheses. Study 1 tested the spillover effect (H1a/b). Specifically, we leveraged a quasi-natural experiment. Considering the establishment time of the SC in Trail blazer Community and the joining date of each user, we selected user data from January 2009 to December 2010. Finally, 1174 users remained, including 182 users who participated in the SC and 992 users who did not. Study 2 explored the relationships between interaction breadth and depth and innovation performance (H2a/b, H3a/b, and H4a/b). A fixed effects regression was conducted using the user-month level data of 2957 users from January 2018 to December 2019.

## 4.2. Variable measures

Dependent Variables. Drawing on previous studies of user innovation [79], we measure the innovation performance of users using two vari ables: $I d e a \_ Q u a n t i t y _ { i t }$ and Idea\_Quality . Idea\_Quantity refers to the total number of user i’s ideas posted in month t. In terms of Idea $Q u a l i t y _ { i t } ,$ we use the scores from other members’ votes to measure the quality of user i’s ideas posted in month t. In the context of OIP, crowd voting is widely believed to have results similar to expert voting at a relatively low cost. Consequently, community members are widely invited by the firm to evaluate ideas, as a means of preliminary idea screening. Ideas with higher quality tend to get more support and scores from crowds, so the number of support or scores servers as a signal to judge the idea quality [43]. If user i has proposed more than one idea in month t, we take the average scores of all ideas in the given month [80].

Independent Variables. We are interested in understanding how users change their idea contribution behavior after participating in a SC compared with users who do not participate. Therefore, our focal in dependent variable is $S C P a r t _ { i t } ,$ a binary variable that takes the value of 1 if user i participated in the SC activities during the given month t and 0 otherwise. Notably, different users participated in SC activities at different points of time during the estimation period.

In addition, we explored how the features of interaction, in terms of depth and breadth, affect users’ innovation performance. In the Trail blazer Community, user interactions in the SC are assigned under a certain topic (e.g., collaboration, sales and marketing, security). The difference in the number of interactions under diverse topics indicates the degree to which users are concentrated on a few categories of problem domains and thus is used to describe the depth of user inter action. The greater the difference in the degree of interaction between users in each topic, the greater the depth of interaction. In statistics, variance reflects the degree of dispersion of a set of data, which meets the needs of this study to measure the depth of user interaction. Hence, aiming to measure the depth of help giving and help seeking, we use the variances of topics involved in user i’s answers and questions during month t, denoted as Depth\_answer and Depth\_question , respectively. In addition, user interaction may span a wide range of topics, and the number of topic categories is an intuitive measure of the breadth of user interaction [22]. Therefore, we used the number of topics involved in user i’s answers and questions during month t as a measure of the breadth of help giving and help seeking, which are denoted as Bread th\_answer and Breadth\_question , respectively.

## 5. Study 1

Study 1 tests the hypothesis on the spillover effect of participating in a SC on individuals’ innovation performance in an IC (H1a/b). However, it is possible that users’ specific unobserved factors may jointly deter mine their decisions to participate in a SC and their effect on innovation performance at different levels. For example, whether the focal user is an employee of Salesforce may simultaneously influence both whether he or she participates in the SC or not and the following impact of the participation. Thus, endogeneity issues could arise due to self-selection, which should be controlled for accurate identification of the effect of interactions in the SC.

To solve the above issue, we utilized propensity score matching (PSM) in combination with difference-in-difference analysis (DID), as recommended by Huang et al. [83] and Wu et al. [84]. They represent the difference in pre- and post-participation in a SC between the treat ment group (users who participate in the SC) and the control group (users who never participate in the SC).

## 5.1. Propensity score matching

PSM aims to match treatment and control group samples whose related characteristics are similar to achieve statistical equilibrium [85]. Following the steps suggested by Goh et al. [86], we first estimated the propensity scores using a logit model that included the users’ back ground variables, which could impact users’ choice or the effort users spend in the SC. Prior research has identified a number of factors that impact the decision to participate in an online community, including users’ gender and tenure in a community [29], users’ concerns about privacy, and their propensity to contribute to and engage in a commu nity [84,86,87]. Therefore, we identified the following covariates: (1) gender (Female ); (2) tenure (Tenure ); (3) whether a user disclosed his or her location (Location ); (4) whether a user disclosed the firm he or she belonged to (Firm ); (5) integrity of personal information, which is measured by the number of personal information items displayed by the user (Integrity ); (6) whether a user is an employee of Salesforce (Rela tionship ); and (7) the total number of ideas, comments, and votes created by the focal user in the IC before his or her participation in the SC (Ideas\_num , Comments\_num , Votes\_num ).

We next matched the users in the treatment and control groups using the one-to-one nearest neighbor matching method [88]. Each user who participated in the SC was paired with a nonparticipating user with similar characteristics, allowing a fairer comparison of participation behavior between the two groups of users. The number of matched sample users is 328. Finally, to assess the matching quality, we per formed t tests to check whether our treated–control matching process was adequately balanced. The results are shown in Table 4, which clearly shows that after matching, the propensity score distributions for the two groups are transformed from obviously different to almost the same. Therefore, the sample achieves a covariate balance, which means that the sample selection bias is mitigated.

## 5.2. DID model

Having constructed a control group of users, we then proceeded to estimate the spillover effects of SC participation on innovation perfor mance in an IC with a DID approach, which allows us to take advantage of panel data to control for both time-specific and user-specific effects. Using data on the treatment and control users, the DID was estimated using the following equation:

$$
\text { InnovPerf } _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text { Treat } _ {i} \times \text { SCPart } _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} X _ {i t} + \mu_ {i} + \theta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}
$$

In the equation, InnovPerf<sub>it</sub> refers to Idea\_Quantity<sub>it</sub> and Idea\_Quality<sub>it</sub>. Treat is a dummy variable that equals 1 if a user is in the treatment group and 0 otherwise. $S C P a r _ { i t }$ is also a dummy variable that equals 1 if a user has participated in the SC and 0 if a user has not participated yet. The coefficient $\beta _ { 1 }$ of the interaction term $T r e a t _ { i } \times S C P a r t _ { i t }$ assesses how the individual innovation performance in the treated group change after participating in the SC contrast to that of the control group during the same period. $X _ { i t }$ represents the control variables related to users’ inter active activities in IC, including CommentsNum and VotesNum . μ rep resents the user fixed effects. θ is a time-period dummy variable, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the error term.

## 6. Study 2

## 6.1. Regression model

To explore the effects of the breadth and depth of interactions in the SC on users’ innovation performance (H2a/b and H3a/b), we estab lished the following two estimation equations with fixed effects:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { InnovPerf } _ {i t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text { Breadth } _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \text { Depth } _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \text { Depth } _ {i t} ^ {2} + \beta_ {4} X _ {i t} + \mu_ {i} + \theta_ {t} \\ & + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

Drawing on the specification of testing moderation effects in inverted U-shaped relationships [90], we investigated whether a wide interaction breadth could moderate the main spillover effects of interaction depth on user’s innovation performance (H4a/b) using the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {InnovPerf} _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Breadth} _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} \text {Depth} _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} \text {Depth} _ {i t} ^ {2} + \beta_ {4} \text {Depth} _ {i t} \\ \times \text {Breadth} _ {i t} + \beta_ {5} \text {Depth} _ {i t} ^ {2} \times \text {Breadth} _ {i t} + \beta_ {6} X _ {i t} + \mu_ {i} + \theta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}
$$

Accordingly, with the above specification, the moderation effect does not depend on any other coefficient than $\beta _ { 5 } ,$ nor on specific values of the moderator. Testing for flattening or steepening is equivalent to testing whether $\beta _ { 5 }$ is significant [90]. ## 6.2. Empirical results

Table 8 reports the main results of the FE regression of M2 and M3. From the table we can see that the breadth of help giving and help seeking both have positive and significant effects on idea quantity and quality, thus supporting H2a and H2b. In addition, Columns (2) and (5) in Table 8 indicate that the coefficients of the quadratic terms of inter action depth are negative and significant, implving a nonlinear relationship between interaction depth of and users’ innovation performance. This result is in line with our expectations regarding H3a and H3b. Though necessary, a significant coefficient of the quadratic terms alone is not sufficient to confirm a quadratic relationship [90], and further verification were conducted in next Section 6.3. H4a/b hy pothesizes that interaction breadth moderates the nonlinear effect of the interaction depth on the innovation performance. Columns (3) and (6) in Table 8 present the regression results for H4a/b. For help giving (i.e., answer), the coefficients of the interaction term $D e p t h _ { i t } ^ { 2 } \times B r e a d t h _ { i t }$ are insignificant, which indicates that the breadth of help giving does not moderate the nonlinear relationship between interaction depth and innovation performance. However, the coefficients of the interaction term $D e p t h _ { i t } ^ { 2 } \times$ Breadth are significant in terms of help seeking, implying that the inverted U-shaped curve between interaction depth and idea contributions flattened as the breadth increased in the case of help seeking. The findings provide partial support for H4a/b (i.e., only in the case of participating in help-seeking behaviors in the SC).

## 6.3. Robustness check for the inverted U-shaped relationship

To test the robustness of the above analysis, we followed the method developed by Lind and Mehlum [91]. First, we used the Wald test to evaluate the joint significance of different variable effects. Then, we further tested whether the slope was sufficiently steep at both ends of the data range. Finally, we performed Fieller confidence interval tests to examine whether the turning point is located well within the data range. The results are shown in Table 9 and are consistent between the two types of interaction activities (i.e., help giving and help seeking). Therefore, H3a and H3b regarding the inverted U-shaped relationship between the depth of interactions and innovation performance were further supported.

## 7. Discussion

Given insufficient innovation output from product users, a number of firm-hosted OIPs have taken various strategies to motivate users’ idea contributions, such as establishing badge mechanisms and implement ing financial rewards [92]. The emergence of a SC provides a new way to promote user contributions. Drawing on the innovation management literature and related research on spillover effects, this article explores the important role of a SC for firm-hosted OIPs by examining the spill over effects of participating in a SC (i.e., seeking or giving help) on users innovation performance in an IC. To alleviate the self-selection problem, we applied the propensity score matching method to match the treated and control groups, further improving the causal identifications. The regression results of the matched samples suggest that users increase both the quantity and quality of their idea contributions after partici pating in a SC. Then we conducted a fixed effects regression to further explore how the breadth and depth of interactions individually and jointly influence user innovation.

The results support most of our hypotheses. First, interaction breadth (including help seeking and help giving) and users’ innovation perfor mance are positively related, while interaction depth and users’ inno vation performance have an inverted U-shaped relationship. The findings suggest that when users seek help from or give help to other members, they likely activate stored knowledge and acquire information unintendedly in a SC for the further ideation process in an IC. In particular, wide interaction enables users to access diverse knowledge and information, which contributes to the generation of new and highquality ideas. At the same time, deep interaction, which means having in-depth knowledge in a certain domain, is beneficial to conceiving ideas under a certain level. but could be detrimental when it exceeds that level, due to cognitive fixation. Moreover, interaction breadth is found to moderate the above inverted U-shaped relationship in the case of help seeking. However, it is not applicable to help giving. To some extent, the results validate the approach to reducing cognitive fixation proposed by previous studies [22]. A difference between help giving and help seeking might be that individuals who answer a large number of questions in certain problem domains are more likely to construct and strengthen their thinking framework than those who ask questions. ## 7.1. Implications

Our research adds to previous work in several ways. First, it contributes to the literature on online innovation platforms. By sum marizing the characteristics of spillover effects, this research extends the application of spillover effects to the context of online open innovation platforms. In previous studies, SC has not received sufficient attention. This paper offers complementary insights by examining the spillover effects of user interaction in a SC on user innovation performance in an IC in OIPs, thus answering calls for studying the value of SCs and its relationship with ICs in OIPs [7]. In addition, previous studies have mainly investigated how users’ idea-focused interaction (e.g., providing feedback or comments on existing ideas) contributes to idea generation through integrating and reorganizing current information [43–45]. Other forms of interactions, such as problem-focused (e.g., help giving and help seeking in this research), are understudied. Our study explores the spillover effects of help giving and help seeking in a SC on users’ idea contributions in an IC. The results suggest that an individual’s innova tion performance benefits from help giving/seeking, thus offering in sights supplementary to previous study on how user interaction affects user innovation.

Second, our work also contributes to the literature on innovation management and organizational creativity. Based on previous research on social interaction, knowledge structure, and individual innovation, this study investigates the different effects of breadth and depth of help giving/seeking on user innovation. Although prior work has offered valuable insights into how users’ interactions contribute to their idea generation, and emphasizes the roles of interaction characteristics (i.e., breadth and depth), less is known about how one interaction charac teristic is moderated by the other. Our study fills this gap by investi gating how interaction breadth and depth interact to affect idea generation. Meanwhile, our findings suggest that research on organi zational creativity should focus on individuals’ interaction structure, as this might explain the different findings in previous studies on the relationship between help giving/seeking and creativity. To be inno vative, broad interaction is critical for individuals because it provides cross-domain knowledge [64]. Deep interaction is also beneficial, as it enhances an individual’s understanding of things [19]. However, its negative impact should be considered to avoid falling into cognitive fixation.

Our research also has managerial implications for firm-hosted OIPs that rely on users’ idea contributions. First, it provides firms with valuable advice for designing OIPS. In reality, most firm-hosted OIPs have created ICs to gain access to external innovation resources.

However, only a few have provided a SC for users to communicate with each other (e.g., Dell, Salesforce, and Xiaomi). Our results indicate that cohosting an IC and a SC have benefits because individuals’ knowledge can be enriched through problem-focused interaction activities per formed in a SC, which in turn stimulate users to generate more and higher-quality ideas. Second, our findings suggest that companies should pay special attention to help-seeking behavior. Many firm-hosted OIPs award virtual medals to the users who offers substantial help to other community members, but often ignore the individuals who ask for help. Our results reveal that help seeking can also play a vital role in stimulating innovation. Therefore, firm-hosted OIPs might consider setting up appropriate incentives for help-seeking behavior. Third, our study shows that the interaction structures in terms of interaction breadth and depth have significant impacts on individual innovation outputs, including both beneficial and detrimental aspects. To take full advantage of the beneficial effects, platforms are encouraged to proac tively invite users to answer or ask questions to promote individuals engagement and prevent them from only focusing on a few problem domains.

## 7.2. Limitations and future research

Despite its theoretical and practical contributions, our research has limitations that make further investigation desirable. First, our findings are limited in generalizability, given that the data were collected from only one OIP. The results might vary for different OIPs across diverse cultural and product contexts. The research assumptions should be repeated across other OIPs. Meanwhile, personal characteristics are key factors in studying user behavior. Although our study was conducted using a fixed effects model and several control variables were considered in the model, some factors, such as users’ absorption capacity and their identity (e.g., customers, employees, or partners) [3,93], are difficult to measure in this research and thus were not considered. Future studie should consider these potential factors to provide a further explanation.

Second, differently from previous literature that focused on idearelated interactions, we examined problem-related interactions. How ever, we did not discuss the two types of interactions at the same time. It would be interesting to compare the difference and examine the joint effect of the two types of interaction on user innovation.

Third, although we have applied a mixed approach of PSM and DID, we acknowledge that the endogeneity concern caused by the user's self selection is still not fully resolved. To completely solve this issue, a randomized field experiment may be required for future research. Finally, we explored help-giving/-seeking behavior in a SC. In fact, such activities might also be observable in an IC, yet little attention has been given to them in prior OIP literature. One possible reason might be the difficulty of conducting content analysis of the interactions and differ entiating various interaction types. Simply treating all interactions as one category might mask the richness of the text. Therefore, it would be interesting for future research to study the role of interaction using the method of text analysis.

## 7.3. Conclusion

As the concept of open innovation becomes more and more popular, an increasing number of companies have established open innovation platforms to gain access to external innovation resources. User innova tion based on such platforms has become an essential part of firm innovation in many areas. How to maintain continuous user engagement and promote high-quality user contributions are crucial to the operation of these open innovation platforms. Based on a theoretical synthesis of innovation management studies, this study explores the spillover effects of users’ interaction behavior in support communities, including help giving and help seeking, on their idea contributions in innovation communities. The findings show that users’ participation in SC in teractions has a positive spillover effect on their idea contributions in

ICs. In addition, interaction breadth is positively related to users’ idea contributions, while interaction depth has an inverted U-shaped rela tionship with users’ idea contributions. Further, this inverted U-shaped relationship is moderated by interaction breadth in the case of help seeking behaviors. Our study provides an innovative perspective to understand the role of interaction on individuals’ idea contribution, offers practical insights into platform management, and opens up op portunities for future research on user interaction in open innovation platform settings.
