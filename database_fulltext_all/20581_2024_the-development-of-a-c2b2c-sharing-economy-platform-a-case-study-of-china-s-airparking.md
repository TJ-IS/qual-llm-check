## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Sharing economy Business model Digital platform Business model innovation C2B2C

## A B S T R A C T

The emergence of the sharing economy has led to a proliferation of novel platform-based business models in recent years. A particularly prominent type of platform is the C2B2C platform, but our knowledge of the development of these platforms is limited. Through an in-depth examination of the development of Airparking, one of China’s most successful parking-space-sharing platforms, our study reveals that a C2B2C platform de velops in a specific sequence across three phases, with distinct required actions and developmental outcomes. We have also identified the facilitating conditions that are conducive to this process.

## 1. Introduction

The sharing economy, also known as collaborative consumption and the access economy [1,2], refers to a peer-to-peer model facilitated by information technology that enables sharing of underutilized goods and service capacity, without transferring ownership through an interme diary [3]. The dominant business model of the sharing economy is the consumer-to-consumer (C2C) model, where asset or service providers and consumers are the roles participating in the two sides (i.e., supply and demand) of the platform, and it is possible to move between them fluidly and simultaneously [4]. Sharing economy platforms based on the C2C model include Airbnb and Uber, which are leading and innovative platforms in the accommodation and transportation sectors across many countries around the globe.

More recently, an emerging C2B2C business model has evolved from the C2C model, and its unique characteristics and business benefits have attracted the attention of both industry and academia. The C2B2C business model is distinct from the C2C business model in that it in troduces an intermediary $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , the middle “B” refers to a business organization serving as an intermediary that facilitates transactions between the two C-sides). This role of the intermediary may be taken on by the platform itself [5], or the platform could work with other busi nesses to manage, maintain, or operate the assets on the platform, while also delivering consignment, after-sales, and maintenance services [6].

On the surface, the B-side of a C2B2C business model shares some similarities with traditional business intermediaries: they both facilitate the efficient allocation of goods or services between providers and customers through capturing, aggregating, and exploiting information about participating parties [7]. The difference, however, is that the B-side entities do not participate in the process of value creation directly. Instead, they play a complementary role to make the process of matching providers to consumers more efficient or effective (see [8]), and they are typically rewarded not by entities that are downstream along the value chain, but by the platform itself [5]. To cite an example, platforms selling second-hand luxury products often employ a C2B2C business model, working with B-side entities that acquire their products from individual sellers on consignment, and refurbishing them for resale. The B-side entities involved are incentivized by these platforms to guarantee the authenticity and quality of products during the refur bishing process. This approach facilitates resale transactions between sellers and buyers, ensuring trustworthy and reliable exchanges [6,5].

The C2B2C model shares the strengths and advantages of the C2C model while mitigating its weaknesses. In particular, C2C platforms facilitate peer-to-peer transactions, where both the providers and the consumers are typically private entities, not businesses or professional operators. Consequently, there is a vast array of entities and individu alized requirements to cater to, which could limit scalability and profits [9]. The C2B2C model, on the other hand, takes full advantage of the asset-light nature of sharing economy platforms, and the assets or ser vices acquired from the providers are managed by the B-side entities in a complementary manner to unlock economies of scale and maximize their profit potential. In addition, C2C business models may lack control over the quality of the assets or services on offer because the burden of quality maintenance is typically borne by the asset or service provider [10]. Having a B-side intermediary in the context of collaborative con sumption alleviates the burden on the asset or service provider, enhancing the efficiency of managing the assets or services while ensuring the quality and consistency of the platform’s offerings [11].

However, despite the advantages of the C2B2C business model and its growing prevalence in the contemporary business landscape [5], our collective knowledge of this business form is still limited because the majority of studies on the sharing economy are based on C2C platforms (see [12]). The development of C2B2C platforms, as well as their im plications for entrepreneurs, stakeholders, and policymakers, has not been studied adequately. Addressing the lack of knowledge on the emerging C2B2C business form is necessary to ensure the effective development and leverage of these platforms, which may be important for their owners and managers to formulate strategies and undertake the appropriate competitive actions to ensure the success of their business. This knowledge would also be useful for policymakers in crafting effective legislation to regulate the way these platforms operate and compete. On a broader level, addressing this gap would also contribute to our ability to unlock the potential of C2B2C platforms for generating business and social benefits, and to extend the benefits of the sharing economy paradigm to a wider audience [11].

The purpose of our study is to address this knowledge gap using Airparking as a case study. Airparking is an “extreme” ([13], p. 653) or “revelatory” ([14], p. 43) case for the purpose of our study, as it is one of the most established and commercially successful sharing economy platforms for parking spaces in China. In line with the knowledge gap we identified, the objective of our study is to derive insights, grounded in the empirical reality of a real-world organization, on how the C2B2C platform that Airparking represents can be developed and leveraged effectively. More specifically, this study aims to answer the following research question: How does the development of a C2B2C platform unfold? To address this question, we draw evidence from accounts of how the firm started its business in parking space sharing in 2010 and subsequently grew to become one of China’s leading sharing economy platforms in this domain. More importantly, we will focus on con structing a theoretical framework that illustrates the various stages of its development progressively and identifying the facilitating conditions and developmental outcomes of each stage.

## 2. Literature review

Sharing has always been one of the most basic human behaviors [15]. In the present age, consumers are paying more and more attention to responsible and sustainable consumption [16]. The sharing economy paradigm is aligned with this shift in orientation, in which technology-enabled ecosystems are created that bring together a mass of users who pay for temporary access to idle products, assets, and service capacity on IT-enabled digital platforms, but without the transfer of ownership [12]. The sharing economy paradigm is part of a broader trend where the era of asset ownership is transitioning to the era of “light” asset sharing, in which owning assets is no longer seen as the ultimate manifestation of consumer desires [17].

The sharing economy has been studied extensively by scholars from a variety of fields over the past decade, and the existing studies can be broadly categorized into three main research streams (see Table 1). The first stream focuses on the nature of the sharing economy. Research aligned with this stream typically seeks to explain the nature and characteristics of the sharing economy, as well as the contextual factors that shape them. For instance, Hossain [12] is of the view that the sharing economy, which relies on peer-to-peer interactions and network effects for growth, is underpinned by the joint consumption of under-utilized assets in ways that improve efficiency and sustainability and enable consumers to pay for temporary access to goods or services. The nature of this new consumption paradigm, in turn, is influenced by five factors: changing consumer behavior, social networks, electronic markets, mobile devices, and electronic services [4].

The second stream is centered on the implications of the sharing economy. Research on this stream is mainly aimed at investigating the economic, environmental, and social impact of sharing economy plat forms. For example, Roma et al. [18] found that the effect of the growing prominence of Airbnb on incumbents’ prices depends on the type of incumbents (low/medium-end versus high-end hotels), the accommo dation period (weekends versus weekdays), and thus the type of cus tomers looking for accommodation. Another example is the work of Meng et al. [19], who sought to explain why the incumbents, who may be large, resource-rich organizations themselves, found it so challenging to compete with the emerging sharing economy platforms. They pro posed that this may be due to a process of digital attrition where the digital options of the incumbent firms are gradually whittled down by the sharing economy platforms, facilitated by a number of contextual influences that are unfavorable to the former.

The third stream is centered on sharing economic business models. Studies that fall into this category seek to specify and explain the value propositions and revenue streams of these sharing economy platforms. To illustrate, Kavadias et al. [20] described how the value proposition of Airbnb was formed, which leveraged a new technology to platformize and overturn the dominant business model in the hotel industry, and how the industry was transformed when Airbnb used its new model to compete with the incumbents. More specifically, unlike the incumbents, Airbnb does not own or manage property. Due to the nature of this business model, Airbnb does not need large investments to scale up, and can therefore charge lower prices than traditional hotels. In a similar vein, Tseng and Chan [21] studied the strategic actions undertaken by Uber and Airbnb to overcome institutional barriers, gain legitimacy, and justify their new activities. These actions enabled the platforms to create positive brand images, integrate internal resources, and bring in external support to grow their business further.

Notwithstanding the academic and practical significance of these studies, what is apparent from our review of the literature is that research on the sharing economy has largely focused on the C2C model, with a significant proportion of studies using Airbnb and/or Uber as their objects of research (see [12]). C2C platforms such as Airbnb and Uber do indeed embody the main features of the sharing economy paradigm, which include (1) unlocking the value of idle assets; (2) allowing consumers to pay for temporary access rather than ownership, using an internet-based platform; (3) and relying on multi-sided in teractions and network effects for business growth [22]. They also function as an interface connecting different groups of users that interact through a virtual marketplace according to a specific set of rules and guidelines [23]. However, they are only one specific type of sharing economy platform [24]. Of particular relevance to our research question is the proliferation of what have come to be known as C2B2C sharing economy platforms. While there are a number of highly successful platforms today whose business models are configured in this way [5, 25], to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies that have delved into the developmental process for these platforms. Our knowledge of how to establish and nurture these platforms, as well as leverage them for economic gains, is both limited and inadequate.

The C2B2C business model is a more recent adaptation of the C2C model. It combines the strengths and advantages of C2C (e.g., enabling the sharing of idle assets through an asset-light business model) while mitigating some of its key weaknesses, such as the need to cater to a kaleidoscopic array of personalized requirements, inconsistent asset or service quality, and the burden of asset maintenance on the part of the providers [11]. These advantages allow C2B2C platforms to improve their operational and management efficiency, benefit from economies of scale, and create greater value for their stakeholders [9,26]. C2B2C platforms, however, tend to be confronted with a different set of chal lenges, as the intermediary layer could introduce a different set of in terests and represent the legacy means of value creation within the industry [11]. Thus, there may be greater resistance and inertia, as these stakeholders may be required to forsake their existing interests and benefits amid uncertainty over the value of the new business model.

C2B2C platforms also tend to bear more economic risks and operational costs than their C2C counterparts, as they are, to a greater extent, responsible for the management, maintenance, and daily oper ation of the assets and services, on top of having to look after both the providers’ and consumers’ interests [5,20]. Moreover, there could be some assets that are reliant on shared services or a common infra structure. In the case of parking spaces, for example, the owner of an individual parking space in a communal car park would find it chal lenging to list his assets on a sharing platform, because access to the parking space is dependent on access to the communal car park. Without co-opting the managers of the communal car park into the process of value creation, the C2C collaborative consumption of this asset may simply not be viable.

C2B2C sharing economy platforms are also distinct from traditional intermediary models in a number of ways (see Table 2). First, traditional intermediaries tend to insert themselves directly into the value chain for a product or a service and derive economic benefits by introducing a mark-up for their role in the value creation process [27]. Conversely, the entities on the B-sides of C2B2C platforms are complementors (see [28]). The platforms facilitate, but often do not participate directly in, the transactions between the B-side entities and consumers. Thus, the way that the B-side entities are rewarded for their contributions to value creation is different, and incentives are typically distributed by the platform and not the consumers [5,29].

Second, traditional intermediaries take on more responsibilities in relation to the quality of the platform’s offerings, whereas these re sponsibilities are shared to a greater extent with the B-side entities for C2B2C platforms. To illustrate, in a study of accommodation-sharing platforms, Au et al. [30] found that on platforms based on a tradi tional intermediary model such as Airbnb, issues related to the quality and cleanliness of the accommodation are seen as the responsibility of the platform, which manages the assets on offer with a rating mecha nism. In contrast, Xbed, a platform based on the C2B2C model, was working with B-side entities (e.g., renovation firms and other service platforms) that took on this burden on the platform’s behalf. In other words, traditional intermediaries are primarily responsible for ensuring the quality of the asset or service on offer, but for C2B2C platforms, the B-side entities are taking on responsibilities associated with the recovery and maintenance of the asset or service in line with the paradigm of collaborative and repeated consumption [3].

Third, the scalability and greater ease of onboarding B-side entitie mean that the variety of stakeholders that C2B2C platforms may have to deal with is far greater because the entities may be bringing on board their own networks of providers and complementors. Consequently, C2B2C platforms will have to manage and cater to a greater diversity of requirements and interests than traditional intermediary models, while facing the challenge of having to align those requirements and interests with their own [31].

In light of these substantive differences, we posit that prescriptions for the development of C2C platforms or traditional intermediary models may not be relevant or applicable in the context of C2B2C platforms. By addressing this knowledge gap, we hope that our study will contribute more nuanced knowledge that enhances our under standing of how various forms of sharing economy platforms can be developed and leveraged. Platform owners and managers may also benefit from the insights generated by our study as we will provide in dications on how to successfully develop their businesses, with empiri cally supported directions on how to develop a C2B2C platform in practice.

## 3. Research method

The case research method is especially suited for our study because it facilitates the in-depth exploration of “how” questions [32] and the inductive derivation of theoretical concepts related to understudied or novel phenomena [33]. To address our research question, two criteria for case selection were identified. First, the target case must be a sharing economy platform based on the C2B2C business model that has achieved a significant measure of success and has a leading position in its chosen market. This is so that we can formulate theory based on the proven experience of the case organization [34]. Second, the development of its C2B2C platform should ideally have been enacted via a variety of means, as this opens up a broader range of possibilities for our theorizing and the identification of the mechanisms that underlie this process [35]. According to these two criteria, the case of Airparking, one of the most successful online parking space sharing platforms in China, was selected.

The case of Airparking is particularly appropriate for this study, as it is a commercially successful sharing economy platform based on the C2B2C business model. In 2023, it is estimated that Airparking is managing over 2000 contracted projects with over 500,000 parking spaces and over 2 million users on its platform. It is currently operating in 14 cities, including Guangzhou, Qingdao, Wuhan, Changsha, and Taiyuan. Its success has also been widely recognized, as it was named in the "Future Unicorn Enterprises" list released by the China Commercial Industry Research Institute in 2018 and awarded the “Most Innovative Enterprise” award by the Guangdong provincial government. More un commonly for sharing economy platforms in China (e.g., see [36]), Airparking has broken even and has been a profitable enterprise since 2019. A review of published articles on the organization further revealed that Airparking had used a variety of creative developmental mecha nisms in the development of its C2B2C platform [37]. Taking into ac count all of these facts, Airparking clearly fulfills both of our selection criteria perfectly. We wish to note here that we are not claiming that Airparking is the most successful C2B2C platform in China, nor that the C2B2C business model is invariably superior to the C2C model. Never theless, Airparking’s evident success despite intense local and interna tional competition makes the organization an “extreme” ([13], p. 653) or “revelatory” ([14], p. 43) case for the purpose of our study.

## 3.1. Case description

Established in 2015 and based in Guangzhou, China, Airparking uses technological advances in real-time data capture, processing, and anal ysis, such as internet of things (IoT) devices, omnipresent communica tions, and cloud computing, to integrate data on idle parking spaces and provide an online parking service platform aimed at alleviating the parking problems of busy cities by connecting the owners of parking spaces and those seeking them.

Airparking offers different technological solutions for different stakeholders, including customers (asset owners), businesses, and property management. What makes Airparking C2B2C is that it collab orates closely with B-side partners, property management companies that manage parking lots on behalf of the property (asset) owners. After the contracts are signed with the property management companies, Airparking takes charge of infrastructure installation and structural transformation for parking lots and enables their operational capabil ities through the implementation of Airparking’s parking management system to deliver the service of parking space sharing. Asset owners can register parking spaces located in the enabled parking lots, while drivers can also reserve parking spaces and complete the transactions on the user app. The managers in the property management companies can use the property management app to track and analyze day-to-day opera tions and capacity utilization. Drivers on the platform can also access services such as pre-booking, rapid positioning, and intelligent match ing, as well as setting up various payment options (e.g., a park first pay later scheme) via the Airparking app.

## 3.2. Data collection

The data collection of this study had two phases: a preparatory phase and a fieldwork phase. The major objective of the preparatory phase was to conceptualize the phenomenon by reviewing and gathering secondary data about Airparking from a variety of sources, including web pages, interviews conducted by third parties, reports, and other published materials [34]. These materials enhanced our basic knowledge of Air parking, as well as the industry of parking services, and supported the formulation of our subsequent interview questions [38].

The fieldwork phase was focused on gathering primary data based on our research question. It was conducted at Airparking’s office, so that there was an opportunity for the research team to immerse themselves in the context of the organization. As part of this phase, a team of five researchers visited the headquarters of Airparking to conduct interviews with representatives from Airparking’s top management, information technology (IT) department, and various business units (e.g., marketing, sales, and human resources (HR) departments). A total of 16 informants were identified and interviewed. The informants were chosen using chain referral sampling, in which informants were referred to the research team by previous informants [39]. The diverse representation in our pool of informants constituted a “a variety of voices” to allow triangulation and enhanced reliability of our primary data [34,40].

A total of 16 face-to-face interviews (see Appendix A) were conducted in Chinese (Mandarin) using semi-structured interview guides. The interview guides had a standard core of questions on the C2B2C business model and the development of Airparking’s platform over time, and additional questions tailored to each respondent’s particular orga nizational roles and the associated aspects of Airparking’s business strategies, operations, and development that the informants could shed light on [40]. For instance, a Product Manager at Airparking was asked about the growth of the business and the IT department over time. The first interview was conducted with an informant (a co-founder of the platform) who could provide an overview of the phenomenon under study. which enabled the mental concept of the phenomenon to be validated or even modified at this early stage of research [34].

In line with the principles of qualitative research, we tried to be sensitive to possible biases and systematic distortions in term of the information we were receiving. In particular, we tried to ensure that each of our findings was triangulated by two different sources of data [41]. The data were collected and analyzed in the original language and only translated at the time of writing [19]. To ensure data accuracy and completeness, all interviews were recorded, transcribed, and collated digitally [32]. Each interview lasted for about 45 minutes to an hour. One member of the research team was responsible for translating all of the data collected, to ensure consistency, but the translated transcripts were checked and verified carefully and independently by the other members. Further, to ensure the accuracy of our data, the transcripts were sent to and checked by the corresponding informants indepen dently [42].

## 3.3. Data analysis

Consistently with the conventions of Glaserian grounded theory [43], the process of data analysis was conducted iteratively and in parallel with data collection, as flexibility is one of the main advantages of the case research method [44]. To manage our voluminous dataset, the strategies of visual mapping and narrative strategies were first used to organize our data [34]. The visual mapping strategy was useful in condensing the dense textual data from our transcripts into a diagram matic form and in sequencing the events, activities, and decisions that unfolded [45], while the narrative strategy was used to create a coherent account of the process of platform development [46]. Both the visual maps and the narrative developed through these strategies were also subsequently validated with our informants to ensure the accuracy of our interpretation of what happened during the process of platform development [34].

Our qualitative data from the interview transcripts and other sources were subject to multiple rounds of open, selective, and theoretical coding, which corresponded to the derivation of a number of first-order concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate theoretical dimensions [47]. More specifically, we used open coding to assign descriptive labels to our data to form first-order concepts, which were, wherever possible, taken directly from the words used by our informants [48]. Examples of our first-order concepts include “taking care of existing stakeholders,” “developing new software modules,” and “explaining the value of our services,” to extract themes informants identified as pertinent in their lingo [49]. Forty-six first-order concepts were identified in total.

We then used selective coding to abstract the first-order concepts to second-order themes [47], which involves creating our own theoretical labels for conditions, actions, interactions, and consequences associated with the phenomenon under study [50]. We were also especially careful that the second-order themes were derived from our data, rather than “force-fitted” from external theoretical frameworks or our own pre conceptions [49]. To illustrate, the first-order concepts of “taking care of existing stakeholders,” “developing new software modules,” and “explaining the value of our services” were abstracted as the second-order themes of “complementor acquisition,” “structural trans formation,” and “user education,” respectively. As a result of selective coding, 10 key steps of the developmental process and three facilitating conditions of Airparking’s C2B2C platform emerged.

Finally, theoretical coding was used to group the second-order themes into aggregate theoretical dimensions [43]. To extend the example we have used previously, the second-order themes of “com plementor identification,” “structural transformation,” and “user edu cation” were grouped under the aggregate dimension of “enhancing operations,” as they were focused on improving the internal operations of Airparking. If we were confronted with data that challenged, or did not fit with the current coding scheme (e.g., evidence that necessitated adding, amending, integrating, or deleting existing first-order concepts or second-order themes–see [51]), the coding scheme would be modi fied and the coding process would be restarted accordingly [52]. In this iterative manner, we were able to move beyond description to a more abstract level of conceptualization [53], distilling our interview data into higher-order themes and aggregate dimensions representing our theoretical interpretation [47].

Our theory was derived and gradually shaped from this process of iterating between data and theory [54], until a state of theoretical saturation was achieved [55], where our inductively derived model could fully explain case data and no new evidence could be found and incorporated to improve our theoretical framework [44]. A sample data structure (as prescribed by [47]) is presented in Appendix B to sum marize some of the first-order concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions that eventuated from our analysis, and illustrate how open, selective, and theoretical coding were conducted.

## 4. Findings

The findings from our study of Airparking indicate that the devel opment of a C2B2C platform is a process that traverses at least three phases (see Fig. 1). As each of the phases is specific to the development of a particular aspect of its business model, we term the phases (1) ensuring supply, (2) enhancing operations, and (3) expanding market, respectively. More importantly, each phase is composed of distinct steps, which in turn lead to different developmental outcomes over time. Along with the process we uncovered, we found that there are also three facilitating environmental conditions that were conducive to all the phases of the process. We present our findings for each of the phases in the following subsections.

## 4.1. Phase 1–ensuring supply

At the point of Airparking’s inception, on-street and public parking spaces were very limited and only accounted for a small proportion of the parking spaces in China. The vast majority of parking spaces were private, provided by property developers and managed by property management companies [56]. Consequently, for Airparking’s business model to work, it required the platform to first identify as many property management companies to work with as possible [57]. The PC product and hardware manager of Airparking explained the importance of working with these partners, since it was unfeasible to negotiate with the owners of parking spaces individually: “Parking lots in China are all managed by property management companies, and we need to coop erate with them to transform and integrate our facilities and equipment. Therefore, the first step is to gain the cooperation of the property management companies.”

We refer to this activity as B-side partner acquisition, defined as the identification and selection of business partners to play a complemen tary role in value creation, improving or augmenting the focal organi zation’s current operational and technological capabilities. This selection may be based on criteria such as resources, partner status, local identity, past association, and established distribution channels in the market. A business is more likely to succeed when partners possess complementary missions, resource capabilities, managerial capabilities, and other attributes that create a strategic fit in which the bargaining power of both sides is evenly matched [58].

Further, with the nature of parking services, property management companies are responsible for managing the car spaces, as well as ensuring the security of cars and the community. This becomes an important consideration for customers when they are choosing a parking service provider [59]. Therefore, as the B-side for Airparking in the C2B2C sharing economy business model, property management com panies would manage the assets on offer (i.e., the idle parking spaces from customers) and facilitate the delivery of parking services to cus tomers. To strike a balance between the adequacy of supply and man agement efficiency, Airparking had to secure partnerships with a critical mass of property management companies [57] and establish long-term contracts with them. Exclusive cooperation is especially required given that the installation of the required systems and infrastructure is necessary for Airparking’s business model to work, which is incompat ible with other systems provided by other companies. The CEO of Air parking explained, “We have to sign a contract with the property management companies. After signing the contract, we will renovate the entrance and exit of the parking lot, encourage the car space owner to join our platform, and educate them to use our service. This is different from the C2C model.”

After Airparking formed partnerships with the property companies to ensure a stable supply of parking spaces, the needs of the stakeholders within the legacy system had to be accounted for. In other words, there is a need for interest alignment to circumvent potential resistance that could scupper the new processes that Airparking was hoping to put in place. Interest alignment refers to accounting for, and making adjust ments to, the interest relationships within a system based on the changes to every stakeholder’s role. The CEO of Airparking emphasized the need for interest alignment: “We need to reconstruct the interest relation ships, between the property management companies, the car space owners, the drivers, and our platform … and make the rewards and incentive mechanisms explicit and transparent.”

In particular, Airparking needed to align the interests of all stake holders within this new model to encourage the existing stakeholders to commit to or join this new system, and ensure that everyone’s interests and welfare were equitably accounted for and would be protected. The reason people participate in the sharing economy and accept this mode of consumption is that there are shared interests, benefits, or profits and/or a more efficient or convenient lifestyle [1]. Therefore, Air parking needs to align the interests for all former stakeholders associ ated with traditional parking services and encourage these stakeholders to join this new system, including the car space owners, the drivers, and the property management companies and others who provide comple mentary services (e.g., security personnel and parking attendants). This is because the participation and support of stakeholders are still needed in the new mode of consumption, and if the interests are not aligned, these stakeholders may actively work against the success or effective functioning of the new system. The importance of interest alignment in the case of Airparking resonates with the findings of management scholars, who found that it is one of the key determinants of collabo rative success for an organization. Conversely, a misalignment of in terests can lead to reduced commitment, which gradually weakens the relationships between stakeholders over time [60].

Following the acquisition of B-side partners and the alignment of interests, our study found that infrastructure installation is the next required step of the first phase to transform the acquired parking spaces, by digitalizing their information and putting in place the equipment and software to enable real-time collaborative consumption. With the nature of parking lots, it is necessary for Airparking to enable traditional car parks to make their supply available to share on the platform through renovating the parking infrastructure, as well as installing license plate recognition systems, which include components such as cameras, gan tries, barriers, sensors, and monitoring software. The facilities manager of Airparking explained, “After we have contracted a car park, we will install the foundational infrastructure, including earthwork and the installation of pipes and wiring.” The VP of infrastructure added, “After renovating (the car park), we will install our equipment and our systems. We can then use this system to take over the entire parking lot."

With the infrastructure installed, the supply of parking spaces ac quired can then be made available to share on the platform. The drivers who use Airparking to reserve a parking space and are recognized by the system can have their transactions processed by the license plate recognition system automatically without any human intervention. This is in line with the existing view of digital transformation, which suggests that the appropriate IT infrastructure makes it possible to explore a broader process of business transformation, while developing new products, services, and markets more quickly [61]. More specifically, infrastructure installation enhances organizational responsiveness because it enables firms to sense and respond to change in the envi ronment and subsequently seize new opportunities by facilitating changes at the operational, structural, and strategic levels [62].

As a result of the first ensuring supply phase, economies of scale were achieved by Airparking as it became more efficient to manage and offer the car spaces on the platform with the support of the B-side entities and the other relevant stakeholders. The accessibility of idle assets for cus tomers is high, which is a critical factor for survival of startups in the sharing economy [16]. According to the CEO of Airparking, “Our biggest competitor, ETCP, has engaged in 5000 projects (car parks) in five years, and we have now close to 2000 projects in only two years.” Compared with its competitors, Airparking’s founding team boasts extensive experience in the real estate industry, which equips them with superior knowledge and contacts, helping them to acquire B-side partners effectively and form collaborative relationships with them. These re lationships allowed them to take a greater diversity of stakeholders into account when reconstructing interest relationships, enabling their new business model. Additionally, working with B-side partners, as opposed to individual providers of parking spaces, meant that the solution developed for one partner could be reused or easily adapted for another. This replicability has allowed Airparking to scale up rapidly from its inception, outpacing its competitors, because it makes the procurement of the necessary infrastructure and supplies more economically viable and unlocks the potential for network effects among Airparking’s drivers and car space owners [63]. This outcome echoes similar findings in the study of Murillo et al. [64], who found that when the availability of assets on offer is high, the likelihood of satisfying consumers in an effective and timely manner is enhanced, and the larger the scale of the platform’s offerings, the less expensive the transactions tended to be.

In addition, Airparking achieved the required stakeholder commitment to ensure an adequate supply of parking spaces to offer with a critical mass of car parks ready to join in this new business model, by identifying and aligning corresponding interests and expectations for each stake holder, such as incumbent staff within the property management com panies, car space owners, and even the residents in the communities in which the car parks are located. According to the co-founder of Air parking, “As long as we reasonably distribute part of our profits to property management companies, they will take the initiative to help us in daily operations and promotion.” This resonates with previous studies that suggest that the transition from a system that relies on labor or simple facilities to one that is fully digitalized with advanced IT capa bilities requires simultaneous organizational and institutional change to be successful [65]. During the process of breaking down the existing system and transforming to the newly emerging system, the incumbent stakeholders could resist this transformation to safeguard their vested interests [66]. When a new system is still new and most vulnerable in its initial stages of development, cooperation with the relevant stake holders and securing their commitment become all the more critical, as the relevant stakeholders are more likely to facilitate the development of the new system positively [67].

## 4.2. Phase 2—enhancing operations

As the scale of Airparking’s business grew, the platform began to realize that parking service is more than simply providing a parking space: It is the complementary services that make it valuable for cus tomers. At the same time, Airparking realized that it was impossible to be at every car park it managed, providing tailored and a range of complementary services to all of its end users. Consequently, com plementor acquisition became the necessary next step in the second phase of the development of Airparking’s operational capabilities. Com plementors are defined as companies that independently provide related products or services directly to mutual customers, which increases the value of each other’s offerings [23,28]. The complementors play an important role in delivery of services or products. Airparking cannot optimize value creation unless all complementary components are pre sent within the ecosystem of parking space sharing [68]. In the context of Airparking, as an online digital platform, complementors are able to co-create value with the platform and include, for example, parking attendants who have been hired to maintain the operation of parking lots in the present ecosystem of parking services; security personnel who have been hired to ensure the security of the end users’ cars and the availability of spaces along the process of providing parking services; and fee collectors at parking booths. These complementors offer different services independently and contribute to the overall experience of parking in this ecosystem. The sales director of Airparking explained the importance of co-opting these complementors in the context of providing parking services in China: “The security personnel, parking attendants, and property managers are all part of the structure. We need to guarantee the interests of all complementors in this structure. Our model now is that the security personnel will maintain the parking lot and help us to recommend users to use our product … in turn, we would reward them for their contributions in this new role … This [mutually beneficial] approach greatly incentivizes the security personnel to accept and participate in the new business model actively.

In realizing this, Airparking adapted its processes to carve out new roles for these complementors, allowing them to contribute additional value while sharing some of their profits with this stakeholder group. This is important because, as previous studies suggest, complementors are integral to the development of platform-based ecosystems, but this is only if what they offer in terms of products or services can be seamlessly integrated with the platform’s own offerings [69].

With the supply of parking spaces and complementors acquired, Airparking subsequently enacted structural transformation to capitalize on this development fully. We define structural transformation as making fundamental changes in organizational structures, decisionmaking flows, and business operations as a result of digital trans formation with the implementation and use of new digital technologies [70]. In the case of Airparking, this transformation involved changes in multiple business dimensions, including the business model (tran sitioning to the C2B2C sharing economy business model), operations (digitalized and IT-enabled business and decision-making processes), and customer experience (from manual to digitally enabled products and services). This simultaneously influences fundamental behavior changes within the organization (in terms of digital skills and organi zational culture) and the entire value system [71].

In particular, Airparking enacted structural transformation by reorienting its business to provide new technical solutions to its B-side partners. The solutions included infrastructure installation with advanced facilities and renovating existing infrastructure to connect Airparking’s systems, such as parking lot management systems and cloud-based online management systems. There were also B-side appli cations for property management companies to manage car parks and parking space and analyze operational data to support performance review and decision-making, as well as C-side applications for drivers and car space owners to share, check, and book parking spaces and facilitate transactions. These initiatives led to increased automation and enhanced efficiency of business operations, as well as raising the quality of parking services and the consumer experience. The VP of operations of Airparking described the systems and functions that were introduced as a result of structural transformation: “Airparking has several modules. The first is our apps, the second is our online management system. After using our system, all operational data will be synchronized to the cor responding car park on our cloud."

This finding is in line with Battleson et al. ’s [72] hierarchy of IT-enabled business transformations. The structural transformation un dertaken by Airparking is at the higher levels in this hierarchy, which will bestow greater benefits on the platform corresponding with a higher degree of changes in organizational routines, such as the logic of structuring (e.g., from focusing merely on individual cities to aggregated regions), reporting relationships (e.g., from producing periodic reports to the real-time capture of daily performance), performance assessment criteria (e.g., from the simple analysis of revenue to more sophisticated analyses such as asset utilization), and informational flow (e.g., from relying on traditional Excel tables and reports to more complex visual izations on apps). Therefore, the role of IT within organizations has evolved from its previous focus on efficiency enhancements (i.e., auto mation) to its current role as a fundamental enabler in creating and maintaining a flexible business network of interorganizational arrangements.

After the structural transformation was completed, Airparking’s systems and applications designed for the B-side entities and com plementor, such as property company managers, owners, and even the security personnel, transformed their original organizational structure and business processes. For this new system to work and operate smoothly, there is a need to empower stakeholders and complementors to embrace and work with the new structure and processes. Conse quently, Airparking provided comprehensive training programs with the aim of user education so that these stakeholders can participate in the new mode of value creation effectively. According to the operations training manager of Airparking, “For everyone who is involved in using our new system, we teach them how to use our facilities and apps, which makes the entire operation of our car parks seamless.” User training and education have been recognized for some time as an external factor that is crucial to the success of new technology-enabled services. At Air parking, it served to enhance usage competence and user perceptions about its services, just as adequate education and training programs for users within organizations have been found to have a significant positive impact on work performance [73].

As a result of the enhancing operations phase, Airparking was able to achieve supply side lock-in, where exclusive transactional arrangements between Airparking and its business partners are established. This pre vents its business partners from contributing to the services of rival platforms [74] because to do so would require the removal of the infrastructure that Airparking had put in place and the reinstallation of new infrastructure. The facilities manager of Airparking explained, “The car park management system will be messy if property management companies implement multiple different systems. Therefore, systems from other firms would be excluded if a certain car park had already implemented our system.” In addition to exclusive cooperation agreements, for complementors, Airparking redefined their roles in the new system and provided them with the relevant knowledge and skills in using Airparking’s systems and apps. This increased switching costs and enhanced their loyalty to Airparking. Airparking caters to the needs of B-side entities (through interest alignment, structural transformation, and advanced technology support), so that there is no need to join a different platform. Furthermore, it would cost too much for suppliers to switch platforms, since this would entail fundamental changes in their whole business ecosystem (e.g., new and different facilities and equip ment, applications and systems, and IT capabilities). This finding sug gests that suppliers and complementors may be gradually becoming more familiar with the knowledge and skills they have accumulated to work with Airparking [75]. They may also be locked in, and therefore less likely to multi-home on other platforms, because the costs of multi-homing or switching have increased [76].

In addition, with the internal structural changes made by Airparking, they now have the enhanced operational capabilities to deliver services to their end users more effectively and efficiently. After the business structure has been transformed and user education initiatives designed specifically for Airparking’s apps and systems have been provided, the participants are now equipped with the operational capabilities to work within the new mode of value creation to deliver services to customers more efficiently and effectively. Airparking also provided services fo car parks to ensure their operational capabilities—for example, to maintain their systems’ stability and reliability and maintain hardware, to ensure the business operates reliably and maintain customer satis faction. Airparking also offers digital tools to help B-side partners enhance their operational capabilities. According to the facilities man ager of Airparking, “The investment in our operations has always been quite large. We offer ongoing service for car parks, and in each car park we have dedicated operations personnel who are responsible for the operation of each site specifically.” This suggests that structural trans formation can lead to long-term enhancements to a platform’s, and its entities’, operational capabilities, which, in turn, can contribute to im provements in service quality and delivery to become an enduring source of competitive advantage [77].

## 4.3. Phase 3—expanding market

With its enhanced operational capabilities in place, and a base of ready customers through Airparking’s effort at user education, like most other platform-based businesses [78], Airparking began to take steps to grow its market for the sustainability of its business in the expanding market phase. To this end, Airparking relied heavily on word-of-mouth marketing, which it enacted by incentivizing their existing customers to stimulate positive referrals in the market and attract more customers [79]. Consumers typically value recommendations from reliable sources more than those from unknown sources, which is one of the most powerful influences on purchasing decisions [80]. In the context of Airparking, there is also a strong reliance on B-side entities and com plementors such as property management companies and parking at tendants for the enactment of their business processes. Combining the two influences, Airparking started to engage these stakeholders to pro mote their services to the residents in their respective communities and social networks, and incentivized them with a commission for referring customers.

The sales manager of Airparking described this marketing tactic: “Security personnel will be asked to help us in promotion activities. For example … if they help us to promote, the extra income they can earn will be around 7000–8000 Chinese yuan. They will be very motivated to do it, and it is not a loss for us. Our cost of acquiring users is very straightforward … and only those who parked in our car parks will be rewarded. Otherwise, there will be no rewards, and part of the invest ment will be recovered." This proved to be an effective tactic because property management companies and complementors may already have established familiarity and trust with the car space owners and resident in their communities and social networks. Consequently, when they engage in word-of-mouth marketing, these new asset owners and cus tomers would be more likely to have greater initial confidence in the credibility of Airparking [81].

Another measure that Airparking adopted is what we term service templating, which we define as the standardization of a platform’s of fering to deliver a consistent service experience, which is made up of a package of physical assets, facilities used by the customers, the essential features of a service, and its associated ancillary services [82]. As a result of the two preceding phases, the foundation for Airparking’s shared parking business is now in place and can be replicated across the country with minimal costs. To take advantage of this potential scalability, Airparking transformed its standardized service package into a template and expanded with a franchising model in other cities. This standardized package included its revenue model, marketing channels, equipment infrastructure, apps, and systems, as well as its training programs. In particular, it selected franchise partners who had experience in and capabilities for parking operations and had the ability to build re lationships with the local government, policymakers, property man agement companies, and car parks in affluent communities in each city. This package and business template allowed Airparking to onboard new franchise partners more easily, while ensuring the consistency of ser vices as they expanded to different cities in China. This approach is different from a traditional franchising model because although Air parking is offering a standardized service package, including a full set of services and products that can be replicated in other cities, the selected partners in this arrangement are directly managed and given less au tonomy by Airparking. In particular, they are not independent entities with legal independence and financial responsibilities, which deviates from the typical model of franchising [83].

The sales manager of Airparking described this franchising strategy as follows: “If we want to expand our business to other cities across China, franchising partners in other cities would be ideal. As our com pany does not have the corresponding resources and connections in other cities, it is very difficult to establish a local team, and the operation costs will be very high. The efficiency will be very low as well. Our company has created a standardized and company-specified operation model in these two years, including the existing technology, platform model, and so on. By franchising, we can share the standard service template to the local operators (partners), let them make profits, and satisfy the universality of parking demand across China.” This is in line with the conventional practice of franchising, where franchisors usually transfer product concepts, brand names, marketing skills, and operating procedures to franchisees. In turn, the franchisees will help the fran chisor to grow its business by executing the overall business strategy and operating in designated markets, becoming a part of the business system and network [84].

When Airparking’s operations began to grow exponentially with these measures, the platform made a conscious decision to collect and store more data about its customers and transactions to refine its oper ations further. This operational data accumulation entails the establish ment of various means of gathering and storing data derived from activities in daily business operations from multiple sources, which provides the foundation for the implementation of business intelligence systems for managers to make better and faster decisions [85]. More specifically, the management of Airparking realized that with market expansion, the volume of operational data generated on the platform has grown exponentially. These different types of data can be synchronized, collated on the cloud, and subsequently analyzed and used by the business intelligence systems of Airparking to understand customer behavior and preferences to improve service quality and support decision-making, consequently increasing profits and customer satis faction [86]. The co-founder of Airparking described the rationale behind this initiative: “We analyzed our daily operational data and found that the data can accurately reflect the reality of our business operations. The data analysis provides insights at a very fine-grained level of the business and allows us to compare this data along different time dimensions.” The accumulation and subsequent analysis of oper ational data are strategically important for a mature platform as it converts data into useful and actionable insights, using business intel ligence to augment human analysis, to support business decision making [87].

In the case of Airparking, the operational data accumulated over time include the location of car parks, the population of the commu nities, frequency of parking, and information about car space owners and drivers, such as their personal information, demographics, social networks, geo-locations, past web searches, and record of past trans actions. All these different types of data are accumulated on the cloud and analyzed using the business intelligence system of Airparking to further refine its C2B2C sharing economy business model, driving further innovation in its products and services. In other words, leveraging its operational data, Airparking began to engage in multiple iterations of service refinement, which refers to the development of new services or values that were not previously available to customers, which are specifically launched to complement or improve the existing offering [88].

For instance, Airparking continually fixed bugs that appeared in apps and systems and developed new versions of apps (e.g., new functions to cater to the needs of both C-side and B-side entities), systems (e.g., advanced management systems), facilities (e.g., advanced facilities to increase the successful rate of license plate in different and adverse situations), and technologies (e.g., leveraging IoT sensors to create smart car parks) in response to the insights gleaned from their operational data. Moreover, Airparking was also proactive about managing and updating aging equipment to maintain their usability over time. Re finements to enhance the service experience, if effective, tend to become standardized over time, as customers assimilate them into their expec tations. Competitors may also imitate these refinements, leading to their diffusion industry-wide [89]. Airparking’s operations are the basis of its competitive advantage, which helps differentiate the platform from its competitors. Consequently, it was necessary for Airparking to refine its offerings for both C-side customers and B-side partners continuously to sustain and enhance this advantage further.

The co-founder of Airparking provided an illustration: “In response to what we learnt from analyzing our operational data … We have developed a new function to solve this problem (of license plate recog nition) called ‘post-check’ (reconciling parking orders with technology after the car has exited the car park). In doing so, the user experience is improved significantly … we are also actively launching second generation products to technically solve, or circumvent, our facilities problems.” The iterative nature of how Airparking used its operational data to improve its services iteratively resonates with the study of Bie mans et al. [90], who found that the benefits of updating and refining a firm’s services over time include (1) increasing the profitability of existing services, (2) attracting new customers to the firm, (3) improving the loyalty of existing customers, and (4) enabling the capture of new opportunities in markets.

As a result of the iterative, data-driven refinement of its services in the expanding market phase, Airparking was able to achieve market coevolution, where the platform was able to adapt its offerings effectively to its customers’ changing demands over time. By constantly providing targeted and reliable services to its customers and analyzing how they use the platform, Airparking can better anticipate and satisfy its cus tomers’ needs by making the appropriate service improvements.

To illustrate, one aspect of Airparking’s market co-evolution began by simply enabling the booking of parking spaces (i.e., customers making bookings on apps that are fulfilled by Airparking and its com plementors). As compared with traditional parking services, the value of this is greater convenience for the drivers. Following the development of the booking service, Airparking evolved its offerings to enable real-time searches (i.e., customers have no bookings, but they can search and find parking spaces in real time to satisfy their needs using live data on available car spaces, geo-location, duration, and price) within 3 s on the app. To meet different needs of car parks, Airparking also integrated all channels into their systems, which they term an “omni-channel solu tion.” In doing so, Airparking can also serve a greater diversity of asset owners and users (one-off customers, commercial car parks, etc.). Air parking also evolved its payment methods to satisfy customers with different preferences, allowing a greater diversity of digital payment methods (Alipay, Wechat pay, E-wallet, etc.), Airparking also provided the option to “pay by credit” (i.e., park first, pay later), which improved the efficiency of car parks, as there is no need to queue to pay for parking. According to the co-founder of Airparking, “We assigned a large number of developers to work on the existing platform, to create new value based on the business data to improve and innovate our products and services.” The need to co-evolve with the market is often cited as a driver of business success, where the long-term benefits for organiza tions include enhanced customer loyalty, higher profitability, increased customer satisfaction, and repurchase intentions [91].

Finally, by leveraging the operational data it has accumulated to improve not only its services but how it operates, Airparking was able to improve management efficiency, which would be crucial to the profit ability and success of any business [92]. The sales manager of Airparking explained that “We have evolved a set of standardized operating pro cedures, which is of great help to improving our overall management efficiency.” This resonates with the findings of previous studies on the organizational implications of data analytics, which suggest that the insights generated from analyzing operational data can be a valuable resource for business, especially in that they can be used to generate improvements and innovations in business processes that enhance organizational performance and operational efficiency [93].

## 4.4. Facilitating conditions of C2B2C platform development

From our data, we also identified three facilitating conditions that were crucial to driving the development of Airparking’s C2B2C platform across the three phases. The first is product standardization and modu larity, which refers to the decomposition of a product into subassemblies and components that can be reassembled easily [94]. Thi enables the standardization of components and increases product vari ety, and allows particularly low operating costs [95]. In the case of Airparking, the platform standardized and modularized its products and services (i.e., renovation and transformation of car parks, parking management systems, and mobile applications). This set of products and services can be pieced together to suit different car parks, parking spaces, and customer needs to offer greater flexibility, as different property management companies can choose ideal bundles of features and services for their car parks according to their constraints and circumstances.

Product standardization and modularity enable Airparking to save costs and improve the efficiency and quality of products/services (i.e., having the car parks managed by property management companies as opposed to managing all of them by itself). They also increase product flexibility and customization and enable Airparking to expand its market and scale rapidly. There are several other benefits of modularity. For instance, Airparking could order product components that can be assembled and implemented in different car parks from manufacturers at lower prices, deriving economies of scale in procurement due to the use of standardized components across product families [95]. In addi tion, Airparking could redesign, update, and test its products more easily due to the decoupling of product functions and software modules, as any new function required can be realized by existing systems through adding new software modules. Moreover, there is greater value for end users due to differential consumption and customization. For instance, Airparking can personalize its services for its various B-side partners with different requirements by providing products in optional modules [96].

According to the facilities manager of Airparking, “The standardization of process and products improves our efficiency and saves costs. We modularized our products and processes into standard ized components and set a package price for each functional module, which simplifies the process of engineering construction.” This notion resonates with the study of Jiang et al. [97], who found that sharing economic platforms can deliberately target modularity as a design element to optimize the organization of their complex activities. This is achieved by dividing the sharing activity and business processes into multiple modules and making them available and accessible to the right users, at the right time.

A second facilitating condition is the presence of adequate idle re sources, defined as tangible or intangible assets or capabilities that could cost a significant amount to acquire and/or retain and are not fully utilized [98]. These resources can be shared among peers who need them for temporary use, while the owner of those resources can gain economic benefits in return [16]. To satisfy societal and individual needs, as well as grow its business, Airparking has to compete for and acquire a significant number of parking spaces, which would otherwise be left idle. The platform also has to acquire and orchestrate the re sources and capabilities of stakeholders and complementors [99] rele vant to the parking business, including those of property managers, security personnel, and parking attendants (e.g., property managers must be willing to tap into their social networks to market and promote the platform). Given that parking spaces in major Chinese cities are so limited, the return on investment can be substantial if the utilization of these existing resources is optimized. The owners of assets, as well as the property management companies, can gain a significant income through the sharing of parking spaces on Airparking [24]. This increases the willingness to share assets and encourages more individuals and orga nizations to participate in the activity of car space sharing [1], enabling the C2B2C sharing economy business model [11]. Airparking’s business scale is also expanded progressively, underpinned by the ability to ac quire idle parking spaces and the resources and capabilities of com plementors in various cities.

According to the CEO of Airparking, “The value of the parking space asset has not been fully unleashed, which (by my estimate) is not even 2 %. And I was inspired by the situation of parking in the hospital. I was thinking: Why can’t these parking spaces next to the hospital be avail able at these time slots to the people who want them?” This reasoning echoes the findings of Benoit et al. [100], who investigated the motives, activities, and resources of actors in the sharing economy. More spe cifically, they found that idle resources tend to create motivation for the development of sharing economy platforms, as well as economic bene fits for resource owners who are willing to exchange their excess ca pacity or products for payment.

A third facilitating condition is the existence of societal needs, defined as the collective requirements of societal constituents for the survival and progress of the entire society and their sub-communities [101]. Parking is an essential part of contemporary living and transportation, but it also represents an intractable problem for people living in large cities, as the resources for parking space tend to be very limited and expensive. This is especially the case in the metropolises of China, and the number of new parking spaces has not kept up with new car sales for nearly two decades [102]. This imbalance between the supply of parking spaces and the demand for parking triggered the initial idea for the business of Airparking. The thinking of Airparking’s founders is that car space sharing would tap into the potential of existing idle parking spaces and support drivers in finding parking spaces more easily when they need them. The CEO of Airparking described this societal need: “The difficulty of parking is a complex problem in China’s large and medium-sized cities. According to CCTV [China Central Television, the national television broadcaster of China] data some years ago, the lack of parking spaces is estimated at more than 50 million across China, and now it is far more than that. And this is a core insight for us … that parking spaces are very scarce.” Indeed, environmental and social con siderations tend to be one of the fundamental drivers of collaborative consumption [16]. For instance, Hamari et al. [1] found that the need to alleviate societal problems created the motivation for many individuals to participate in the sharing economy, which led to the global prolifer ation of sharing economy platforms.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

Our study makes several contributions to the literature on the sharing economy. The first contribution is a baseline theory of the less frequently studied C2B2C sharing economy business model that future research can build upon. Unlike the majority of theories and studies of the sharing economy, which are based on the C2C model, our study reveals that there are benefits such as management efficiencies to be gained with a business serving as an intermediary [103,104]. With our study of Airparking, we have provided an account of how these plat forms can be developed and how their benefits can be unlocked. A corollary of this is that there is more than a single way to cater to collaborative consumption paradigm. This implies that future research can build on our study to delve deeper into the various forms in which sharing economy platforms can be configured [24] and explore if some forms are more appropriate for a given business context than others.

A second significant contribution is the enhancement of our under standing of the different forms of sharing economic platforms and business models. In particular, through our study of Airparking, we have shed light on the unique attributes of C2B2C platforms that differentiate them from the C2C platforms and traditional intermediaries. More specifically, the C2B2C model, by incorporating a professional B-side entity to manage and operate assets or services shared on the platform, retains the essence of the sharing economy in that it stays asset-light [3]. At the same time, it mitigates or avoids certain drawbacks associated with the C2C model. For instance, individual providers in a C2C model often lack management and operational capabilities [9], and there can be a lack of control over the quality of assets or services offered [10]. By highlighting these improvements on the C2C model, our study has contributed insights that could further unlock the potential of sharing economy platforms and the collaborative consumption paradigm. This also suggests that future research could explore the nuances of alter native sharing economy business models, or delve deeper into the evo lution of the C2B2C model, and examine the contexts in which the models are more or less appropriate for.

The third contribution lies in how we have specified and explained the workings of the underlying mechanisms through which a C2B2C platform can be developed. In particular, our study suggests that it is important for C2B2C platforms to develop the B-side first to ensure supply [82]. Subsequently, if the market is saturated by traditional companies and the conventional means of consuming a service is deep-seated, our study suggests that there is a need to offer a mutually reinforcing suite of services through complementors before seeking to scale the business [105]. The identification of this specific sequence of phases, as well as the steps and developmental outcomes of each phase, is a novel conceptual innovation. In deriving these insights, this study also sheds light on the precise nature and constituent activities of the mechanisms that underpin the development of a C2B2C platform. Future research on this type of platform can use this study as a starting point to validate, develop, or identify the boundary conditions of the presented process model. In addition, this study may catalyze future studies on the alternative means of C2B2C platform development, as well as the development of other platform types.

## 5.2. Practical implications

Our study also has significant implications for practice, especially for three groups of stakeholders that are related to the sharing economy. The first group consists of owners and managers of sharing economic platforms. For this group, our study can provide indications on the conditions that are conducive to the development of a C2B2C platform, how to identify and capitalize on the potential opportunities under these conditions, and the steps platform owners must undertake in order to establish and nurture their platforms, as well as the challenges they may encounter on the way. Our study further sheds light on how to overcome these challenges and the measures that could be adopted in each phase. Therefore, we believe that our study can serve as a baseline theory fo formulating specific guidelines for this group of stakeholders so that these platform owners and managers can make the most of the resources invested in developing and managing their platforms.

The potential business partners of the C2B2C platforms constitute the second stakeholder group that may be informed by our study. Our study suggests that the B-side partners are critical for the development of the C2B2C business model, and cooperation between these partners and the platform is one of the most important factors in success in the sharing economy. Also, even as sharing economy platforms tends to engender business and technological innovations, our study suggests that it is nevertheless important to support the B-side partners, which may be traditional businesses, to engage in digital transformation [65]. This enables them to operate more efficiently and collaborate with the focal C2B2C platform more effectively. Therefore, for this stakeholder group, our study can help them to identify the platforms and business models that are more competitive, because there may be many different plat forms that these businesses can work with. Traditional businesses, in particular, may have psychological concerns about the risks of a less familiar business model [106]. Our study reveals the developmental outcomes that C2B2C platforms should achieve along the various phases of its development, and in doing so, the process model we proposed can help potential B-side partners make decisions on which platform to work with.

Finally, the third stakeholder group consists of the government au thorities and policymakers who oversee and regulate the sharing econ omy sector. With the rapid development of the sharing economy, the C2B2C business model may become more and more commonplace in the future [5]. Therefore, for this stakeholder group, our study can offer insights to help them identify which platforms are likely to succeed so that they can offer more support and resources to these companies with great potential and, subsequently, increase their chances of surviving and becoming a sustainable business. For instance, these companies can be provided with appropriate assistance, such as strategic advice or a regulatory sandbox for new business ideas, which protects the resources invested in these platforms and minimizes the likelihood of business failure.

## 6. Conclusions

## 6.1. Limitations and future research

Our study is not without limitations. First, the research design is based on a single case study, and the issue of external validity or generalizability has always been a common criticism of research based on a single case [52]. Although research based on a single case can be considered “a typical and legitimate endeavor” ([107], p. 231), we must acknowledge that the statistical generalization of our findings and ar guments is not possible. Nevertheless, we contend that our findings and arguments are generalizable, in line with the principles of analytical generalization [14]. In any case, future research can be directed at

validating the propositions of our process model, so that the potential boundary conditions of the model can be identified and more clearly delineated.

A second limitation is that although we were able to identify a number of phases and mechanisms relevant to the development of C2B2C platforms, we are constrained by the limits of the data collected and must acknowledge that other variations to the process we found may be possible. Indeed, the development of any platform-based busi ness tends be both context and path-dependent [19], and since both of these aspects (the environmental conditions and historical context of a business) are highly variable, they may give rise to a myriad of other outcomes, both positive and negative. While it is certainly impossible to capture all the possible ways to develop a C2B2C platform within a single study, we submit that our model can nevertheless serve as a baseline for subsequent studies to build upon, because many of its constituent constructs do not appear to be context- or organization-specific. Nevertheless, other potential strategies and mea sures for developing a C2B2C platform that have not been uncovered in our study would certainly be a fruitful avenue for future research.

## 6.2. Summary

In this study, we have explored the development of a C2B2C plat form, given the growing prominence of this business form in the age of the sharing economy. Our case study of Airparking reveals that the development of a C2B2C platform can unfold across three phases. Each of these phases entails a series of steps (supply acquisition, operation enhancement, and then market development), which, when enacted in a specific sequence, give rise to a number of developmental outcomes. With these findings, our study complements the growing body of liter ature on the sharing economy, and more importantly, it may serve as a useful reference for future studies of the sharing economy, the novel business models that have emerged under this new consumption para digm, and digital platform development. Our study may offer guidance for the owners and managers of C2B2C platforms to optimize the development of their platforms and extend the benefits of the C2B2C model more broadly. In addition, our study could offer insights for government authorities and policymakers to help them better support the C2B2C platforms that are proliferating in the emergent sharing economy [5].
