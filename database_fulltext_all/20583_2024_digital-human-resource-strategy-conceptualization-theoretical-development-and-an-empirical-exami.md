## A B S T R A C T

Companies increasingly rely on individuals’ skills, enthusiasm, and creativity, all the while striving to fully embrace digitalization as an integral aspect of the business. Technological mediation has altered the design and functionality of work, although the Information Systems (IS) literature about human resource management (HRM) provides little empirical evidence of the synergy between the two fields. To shed light on how these interlinked systems generate performance gains, we draw on the sociotechnical perspective to propose the concept of digital HR strategy as an explicit recognition of the fusion and integration of HR processes and digital technologies. This paper also theorizes and empirically demonstrates that a digital HR strategy improves orga nizational performance. The model is empirically tested using archival data from a sample of 351 firms.

## 1. Introduction

The increasing impact of digitalization has brought about substantial transformations in how firms compete and deliver new digital products and services [1,2], highlighting the pivotal strategic role that informa tion technology (IT) plays in the design and execution of a firm’s busi ness strategy [3]. Consequently, IT executives are assuming more strategic and business roles to ensure that digital technologies create and add value to business functions. As such, firms are moving from a silo functional structure to an integrated process-driven structure, where technology cuts across traditional divisions [4]. This has led Strategic and Information Systems (IS) scholars to think that it is no longer about aligning the IT functional strategy with the company’s business strategy, as discussed by Coltman et al. [5], but about the fusion of both strategies in a digital business strategy that provides direction and momentum in the current complex and digital competitive environment [6,7]. A digital business strategy has been defined as the “organizational strategy formulated and executed by leveraging digital resources to create differential value” ([8], p. 472). An effective digital business strategy moves beyond using IT in some business areas but it refers to a continuous process of leveraging IT in all the firm’s business processes [8].

The increasing influence of technology is evident in both our professional and personal spheres, coinciding with a shift in how in dividuals perceive their roles [9]. Previous research noticed that “of the various forces affecting HR, the development of technology is perhaps the most significant” ([10], p. 54). This is relevant for two main reasons. On the one hand, human capital has been defined as the “most universally valuable and imperfectly imitable resource” ([11], p. 443) and a central driver of strategy and performance [12]. The significant impact of human capital on organizational success has prompted researchers to conceptualize Human Resource Management (HRM) in novel theoretical frameworks, depicting it as a dynamic ecosystem composed of numerous interconnected elements, including technology [13]. On the other hand, the intense rivalry for skilled human resources leads to a continuing struggle in the "war for talent” that is pushing organizations to digitally transform the workplace and improve employer branding [14]. All of this encourages practitioners and academics to reconsider traditional elements of HRM and examine the evolving workforce dynamics, keeping in mind that only effective Human Resource (HR) practices can enable knowledge renewal and facilitate an organization’s success in the knowledge-based competition [15,16].

A predominant number of studies from the HRM and the IS fields have examined the use of IT to support HR practices under the banner of electronic HRM systems (i.e., e-HRM) or HR information systems (i.e.,

HRIS) [17–21], where the enabling role of IT in recruitment and training has received the most attention (for a recent review, please see [22]). For example, Maier et al. [23] focus on how the e-recruiting system affects job-related attitudes among HR personnel, and Charlier et al. [24] examine how e-learning arrangements affect employees’ job embeddedness. Now, HR managers are expected to transform their de partments and HR practices from support functions to providers of strategic business value and change agents [25]. Since both IT and HR functions are evolving to assume new organizational roles, it is unsur prising that HR and IT executives work together to rethink and redesign how employees work [26]. Although the transition from mediation to ward intensive technology utilization in HRM is underway, it has yet to find comprehensive representation in contemporary e-HRM research [27].

We draw on the sociotechnical theory to shed light on how HR processes and technologies are tightly intertwined and how they create business value [28,29]. This perspective emphasizes that organizations are complex systems where technology and social aspects are inter connected [30]. In the context of HR, this means acknowledging that HR processes and technology systems are interdependent [24]. Under this theoretical lens, this research proposes that the integration of technol ogy into HR practices is leading to the transformation of traditional HR strategies into digital HR strategies. While HR strategy is understood a the functional strategy that an organization designs and implements for managing its people to optimal effect [31], a digital HR strategy refers to the portfolio of HR initiatives, activities, and practices to pursue HR outcomes (e.g., employee experience, satisfaction, creativity, produc tivity) resulting from the selection, use, leverage and merger of digita technologies with HR practices [32.33]. Previous research on IT HRM provides little empirical support for the synergy between IT and HR practices, assuming that synergy exists [34,35]. Following this line of thought, we propose that the worth of technology is contingent upon it interconnection and harmonization with other organizational assets and capabilities $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ HR practices) [29]. In conjunction with this approach, we draw on the IS Business value framework [36] to assert that the design of a digital HR strategy exerts a positive impact on firm perfor mance. This proposition is grounded in the theoretical framework on the business value of IS [37], which emphasizes the critical role of IT re sources in shaping firm performance and fostering competitive advan tage. Some scholars even suggest that demonstrating the value of investing in IT is key to the contribution of the IS discipline [38]. By integrating insights from these frameworks, we aim to illuminate the significance of the synergy between technologies and HR practices to enhance performance outcomes. From previous HRM research, we learned that the effectiveness of HR systems hinges on their ability to foster a positive impact on employees and motivate them to actively contribute to important organizational outcomes [39]. From the IS field, we know that technological mediation provides access to more and better information, improves efficiency and reduces costs, transform employee experience, and fosters innovation among employees [40,41]. Thus, we believe that a digital HR strategy where HR processes and technologies are integrated and create synergistic effects can explain variations in firm performance because it creates HR and IT synergies, transforms employee experience, reduces operating costs, improve employer branding, embraces data-driven managerial decision-making, and generates new revenues

In an effort to provide theoretical and empirical insights into how HR strategy and digital technologies are intertwined, we also follow previ ous IS research on IT-business strategic alignment and digital business strategy [8,42]. A digital business strategy is the result of a business-IT fusion, an emerging perspective where business strategy is intertwined with IT strategy and both must be considered simultaneously [7]. Following this line of research, we operationalize a digital HR strategy as the fusion (i.e., interaction) of digital and nondigital components and posit that a digital HR strategy implies the fusion at a functional level of IT and HR strategies. Our research model, which proposes that a digital

HR strategy can explain variations in firm performance, is tested using archival data from a sample of 351 European firms and provides several key contributions to the academic fields of IS and HRM. First, by inte grating a sociotechnical view together with the IS business value framework and drawing on prior research on digital business strategy, we introduce, conceptualize and operationalize the concept of digital HR strategy. Second, we contribute to and advance knowledge and understanding of digital business strategy literature by theoretically proposing that the fusion between IT and business strategy (i.e., digital business strategy) requires the fusion of functional strategies such as the one of IT and HR strategy, resulting in a digital HR strategy. This pro posal has theoretical and managerial implications for strategies at functional levels and extends the scope of prior research on digital business strategy. Third, despite the pervasive impact of technology on HR, limited research has examined whether the integration of the two systems enables organizations to achieve their organizational goals [20]. Therefore, we theorize, argue, and empirically demonstrate whether a firm’s digital HR strategy improves firm performance.

## 2. Theoretical background and conceptual development

## 2.1. Sociotechnical theory

A firm’s digital business strategy emerges as a response to the widespread process of digitalization that organizations are currently undergoing [3]. The term digitalization was coined to describe a soci otechnical phenomenon [43]. This development has prompted Organi zation and IS scholars to refer to the organization or to any organizational work system (i.e., a department) as a ‘sociotechnical system’,.,” that is a system made up of the combination of social and technical systems [30]. Researchers rooted in the sociotechnical systems tradition propose that an organization’s performance correlates directly with how well the social and technical subsystems are "cohesively optimized" [44] and that the materiality of a new technological artifact could be leveraged in diverse ways to facilitate various tasks and/or task allocations [30]. Therefore, this theory recognizes the importance of the joint interaction between technological systems (technology needed to transform inputs into outputs) and social components (individuals and the knowledge, skills, and cultural values they bring to the work envi ronment, as well as the reward system, power relations or communi cation networks) that exists in the organization [24,28]. Consequently, this perspective suggests that there are four components of a system (i.e., task, technology, people and structure) that are connected to comple ment and reinforce each other [45]. To attain the desired outcomes, any work system must deal with the four components in an integrated manner [44].

The sociotechnical perspective has been used in previous IS and HR research to better understand the role of information systems in orga nizations [29,46] and explain the potential relationship between tech nology and employee attitudes [24]. This research follows the theoretical underpinnings of the sociotechnical theory to propose that the HR department (i.e., a work system) should be designed in a way where social components are integrated and interdependent with tech nical components. In this specific setting, people (i.e., social component) focuses on the individuals within the organization who are involved in HR activities, including HR professionals, managers, and employees, and includes employee responsibilities, employees’ technical skills as well as soft skills, or employees’ willingness to adopt new technologies. Structure, also referring to a social component, refers to the organiza tional design, processes, and governance mechanisms that shape how digital HR practices are organized and managed. It may involve communication channels and style, relationships and collaboration among employees, or coordination mechanisms [47]. A well-defined structure supports the fusion of HR activities with digital technologies, facilitates collaboration, and enables agility in responding to changing needs and priorities. Alternatively, technical components include technology, often conceived from a tool view [48], such as websites, intranet, social media platforms, laptops, mobile technology, simula tions platforms or collaboration platforms for telecommuting, thus mainly referring to hardware and software [49]. Within the technical subsystem, tasks or processes pertaining to the HR department such as recruitment processes, e-learning and development processes, employee training, performance management, socialization or compensation are considered [25]. Given that sociotechnical theory contends that achieving desired results requires the explicit recognition of the inter dependence of the two subsystems [28], we propose that this integration results in what we call a digital HR strategy. Therefore, we use the soci otechnical perspective to conceptualize a digital HR strategy as the functional strategy formulated and executed to manage people to optimal effect by leveraging and integrating digital technologies into HR practices [31,49]. Based on the sociotechnical theory, we propose that the joint cohesion and integration of the two systems will result in positive performance outcomes due to the generated synergy that is superior to that of the standalone systems.

## 2.2. Digital HR strategy: Theoretical and conceptual development

IS literature proposes that firms cannot be competitive or successful if their business and IT strategies are not aligned [42,50]. Organizations perform better when their key IT goals, IT resources, and IT initiatives are aligned with the business strategic goals and business strategy, and they use appropriate IT-business governance structures to supervise the investments and deployment of these IT resources [51.52]. This also suggests that the concept of fit is a result of the increased complexity firms face nowadays [6]. However, previous literature also recognizes that research on fit and alignment fails to comprehensively capture the current dynamics within organizations or the pragmatic hurdles encountered by managers [13]. The advent of digital technologies is causing companies to formulate entirely novel strategies [53] and pushing all facets of company processes to digitize [7]. In order to better reflect the complexities and dynamics of contemporary models of strategy and organization, a digital business strategy emerges as "the organizational strategy formulated and executed by leveraging digital re sources to create differential value" ([8], p. 472) (i.e., IT strategy is not subordinated to business strategy) [54,55]. This has also given rise to a body of research dedicated to unraveling the components of an effective digital business strategy [3,7,8,54].

Along with previous literature on digital business strategy, the sociotechnical perspective suggests that the integration and interde pendence between the social components of a firm and the technical system are needed to deliver additional business value in a work system [49]. On the one hand, and following previous literature on digital business strategy, we propose that the business-IT fusion occurring at the business level can also happen at the functional level (i.e., HR-IT fusion), resulting in what we call a digital HR strategy [7]. On the other hand, consistent with the sociotechnical perspective, a digital HR strategy is introduced as a coordinated and integrated bundle of digital technologies and high-performance work practices that create syner gistic effects and result in better instrumental outcomes [49,56]. Therefore, a digital HR strategy involves managing its people to optimal effect by selecting, using, leveraging, and merging digital technologies with the firm’s HR practices [31,49]. According to this conceptualiza tion, a firm’s digital HR strategy goes beyond the fit between a firm’s IT strategy and HR strategy (i.e., IT-HR alignment), but is a fusion and integration of the two fields [7].

Prior HRM research has conceptualized and measured HR strategy as the firm’s configuration of several HRM practices [12,35,57]. From an analysis of the broad HRM literature, Wright and Boswell [58] identified six categories of HR processes: selection, training and development, recruitment, compensation, performance management, and partic ipation/work design. Later, Combs et al. [59] recognized a wider range of practices: flexible work, participation, teams, training, internal promotion, information sharing, compensation level, incentive compensation, employment security, selectivity, HR planning, perfor mance appraisal, and grievance procedures. Wirtky et al. [27], in a re view of prior e-HRM research, identified that technology supports most of the HRM practices examined. Consistent with previous research, a digital HR strategy is proposed as a set of HRM practices that cover the full employee lifecycle and where technologies are integrated. In particular, we propose 9 HR practices composing the digital HR strategy: communication channels (previously identified as information sharing), learning and development programs, compensation and benefits, flex ible working conditions (also found as flexible work or flexible job assignment), networking activities (such as participation and teams activities), onboarding activities (included in job design practice), career and succession management (related to internal promotion and performance management), secondary benefits, and well-being pro grams (where employment security and grievance procedures are re flected) [12,25,57,59]. Each HR practice is composed of several HR and digital HR subpractices (Table 1).

As a practical example, Unilever has changed its hiring and training process by experimenting with social media to spread job adverts, online games to assess skills, and artificial intelligence to filter candidates [60]. Similarly, Cisco has organized hackathons to build new HR products such as “Ask Alex,” a voice command app to quickly answer HR ques tions. Ford Motor Co. integrated its systems into its well-being program by incorporating an online healthcare site into its portal. The company reduced its costs because it was able to negotiate better deals with health insurance providers [61]. Although evidence shows that companies are integrating technologies within HR practices, HR practices are not deployed in isolation, but rather as part of an entire configuration of practices covering the full employee lifecycle and composing the digital HR strategy of the company [62].

## 3. The proposed research model: impact of digital HR strategy on firm performance

We posit that a digital HR strategy can explain variations in firm performance (Fig. 1). First, while there is significant debate about the mix and selection of high-performance work practices, systems theory suggests that synergy among components delivers greater value than components on their own [29,63]. A digital HR strategy emerges as the fusion of digital and nondigital HRM practices and initiatives that uni formly relate to and complement each other, creating synergies among them [64]. The individual HRM practices that make up the firm’s digital HR strategy support each other in improving specific workforce char acteristics, creating an effect greater than the sum of individual best HRM practices [65]. As a result, the firm may communicate consistent and reinforcing messages to employees fostering their productivity that is best served by the synergetic interactions among digital and non digital HRM practices.

Second, a digital HR strategy focuses on employee experience as it supports changes in working styles and allows more effective commu nication and collaboration among employees due to the merger and integration of offline and online communications and the support of virtual work environments [40,66]. Such employee orientation helps to build customer satisfaction and foster innovation and creativity among employees, all of which will directly impact firm performance [33,67]. For example, Best Buy, a large consumer digital retailer, uses an enter prise social media called Blue Shirt Nation, which allows the free ex change of ideas among employees, discusses organizational initiatives, co-creates knowledge, and improves firm performance [68]. Similarly, DBS bank empowers employees with enterprise portals to share data, unified communications infrastructure, videoconferencing systems, and enterprise social media platforms to transform and improve employee experience, enhancing customer experience and satisfaction and DBS performance [26].

Third, a firm’s digital HR strategy contributes to resource optimization and cost reduction (e.g., administration costs, communi cation costs, travel and training costs, and turnover-associated costs). For example, the digitalization of learning and development practices such as e-learning, mobile-enabled learning, or online training sessions provides greater flexibility, efficiency, and convenience for trainees and decreases costs compared to those of traditional training methods [24]. Similarly, the digitalization of formal procedures for new employees may also save costs. At Cisco, new employees use Cisco’s portal to order office equipment and corporate mobile phones and choose and enroll in healthcare and benefits programs. These activities, which usually take a week in many companies, are completed in one day at Cisco [61]. In addition, a digital HR strategy can also help to streamline HR processes and eliminate manual tasks. At DBS, the adoption of robotics process automation has reduced the average handling time for HR personnel by 87% [62].

Fourth, a firm’s digital HR strategy may also positively impact employee motivation and engagement and retention of workers, improving its employer branding and therefore, the firm performance [24]. ## 4. Research design and methodology

## 4.1. Sample

We tested the proposed research model with a sample of 351 Euro pean firms certified as top employers in 2015 by Top Employers Institute (in short, Top Employers Institute database (https://www.top-emplo yers.com/es/certified-top-employers-search/). Top Employers Institute was established 30 years ago and is a leading organization recognizing excellence in HRM practices, certifying 1691 organizations in 120 countries. Top Employers Institute provides a certification program that enables organizations to assess and improve the workplace environ ment, HR strategy, and digital HR strategy. The Top Employers Institute database allows discovering top employers certified with the official country, regional, or global certification seal. This database includes information about the HRM practices and HR strategy implemented in certified firms, offering an excellent and rich dataset to examine this study’s research question. The sample firms come from 15 different in dustries: consumer goods (62), automotive (46), IT and telecommuni cations (45), banking and finance (37), healthcare and pharma (30), engineering (24), construction (17), professional services (17), energy and utilities (16), chemicals (13), retail (12), food (9), manufacturing (9), transport (8) and others (5). These kinds of databases have been used and widely supported by prior research [14,78–80].

## 4.2. Data

We combine data from two different databases. We first gathered data on digital HR strategy from the Top Employers Institute database. This dataset relies on the Top Employers Institute certification program, a 3-year program consisting of a rigorous and thorough analysis of firms HR best practices. Before a firm participates in the program, a first scan is done to check whether it meets certain criteria: having at least 250 local or 2500 international employees as well as advanced, formalized HR practices. Once a firm is eligible for the certification program, the program starts with the firm participation in the HR Best Practices Survey. The survey is developed in an iterative process with experts and certified firms and is updated on an annual basis to reflect current HR topics. Overall, it relates to six main HR topics affecting people devel opment: steer, shape, attract, develop, engage and unite. More than 600 HR practices are examined as well as the technology supporting those practices. After the survey is completed, a validation phase in which an external audit takes place checks for the correctness of the answers. Finally, the certification and feedback phases mark the conclusion of the process.

This type of data is extremely valuable and has the potential to offer rich insights into the phenomenon of digital HR strategy. First, the dataset is a result of a rigorous procedure administered by a well-known and reputed consulting company that operates in the HR field. Second, since participants participate on a voluntary basis, it can be assumed that participating firms complete the survey in a reliable and rigorous way. Third, the practices included in this research come from an exhaustive evaluation of firms’ HR practices on an annual basis and discussion with HR experts. Although previous literature on HRM practices has been examined to check for the consistency of the practices we present here, it is worth noting that the set of practices comes from the real world. Some firms may be reluctant to adopt certain high performance work practices due to the lack of a clear and coherent taxonomy that identifies the range of available practices and their relationship to performance outcomes. For these reasons, we believe that the measures adopted in this research are valid and reliable.

After digital HR strategy data were collected, we collected firm performance data from the ORBIS database (https://www.bvdinfo.com/ en-gb/our-products/data/international/orbis). ORBIS is an outstanding database of companies provided by Bureau van Dijk with information on more than 400 million companies and entities across the globe.

## 4.3. Research model specification: composite model

IS and HRM research aims to assess complex research theories that can include two types of theoretical concepts: behavioral constructs (latent variables) and design constructs (also called composite con structs, artifacts, or emergent variables) [81,82]. Behavioral construct or latent variables refer to those variables that must be inferred through a measurement model because they cannot be observed directly [83]. They usually represent concepts that exist in nature, such as personality traits or individual behavior [82]. Existing literature proposes two ways to measure behavioral concepts: reflective and causal-formative mea surement models. The reflective measurement model (i.e., common factor model) assumes that $" a$ set $o f$ indicators is a measurement error-prone manifestation of an underlying latent variable" ([82], p. 3). Many management studies use reflective indicators, but the HRM field has traditionally used formative measures [84], which can be specified and modeled as causal-formative or composite-formative measurements [82,85]. The causal-formative measurement model changes the direc tion of causality between the indicators and the construct, and it as sumes that the indicators cause the latent variable [14,86]. Composite-formative, composite constructs, or emergent variables model the construct of interest to represent theoretical concepts [82,87]. Composite constructs are understood as theoretically justified and human- or company-made constructions that serve as a proxy for the concept under investigation [80,88]. Composite constructs represent "emergent, strong, complex, and firm-made concepts" ([89], p. 6), where the indicators are the ingredients of the concept, not causing the variable but making up or composing the concept. Consistent with prior HRM literature that proposes to model bundles of HRM practices as formative [90], this study considers that all constructs included in the proposed research model are composite constructs.

## 4.4. Measures

## 4.4.1. Digital HR strategy

We measured digital HR strategy with information collected from the Top Employers Institute database in 2015 (Table 1). A digital HR strategy refers to the functional strategy formulated and executed to manage people to optimal effect by leveraging and integrating digital technologies into HR practices [31,49]. Since prior research has conceptualized and measured HR strategy as the firm’s configuration of HRM practices $[ 1 2 , 3 5 , 5 7 ]$ , we measure digital HR strategy through the firm’s configuration and implementation of nine digital and nondigital HRM practices: communication channels, learning and development programs, compensation and benefits, flexible working conditions, networking activities, onboarding activities, career and succession management, secondary benefits, and well-being programs. These practices have been widely recognized by previous HR and e-HRM research, although they may have been named differently [12,25,57, 59]. Each digital HRM practice was composed of several subpractices; some of them included a digital HRM component, while others did not have a digital component of the digital HR strategy. Digital HR strategy, denoting the fusion of IT strategy and HR strategy, is assessed by the interaction between the digital and nondigital components of the HRM practices. This is done in line with prior research that recognizes that fusions between strategies imply that they must be considered simul taneously [7,8]. Thus, digital HR strategy is operationalized as a first-order composite construct where its indicators result from the interaction (i.e., multiplication) of the addition of digital HRM sub practices (i.e., components) and the addition of nondigital HRM sub practices (i.e., components) included in each HRM practice.

## 4.4.2. Firm performance

Firm performance is the key endogenous variable in the proposed research model. Firm performance is measured with data collected from the ORBIS database in the 2015–2022 period. This is done to avoid the effect of exceptionally favorable or unfavorable years [88,91]. In this case, it is particularly important since years 2020, 2021, and even 2022 have been significantly affected by the COVID-19 crisis. Prior IS litera ture has assessed firm performance in terms of revenue growth, opera tional excellence, and profitability (Rai et al., 2006) and agreed that firm performance is a multidimensional concept [41,92]. Since we are examining the impact of a firm’s digital HR strategy on firm perfor mance, we deem it important to consider both employee-related mea sures (e.g., employee productivity) and financial accounting-based measures. Therefore, firm performance is operationalized as a first-order composite construct composed of four key performance indicators: operating revenue, sales per employee, return on assets (ROA), and liquidity ratio. Each indicator that shapes the construct of firm perfor mance is the average for 2015–2022. Operating revenue is the revenue a firm generates from its primary business activities. Sales per employee is a measure that assesses employee productivity [57] and is calculated by dividing the income a company generates from selling goods or services by its number of employees. ROA is a good proxy of a firm’s financial performance that represents the firm’s economic profitability [92]. ROA was calculated as net income over total assets. The liquidity ratio is a financial indicator that assesses the availability of cash and the firm’s capability to take strategic actions [88].

## 4.4.3. Control variables

We controlled for firm size, industry, and firm age on firm perfor mance to account for any difference in performance attributable to organizational resources or interindustry differences [78]. It is rational to expect that larger and more experienced firms can invest more re sources in developing other organizational capabilities so that firm performance may vary by size and age [93]. Thus, we controlled for firm size and firm age on firm performance. Firm size was measured as the natural logarithm of the average number of employees between 2015 and 2022 with information collected from the ORBIS database [89]. Firm age was measured through the natural logarithm of the number of years the firm operated until 2022 [94]. The effects of a firm’s digital HR strategy on firm performance can also be affected by industry. We thus controlled for industry on firm performance. We measured industry as a composite construct shaped by 14 categorial industry variables, as fol lows. We classified industries into 15 groups and set the most important industry as the reference group [88]. In this case, the reference group was consumer goods. Then, we created for each observation 14 dummy indicators (categorical variables), giving one if it belongs to the industry and 0 otherwise. The industry group was not included to avoid perfect multicollinearity. Therefore, the industry was computed as a first-order composite construct composed of 14 indicators, 14 groups of industries to get equidistant measures [82,88].

## 5. Empirical analysis and results

We use composite-based structural equation modeling (SEM) to test the proposed research model as it is the way to estimate composite constructs and models [82]. We used the statistical software ADANCO 2.3.3 Professional for Windows (https://www.composite-modeling. com/). Since all constructs of the proposed research model were speci fied as composite, this estimator and this software are optimal for testing our composite model and obtaining consistent estimations [95,96]. This software allows performing a bootstrapping algorithm with 4999 sub samples to obtain the level of significance of weights and loadings for each indicator and the level of significance of path coefficients.

## 5.1. Confirmatory composite analysis

As a first step, we performed a confirmatory composite analysis to assess the overall fit of the saturated $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ measurement) model [88]. The evaluation of the overall model fit of the saturated model allows the assessment of the validity of the measures, checks whether they are correct, and avoids the potential misfit attributed to misspecifications of the composite constructs [14,87]. We used the weighting schemes of correlation weights (mode A) to estimate the research model as it ig nores collinearity among predictors and generates better estimations when there is a strong correlation between indicators [97]. A confir matory composite analysis compares the empirical correlation matrix and the model-implied correlation matrix, analyzing the composite model’s adequacy by calculating the standardized root mean squared residual (SMRM), unweighted least squares (ULS) discrepancy $( \mathrm { { d } _ { U L S } ) , }$ and geodesic discrepancy (d ) [87], three well-accepted measures of the discrepancy mentioned above [82]. SMRM is an approximated measure of the discrepancy while d and d are exact measures of the discrepancy. Table 2 shows that all discrepancies are below the 95%-quantile of the bootstrap discrepancies [82]. This means that with a probability of 5%, we can ensure that our composite model’s structure is correct. After that, we proceed with the evaluation of the specific properties of our measures.

## 5.2. Evaluation of the measurement propertie

After assessing the adequacy of the fit of the model with a saturated structural model, we assessed our composite constructs by evaluating their multicollinearity, weights, loadings, and level of significance [82, 98] by running a 4999 subsamples bootstrap analysis. Although com posites estimated in mode A ignore multicollinearity, we checked that the VIFs are not greater than the recommended threshold of 10 [82]. Table 3 shows that the VIF values range from 1.004 to 2.703, suggesting that multicollinearity is not a problem in our data. We also checked weight and loading value and their level of significance. All composite weights, ranging from $0 . 0 9 6 ^ { * }$ to $0 . 6 6 9 ^ { * * * }$ and loadings estimates, ranging from 0.536\* to 0.863\*\*\*, are significant at 5% except for one of the indicators of firm performance: ROA. However, we decided to keep this indicator to preserve content validity as prior IS research has considered accounting-based metrics such as ROA to be one key measure of firm performance [99,100]. Besides, the overall fit evaluation of the saturated model provided support to the structure of composite mea sures of firm performance.

## 5.3. Assessment of the structural model and test of hypotheses

To assess the structural model $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ estimated model) and test the hypothesis involved in the proposed research model, we first evaluated the estimated model’s overall fit [82,87]. As in the confirmatory com posite analysis, the three discrepancies should be evaluated to assess the overall model fit. The proposed research model should not be rejected based on the alpha level of 0.05 because all the discrepancies are below the 95%-quantile of the bootstrap discrepancies [82,87]. Therefore, the proposed research model shows a good structural model fit (Table 4). This indicates that the proposed research model is a good theoretical representation of how firms design and execute a digital HR strategy and its potential influence on firm performance.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

## 6.1. Summary of results and findings

The rapid pace of digitalization and digital transformation is pushing companies to create entirely new strategies while at the same time reshaping both workplaces and fundamental elements of HRM $[ 1 6 , 5 3 ]$ Digital technologies such as digital tools and enterprise social media are changing the dynamics of work, forcing HR executives and firms not only to comprehend and adapt to these changes but also to cultivate a heightened level of technological proficiency to remain competitive and sustainable [25,26]. Indeed, technology has emerged as the foremost influential factor affecting HR, a domain that is also characterized by an ongoing talent acquisition war [14]. As a result, organizations find themselves compelled to embrace these technologies, and scholars are led to depict HRM in novel theoretical frameworks [13]. Furthermore, previous research has indicated that effective HR practices are essential for an organization’s success in knowledge-based competition [15]. However, although the use of technology in HRM is widely acknowl edged, previous research on IT HRM provides little empirical support for the synergy between the two fields.

This research attempts to understand and explain, in both theoretical and empirical terms, how HR strategy and digital technologies are intertwined into a digital HR strategy and how such a strategy could explain firm performance variations among firms. To do so, this research relies on the theoretical foundations of the sociotechnical theory, IS business value research, and digital business strategy. On the one hand, the sociotechnical perspective recognizes the importance of the joint interaction between technological systems and the social components of an organization or work system to achieve desired outcomes [28]. This theory is useful for conceptualizing a digital HR strategy as a coordi nated and integrated bundle of digital technologies and high-performance work practices $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , technical components) and social components (i.e., individuals, their interrelations and attributes) that are interdependent on each other and create synergistic effects, resulting in better instrumental outcomes [49,56]. This theoretical lens is also suitable for understanding the concept of synergy between HR practices and digital technologies, a concept that underlies a digital HR strategy and that contributes to better outcomes compared to the effect of standalone elements [29]. We also draw on the well-established IS business value framework that recognizes the value of technologies in enhancing firm performance and achieving competitive advantage [37]. On the other hand, we relied on previous research on digital business strategy. This type of strategy has been understood as the fusion and integration of the IT strategy (i.e., a functional-level strategy) and the business-level strategy [7]. Therefore, it is a strategy that goes beyond the alignment between the firm’s systems and its business strategy, but it merges IT strategy and business strategy [8]. This idea of enlarging the concept of business strategy by including digitalization has also given rise to a body of research dedicated to unraveling the components of an effective digital business strategy [3,7,8,54]. We propose that to execute an effective digital business strategy, the fusion of a firm’s digital ini tiatives and functional $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ HR) strategies is required.

A digital HR strategy is introduced as a coordinated and integrated bundle of digital technologies and high-performance work practices that create synergistic effects and result in better instrumental outcomes [49, 56]. It refers to a set of HRM practices that cover the full employee lifecycle and where technologies are integrated. A digital HR strategy involves managing its people to optimal effect by selecting, using, leveraging, and merging digital technologies with the firm’s HR prac tices [31,33]. According to this conceptualization, a firm’s digital HR strategy goes beyond the traditional notion of alignment between a firm’s IT strategy and HR strategy. It refers to the evolution of the IT-HR strategic alignment into the concept of fusion, understood as the merger and integration of IT and HR practices, goals, and activities [7]. Since HRM practices serve as actionable tools that managers employ to in fluence and achieve results, we propose that a firm’s digital HR strategy can explain variations in firm performance [35]. The technological integration into HR processes will provide access to more and better information, and increase the efficiencies, quality, and even speed of processes.

We operationalize the concept of digital HR strategy and empirically test the proposed research model using archival data from a sample of 351 large European firms. We find empirical support for the concept of digital HR strategy and discover that digital HR strategy improves firm performance. We theoretically argue that firms’ digital HR strategy can explain variations in firm performance because it creates HR and IT synergies, transforms employee experience, reduces operating costs, improves employer branding, embraces data-driven managerial decision-making, and generates new revenues.

## 6.2. Incremental theoretical contributions to IS and HRM research

This paper makes three key contributions to the academic fields of IS and HRM. First, drawing upon the sociotechnical theory, IS business value framework, and previous research on digital business strategy, we introduce, in both theoretical and empirical terms, the concept of digital HR strategy. A digital HR strategy is introduced as a coordinated and integrated bundle of digital technologies and high-performance work practices that create synergistic effects and result in better instrumental outcomes $[ 4 9 , 5 6 ]$ I. It refers to a set of HRM practices that cover the full employee lifecycle and where technologies are integrated. Although there is a widespread belief that the dynamics around HRM have changed, current research provides limited insights into how digita transformation and workplace transformation efforts are interlinked We consider that more theory-based research is needed on the processes and pressures that induce organizations to adopt and change their configuration of HR processes. The understanding of how to intertwine digital and workforce transformations is overlooked by previous IT HRM lines of research. Drawing on the sociotechnical lens, we argue that HR processes and technology systems are interdependent and able to create synergies when they are designed in a digital HR strategy [101] Drawing on the IS business value line of thought, we emphasize that the value of technology is crucial in attaining competitive advantage and contingent upon its harmonization with other resources and capabilities such as HR systems [37]. In addition, empirical evidence around the concepts of digital strategies is scarce. This study proposes that the fusion of digital and HR processes can be operationalized as an inter action between digital and nondigital practices. Second the role of IT and IT investments has been traditionally studied from a business-level perspective and from an IT-business strategic alignment view [7,102]. In this research, we first propose that the alignment perspective does not fully reflect the realities of today’s organizations and, second, contribute to and advance knowledge and understanding of the existing digital business strategy literature. In doing so, we theoretically propose that implementing a digital business strategy successfully requires digitizing functional strategies. This aligns with the broader research efforts aimed at uncovering the key elements necessary for the successful execution of a digital business strategy. This proposal also has theoretical implica tions for digitizing lower strategic levels (i.e., functional and opera tional). which help to extend the scope of current research on digital business strategy. Finally, this study theorizes, explains, and empirically demonstrates whether a firm’s digital HR strategy improves firm performance. This empirical analysis provides evidence for the theo retical proposition that integrating IT and HR strategies can lead to improved firm performance. Although there is a widespread belief about the negative effects that technology brings to the workplace and employee experience, this contribution is in line with the concept of horizontal fit, which suggests that the fit and coherence among digital and nondigital HRM practices will lead to specific outstanding business benefits. This contribution also aligns with the theoretical un derpinnings of the sociotechnical perspective, suggesting that the interplay between technology and social components of a system leads to improvements in a firm’s performance and validates the theoretical framework with real-world data. In this regard, this research contributes to the sociotechnical theory by applying its theoretical foundations on a digital business strategy and IT-business strategic alignment setting, that, integrated with the IS business value framework, provides a more comprehensive understanding of digital HR strategies at the firm level. This is the third key incremental theoretical contribution to HRM and IS research.

## 6.3. Limitations and opportunities for future impactful IS and HRM research

This study has identified certain limitations that, in turn, suggest promising avenues for future impactful research in the fields of IS and HRM. First, our conceptual proposal and results can be generalized only to European firms. Future research should broaden its scope to encom pass firms in diverse geographical contexts such as the United States Asia, and Oceania. This expanded research could investigate how these firms formulate and execute digital HR strategies and assess whethe such strategies also influence firm performance. For example, in a recent literature review, Cooke et al. [103] focus on China as a particula example because of the specific cultural and institutional context in which HR strategy and HRM practices are formulated and implemented. Second. although the elements included in the construct of digital HB strategy align with previous research [25,34,59], it is worth acknowl edging that the measurement of digital HR strategy may introduce some bias when relying on secondary data. Consequently, there is a compel ling need to delve into the conceptualization of a firm's digital HR strategy, examining its underlying mechanisms and pathways fo generating value within organizations. However, since our set of mea sures comes from a real work examination, we also encourage future scholars to get inspired and retest this or similar core propositions in other contexts. Additionally, we also recognize that organizations exhibit diverse approaches to HR strategies. While some organization aim to build long-term emplovee relationships and deploy more re sources on career and succession development practices, others demand immediate short-term employee productivity gains and invest more organizational resources in compensation and benefits practices. We encourage IS and HRM scholars to classify, explore, and test potentia different digital HR strategies. As the IS field is still in its initial stages of investigating strategic choices related to digital business strategies for firms, this area holds promise for research across multiple academic domains [104]. Examining firms' strategic choices regarding digital HR strategies promises to become a fruitful research topic in IS and HRM research. Third, we also recognize the fact that there may be othe variables affecting firm performance. Although we controlled for the effect of firm size, firm age, and interindustry differences, there are othe internal and external factors that also impact firm performance, such as environmental conditions, competitive landscape, research and devel opment investment. and implementation of an enterprise system. This paper aims to present a parsimonious theory on the effect of digital HR strategy on firm performance that is also replicable and readily comprehensible [105].

Future IS and HRM research can benefit from the theoretical devel: opment and empirical testing of the IT and organizational capabilities that may enable $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ enabling role) or amplify $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , moderating role)

the execution of a firm’s digital HR strategy and its value appropriation. Future research can also investigate a firm’s digital HR strategy’s effects on creativity and innovation performance. We encourage IS, HRM, and Strategy scholars to examine theoretically and empirically test how a firm’s digital HR strategy interrelates with other digital functional strategies (e.g., digital marketing strategy) and shapes and enables the firm’s execution of digital business strategy. Although our focus was on HR, a digital business strategy may require fusions in other business processes such as marketing, operations and supply chain. Exploring the digitalization of different strategic levels promises to come up with novel debates, rethinking, and new knowledge. Finally, this study has important implications for IS and Strategy scholars. Improving the theoretical clarity of the role of IT in executing functional-level strate gies can help IS and Strategy scholars enhance their understanding and advance our knowledge and continued research about the business value of IT.

## 6.4. Lessons learned for IT and HR executives

For many years, the aim of HR IT was to automate and integrate HRM practices. HR department and HR team are now crucial to lead the firm’s digital transformation and acceleration. Digital technologies transform the way firms interact with customers, optimize and develop operations, improve the employee experience, and offer business models to the market. It is strategic for the momentum that HR executives become tech-savvy and work with IT executives collaboratively. The findings of this study provide important lessons learned and inspirational insights for IT and HR executives on this digital HR momentum. First, we pro pose, introduce, highlight, and develop the concept of digital HR strategy theoretically. In a business world where some companies still struggle with formulating a deliberate HR strategy and formal HRM practices, this study proposes the design of a digital HR strategy to be ahead of and outperform direct competitors. This study highlights the growing sig nificance of integrating technology into HR practices. In addition, this research also sheds light on the configuration of a successful digital HR strategy. In fact, some firms may be reluctant to adopt certain highperformance work practices due to the lack of a clear and coherent taxonomy that identifies the range of available practices and their relationship to performance outcomes. This research proposes a set of specific measures of digital and nondigital HRM practices that can be used and merged to formulate a firm’s digital HR strategy and gain digital momentum. We explain how a firm’s digital HR strategy includes the merger and integration of digital technologies and digitalization initiatives that can be useful and differentiate a firm’s key HRM prac tices: communication channels, learning and development programs, compensation and benefits, flexible working conditions, networking activities, onboarding activities, career and succession management, secondary benefits, and well-being programs.

Second, this study also points out that HR managers are expected to transform their roles from support functions to providers of strategic business value and change agents. We emphasize the need for HR managers to adapt to changing expectations and conditions and deploy strategies that align with business objectives such as digital HR strate gies. Similarly, this research also acknowledges the fact that IT execu tives are assuming more strategic and business roles to ensure that digital technologies create and add value to business functions. Since both roles are growing into new organizational roles, we highlight the cohesive work of HR and IT managers to ensure that technology sup ports HR goals effectively.

Finally, this study posits and explains theoretically that a firm’s digital HR strategy improves firm performance. We provide theoretical arguments and demonstrate empirically that digital HR strategy im proves firm performance. Firms’ digital HR strategy improves firm performance because it may create HR and IT synergies, transform employee experience, improve employer branding, reduce operating costs, generate new revenues, and facilitate data-driven managerial decision-making. IT investments should be made where the value is realized. HR executives and HR strategy should be merged with the IT strategy to select how, when, and which digitalization initiatives will create value in HRM practices and HR initiatives. In this sense, the mindful selection of the firm’s digitalization of HRM practices and engaging and converting HR executives to techie-savvy can help orga nizations differentiate their HRM practices, HR strategy, and employee experience from those of direct competitors to compete better and sur vive in the long run.

## 6.5. Conclusions

Firms strive to grasp the role of digitalization within their operations and its transformative impact on current workplaces and HRM practices. Although the integration of technology into HR processes has been previously studied, most research has treated these IT-enabled HR practices in isolation. We believe that there is a need for more theorybased and empirical explanations of how HR and IT create synergistic effects to improve firm performance. To shed light on how firms can successfully combine their digital and HR processes, this research pro poses the concept of digital HR strategy. A digital HR strategy refers to a set of HRM practices that cover the full employee lifecycle and where technologies are integrated. A digital HR strategy involves managing its people to optimal effect by selecting, using, leveraging, and merging digital technologies with the firm’s HR practices. Drawing on the soci otechnical and IS business value perspective along with previous research on digital business strategy (i.e., business-IT fusion), we pro pose that the fusion of technologies and HR practices generates better results than those of the isolated elements on their own and therefore that a firm’s digital HR strategy enables companies to improve firm performance. This study argues that digital HR strategy can explain variations in firm performance because it creates HR and IT synergies, transforms employee experience, reduces operating costs, improves employer branding, embraces data-driven managerial decision-making, and generates new revenues. The proposed research model was tested using archival data from a sample of 351 European firms. After con trolling for firm size, firm age, and industry and performing several model fit evaluations and robustness checks, we find support for the concept of firms’ digital HR strategy and discover that it improves firm performance. We hope this study inspires IS, HRM, and Strategy scholars to discuss and develop this promising research topic in future academic and managerial research. Quo Vadis?
