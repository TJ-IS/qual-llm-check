## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Malicious contents Social media Vulnerability Active exploitation

## A B S T R A C T

While Twitter remains one of the most popular social media networks within the information security com munity, threat actors continue to abuse the platform to create, share, and spread malicious contents. In this study, we focus on whether Twitter- and vulnerability-related features can help predict vulnerabilities known to be actively exploited. Using a sample of 6004 tweets, results show that Twitter features (tweets and quote tweets) that combine the benefits of both content creation and sharing can predict active exploitation of vulnerabilities Furthermore, findings show that certain technical and vulnerability-related features are also capable of pre dicting active exploitations.

## 1. Introduction

Social media has become a fundamental source of information and knowledge sharing for millions of people and organizations worldwide. According to a survey in the United States, more than half of Twitter users get their daily information from the platform [1]. Given its real-time information sharing, Twitter has become an essential aggre gator of all security-related news and an important differentiator to identify security threats as they occur. Indeed, based on a Forrester report, 44 % of organizations consider intelligence gathered from social media platforms, such as Twitter, a crucial tool to their digital risk protection [2]. Furthermore, Twitter is an alternative source of infor mation that can be used to defend organizations against threats in real time [3]. As security-related information gets tweeted and shared over Twitter, it informs organizations, software vendors. and end users about recent vulnerabilities, exploits, and patch availability. For example, Log4Shell, a dangerous remote code execution vulnerability that impacted millions of computers, became widely known when a security researcher shared the proof-of-concept (PoC) exploit on Twitter [4]. Thereafter, security professionals and researchers started tweeting and sharing information about what they uncovered, including steps to mitigate the risk potential.

To enhance engagement, Twitter incorporates features that enable users to create, share, and consume content. Features such as retweeting, replying, and hashtagging allow users to reach others, disseminate, and tailor content to their audience. While these features can be beneficial to spread useful content, such as security advisories and remediation techniques, they can also be abused by threat actors to create, share, and spread tweets about exploits, attack techniques, and promote malicious activities against enterprise assets. For instance, when a working exploit known as ProxyLogon, was shared on Twitter, the exploit code was removed within hours to avoid being abused by threat actors [5]. Such features serve as an indication of risk signal [6,7].

Given the wide reach of Twitter along with the rapid dissemination of information, it has become the go-to resource for hackers and cybercriminals. As a result, a large amount of research has studied the role of Twitter’s features in predicting and disseminating securityrelated content [8–12]. However, with this growing body of research from academics and practitioners, there still exist several research op portunities that have not been explored in the Information Systems (IS) field. First, while prior works give insights into the role of Twitter fea tures in identifying cyberthreats and vulnerabilities, there is little un derstanding of how Twitter’s features may help predict future cyberthreats. Specifically, with the presence of various features that enable users to create, contribute, and disseminate content, under standing the predictive capabilities of such features is essential. Second, existing studies have not yet proposed a predictive model of actively exploited vulnerabilities, rather they examined models that alert about potential cybersecurity events. This is a significant area since actively exploited vulnerabilities pose the greatest threat. Third, despite the availability of literature analyzing tweets that mention vulnerabilities [8–10,12,13], such studies do not take into consideration tweets that contain only critical security information such as exploits. This is crucial given that tweets with critical content might be more likely to be abused by threat actors and impact the likelihood of exploitation. Therefore, our study aims to fill the gaps in the literature and provides an answer to the following research question: RQ: Can Twitter’s content creation, contri bution, technical features, as well as vulnerability features help predict future cyberthreats?

To address this research question, we use a secondary dataset gath ered from Twitter, the National Vulnerability Database (NVD), and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) between 2015 and 2021. We then fit a binary logistic regression model and analyze the data using a sample of 6004 tweets. Our paper extends prior work on vulnerability exploitation and makes important contributions to the IS field. First, this work extends the literature on information diffusion and exploitation risk by providing new insights into the importance of social media in capturing security-related knowledge and predicting potential threats. Second, the empirical results of this research address the limi tations of prior work by examining the dissemination of malicious content and analyzing how Twitter- and vulnerability-related factors affect active exploitation of vulnerabilities. Third, results of this research should be of practical value to organizations in cybersecurity explora tion [14] by identifying important factors that predict active exploits and monitoring tweets that may increase the risk of cyberthreats. Spe cifically, the proposed model can be employed as a decision support tool for security professionals to alert firms to potential threats and help them allocate their assets more efficiently. Fourth, given the dynamic nature of information security, the proposed model can be reevaluated to analyze new vulnerabilities and threats that evolve and emerge on Twitter, and thus offer new insights.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the theoretical foundation of this study and review relevant literature. Section 3 includes our research model and hypothese development. This is followed by Section 4, which describes the meth odology, a discussion of data collection, and empirical analysis. Section 5 presents the results and findings. Finally, this study concludes with Section $^ { 6 , }$ which includes a discussion of our research findings, impli cations, limitations, and future directions.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and related literature

To set the context of this research, we draw on theories and empirical results from prior studies on information diffusion, particularly in the domain of social media. Specifically, we link the theoretical foundation of our study to the literature regarding factors that influence diffusion of information in Twitter, and we focus on studies that examine the dissemination of security-related content.

Information diffusion is the mechanism in which information dis seminates between users within a network [15]. With the emergence of social media, information diffusion has received more attention in the IS literature (e.g., [16,17]). Prior work has attempted to understand how information propagates in online communities by proposing different models of information diffusion [18–22]. For instance, Hoang and Mothe [19] examined whether retweetability and retweet rate of a given tweet can be predicted. The authors showed that information diffusion is impacted by content-based, user-based, and time-based features. Lee et al. [21] revealed that emotional content in social media networks is positively associated with the spread of information and higher user engagement. Huang et al. [20] concluded that higher quality content results in more widespread information as measured by the number of views, comments, and likes. Information diffusion has also been found to be influenced by the spread of other similar content [18,22]. Further more, prior work has analyzed how learning and network effects have a multiplier effect on the diffusion of social media content [23]. The impact of social influence on the traiectory of diffusion has also been the focus of prior work. Specifically, studies have shown that the success of information diffusion depends on the network size of content creators that are able to broadcast content to a wider audience [24,25]. Studies on information diffusion in Twitter have examined topics such as user influence [26], influence maximization [27,28], and modeling of in formation diffusion [29]. For example, Rezaie et al. [26] identified the most influential users on Twitter using four sets of features from user profiles, activities, actions performed, and tweets. Jendoubi et al. [28] proposed evidential influence maximization models using user impor tance in the network structure and the popularity of a user’s tweets. Kumar et al. [29] proposed a novel algorithm that relies on user activity, topic significance mentions, and similarity score to model the informa tion diffusion process in Twitter.

In the information security field, an extensive body of work has analyzed the diffusion of security-related content over social media networks as well as Twitter’s ability to predict vulnerabilities and cybe risks. For example, Subroto and Apriyana [11] utilized text mining and statistical machine learning to predict cyber risks using exploit-related tweets. The authors concluded that tweets that mention certain words (e.g., Apache, shell command) denote the highest cyber risk. Additiona works have used vulnerability-related tweets to analyze discussions over Twitter and predict cyber risks. For instance, Schiappa et al. [9] used topic analysis and text mining of Tweets and reported how Twitter is the most common platform for discussions of new, high-impact vulnerabil ities. Furthermore, the authors concluded that the number of vulnerability-related tweets are positively correlated with the number of exploit related articles. Mital et al. [13] proposed a system that collects vulnerability-related tweets, extracts various content, and issues timely threat alerts to security analysts. However, while the focus of the aforementioned studies was on identifying potential cyber risks, these risks do not imply active exploitations of vulnerabilities. With respect to content diffusion, Horawalavithana et al. [8] employed machine-learning to examine how vulnerability-related tweets dissem inate across Twitter. The authors used count of tweets, users, retweets, retweeting users, the authors to measure volume and time metrics and showed that vulnerability mentions on Twitter are significantly higher and appear earlier than other social media platforms. Syed et al. [12] examined the influence of tweet source, content, and technical features on retweeting rate of software vulnerabilities and concluded that tweet that contain information about exploits, advisories, and root cause are more likely to be retweeted. Furthermore, the authors reported that tweets sent by influential users increase retweet count and that tweet that include hashtags and links negatively impact retweet count. Shrestha et al. [10] showed how the spread of vulnerability-related discussions across Twitter is wider and deeper for severe vulnerabil ities and for those that impact Windows operating system. Sabottke et al. [30], Huang and Ban [31], and Chen et al. [32] proposed early exploit detection systems that utilized different methods including supervised machine learning classifiers, topic mining, and binary classification. By considering quantitative and qualitative characteristics of tweets and vulnerabilities, these studies were able to predict PoC exploits and real-world exploits; however, one common drawback is the lack of a reliable source that can identify actively exploited vulnerabilities. More specifically, such studies used sources such as Exploit Database (ExploitDB), which is not a good indicator of exploits in the wild because it is limited to PoC exploits. Furthermore, their findings were limited to Microsoft and Adobe products given the limited publicly available in formation about real exploits. To our knowledge. this is the first study that utilizes the known exploited vulnerabilities (KEV) database that reflects real exploits in the wild. Furthermore, with the exception of Subroto and Apriyana [11], prior studies did not distinguish between potentially threatening posts and non-malicious discussions of vulner abilities. This is important given that threatening content might be more likely to be abused by threat actors and impact the likelihood of exploitation. Table 1 provides a summary of prior works.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

Building on prior literature, we use information diffusion to denote the dissemination of exploit-related content on Twitter. Specifically, we focus on how Twitter- and vulnerability-related features can predict active exploitation of vulnerabilities. We adopt the typology proposed by Muntinga et al. [33] to group Twitter features based on the degree of user engagement. According to Muntinga et al. [33], users exhibit three different engagement activities on social media platforms that include the creation of, contribution to, or consumption of content. As such, in this study, we use tweets to reflect content creation. We also use replies, retweets, and quote tweets to reflect content contribution. However, content consumption was not included since Twitter does not report the views of tweets. Finally, following prior studies [12,34], we use hash tags, mentions, and links to reflect technical features of tweets. Vulnerability features, on the other hand, refer to the technical details of a vulnerability and the ease by which a vulnerability may be exploited [35]. Specifically, we assess the impact of vulnerability-related features on active exploitation using vulnerability’s access vector, access complexity, privilege requirement, and user interaction. ## 3.1. Twitter features

## 3.1.1. Content creation

According to Muntinga et al. [33], content creation refers to the process of generating new content over social media and is deemed the most engaging activity on the spectrum of user engagement behaviors. Unlike other engagement activities, content creation involves both so cial approval and personal satisfaction [36]. Prior works have demon strated the ability of content creation on Twitter to predict a wide range of outcomes from marketing and economics domains to healthcare and politics [37–39]. Twitter content creation consists of short-message “tweets" that are broadcasted and shared with followers. Tweets have been used by security professionals to gather intelligence, incidents, and the latest security-related information [40]. They have also been abused by threat actors to look for exploits, distribute malware, and recruit hackers [3]. Prior work has used the number of tweets as a proxy for investors’ attention in financial trading [41,42], population density [43], and to predict opinion leadership [44]. In the information security field. existing studies have used tweets to build prediction models of exploits [13,32,45]. For example, the number of tweets was used to predict the timeline of when a vulnerability will be exploited [32]. Furthermore, tweets were analyzed to extract intelligence related to cybersecurity, threat alerts [13], and vulnerability management [45]. In the context of this study, we use the number of tweets as a proxy of content creation and for identifying the widespread diffusion of exploit-related content. We anticipate that as more exploit-related content is created, this will increase the dissemination of exploits and the possibility of information spreading to threat actors as well as the likelihood of active exploitation. Based on this discussion, we derive the following hypothesis:

## 3.1.2. Content contribution

Content contribution reflects active participation on social media platforms. It relates to user-to-user and user-to-content interactions [33]. Existing studies have examined content contribution and demon strated its positive impact on various outcomes that range from brand loyalty and image [46,47] to firm’s business revenue [48], and movie box office performance [49]. Compared to content creation and con sumption, content contribution is in the middle level of user activeness as it involves activities such as commenting, replying, liking, and rating.

In this study, we focus on content contribution on Twitter via three features: retweeting, replying, and quote tweeting. Twitter enables user to contribute to conversations by commenting on tweets using the reply feature. Specifically, when replying to a tweet, users can shape how the original content is processed by adding new value, confirming, or arguing against the original content [50]. By engaging in discussions with others, replying promotes conversations and increases the dissemination of the original content [51]. Indeed, the frequency of direct interactions (replies) positively influences the diffusion of infor mation in Twitter [52]. In addition, replies are useful in predicting link influence strength [53]. Prior work has examined factors that spark and influence conversations in Twitter [10 54] For instance, Torres et al [54] extracted content and contextual features and concluded tha conversations are more likely to be initiated by popular Twitter user with a high activity level. However, content type was an important factor for less popular Twitter users. In the information security field, tweets that mention severe vulnerabilities generate the most replie (viral discussions) when compared to less severe vulnerabilities [10]. A second content contribution feature is retweeting. According to Twitter [55], retweeting is the process of sharing a tweet publicly with fol lowers. A retweet is regarded as a primary mechanism of information diffusion where users disseminate the same information to others [56–58]. Furthermore, retweet enables users to make use of someone else's self-expression to link to their own [58]. Ouote tweet, on the other hand, is a retweet with comments. More specifically, a quote tweet al lows users to embed additional information while preserving the content of the original tweet [55]. The relationship between each of retweet, quote tweets, and information diffusion has been of interest to re searchers from various domains. For example, source trustworthiness, expertise, attractiveness, and the number of multimedia are positively related with retweeting [59]. In addition, quote tweets have been shown to increase political discourse and its diffusion [57]. Prior work has also shown that emotions are a driver of information diffusion and that tweets with positive or negative emotions are retweeted faster than neutral ones (Stieglitz & Dang-Xuan, 2013). In the context of informa tion security, Syed et al. [12] attempted to predict the factors that affect retweetability of software vulnerability tweets and concluded that tweets that contain patch, advisory, or exploit information result in more retweets.

In this study, we argue that exploit-related tweets that generate more contributions through retweets, replies, and quote tweets, convey a higher interest in the exploit being discussed. For example, as users reply to an exploit-related tweet in the form of an elaboration, question, appreciation, or a reaction, it results in a higher volume of interest and an increase in the activity level. Furthermore, because replies are pub licly viewable, we anticipate that they will result in more diffusion and will likely reach more users, including threat actors. Similarly, as exploit-related tweets receive more retweets or quote tweets, this will increase the spread of information to other users, including threat ac tors, and result in more likelihood of active exploitation. Based on this discussion, we anticipate that more contributions will positively amplify the dissemination of the original tweets and result in more likelihood of active exploitation.

Hypothesis 2: There is a positive correlation between content contribution on Twitter and active exploitation of vulnerabilities.

## 3.1.4. Technical features

One of the important features of a tweet is the hashtag. A hashtag, written with a # symbol, is a way to label a tweet so it can be categorized and searched by future users. According to Twitter [60], hashtags are used to index keywords or topics to allow people to follow subjects they are interested in. Prior research has examined characteristics of hash tags, such as the number of occurrences, length, and orthographic fea tures and showed mixed results as to the effectiveness of hashtags in information diffusion [12,61–65. For example, Li et al. [61] analyzed the diffusion participation of hashtags among users and showed how hashtags can lead to higher influence spread for targeted marketing. Word choices of hashtags have been found to lead to greater user involvement in online communities and improve the spread of ideas [65]. Romero et al. [63] reported that hashtags of different types and topics exhibit different mechanics of spread. More specifically, the au thors concluded that Twitter users adopt a hashtag after repeated exposure to it. In addition, prior work has found that hashtags are positively correlated with information diffusion, and they are an important factor that governs a tweet’s ability to draw retweets [64]. On the contrary, other studies have reported that hashtags reduce the number of retweets [12,62,66]. Specifically, the number of hashtags was cited as a reason for the negative impact on the retweet count [12,66].

Similar to hashtags, Twitter provides users with the ability to mention (tag) other users within a tweet. A mention is used to attract other users’ attention and associate them with the content of a tweet. Prior research on information diffusion has studied the usage of men tions as a form of a social link between users [66,67]. More specifically, it has been shown that employing mentions increases the number of retweets [66]. Other studies on influencer marketing effectiveness showed that mentions enhance content value by making tweets more informative [67]. Mentions are also strong predictors of all aspects of information diffusion in terms of how quickly a tweet produces another tweet, the number of child nodes it can produce, and its traveling dis tance in the diffusion network [68]. Building on previous research, we argue that using mentions resembles a social act because they facilitate a conversation between Twitter users. Moreover, this form of interaction may propagate relevant content between users and results in a wide spread of information (Rogers, 2010).

Besides hashtags and mentions, a tweet may also contain embedded links that can be relevant webpages or multimedia content (e.g., video, photo, audio). Previous studies that examined the relationship between embedded links and information diffusion had contradictory results ([12,66,69,70]; Malhotra et al., 2012; Nagarajan et al., 2010; [64]). For instance, Sharp et al. [69] and Son et al. [70] concluded that embedding links within tweets increases the dissemination of knowledge and spread of tweets. Embedded links also have a strong relationship with the rate of retweet [64]. Furthermore, the rate of retweets varies based on the type of link being shared [64]. In a similar study, Nagarajan et al. (2010) concluded that tweets containing hyperlinks to informative posts, videos, and photos generate a denser retweet network. On the contrary, Syed et al. [12] and Soboleva et al. [66] showed that including links within tweets negatively impacts the number of retweets. Malhotra et al. (2012) found that embedding a link within a tweet does not have an impact on retweet rate. In the context of the present study, tweets involving exploits often have textual and non-textual content such as videos of PoC exploits, screenshots of exploit code, and links to code repositories. While text-based tweets are relevant in information diffu sion, we argue that embedding links captures more attention and promotes the spread of information especially for links to media-rich content (Goel et al., 2015).

Based on these discussions, we argue that technical features of tweets may have a positive impact on the rate of information diffusion. For instance, the repeated exposure and persistent use of hashtags may lead to more attention towards a specific exploit, which positively affects active exploitation. More specifically, the inclusion of hashtags within tweets may result in a greater likelihood of exploits being discovered by threat actors. Similarly, as more mentions appear within a tweet, this may lead to a highly engaged community around an exploit. Further more, for exploit-related tweets, sharing media content via links can promote more discussions and engagement among users and it may translate to a higher rate of diffusion. Thus, we argue that exploit-related tweets that integrate technical features positively correlate with more active exploitation. With this discussion, we posit the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: There is a positive relationship between technical features and active exploitation of vulnerabilities.

## 3.2. Vulnerability features

## 3.2.1. Access vector

This metric refers to the context by which a vulnerability gets exploited [35]. Specifically, access vector specifies the proximity of a threat actor to the vulnerable system. While some vulnerabilities can be exploited remotely or from adjacent networks, others require physical or local network access. Vulnerabilities with remote access vectors enable threat actors to exploit them over the Internet. Furthermore, by relaying through multiple hosts, attackers can hide their identities and remain anonymous. Vulnerabilities with local access vectors, on the other hand, require attackers to be in close proximity or have physical access to the targeted system. Therefore, they are riskier and provide less anonymity. For instance, the Wi-Fi vulnerability attack known as FragAttack re quires adversaries to be within radio range of the vulnerable network to inject the malicious code [71]. Thus, they are less susceptible to attacks given the proximity needed to exploit them. Indeed, prior research has confirmed that vulnerabilities with remote access vectors are more favorable and are more likely to be exploited than those with a local access vector [72]. Based on this discussion and in the context of this research, we posit that exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabil ities with remote access vectors are more likely to be actively exploited than those with local access vectors. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4: There is a positive relationship between exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with remote access vectors and active exploitation.

## 3.2.2. Access complexity

Access complexity refers to the requirements needed to exploit a system. According to CVSS [35], access complexity can either be low or high. Low access complexity implies that a vulnerability requires less conditions to be exploited, whereas high access complexity means that a vulnerability needs a significant amount of effort before exploitation. For example, exploiting a kernel race vulnerability is deemed difficult due to the amount of time and extensive professional knowledge required. Since vulnerabilities with low access complexities do not require specialized access conditions, a threat actor can expect higher likelihood of success in exploiting vulnerable systems. Indeed, the recent exploitation against the Apache web server has been reported to circu late on Twitter given its simplicity, low access complexity, and ease of carrying the attack [73]. Given the focus of this study, we anticipate that exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with low access complexities to be more susceptible to active exploitation compared to those with high access complexities. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5: There is a positive relationship between exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with low access complexity and active exploitation.

## 3.2.3. Privileges required

Privileges required refers to the authorization level needed by an attacker to exploit a vulnerable system. While some attacks require no privileges for successful exploitation, others require low privileges (e.g., basic user capabilities) or more sophisticated high authorization levels (e.g., root, administrative). For example, exploiting a system with multilayer security may require an attacker to launch several privilege esca lation attacks to reach the target. On the other hand, exploiting a pub licly accessible web application through SQL injection may require low or no user privileges. As a vulnerability requires higher privileges to exploit, the risk of exploitation decreases as it becomes more difficult for the attacker. Specifically, a threat actor may need to gain unauthorized access to higher level privileges to the vulnerable system prior to launching an attack. For instance, the critical vulnerability in Microsoft Windows, known as PrintNightmare, allowed any user, even those with a guest account, to obtain system privileges by exploiting the print spooler service [74]. This PrintNightmare vulnerability required less effort from threat actors given its limited constraints. In the context of this study, we argue that exploit-related tweets that mention vulnera bilities that require low, or no privileges will be more appealing to at tackers and therefore more likely to be actively exploited. We posit the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6: There is a positive relationship between exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with low or no privilege requirements and active exploitation

## 3.2.4. User interaction

User interaction describes whether a user involvement, other than the threat actor, is required to compromise the vulnerable system [35]. Specifically, an attacker may not be able to exploit a vulnerability without the participation of a separate user in activities such as down loading a malicious file, installing an infected program, or opening an infected website. If user interaction is not required, then a vulnerability is deemed more dangerous because it is easier to carry out an attack. For example, a vulnerability in the messaging software Zoom enabled threat actors to run malicious code by sending crafted messages to other users without requiring their involvement [75]. On the contrary, a recent vulnerability in the Microsoft Windows support diagnostic tool enabled attackers to remotely execute arbitrary code on compromised systems; however, it required user interaction in downloading and opening a malicious file [76]. As attackers choose exploits that necessitate the least number of constraints and efforts, we anticipate that exploiting vul nerabilities that do not require user interaction are more favorable. Thus, we posit that exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with no user interaction will be positively correlated with active exploitation.

Hypothesis 7: There is a positive relationship between exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with no user interaction and active exploitation.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Binary logistic regression

Binary logistic regression (LR) is a statistical technique that can predict the relationship between a set of predictors and a binary outcome variable [77]. LR makes no assumptions about the distribution of the predictor variables; however, it assumes that there are no issues of multicollinearity that can lead to inflated variances and standard errors [78]. For LR models. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and pseu-$\scriptstyle \mathbf { d o - R } ^ { 2 }$ are often used to examine the overall fit of the model and to measure how useful the independent variables are in predicting the outcome variable [79]. A pseudo- $\cdot \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ is an approximation of $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ that is applied in ordinary least squares regression where a larger value implies a better model fit. In this study, we use Nagelkerke’s $\mathrm { ~ \tt ~ R } ^ { 2 }$ , an adjusted version of the Cox and Snell $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } ,$ , which reports $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ value from 0 to 1 [80].

The equation of a binary logistic regression is as follows:

$$
p = \frac {e ^ {b _ {0} + b _ {1} x _ {1} + \ldots + b _ {i} x _ {i}}}{1 + e ^ {b _ {0} + b _ {1} x _ {1} + \ldots + b _ {i} x _ {i}}}
$$

where $p$ is the probability that the event of interest will occur given the coefficient $\mathbf { b } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ of the independent variable x .

The regression coefficients of LR are obtained using maximum like lihood and they are used to estimate odds ratios for each predictor. If the odds ratio is higher than 1, then this means that the event of interest is more likely to occur as the predictor increases; however, if the odds ratio is less than 1, then the event of interest is less likely to occur as the predictor increases. Finally, if the odds ratio is equal to 1, this implies that there is no relationship between the predictor and the outcome variable.

## 4.2. Data collection

In this study, we collected data from the three sources: the NVD, the KEV<sup>1</sup> catalog published by the CISA, and Twitter. NVD is a U.S. gov ernment repository maintained by the National Institute of Standards and Technology that contains information about vulnerabilities. NVD catalogs vulnerabilities using a unique identifier known as the common vulnerability enumeration (CVE). When a person or an organization discovers a new vulnerability, it is reported to the CVE program. The reporter requests a unique CVE ID that is reserved for the discovered vulnerability. Once the vulnerability is verified, a CVE ID is assigned by the CVE numbering authority and a record is published. Each published vulnerability contains several characteristics such as the attack vector, the severity score, the affected vendors, and the affected products. The second data source is the KEV catalog, which contains a list of vulner abilities that are actively being exploited in the wild. Based on CISA, a vulnerability is added to the KEV catalog if the following criteria is met: (1) the vulnerability has a CVE assigned, (2) there is indication that the vulnerability is being actively exploited in the wild, and (3) there is a remediation method available. Once a vulnerability meets these criteria, it is added to the catalog with additional details such as the affected vendor, vulnerable product, and the recommended mitigation method. The KEV catalog is updated continuously for any emergent threats, even for older vulnerabilities. For instance, since exploits for some of the oldest vulnerabilities continue to be developed and sold in the market [81], the KEV catalog contains dated vulnerabilities that go back to 2002. Appendix A includes a sample from the KEV catalog.

The third data source is Twitter. Twitter is a social media network and a microblogging platform that enables users to send short messages (tweets) and engage with other users through features such as retweeting, replying, and quoting. Our choice of using Twitter was based on its popularity, large user base<sup>2</sup>, importance in the information se curity field, and the ability to collect data.

The data collection process started with NVD, where we obtained a list of all published vulnerabilities (N = 98,857) between 2015 and 2021. We then created a script to search for any vulnerability mention on Twitter using the CVEs from NVD. Once a match is found, we download the tweet content, the number of replies, quote tweets, and retweets. To ensure that the collected tweets are not generated by Twitter bots, we used Botometer v4, a supervised machine learning classifier that can distinguish humans from automated Twitter accounts. To avoid any misclassification issues associated with Botometer [82], we only removed Twitter accounts that self-declared as bots. Next, we filtered tweet messages that contained information about exploits. This was done by searching through all tweet messages that mentioned keywords such as exploits, proof-of-concept exploit, exploit code, and exploit kits. In addition, we used specific hashtags (e.g., #exploit, #PoCexploit) to filter tweets. Appendix B includes a sample of exploit-related tweets. Thereafter, we extracted the number of hashtags, mentions, and links included in each tweet. Then we aggregated the data for each vulnerability, using CVE as a unit of analysis, and computed the total number of tweets and the average number of replies, quote tweets, retweets, hashtags, mentions, and links. To differentiate between tweets referring to actively exploited vulnerabilities and those discussing vul nerabilities without active exploitation, we used the KEV catalog. The catalog consisted of a list of actively exploited vulnerabilities (N = 848) and it referenced vulnerabilities by their CVEs. Thus, we cross-referenced the CVEs obtained from the catalog with our tweets dataset and labeled tweets that referenced actively exploited vulnera bilities as 1 while the remaining tweets were labeled as 0. Furthermore, to rule out issues related to lead-lag effect between the predictors and the outcome variable, we checked the data to make sure that the pub lication dates of tweets occurred prior to the vulnerability being added in the KEV catalog. Based on the sampling procedure of the dataset, as outlined in Table 2 and Fig. 2, and after removing data points with missing observations, the final sample size was 6004 observations.

Fig. 3 shows the trend of tweets that mention exploits between 2015 and 2021. Based on the graph, 2018 had the highest number of mentions (N = 1768), whereas 2015 had the lowest number (N = 274). Descrip tive statistics and correlation matrix are shown Table 3.

## 4.5. Control variables

In this study we controlled for four variables: vulnerability severity rating, the number of vulnerable vendors, the number of vulnerable products, and the number of vulnerable versions. Severity rating is a severity assessment based the common vulnerability scoring system (CVSS) open framework. Developed by the National Infrastructure Advisory Council, this framework prioritizes vulnerabilities from highcritical to low-critical using a numerical scale from 0 to 10 [35]. Furthermore, following prior studies, we also controlled for the number of affected vendors, products, and versions for each vulnerability [72, 83]. Since vulnerabilities can affect one or multiple vendors, products, and versions, such vulnerabilities may generate more discussions and engagement among online users and appeal more to threat actors.

## 6. Discussion

This study examined the factors that can predict active exploitation of vulnerabilities on social media. Using exploit-related tweets sampled over a span of seven years from 2015 to 2021, a binary logistic regres sion model was used to address our research question. Through the theory of information diffusion, results reveal that actively exploited vulnerabilities can be predicted using Twitter’s content creation, contribution and technical features, as well as vulnerability-related features.

With respect to Twitter features, findings showed that content creation as measured by the numbers of tweets is essential in predicting active exploitation. Specifically, as more exploit-related tweets are published, the likelihood of active exploitation increases. Not surpris ingly and consistent with existing literature on information diffusion [29], with more exploit-related tweets, information and knowledge disseminates and spreads to a diverse group of users, including threat actors, thus increasing the likelihood of attacks. Furthermore, results showed that the only content contribution feature that helps predict active exploitation is quote tweets. This may be attributed to the fact that the comments embedded in quote tweets generate more engage ment, facilitate conversation, and spread information among Twitter users, including malicious actors [87]. Another possibility is that because quote tweet provides a mixture between content creation and content contribution, it derives the benefits from both engagement ac tivities. Interestingly, quote tweet is the only feature that enables users to retweet with comments and creates new threads where followers can join the conversation. Thus, in the context of this paper, quote tweet of malicious content may be exposing the information to a larger number of hackers and threat actors.

With respect to technical features, the average number of links and mentions within tweets were positive predictors of active exploitation. One explanation is that exploit-related tweets that include links allow users to share supplementary information over and above tweets. For example, including links to rich media content attracts users who can view videos that demonstrate how exploits can be carried out and im ages of code snippets that verify exploits. Similarly, tweets that include links to PoC exploit code repositories, such as GitHub, can be used by malicious users to launch attacks [4]. Furthermore, exploit-related tweets that include mentions reach more users [88]. Thus, threat ac tors are able to mention other hackers to direct their attention to recent exploits. Indeed, prior work has attributed the use of mentions as a mechanism to the increase virality of tweets [89]. These results are in line with prior works which reported the positive impact of links and mentions on information diffusion ([69,70]; Nagarajan et al., 2010). Interestingly, results showed that exploit-related tweets that contain more hashtags are less likely to be actively exploited. A possible expla nation is that the incorrect use of hashtags may impede the diffusion of information on Twitter [12,66]. For instance, as users create their own unique hashtags for exploits, this may negatively impact content dis covery. Another possibility is that including multiple hashtags can result in information overload and negatively impact diffusion [66].

As for vulnerability features, results showed that certain metrics can predict active exploitation. In particular, exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with low access complexity are more likely to be actively exploited than those with high access complexity. This obser vation is consistent with previous research which reported attackers preference for less complex and easier to exploit vulnerabilities compared to those with higher access complexity that require advanced skills [72]. In addition, findings revealed that exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with no or low levels of privilege requirements are more likely to be actively exploited. This may be attributed to the fact that targeting vulnerabilities that require no or low levels of privi leges are deemed easier to exploit since they rely on hard coded cre dentials or social engineering practices [35]. Vulnerabilities with higher levels of privileges, on the other hand, are more difficult to obtain given the increased amount of difficulty and effort needed to obtain creden tials. In terms of user interaction, our findings showed that exploit-related tweets that mention vulnerabilities with no user inter action are more likely to be actively exploited. A possible explanation is that tricking users into running malicious code requires extra effort compared to vulnerabilities where no participation is needed. Finally, results yielded that exploit-related tweets which reference vulnerabil ities with higher severity ratings are more likely to be actively exploited One explanation for this finding is that malicious users are more inter ested in exploiting vulnerabilities with high severity levels since they lead to further compromise of the targeted system. Indeed, as the number of critical vulnerabilities has doubled since 2022 (Trend [90]), it is of no surprise to find a correlation in the increased interest within social media networks such as Twitter.

## 6.1. Implications

This work contributes to both theory and practice and advances our understanding of active exploitation of vulnerabilities. From a theoret ical perspective, this study contributes to information diffusion theory by proposing a novel model that describes how vulnerability-related content disseminates on social media. Information diffusion is one of the essential functions to achieve information sharing and is a popular research topic in social media analysis. Likewise, information diffusion can signal to the emergence of cyberthreats. We show, using information diffusion theory, that cyberthreats can be predicted by Twitter’s content creation, content contribution, and technical features as well as vulnerability-related features. Thus, this research and data discussed throughout contributes to cybersecurity literature and complements previous work on diffusion of information [16,17]. In addition, this research furthers our understanding of the importance of examining malicious contents such as exploits and thus contributes to the cyber security diffusion literature that highlights the need to understand the diffusion of undesirable contents [91].

Furthermore, this study answers the call by Lowry et al. [92] for more research that can leverage big data to further our understanding of information security. While earlier works have added to our knowledge of vulnerability mentions on Twitter (e.g., [8–10,12]), further research was needed to overcome the limitations. Our study attempted to bridge this gap by examining the impact of other as-yet unexplored factors on the active exploitation of vulnerabilities. This is a key contribution of this work as it captures relevant information about Twitter- and vulnerability-related features and the applicability of binary logistic regression. Furthermore, unlike prior studies, to our knowledge, this is the first study that investigated tweets that contain critical security in. formation (e.g., exploits).

As exploits and vulnerabilities continue to spread over social media channels, our findings suggest four important implications. First, we encourage firms to monitor Twitter for exploit-related tweets when performing risk assessment for potential cyberattacks. For example, firms and software vendors can predict which vulnerability is more likely to be exploited based on risk signals obtained from monitoring stream of Twitter messages. In addition, as firms prioritize which vulnerability is more susceptible to exploitation, our results can aid in focusing their efforts on a limited group of vulnerabilities based on their features. For example, given the importance of a vulnerability’s access complexity, privileges required, and user interactions, firms can take these features into consideration when monitoring tweets. Second, our proposed model can be used as a supplement to existing detection methods used at the organizational level such as threat intelligence re ports, honeypots/traps, and intrusion detection systems. Moreover, our model can aid software vendors in making more informed decisions about the timing and prioritization of patch development when dealing with actively exploited vulnerabilities. Third, given that our model was trained and tested on publicly accessible data, this should be a welcoming news to practitioners that are able to replicate the results through open access to tweets. Such findings can benefit security man agers who want to better understand the strategic role of IT security and address IT security risks for digitalization projects [93]. And last, results of this research should be of significance to consumers that use vulnerable mission-critical systems to make better decisions about discovering and reducing cyberattacks, allocating human and capital resources, and investing in security measures [94].

## 6.2. Limitations and future research

While this study makes important contributions to the IS field, our

results should be interpreted in the light of some limitations that offer avenues for future research. First, given that this study used a cross sectional design for data collection, we cannot establish causality. However, the conceptual framework used in this study is derived from theory that suggests that the relationships are causal in nature. Future studies may benefit from using a longitudinal research design that val idates the proposed hypotheses. Second, this study adopted the depen dent variable from a single source, namely CISA. Based on our review of other sources, CISA was the only database that offered a list of known exploited vulnerabilities that is continuously updated. A third limitation is that our analysis collected data from public Twitter accounts; there fore, our findings do not consider tweets that are published by private accounts. Thus, future research may benefit from using alternative social media channels that provide open access to exploit-related information that could increase the explanatory power of the model. Furthermore, this study used data extracted from one social media platform. Future studies may consider using multiple social media platforms to validate the current model, reveal additional predictive factors, and increase the accuracy of the proposed model. Other future research initiatives may explore the effect of unexplored factors of Twitter on active exploitation. This can include the rate of diffusion and engagement of tweets, virality related attributes, and characteristics of the source of tweets. Further more, future research may also examine additional dependent variables that are part of the vulnerability lifecycle including discovery, disclo sure, and containment.
