## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Augmented reality attributes Perceived augmentation Simulated physical control Response time Brand associations Brand equity

## A B S T R A C T

The execution of augmented reality (AR) has become a new method for companies to create a favorable shopping experience. This study investigates consumer-based brand equity (CBBE) in the mobile AR context, especially regarding high- vs. low-innovativeness consumers. We identify three key attributes to signify AR’s embedding and embodiment experience—perceived augmentation, simulated physical control, and response time—and confirm their positive influences on overall brand equity through cognitive, affective, and sensory brand asso ciation dimensions. The findings also demonstrate that consumer innovativeness moderates the influences of these AR attributes on brand associations. The study provides valuable implications for researchers and managers.

## 1. Introduction

Motivated by the popularity of 5G and mobile shopping, companies have increasingly provided consumers with augmented reality (AR) services in B2C commerce, such as mobile AR shopping apps, to enhance their brand attractiveness. Augmented reality involves a series of tech nologies that overlay a user’s real-life environment with computergenerated information and images to create an integrated interface [1]. Compared with virtual reality, which presents content in a virtual world separated from reality, AR integrates the virtual content into a user’s perception of the real world [2]. Therefore, an AR-based online shopping experience allows customers to examine the match between products and themselves or their physical environments virtually [3]. To facilitate this unique shopping experience, AR-based shopping apps need to excel in both embedding and embodiment dimensions to support consumers’ evaluation of product offerings [4,3], including mapping virtual images and information onto consumers’ real-time physical en vironments (i.e., embedding), as well as allowing consumers to simulate physical interaction with the products effectively and efficiently (i.e., embodiment). For example, through IKEA’s AR app, IKEA Place, a consumer can realistically and seamlessly map the virtual image of a sofa in its original size into a living room (i.e., embedding). In order to check a variety of placements in the room from different angles easily, the consumer can move and rotate the sofa virtually (i.e., embodiment effectiveness) in an instantaneous manner (i.e., embodiment efficiency).

AR service is a promising marketing tool for promoting customer retention and growth. For example, Nike has successfully utilized AR within its mobile app to facilitate immersive brand experiences, creating customized offers, promoting products, and supporting its brand story telling (e.g., [5]). Deloitte’s research finds that brands with AR experi ence can capture nearly double the visual attention of their non-AR equivalents [6]. Additionally, to many consumers, AR offers a risk-free trial experience and gives them more confidence about product qual ity. The research further shows that 41 % of the customers with AR shopping experience prefer a brand that provides AR service for future purchases [6].

However, despite surging consumer interest and enthusiasm for AR technology, businesses are somewhat conservative in adopting AR in their marketing efforts. According to a study by WBR Insights, only 9 % of the senior retail executives believed they had the capability to support advanced technologies such as AR, while 52 % of retailers expressed that they were not ready to integrate these technologies into their shopping experiences [7]. A recent report by Avasant Research [8] concludes that businesses are still in the early stages of adopting virtual reality and augmented reality solutions. The hesitancy in adopting AR shopping services can be attributed to several factors.

First, crafting a desired AR shopping experience for a brand is nothing short of a challenge. Many companies have failed to boost their brand attractiveness through mobile AR presentations due to poor AR shopping experiences. For example, China’s largest online retailer, Taobao, launched an AR service on its platform in 2017 but withdrew it two years later [9]. Poor AR experience, including the fuzzy overlay of online products on the physical environment, difficulty in virtually moving and rotating online products, and long response delay, led to negative brand experiences, risking the brand’s attractiveness. To invest in AR shopping services that can drive both short-term and long-term business outcomes, companies should pay attention to insights that can better connect AR services to the companies’ brands and utilize AR unique attributes to facilitate comprehensive branding outcomes.

For companies, consumer-based brand equity (CBBE) is an important comprehensive branding outcome, as it encompasses consumers cognitive, affective, and behavioral responses toward a brand. It is critical for increasing business cash flow [10] and creating differential advantages on the basis of nonprice competition [11]. Consumers ac quire new brand-related information through direct experience with a brand, such as using the branded product and participating in promo tional activities [12]. Such new knowledge of the brand can become brand-related associations, ultimately affecting CBBE. In the context of AR shopping, a brand’s AR service can provide consumers with a direct and unique experience with the brand, which will impact consumers brand associations and, subsequently, CBBE.

However, extensive research on the impacts of AR attributes in the AR shopping context has focused on product-related outcomes such as product learning, purchase intention, and product choice (e.g., [13–15]). While researchers find evidence that offering AR marketing experience can positively affect brand equity [16], which and how specific AR attributes affect brand equity remain unclear. On the other hand, studies that explore the impacts of specific AR attributes have focused only on single aspects of branding outcomes, including brand attitude, engagement, love, and brand usage intention (e.g., [17–19]). The current literature has not yet connected AR attributes with comprehensive branding outcomes such as CBBE nor examined the mechanism through which AR attributes lead to CBBE.

Second, companies are unclear about how they should utilize AR and feel the challenges of using AR to reach their target markets at scale [20]. Allied Market Research [21] projects the global AR market to reach \$441.4 billion by 2031. As this technology innovation further diffuses among the global consumer audience, the consumer majority who are less innovative than early adopters will become the driver for the increased usage of AR. Therefore, for businesses to scale up AR marketing efforts, it is crucial to understand how to customize AR shopping experiences to appeal to the consumer majority. Existing empirical evidence suggests that consumers with lower levels of inno vativeness exhibit different preferences than their more innovative counterparts when interacting with AR technology (e.g., [22]). How ever, no research has investigated the branding implications of AR for consumers of different innovativeness, specifically low-innovativeness consumers.

To fill the current research gaps and shed light on these important managerial issues, our study pursues two research objectives: (1) to explore how AR attributes (embedding, embodiment effectiveness, and embodiment efficiency) influence overall brand equity through various brand associations and (2) to understand how the impacts of AR attri butes on promoting firms’ branding outcomes vary across consumers with different innovativeness. The contributions of this study are multifold: First, while past research concentrates on two unique AR attribute dimensions—embedding and embodiment (e.g., [13,23])— this research applies a broader framework to classify AR attributes and examines three dimensions simultaneously: perceived augmentation for measuring environment embedding, simulated physical control for embodiment effectiveness, and response time for embodiment effi ciency. Second, this study enriches knowledge of the branding effect of

AR attributes from the brand equity perspective. While prior studies emphasize single aspects of the brands, such as brand attitude and brand love (e.g., [17–19]), we explore the comprehensive branding outcomes of AR attributes. Third, the mechanism of how AR attributes lead to brand equity has not been unveiled. We propose and examine three kinds of brand associations (cognitive, affective, sensory) as the paths connecting AR experience to consumer-based brand equity. Last, we emphasize the need to customize the AR experience by different con sumer segments as the technology diffuses from innovative early adopters to the less innovative consumer majority. We analyze how the relationships between AR attributes and branding outcomes may differ among consumers with different levels of innovativeness. Our findings provide practical insights into how companies can customize AR offer ings toward different consumer segments based on their preference.

We organize the rest of the paper as follows. Section 2 discusses the key variables of interest in our study, identified through comprehensive literature reviews on AR attributes, branding outcomes of AR in teractions, and consumer heterogeneity in AR experience. In Section 3, we establish the theoretical framework and propose the hypotheses. Section 4 presents the empirical study, while Section 5 details the ana lyses and hypothesis testing results. Last, we discuss the theoretical and managerial implications in Section 6, followed by a reflection on limi tations and future research directions in Section 7.

## 2. Conceptual foundation

## 2.1. AR attributes

Augmented reality involves a series of technologies that overlay a user’s real-life environment with computer-generated information and images to create an integrated interface [1]. A particular reality is augmented when AR precisely superimposes virtual imagery on real-life objects instantly [24]. As a service experience, an AR-based shopping experience enables customers to examine the match between products and themselves or their environments [3]. Moreover, augmented reality could be considered a new marketing activity that integrates virtual objects or digital content into consumers’ perceptions of the physical environment [2]. This activity extends existing marketing and brand promotion tools such as product presentation, advertising, and narrative marketing [2].

Branded companies that provide AR product presentations can uti lize different AR attributes to craft unique consumer experiences with the product and brand. Past studies have investigated various AR attri butes to facilitate the understanding of consumer responses to personal technology experiences, such as novelty, interactivity, vividness [18], virtuality [25], personalization/customization [26,27], media richness, narrative experience [28], aesthetic quality [29], information qual ity/perceived informativeness [30], system quality, and service quality [31]. Most of these studies explicate the impacts of AR based on common interactive media attributes, and researchers identify some key AR at tributes, including interactivity, vividness, media richness, and person alization. However, this category of attributes makes it hard to explain the difference in consumer experience between AR and other interactive technologies [4].

According to Hilken et al. [4], it is crucial to focus on some con ceptual dimensions that can highlight the uniqueness of AR, that is, offering consumers an integrated interface for checking online products directly in an individual’s physical environment. These dimensions include perceived augmentation (i.e., augmentation quality), augmented self, self-location, haptic imagery, imagery generation and transformation, environment embedding, simulated physical control, and symbol sets. Based on situated cognition theory, Hilken et al. [3] classify AR features into two broader categories: embedding and embodiment. Embedding implies the capability of AR to map virtual images and information onto consumers’ real-time physical environ ments, thus supporting consumers’ evaluation of product offerings.

Embodiment refers to the degree to which customers can use AR to simulate physical interaction with products or services [4]. When product (or service) information is integrated with the instant decision scenario in real-time (i.e., embedded), and customers are allowed to interact physically with products or services (i.e., embodiment), situated cognition enables people to understand the value of products [4].

Applying Hilken et al.’s [4] framework, we summarize the embed ding and embodiment attributes studied by the existing literature in Table 1 (a more detailed literature review can be found in Appendix A). We find that within the embodiment attribute category, researchers seem to touch on two aspects: effectiveness and efficiency. Embodiment effectiveness focuses on the AR service’s physical interaction efficacy, such as simulated physical control and interactivity (e.g., [23,30]), while the efficiency dimension deals with the AR service’s physical interaction responsiveness, such as response time and system quality (e. g., [30,29]). Prior studies discuss only the effectiveness dimension of AR embedding attributes because the initial mapping of product images onto the physical environment usually happens in real time, implying high efficiency. However, embodiment attributes are more likely to have latency issues, as the AR app needs to track and respond to user input [32], resulting in variation in efficiency.

This study aims to explore the impacts of AR attributes on promoting firms’ branding outcomes, applying the embedding and embodiment framework. It is important to investigate all three aspects of AR attri butes (i.e., embedding, embodiment effectiveness, and embodiment ef ficiency), as they may jointly form a more complete experience with an AR service. Following the literature, we investigate AR’s perceived augmentation (e.g., [52]) for an AR’s embedding capability. Perceived augmentation involves AR’s ability to superimpose virtual content on physical surroundings [35] and is an attribute exclusive to AR. It can be depicted as the extent to which users can obtain integrated and authentic experiences by interacting with virtual objects situated in the physical environment [35,48]. Even though other attributes related to embedding capability have also been studied widely in the literature, including vividness and media richness, these attributes mainly deal with sensory richness. For example, vividness is the ability of a tech nology to produce a sensorially rich mediated environment [53], while media richness describes a technology’s ability to create and deliver various sensory stimulations and multiple cues [54]. We choose perceived augmentation over attributes related to sensory richness for two reasons. First, attributes such as vividness do not directly describe the AR’s ability to project virtual objects to the space in which they are situated (environment embedding). Second, while sensory richness deals with the extent and variety of stimulation, perceived augmentation emphasizes the realism a user perceives from the virtual objects [35]. In the online shopping context, realistic projection of products aligns better with consumers’ goal of accurate product perception and assessment. Thus, we use perceived augmentation to better capture the unique embedding capability afforded by AR technology.

We follow Hilken et al. [3] and use simulated physical control to investigate the effectiveness of embodiment. Simulated physical control refers to the ability of AR to simulate real-life control over a product, such as moving and rotating it [3]. Other relevant variables identified in the literature include interactivity and perceived control, both reflecting the extent to which users choose, manipulate, and modify the form and content of a mediated environment. However, these attributes are also common among other interactive technologies and cannot differentiate AR from less advanced interactive technologies, such as instant messaging and social network media [4]. In contrast, the concept of simulated physical control implies a specific context in which users engage in real-time physical interactions and operations with virtual elements, distinguishing AR from less advanced interactive technolo gies. In addition, in the context of AR services, researchers often use interactivity to describe both the effectiveness and efficiency dimensions of embodiment (e.g., [35,15]). For example, Javornik [35] describes both perceived control and perceived responsiveness as different types of interactivity. Yim et al. [15] define interactivity as encompassing speed, mapping, and range aspects. This study proposes to separate the effectiveness and efficiency dimensions to study their respective im pacts; therefore, we select simulated physical control to study embodi ment effectiveness and highlight the advanced attribute of AR.

Last, we incorporate response time to measure AR embodiment effi ciency. Response time describes how rapidly the AR system replies to consumers’ requests [29], and is a concept similar to perceived responsiveness [30]. Although system quality, a related attribute stud ied in the literature, captures some degree of embodiment efficiency, it also touches upon embedding and embodiment effectiveness di mensions, such as the system’s accuracy and reliability [30]. Again, to separate the impacts of three aspects of AR attributes, we utilize response time to capture embodiment efficiency.

Overall, to address Hilken et al. [3]’s suggestion, our study focuses on embedding and embodiment attributes that accentuate the superi ority and uniqueness of AR technology. Moreover, our literature review indicates that even though the literature has investigated various embedding and embodiment attributes, this is the first study to examine perceived augmentation, simulated physical control, and response time together, which allows the capabilities of AR to be more comprehen sively and aptly investigated.

## 2.2. AR attributes and branding outcomes

Obtaining a deep understanding of AR experience and branding outcomes is critical for companies’ AR strategies, as branding creates differential advantages on the basis of nonprice competition [11]. A successful brand not only positively impacts transactional outcomes but also cultivates consumers’ long-term buying behavior [55] and increases a business’s long-term cash flow [10]. However, the existing literature on AR shopping experience places a heavier focus on product- or app-related outcomes such as app adoption and usage intention (e.g., [25,42,48]), purchase intention (e.g., [13,38,56]), product sales [32], and price premium [41]. Compared with this extensive stream of liter ature, the discussion of the relationship between AR shopping experi ence and branding outcome is somewhat limited.

Haumer et al. [16] show that the provision of AR service positively affects brand equity through dimensions of brand awareness, associa tions, and loyalty. However, they did not delve into which or how spe cific AR attributes affect brand associations and brand equity. On the other hand, as shown in Table 1, a few studies have analyzed the branding outcomes of specific AR attributes; however, these studies only examined single aspects of branding outcomes, such as brand attitude (e. g., [2,36,45,44]), brand engagement, brand usage intent, and brand love (e.g., [18,50]). While these studies have provided valuable insights into the impact of AR on branding efforts, the existing literature is far from providing comprehensive guidance for branding activities. In this study, we focus on consumer-based brand equity as the outcome of AR shop ping experience. Consumer-based brand equity (CBBE) describes the impact of consumers’ brand knowledge on their differential response to the brand’s marketing [12]. CBBE captures comprehensive consumer reactions, including evaluations, preferences, and behavioral intentions toward a brand [12].

CBBE is formed based on a consumer’s knowledge of the brand, which comprises a brand node in memory and various associations linked to that node [12]. Brand associations are “anything associated with consumers’ memories of the brand” [11], including product-related and non-product-related attributes, functional, experiential, and sym bolic benefits derived from brands, and brand attitude [12]. Through direct and indirect experiences with branded products and services (e.g., AR shopping apps), consumers form various brand associations. Together, these brand associations constitute the brand image and the brand’s meaning to a consumer. As a result, positive brand associations will improve the overall brand equity (e.g., [57,58]).

Based on Keller’s [12] theory and an industry expert’s experience [59], Cho et al. [60] propose a framework to capture the three di mensions of brand associations, namely, cognitive associations, affective associations, and sensory associations. Cognitive associations involve individual stories and interactions linked with a brand and its products, which embody consumer brand-related beliefs, ideas, and assessments based on product (or service) attributes and symbolic meaning [60]. Cognitive associations consist of functional benefits (e.g., problem resolving, fulfilling safety needs, etc.), symbolic benefits (e.g., reputa tion, social approval, etc.), non-product-related attributes (e.g., price, usage, or user imagery, etc.), and attitudes [61]. Affective associations refer to consumers’ subjective feelings about a brand, such as excite ment, cheerfulness, delight, and pleasure [62]. Affective associations capture the emotional elements of experiential benefits and are shaped by consumers’ enjoyment of and commitment to a brand [60]. Sensory associations consist of pleasant multisensory associations. These multi sensory associations are formed through a consumer’s brand experi ences associated with visual, auditory, olfactory, and tactile sensations derived from products, shopping environment, and advertisements [63]. Sensory associations thus include product-related attributes (e.g., product appearance, texture, color, and packaging), retail environment-related attributes $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . ,$ , scent, lighting, music, in-store display, and advertisement), and the sensory elements of experiential benefits $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } ,$ sensory enjoyment) $[ 6 1 , 6 3 ]$ . Extant branding research has supplied empirical evidence that cognitive, affective, and sensory as sociations can strengthen brand equity (e.g., [64–66]).

Even though the branding literature affirms that consumers perceived marketing stimuli and the subsequent psychological responses influence consumer-based brand equity, such a connection has yet to be tested within the context of AR shopping experience. In order to advance our understanding of consumer-based brand equity through mobile AR shopping experience, we theorize and empirically test how AR’s embedding and embodiment attributes affect brand equity through brand associations.

## 2.3. Moderating roles of consumer characteristics

As shown in Table 1, existing studies evidence that consumer char acteristics may moderate the effects of AR attributes or AR-based experience on product-related and app-related outcomes. These char acteristics include cognitive innovativeness [22], autotelic need for touch [34], expertise [38], style of processing, privacy concerns [3], body surveillance, fashion consciousness [43], body image [42], pro cessing type [14], and prior experience [23]. However, no study has investigated whether the connections between AR attributes and branding outcomes also vary due to consumer heterogeneity.

From a practical perspective, consumer characteristics regarding innovation play a significant role in the acceptance and adoption of innovative interactive technology [22], such as mobile AR shopping apps. Consumers with lower levels of innovativeness may tend to resist new technologies that remain relatively nascent in the consumer land scape due to their limited knowledge and involvement in new technol ogy [22]. Therefore, deploying new technology without ensuring sufficient consumer acceptance may pose risks to brand reputation. For instance, Smink et al. [45] suggest that being a relatively new technol ogy to consumers, AR can evoke feelings of intrusiveness, which sub sequently diminishes consumers’ brand attitude and purchase intention. As AR technology becomes more prevalent among consumers, con sumers with lower innovativeness will drive the increased use of AR apps. Therefore, it is worth investigating how consumers of low inno vativeness may form brand associations differently through their in teractions with AR technology.

Furthermore, in their analysis of AR try-on system attributes and consumers’ adoption and engagement with the AR system, Huang and Liao [22] find that consumers’ cognitive innovativeness plays a complex role in moderating the effects of AR attributes. Specifically, the authors find that some AR attributes $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g . } } _ { }$ , perceived service excellence and perceived aesthetics) are only relevant to high cognitive innovativeness consumers. In contrast, other attributes (e.g., perceived ease of use and perceived playfulness) only affect low cognitive innovativeness con sumers. Our comprehensive literature review shows that among the consumer characteristics researchers have examined, consumer innovativeness is among the very few characteristics (other characteristics include body surveillance and fashion consciousness) that exhibit diverse moderating effects across various AR attributes [22,43].

Given the practical significance and theoretical complexity of con sumer innovativeness characteristics within the AR context, we choose to investigate consumer innovativeness as a boundary condition regu lating the effectiveness of AR attributes in building brand equity. In the next section, we will present the theoretical rationale that underpins our research hypotheses.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Our research focuses on the impacts of AR embedding and embodi ment attributes on brand equity through brand associations due to the direct AR experience provided by a brand. Specifically, we study three kinds of AR attributes: perceived augmentation for AR embedding, simulated physical control for AR embodiment effectiveness, and response time for AR embodiment efficiency. Following the literature’s suggestions (e.g., [60]), we investigate the mediating roles of three brand association dimensions: cognitive, affective, and sensory associ ations. Last but not least, as the research suggests, AR-based experiences vary among different consumers; therefore, we examine if the impacts of these AR attributes on brand associations vary across consumer groups with different levels of innovativeness.

Brand associations are created primarily based on direct and indirect experiences with the branded products or services [12]. AR attributes enhance the realism of product experiences, such as spatial presence, immersion, and the feeling of ownership. They can facilitate decision making by reducing cognitive load and increasing cognitive fluency, thus enhancing decision comfort and choice confidence. In addition, interactive experience with an AR service can stimulate cognitive and affective responses [35]. As a result, AR attributes can influence con sumer attitudes toward branded products and services and, ultimately, a brand. Therefore, we posit that AR attributes impact a consumer’s in teractions with a brand and its products, and these interactions create various types of brand associations, which subsequently affect brand equity. The positive effects of three brand association dimensions (cognitive, affective, and sensory associations) on overall brand equity have been established in the literature [60,67]. Thus, this study will collectively test them as a mediating mechanism in the framework explaining the effects of AR attributes on brand equity.

## 3.1. Impacts of AR embedding and embodiment attributes on cognitive brand associations

According to cognitive fluency theory [68], during a purchase de cision, consumers evaluate products and choose a brand based on not only the brand information they have but also how easily they process that information. Cognitive fluency then describes the ease of informa tion processing people experience subjectively [69,70]. Because the brain has limited cognitive resources, and familiar information is easier to process than novel information [70], if consumers are familiar with brand or product information during brand interactions, they may perceive more cognitive fluency and consume fewer cognitive resources.

Strong perceived augmentation implies that physical reality and virtual content merge seamlessly, creating an authentic and situated experience [3]. Strong perceived augmentation enables consumers to check the “real” display of products virtually in the real world. Compared with the ways consumers imagine and evaluate products based on text, pictures, or video information in their minds, AR with strong perceived augmentation allows an acquainted and straightfor ward approach to processing information, which can result in a stronger feeling of cognitive fluency [13]. Simulated physical control means that AR allows consumers to utilize the same bodily actions that are used for a physical product to control a virtual object [3]. When the level of simulated physical control is high, consumers can easily move and rotate products virtually through their bodily actions [3], which increases the processing fluency of mental imagery.

Mental imagery is a process of generating images or presenting visual information in working memory [71], and it plays an important role in information processing. For example, when shopping for furniture off line, consumers often rely on mental imagery to visualize how a particular piece of furniture would fit into their homes. In an online shopping environment, where consumers cannot physically inspect products, they are more likely to engage in mental imagery to aid product evaluations and purchase decisions [47]. AR emulates the mental imagery process by allowing consumers to move, rotate, and spatially relocate products and thus effectively promotes the ease of information processing [14,72]. Moreover, an AR service system should reply to consumers’ requests within an acceptable response time and help them obtain the requested information rapidly [29]. The faster an

AR service system responds and the less time consumers spend obtaining the information, the more convenient information processing is. Hence, an acceptable response time contributes to a high level of cognitive fluency.

Overall, perceived augmentation, simulated physical control, and response time facilitate cognitive fluency. Cognitive fluency reduces consumers’ consumption of cognitive resources associated with infor mation processing [73]. Compared with offline shopping, the online shopping environment requires a higher cognitive load because a large amount of information is processed visually [13]. Thus, with AR service in online shopping, increasing cognitive fluency can enable consumers to have more cognitive resources to process more information about a brand and engage with the brand more thoroughly, forming brand-related thoughts (e.g., functional and symbolic benefits of the brand and branded product), evaluations, and inferences based on product and service attributes [60]. In addition, AR interactions can reduce the cognitive resources demanded of consumers in learning and evaluating a brand and its product, increasing the ease with which consumers process the brand information and make purchase decisions. The ease of information processing can function as a heuristic cue and capture consumers’ attention [47]. Thus, higher levels of AR embedding and embodiment attributes lead to stronger cognitive associations.

H1a. Perceived augmentation positively influences cognitive associations.

H2a. Simulated physical control positively influences cognitive associations.

H3a. Response time positively influences cognitive associations.

## 3.2. Impacts of AR embedding and embodiment attributes on affective brand associations

Decision comfort is the extent of relaxation, satisfaction, and happiness one experiences in making a particular decision [74]. Deci sion comfort involves a mild and positive mood, and any factor related to decision making that reduces anxiety, worry, or nervousness will affect an individual’s decision comfort [74]. According to Keller [12], a new brand-related experience can add positive/negative associations to the focal brand primarily through association transfer, a process through which the new brand-related information is processed and integrated into the existing brand knowledge. In the AR context, if a branded AR app can induce decision comfort during shopping tasks, such a positive mood associated with the brand’s AR experience can be transferred to the originating source (the brand) as an affective association, becoming a part of brand responses [45].

AR services with high perceived augmentation not only increase the experiential values but also decrease decision uncertainty [75,3]. Strong perceived augmentation contributes to a more realistic AR shopping experience and hence a more accurate and reliable evaluation of prod ucts virtually. Therefore, strong perceived augmentation can reduce consumers’ concerns and anxieties associated with decision making, thus increasing decision comfort [23]. AR apps can create a virtual try-on environment with a first-person perspective; consumers can project a product onto their bodies or into their living environments. AR with high simulated physical control can simulate a more realistic first-person try-on experience, as consumers can easily move or rotate products. In the context of online shopping, free control over a product with synchronous self-presentation is more likely to evoke the engage ment of a self into the target [15] and promote a sense of ownership control and self-referencing [17]. Thus, consumers’ psychological ownership, the feeling of possession toward a certain object or a sense of “mine,” is enhanced [76]. This sense of ownership gained through interaction with AR empowers consumers to feel more comfortable with a product buying decision [23]. Therefore, a high level of simulated physical control promotes decision comfort. The quick response of an AR service system increases the frequency of interaction between consumers and the system, encouraging consumers to explore a variety of product characteristics fully. Furthermore, when AR response delay occurs, consumers become anxious and even suspect that the delays are due to their own problems [77]. As a result, delayed response time re duces the level of decision comfort. Short AR response time can alleviate consumers’ anxieties and promote decision comfort.

Therefore, perceived augmentation, simulated physical control, and quick response positively affect consumers’ decision comfort. Decision comfort, which makes up a mild and positive emotional response [3], can contribute to the enjoyment and commitment of interacting with a brand. Thus, higher levels of AR embedding and embodiment attributes lead to stronger affective associations.

H1b. Perceived augmentation positively influences affective associations.

H2b. Simulated physical control positively influences affective associations.

H3b. Response time positively influences affective associations.

## 3.3. Impacts of AR embedding and embodiment attributes on sensory brand associations

As discussed in the previous section, sensory brand associations are formed through a consumer’s brand experiences associated with the senses [63]. Sensory stimulations related to the brand, such as brand logo design, product texture, package design, and retail store music, can all contribute to consumers’ sensory associations [61]. The media richness theory argues that media vary in the ability to transmit and process rich information [54]. Richness is a medium’s capability to generate and convey various sensory stimuli and cues. Media richness is related to the number of stimuli and cues provided by a medium and the medium’s ability for immediate feedback [28,54]. Rich media include face-to-face, TV, telephone, radio, personal and impersonal written documents, and digital documents. Face-to-face has the greatest richness among these media [54].

When the perceived augmentation of an AR service is strong, online products and physical reality are well overlaid and integrated. The AR reality–virtuality continuum can provide product information and decision-making cues in a way similar to face-to-face conditions. Moreover, when simulated physical control and response time levels are high (i.e., short response times), consumers can manipulate products virtually in a seamless manner close to reality. As a result, AR services can induce an authentic experience similar to the one under the rich face-to-face condition. Hence, the higher the levels of perceived augmentation, simulated physical control, and response time are, the closer the consumer experience of AR services is to that of the face-toface medium. Correspondingly, consumers perceive higher media rich ness of AR services. When media richness is high, the AR service system can generate more brand-related sensory stimuli (e.g., virtual display of a product in a physical environment from multiple angles). These sen sory stimuli may include product package and design, brand color, and brand display. Perception of these sensory stimuli can be transferred to the brand as sensory associations of the branded app usage experience [2], thus enriching customers’ sensory experiences with the brand. In addition, AR service with high media richness can provide immediate feedback to customers’ instructions by conveying sensory stimuli in real time, thus enhancing customers’ sensory enjoyment of the brand. Therefore, higher levels of AR embedding and embodiment attributes lead to stronger sensory associations.

H1c. Perceived augmentation positively influences sensory associations.

H2c. Simulated physical control positively influences sensory associations.

H3c. Response time positively influences sensory associations.

## 3.4. Moderating effects of consumer innovativeness

Consumer innovativeness refers to the extent to which an individual is inclined to try new and different products, technologies, and experi ences [78]. High-innovativeness consumers prefer trying new technol ogies to achieve performance and thus are more concerned about the critical features that facilitate accomplishing a task [79]. Accordingly, they are motivated to invest time and effort in resolving problems related to new technology adoption. In contrast, low-innovativeness consumers are more concerned about the amount of effort required rather than the result of using new technology. Hence, they are less willing to devote energy to overcoming the obstacles in adopting new technology [79]. When using AR services, high-innovativeness con sumers pay more attention to the usefulness, aesthetics, and service excellence of AR information technology. In contrast, low-innovativeness consumers care more about the playfulness and ease of use of AR [22]. Therefore, consumers with different degrees of innovativeness may emphasize different AR attributes, leading to dif ferences in forming brand associations based on AR attributes.

Specifically, AR allows consumers to examine the match between products and themselves or their environments by superimposing virtual content on physical surroundings and simulating real-life control over the virtual content. In other words, perceived augmentation and simu lated physical control are critical AR attributes that impact consumers ability to accomplish shopping tasks as they deal with the effectiveness of learning and evaluating brands and products. Thus, perceived augmentation and simulated physical control will be more salient for high-innovativeness consumers in forming brand associations. In contrast, low-innovativeness consumers pay more attention to the magnitude of the effort and time involved in a task rather than the outcome of the task [79]. A superior response time attribute implies that less waiting time is demanded when using AR. Accordingly, AR’s response time is expected to play a more critical role in forming brand associations among low-innovativeness consumers. Therefore, the following hypotheses are predicted:

H4a. The influences of perceived augmentation on brand associa tions will be stronger for high-innovativeness consumers than for low-innovativeness consumers.

H4b. The influences of simulated physical control on brand associ ations will be stronger for high-innovativeness consumers than for low-innovativeness consumers.

H4c. The influences of response time on brand associations will be weaker for high-innovativeness consumers than for lowinnovativeness consumers.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Research design

Our study used two brands (Ray-Ban and Schwarzkopf) that offered AR services on the Taobao retail application. We selected the brands and retail platform for three reasons: First, Taobao is the largest consumer ecommerce retail platform in China. Second, the products are sunglasses and hair dyes, which are common consumer product categories. Third, brand premium accounts for a significant proportion of the product prices of both brands. Screenshots of the AR services are presented in Appendix B.

The lab study was conducted in a laboratory, and an investigator supervised and facilitated the procedure. We recruited participants from two Chinese universities and offered gifts (e.g., candies or drinks) as a reward for their participation. We selected participants who had never bought or used either brand’s products to ensure that the “noise” of prior brand associations was eliminated from our testing. A total of 26 ses sions were conducted, each with 24–30 participants. We randomly assigned the participants to experience either Ray-Ban’s or Schwarz kopf’s AR service. First, participants were instructed to scan one of the two QR codes with the Taobao app and enter the AR service. Then, they familiarized themselves with the AR service by following a series of instructions. Specifically, we asked the participants to select two prod ucts from the brand that they were more interested in and use AR to check how well these products fit them. For each selected product, a participant would complete the following actions: clicking try-on to try the item, moving the head to virtually assess whether the chosen item fit the participant’s face, zooming into and out of the image to explore visual product details such as color and materials, taking a screenshot of trying on the product, and adding the item to the online shopping cart. Finally, participants followed the link embedded in the AR service interface to browse the brand’s store website. Upon completing these tasks, participants filled out a questionnaire about their experience. The questionnaire asked the participants questions about the AR attributes, brand associations, overall brand equity, their innovativeness, and some demographic information.

A total of 774 initial samples were collected. We then removed a sample from the dataset if any of the following incidences occurred with that sample: giving a wrong answer to the attention test question, selecting the same score for most of the scale items, providing unrea sonable age information, and providing incorrect screenshots. After removing invalid samples, we obtained 712 valid samples for the data analysis. Table 2 summarizes the participants’ demographic and back ground information.

As indicated in Table 3, we adopted the measurement scales from the existing literature with a few adjustments in the wording to fit the sce nario of this study. ## 5. Data analyses and results

Our empirical model focuses on three AR attribute variables, three brand association variables, and overall brand equity. To control for user heterogeneity, we added demographic variables including gender, age, and education, as well as measurement of participants’ prior AR shop ping experience to our model to test their impacts on the dependent variable, overall brand equity.

We tested the proposed model using a partial least squares-based structural equation model (PLS-SEM). This study adopted a PLS-SEM for three reasons. First, researchers have considered PLS-SEMs as a primary statistical instrument of behavior research for over a decade [25]. Second, the tool is able to maximize the endogenous variables explained variance and provide robust solutions for sophisticated models and non-normally distributed data [30]; Third, a PLS-SEM fa cilitates a complex data analysis due to the capability to predict a study-specific model [84]. Thus, a PLS-SEM was used to assess the structural model, mediation, and multi-group analysis in this study.

We employed SmartPLS software to conduct the analyses, including assessing the measurement model, as well as testing the main effects and mediation effects. We utilized bootstrapping to test all the mediating effects. Specifically, we generated 5000 bootstrap samples and obtained the bias-corrected 95 % confidence intervals for the estimated co efficients. We present and discuss the analysis results in the following section.

## 5.1. Reliability, validity, and common method bias

For all the constructs of the study, the composite reliability and co efficient α values (see Table 3) are greater than 0.7, demonstrating good internal consistency and reliability. The factor loadings are significant (p < 0.001) for all constructs and range from 0.656 to 0.912. Moreover, all constructs’ average variance extracted (AVE) values exceed the mini mum threshold of 0.5. Therefore, the measures of all constructs have acceptable convergent validity.

Following Fornell and Larcker’s [85] suggestion, the discriminant validity of the measures was evaluated. As indicated in Table 4, the square roots of AVE of all constructs (the diagonal elements) are greater than interconstruct correlations (the off-diagonal elements), supporting the discriminant validity of the measures.

Harman’s single-factor test [86] was performed to assess common method bias (CMB). The outputs of the principal component analysis show that all self-reported items are composed of eight potential factors. The first principal component accounts for 38.916 % (<50 %) of the total variance, implying that it does not explain most of the variance. The model provides a total variance explanation of 63.910 %, indicating a good model fit. In addition, according to Liang et al.’s [87] method, a common method factor was introduced into the structural model, con sisting of all constructs’ indicators. The results demonstrate that the average method-based variance is 0.389, and the average substantively explained variance is 0.665. Most of the method factor loadings are insignificant. Last, the multicollinearity tests for indicators and inde pendent variables were performed by examining the variance inflation factor (VIF). As indicated in Table 3, none of the VIF values exceeds a value of 5.0 [88]. Therefore, it seems that CMB is not a serious problem in this research.

## 5.5. Moderating roles of consumer innovativeness

We performed a multi-group analysis to examine the moderating roles of consumer innovativeness. We divided the samples into two groups (consumers with low and high innovativeness) using a median split of the value of consumer innovativeness. The testing results (see Table 8) show differences in the influence of AR attributes between high and low-innovativeness consumers. Confirming H4b, the impacts of simulated physical control are greater for high-innovativeness con sumers than for low-innovativeness consumers. Specifically, we find the effects of simulated physical control on cognitive associations and af fective associations significantly different between the two consumer groups, but not the impact on sensory associations. Therefore, H4b is partially supported. Confirming H4c, response time has a greater impact among low-innovativeness consumers. Specifically, for lowinnovativeness consumer groups, response time significantly affects all three kinds of brand associations. However, for high-innovativeness consumers, response time only affects sensory associations, not cogni tive or affective associations. Nevertheless, the coefficient difference is only significant for the response time → cognitive associations path. Thus, H4c is partially supported. Inconsistent with our expectation stated by H4a, the impacts of perceived augmentation on brand asso ciations do not vary significantly between the two groups. Hence, H4a is not supported.

The results of the moderating tests provide compelling evidence for the significant role of consumer innovativeness in processing AR attri butes and forming brand associations through AR interactions. Furthermore, the moderating effect of consumer innovativeness varies across different AR attributes. Specifically, the results suggest that, regardless of their level of innovativeness, consumers exhibit similar attention toward the embedding attribute (i.e., perceived augmenta tion). However, high-innovativeness consumers place a greater weight on embodiment effectiveness (i.e., simulated physical control), whereas low-innovativeness consumers assign greater importance to embodi ment efficiency (i.e., response time).

AR allows consumers to control products virtually in their real-life environments, thus more effectively evaluating a brand based on their interactions with the products. The faster response of AR services allows consumers to learn requested product information with less time, facilitating more detailed personal evaluations of the brand. Overall, these AR attributes can increase cognitive fluency in information processing and, subsequently, allow consumers to form stronger and more thorough cognitive associations of the brand.

Second, the study confirms the positive effects of perceived augmentation, simulated physical control, and response time in enhancing the affective associations of the brand. AR services with high embedding and embodiment attributes can integrate physical reality and virtual content in an interactive, responsive, and realistic manner to create an authentic and immersive experience. Such realistic and seamless interaction contributes to a feeling of decision comfort and builds positive affective associations of the brand. Several studies have tested how AR attributes may elicit affective responses. For example, Mclean and Wilson [18] verify the effects of AR attributes on the enjoyment of using the technology. Different from their study that focuses on general media characteristics, our study emphasizes the unique AR properties that differentiate AR from other interactive tech nologies. In addition, Hilken et al. [3], Heller et al. [14], and Song et al. [23] test the effects of AR attributes on decision comfort. Our findings suggest the potential for the positive experience of using the technology, such as decision comfort and enjoyment, to be transferred to affective associations of the brand.

Third, the findings verify the effects of perceived augmentation, simulated physical control, and response time on brand sensory associ ations. Perceived augmentation implies the visible merging of virtual objects with an individual’s physical surroundings. AR provides con sumers with augmented display in a vivid depiction of the future reality, resulting in an appealing visual experience. Thus, perceived augmen tation of AR enables consumers to achieve a more excellent sensory experience than online stores without AR. Simulated physical control attaches some sensory feelings of physical products to the online shop ping experience, facilitating the “real” experience of the online product [3]. The prompt response of the AR system enables the rapid and smooth conveyance of multiple sensory cues to consumers using the AR service. As a result, compared with non-AR online stores, the capability to physically control a virtual object and the prompt response of AR functions can facilitate evoking better sensory feelings in consumers through visual, tactile, and other sensory cues during the virtual ex amination and interaction with products.

Fourth, the results indicate that both cognitive and affective associations influence overall brand equity positively, and the impact of affective associations is stronger than that of cognitive associations. This result suggests that in the context of mobile AR experience, the emotional connection between consumers and a brand holds more weight for brand equity than beliefs, thoughts, and evaluations about the brand. Cho et al. [60] demonstrate a positive relationship between sensory associations and overall brand equity in the non-virtual envi ronment. However, our results indicate that in the AR mobile shopping app context, the influence of sensory associations on overall brand eq uity is nonsignificant. This could be attributed to the limited availability of brand sensory stimuli in the AR mobile shopping app environment compared with physical stores. Current AR mobile shopping app designs typically focus on presenting product images [72]. Consequently, the formation of brand-related sensory associations may be constrained to visual cues associated with the branded products, such as the product and packaging images, as well as displayed brand logos. Sensory expe rience is processed intuitively and formed in a fast, subconscious, sim ple, and heuristic way [92]. The AR shopping app environment may not generate strong enough sensory stimulation to create a strong and enduring associative memory of the brand in consumers’ minds. Hence, sensory associations are not strong enough to influence brand equity.

This speculation is also supported by Zhao et al. [93] and Hilken et al. [72]. Zhao et al. [93] believe that consumers usually face the challenge of lacking sensory information when shopping in an online environment (e.g., a mobile app environment). Hilken et al. [72] find that in online shopping, AR is not as effective as other interactive technologies such as VR when it comes to influencing brand attitude. The authors argue that this is due to the fact that VR is able to create more immersive visual representations (i.e., sensory stimuli) of branded retail context (e.g., virtual flagship store environment), thus exerting a greater impact on brand attitude. Our results provide further evidence that the sensory associations generated by AR shopping app alone may not be strong enough to be transferred into the overall brand equity.

Finally, the results support the conclusion that consumer innova tiveness can contribute to the differences in the effects of AR attributes on brand. The results indicate that simulated physical control and perceived augmentation are more important in affecting the cognitive and affective associations of the brand for high-innovativeness con sumers. This finding confirms the argument that high-innovativeness consumers are more inclined to concentrate on a new technology’s pivotal characteristics that facilitate the direct accomplishment of a task [79]. However, the effect of response time on cognitive associations becomes more important for low-innovativeness consumers, while this effect is not significant for high-innovativeness consumers. This is because high-innovativeness consumers are less hesitant to invest time in resolving problems when using AR technology and are less concerned about the magnitude of time spent in AR technology adoption [22]. In contrast, low-innovativeness consumers lack involvement with new technology [22], making them more sensitive to the time it takes to use new technology.

On the other hand, we find that some connections between AR at tributes and brand associations are consistent between consumer groups. First, it is noted that consumer innovativeness has nonsignifi cant effects on the relationship between perceived augmentation and brand associations. A potential reason that high- and low-innovativeness consumers place similar weight on perceived augmentation in forming brand associations could be related to the type of AR application we tested in the experiments. We tested a type of AR application others may refer to as “AR mirrors” that “depict augmented looks that are trans formational, virtually modifying one’s face in the mirror” ([94], p. 170). Javornik et al. [94] argued that such virtual enhancement can signifi cantly augment the appearance of consumers and impact a consumer’s sense of self. These AR applications can increase consumers self-awareness. Therefore, we suspect that even though high-innovativeness consumers would care more about AR attributes that facilitate task completion than low-innovativeness consumers, under conditions where AR applications may activate consumers self-awareness and sense of self, perceived augmentation, which deals with the authenticity and quality of the augmented images, is as important for low-innovativeness consumers as it is for high-innovativeness consumers. Therefore, both high- and low-innovativeness consumers would pay attention to perceived augmentation when forming brand associations.

Second, even though the connection between response time and af fective associations is significant for low-innovativeness consumers but nonsignificant for high-innovativeness consumers, the difference be tween the two groups is not large enough to be statistically significant. This finding suggests that even though high-innovativeness consumers are not as sensitive as low-innovativeness consumers to the time it takes to use technology and are more willing to spend time interacting with AR apps, the enjoyable experience stemming from AR service’s quick response may not vary significantly between two consumers groups.

Third, AR attributes’ impacts on sensory associations are positive for both groups, and these impacts do not differ significantly between groups. This result implies that for both high- and low-innovativeness consumers, AR embedding and embodiment attributes have similar effectivity in arousing and forming sensory associations.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

This study is the first empirical study of the relationships among AR embedding and embodiment attributes, brand associations, and brand equity, resulting in a more effective model that explains the brandrelated outcomes of AR attributes. The findings contribute to the exist ing literature with granular knowledge of how augmented reality ex periences impact consumer value and business performance and provide valuable guidance for companies in carrying out augmented reality marketing campaigns.

First, our research demonstrates the significance of AR experiences in improving brand equity. The mobile AR presentations should be regar ded as a new marketing campaign that helps to build a company’s brand image and equity, in addition to immediate behavioral outcomes. Haumer et al. [16] investigate how the presence of AR affects overall brand equity through brand equity dimensions, including awareness, association, and loyalty. Our research further refines three characteris tics unique to AR experiences and divides brand associations into three dimensions, cognitive, affective, and sensory associations, extending the study of Haumer et al. [16]. Existing studies have only explored the impacts of AR attributes on the overall brand attitude, brand love, brand engagement, and brand usage intentions [17,18,36]. Our study further extends the research on brand-related effects from the perspective of brand equity. By investigating the particular variation in three types of brand associations triggered by AR embedding and embodiment attri butes, we obtain new insights into the drivers of brand equity and a deeper comprehension of how brand associations mediate the effects of these drivers on brand equity in the mobile AR context.

Second, though various embedding and embodiment attributes have been investigated in the AR literature, little empirical research has examined the combined effects of these unique AR attributes, particu larly in terms of embedding, embodiment effectiveness, and embodi ment efficiency. Our research identifies three corresponding constructs of AR’s unique attributes from all three AR experiential aspects. An emphasis on AR’s uniqueness highlights the technology’s unique value propositions, which are still underutilized in real-world business prac tices. The present study extends prior research by examining AR attri butes through the lens of situated cognition theory and refining the framework for constructing AR experiential factors.

Third, our findings suggest that AR mobile shopping apps can contribute to brand equity through cognitive and affective associations derived from the enhanced product and purchase experience. However, AR shopping apps that focus on product presentations may not generate enough context-related brand sensory stimulation [72] to impact brand equity through sensory associations. Our study identifies a limiting condition of Cho et al.’s [60] theory on the relationship between sensory associations and overall brand equity based on the non-virtual envi ronment. This relationship may not be applicable to the AR shopping app environment.

Fourth, this study confirms that consumer innovativeness moderates the effects of simulated physical control on cognitive and affective as sociations as well as the effect of response time on cognitive associa tions. Huang and Liao [22] investigated the roles of cognitive innovativeness in moderating the impact of technology attributes and experiential value in the AR context. This research expands Huang and Liao’s [22] study by investigating how consumer innovativeness in creases or decreases the influences of AR attributes on brand associa tions. By investigating the moderating roles of consumer innovativeness, we acquire a further understanding of why the effects of simulated physical control and response time in building brand equity vary across different consumers. When examining AR’s embodiment attribute, re searchers pay more attention to the effectiveness dimension than effi ciency or tend to use a single construct to capture both effectiveness and efficiency dimensions, such as interactivity. By comparing consumers with different innovativeness, we highlight the context in which sepa rating the two dimensions is necessary and important, as they have different implications across consumer segments. Our investigation provides guidance on the appropriate treatment of the embodiment attribute depending on the research context.

## 6.3. Practical implications

Our research provides critical practical implications for companies in implementing augmented reality and building brand equity through AR attributes in the mobile context.

First, the study’s results suggest that a company can offer a branded AR shopping experience to shape cognitive, affective, and sensory brand associations and ultimately enhance brand equity. This result confirms the importance of connecting the AR experience to a brand to boost branding outcomes. The findings also facilitate managers’ understand ing of how consumers perceive and respond to AR service, reducing the trial-and-error cost of implementing augmented reality. AR provides the try-on experience in an authentic way, since it allows customers to evaluate the online offerings in real environments without exposure to physical products. Hence, AR-based service augmentation should be popularized as a cost-efficient alternative to current product trial ser vices [3]. An available measure is to highlight the “AR trial” function with an eye-catching icon on the product display page. Furthermore, managers may launch AR interactive marketing programs in line with social hot spots and festivals to encourage consumers to form positive affective associations with the brand. For example, a likable and amiable virtual avatar presented by AR may increase consumers’ affinity to the brand.

Second, it is critical for managers to acknowledge the importance of both embedding and embodiment features in crafting successful AR experiences. Managers can focus on continuously optimizing augmented reality features to improve consumers’ AR experience rather than simply displaying branded products through AR. Our findings indicate that AR embedding attributes, specifically the perceived augmentation, are deemed significant by all consumers, regardless of their level of inno vativeness. Perceived augmentation is the unique attribute that differ entiates AR from other technology; therefore, it serves as a gatekeeping factor that affects the user experience of AR interactions. An ineffective projection of a product onto the physical environment can potentially hurt a brand if the poor embedding causes distortion in product perception and associations (such as product color, lighting, or texture) that negatively impact perceived product quality and brand reputation. To improve perceived augmentation, the 3D models of online products should be optimized to be more realistic and consistent under different physical environments. Moreover, practitioners should improve algorithms of AR service to enhance the seamless integration between online products and the reality, thus creating a vivid shopping experience.

To improve AR embodiment effectiveness through simulated phys ical control, AR service should enable consumers to evaluate products from a variety of angles. Additionally, more control functions on prod ucts can be deployed in the AR service to promote active interactions between consumers and the brand, thus strengthening consumers emotional connection with the brand. To improve AR embodiment ef ficiency, managers should pay attention to reducing the lag and frame loss of the AR system to ensure clear screen display and smooth switching, which in turn improves consumers’ interactive experience with brands.

As mentioned in the previous section, we suspect that the AR mobile shopping app environment may not offer sufficient opportunities to generate intense brand sensory stimulation that can significantly influ ence the overall brand equity. While companies could experiment with incorporating more branding sensory elements into AR shopping apps, there is a potential risk of distracting consumers from product learning. Therefore, we suggest that companies can coordinate their branded AR shopping experiences and other brand promotions. For instance, com panies can utilize other interactive AR tools to promote brand-related sensory when there is more flexibility in incorporating brand sensory elements, such as AR gaming apps and AR filters on social platforms. Companies can also leverage AR and VR technologies together to enhance branding outcomes [72].

Third, this study confirms that consumer innovativeness moderates the impacts of AR attributes on cognitive and affective associations. Accordingly, managers should consider the AR attributes identified in this study when designing AR apps for brands targeting different con sumer segments. Companies should aim to improve the AR user expe rience for different consumer groups based on their preferences. Consumers with less innovativeness tend to have more reservations about the new technology. In addition, they are more likely to own older mobile devices with limited capacity for apps utilizing advanced tech nology and updates. Our findings suggest that when a brand is geared more to conservative or low-innovativeness consumers, it may benefit from AR shopping apps with fast response and adequate product control functionalities. On the other hand, if the brand targets highinnovativeness consumers, more comprehensive AR shopping apps that enable creative, unique, and superior product interactions through simulated physical control may play a more significant role in brand differentiation and gaining brand equity. The finding also provides valuable insights for firms aiming to expand their reach through AR offerings. As the adoption of AR shopping apps among the company’s consumer base extends from the high-innovativeness early adopters to a broader consumer segment with lower innovativeness, companies should strive to balance the improvements in embodiment effectiveness and efficiency.

Finally, our findings demonstrate that manufacturer brands can partner effectively with retail platforms like Taobao to develop AR shopping experiences and utilize the technological resources supplied by online retail platforms to increase consumer brand associations and, subsequently, brand equity. The benefits of partnering with large retail platforms to develop AR experiences, as compared to developing brands own AR apps, are multifold. For instance, cost saving is a major benefit for manufacturers to partner with retail platforms for developing and maintaining the AR experience. Manufacturer brands can leverage large online retail platforms’ technological resources and market insights in developing and enhancing AR features. In addition, compared with developing and promoting a brand’s own AR app, partnering with retail platforms to develop branded AR services is more effective in reaching a wider audience present on the retail platforms and hence enhancing brand exposure. Manufacture brands can also use AR options as a method of differentiation on retail platforms to attract consumers.

## 7. Limitations and future research directions

This research has the following limitations. First, this study tests only the applications of mobile AR apps for sunglasses and hair dyes. Similar research could be conducted on other product categories and in other settings (such as physical stores) to validate the results and enrich the findings. Second, this research does not count for purchase scenarios and other consumer characteristics, such as product contextuality, the na ture of use, consumers’ information or visual processing style, or con sumer expertise. Future studies could examine these variables moderating role in the impacts of AR attributes on brand equity. Third, our research focuses on the mediating effects of brand associations, and future research could explore the mediating roles of other brand equity dimensions in the relationship between AR attributes and overall brand equity. Fourth, our study builds the connection between AR attributes and affective brand associations. We propose that decision comfort acts as the underlying mechanism of this link. However, it is important to note that our argument is derived based on theory, and we do not empirically test this mechanism. Previous studies have established the connections between AR attributes and decision comfort (e.g., [3,14]); future studies could further verify the connection between AR attributes and affective associations through decision comfort. Fifth, our study finds a potential limitation of AR shopping environment in impacting brand equity through sensory associations. Future research can further investigate the connection between sensory associations and brand eq uity in other online environments (e.g., VR shopping environment).

Finally, we find AR services’ perceived augmentation positively affect brand cognitive, affective, and sensory associations for both high- and low-innovativeness consumers. We suspect that it may be due to the type of AR app (AR mirror) we tested in our studies, which prompted par ticipants’ self-awareness and sense of self. Future research can investi gate the differences between consumer groups using AR apps that augment objects (e.g., [35]), such as furniture, instead of augmentation involving consumers themselves.
