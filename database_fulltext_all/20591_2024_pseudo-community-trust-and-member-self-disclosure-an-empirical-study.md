## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Social networking sites Self-disclosure Pseudo-community trust Mediation Moderation

## A B S T R A C T

This paper introduces a novel theoretical lens of pseudo-community trust and studies its effect on member disclosure on social networking sites (SNSs). We collect survey data from 229 SNS users and validate the research model using the structural equation modeling method. Findings indicate the effect of pseudo-community trust in promoting member self-disclosure and reveal the contingency of its effect. Moreover, our findings report important antecedents to pseudo-community trust and evidence their indirect effects on member self-disclosure through pseudo-community as a mediator. This research contributes to the trust literature and self-disclosure literature on SNSs.

## 1. Introduction

The popularity of social networking sites (SNSs) has grown at an astonishing rate. Yet, studies have shown a decline in members sharing their personal information (a.k.a., “original sharing”) across popular platforms such as Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram ([1],Kats, 2018, [2]). A decline in members’ disclosed information endangers the SNS business model. On SNS platforms, members’ disclosed information (e. g., personal activities, locations, comments/posts) allows computer al gorithms to build detailed profiles of online users. For example, the New York Times identified about 100 data points that Facebook used to build user profiles [3]. These user profiles are then capitalized by SNS pro viders to generate business revenues in multiple ways. In the example of Facebook, each user profile is estimated to generate a profit of \$28.57 per year (Harding 2018).

First, SNS providers sell access to member profiles to advertising companies that rely on these profiles to launch targeted advertising campaigns. For example, Facebook reported \$113.6 billion in adver tising revenue in 2022. If SNS providers do not have sufficient member data to construct and enhance user profiles, they may witness a decline in the worth of their profiles and a reduction in their advertising income. Second, SNS providers grant access to member profiles to other clients, who can then use these data to inform their own business decisions upon purchasing it. Pam Dixon, the founder and the executive director of the World Privacy Forum, commented that “a profile of your data could be purchased by a healthcare plan and used to determine your premiums.

Or, if you were applying to a private college, they might buy your data to find out if you can afford tuition before deciding to admit you” (PM, 2018). When member-disclosed information declines, these providers suffer from a loss of revenues because their data about members will become outdated or incomplete. Finally, SNS providers trade member disclosed data in exchange for even more data about their members from other service providers, such as Amazon, Microsoft, and Netflix. For example, Bing.com receives data about Facebook users’ friends, regardless of user consent to grant that access, and Netflix receives ac cess to Facebook users’ messages. Data sharing activities allow SNS providers to expand and improve their business operations. A decline in member self-disclosed data weakens an SNS provider’s ability to engage in data-sharing partnerships, which slows its growth. When these ac tivities are considered, SNS providers cannot afford a decline in member disclosure. Any decline in member disclosure will reduce the value of user profiles and subsequently will jeopardize the financial health and strategic growth of these multibillion-dollar corporations.

SNS providers need directions to boost member self-disclosure. When members do not feel that their personal information is being sufficiently safeguarded, they are hesitant to share it. There are two key types of safeguards: technical controls, such as privacy settings, and psycholog ical safeguards, such as trusting beliefs. The 2019 Pew Research report found that only 14 % of Facebook users knew how to change their pri vacy settings, showing the technical safeguard to be an unsuccessful option (Vogels, 2019). In contrast, trusting beliefs have received some support as a viable solution, despite confusion about the exact nature and relationships among the different types of trusting beliefs [4,5]. In this paper, we develop a new theoretical lens of pseudo-community trust to better understand the trust phenomenon on SNS, and empirically validate its effect on member disclosure. We examine the following research questions: (1) “What is the effect of pseudo-community trust on member self-disclosure and its contingency?” and (2) “How does pseudo-community trust develop on SNS?” New knowledge will allow SNS providers to develop effective strategies that encourage member self-disclosure. Our findings indicate that pseudo-community trust not only directly promotes self-disclosure, but also mediates the effects of other factors (site usefulness, social presence, and network size) on self-disclosure.

We organize the rest of the paper as follows. The subsequent section reviews the relevant literature and introduces the novel theoretical underpinning of the current study. Next, we present our research model along with research hypotheses. This is followed by an elaboration on research methodology and data analysis. We conclude the paper by discussing its theoretical contributions, practical implications, limita tions, and avenues for future research.

## 2. Literature review

In this section, we first review the existing literature on SNS member self-disclosure. Next, we investigate the trust phenomenon within the context of SNS to (1) introduce a novel theoretical lens of pseudocommunity trust and (2) explain the need for pseudo-community trust within the SNS context.

## 2.1. Prior studies on SNS member self-disclosure

Self-disclosure refers to personal information one shares with others. This may include “any information exchange that refers to the self, including personal states, dispositions, events in the past, and plans for the future” ([6], p.152). Member disclosed information is imperative for the long-term sustainability of SNSs (R. [7]). The SNS literature has generated rich insights into member self-disclosure. Hallam and Zanella [8] found that members’ disclosure behavior is affected mostly by social networking activities, especially for those who are yet to experience a major privacy breach. Zhang et al. [9] found that motivations for rela tionship maintenance and self-presentation mediated the effect of stress on self-disclosure. Walsh et al. [10] reported that members’ perceived network responsiveness would positively affect their self-disclosure behavior on the SNS. Osatuyi et al. [11] found that the subjects disclose more on the network when they know how to coordinate disclosure boundaries, and especially, when they have learned from previous privacy infringements. More example literature can be found in Table 1.

While prior studies have generated significant contributions to the research on member self-disclosure on SNS, the potential role of trusting beliefs has not been fully examined. On SNS platforms, the fundamental threat to member privacy is dishonest behaviors in handling privacy (e. g., misuse, unauthorized disclosure), which result in privacy breach incidents [14,23]. To this end, trusting beliefs act as an important psy chological safeguard against one’s perceived risks of privacy breach, hence promoting members to share personal information [24]. The extant community psychology literature has cautioned critics not to apply the “community” label to online systems too casually and has warned self-proclaimed “communities” as they may not truly function as communities [25,26]. As we will elaborate later in the paper that SNS platforms do not represent true communities, prior SNS literature has formed misconceptions of trust, such as community trust that does not exist on SNSs [27–29]. Consequently, there exist opportunities for new studies to properly conceptualize trusting beliefs (e.g., community trust or pseudo-community trust) before they inform and guide future SNS research. New studies are also warranted to explore the antecedents, effects, and contingency of pseudo-community trust on self-disclosure [30]. The current study fills these noticeable research gaps, thus contributing to the literature on trust and SNSs.

## 2.2. From dyadic to community trust and pseudo-community trust

SNSs have often described themselves as a “community” where members trust each other and the company, who plays the role of “administrator” through offerings of applications [31]. A real commu nity is a group of individuals, sharing a set of morals and goals, who have agreed upon a set of policies and procedures to communicate honestly with each other to achieve their common goals [32]. Accordingly, Per kins et al. [33] described community trust as “trust in one’s neighbors” and considered it a community-focused cognitive (both perceptual and meaning making) process of individuals, which affects formal and informal pro-social community behaviors. Therefore, community brings in a spatially bound aspect to trust [34]. This one-to-many trust is defined as a member’s subjective belief that interactions with others in a community will occur in a manner consistent with his or her expecta tions of trustworthy behavior [33].

Community trust extends from the traditional, one-dimensional view that only generalized trust and particularized trust exist. Generalized trust is normative and it reflects a belief in human benevolence (e.g., “in general people can be trusted”) – a highly stable value structure [35,36]; on the other hand, particularized trust is relational and is based on solid interactions between specific individuals [37–39]. A growing body of social literature has, however, questioned the one-dimensional di chotomy of trust [40–42]]. Sztompka [43], for example, argued that it is extremely difficult to delineate exactly where to draw the line between particularized trust and generalized trust. The dichotomous under standing of trust relies on an organizational principle that needs to be problematized and addressed: “whether persons are known to each other or not” ([44], p. 321). Where community is concerned, whether people trust their community is not determined solely by recognizing others because hardly any individual knows all community residents personally. Nor is community trust shaped solely by universal cosmol ogies about whether or not the world is a benevolent place. Indeed, community trust is defined by social boundaries and one’s experience and presumption of a local community, but it is not defined by degrees of familiarity – if the selected persons in question are friends or foes [33]. For example, Wollebæk et al. [44] found that members of multiple Swedish municipalities display distinct trusting beliefs toward other members in the same neighborhoods while showing little or no variation in their evaluations of trustworthiness of mankind in general (general trust) or family (particularized trust).

Both conceptually and empirically different from generalized or particularized trust, community trust renders an appropriate and necessary tool to understand the origins, attributes, and conditions for developing and conserving trust within a community setting. It is a result of personal experience with people sharing a space, as well as socially formed perceptions of that shared space as a context, which surpasses individual experience and reaches into the dominion of col lective, “imagined” memory as suggested in the “imagined commu nities” by Anderson [45]. In contrast to generalized or particularized trust, which is largely insensitive to local contextual characteristics (e.g., community member makings), community trust is sensitive to these factors. Finally, existing literature has found community trust to be much more directly connected with communal activities in a social system than generalized or particularized trust [34].

For SNSs, Piskopani and Mitrou [[30], p.3] noted that “community trust" considers (a) the trust between members and a community. (b) the trust between members, and (c) the trust between members and the community provider. However, the extant community psychology literature has cautioned critics not to apply the “community” label to online systems casually and has warned self-proclaimed “communities” as they may not truly function as communities [25,26]. Armstrong and Hagel [46] pointed out that true online communities exist when members share the same interest, experience, goals, or needs, and identified the four major categories of online communities to be interest communities, relationship communities, fantasy communities, and transaction communities. Fernback and Thompson [[47], p.4] recog nized genuine online communities as “social relationships forged in cyberspace through repeated contact within a specified boundary of place that is symbolically delineated by topic of interest.” In accordance with this, Peck [32] pointed out that it is a “pseudo-community,” rather than a unified community, which exists on social systems where mani fold groups refer to themselves as “communities” but are deficient in engagement and acceptance of individual differences to work as one. Put differently, members of a community will share the same goals whereas those of a pseudo-community may share incompatible goals.

The distinction between community and pseudo-community has sig nificant implications for the study of SNSs. Despite the multimillion member sizes of the leading SNSs, members do not have repeated con tact with the vast majority of their peer members. Additionally, one may join multiple groups on a SNS because SNSs do not have a unified common subject of interest. Popular interests on SNSs are far-reaching with prominent examples being the arts, music, movies, food, sports, science, and politics. Ultimately, member interactions give rise to the development of one colossal pseudo-community encompassing thou sands of small-scale “associations” that are distinct from one another. Arnett [48] cautioned against confusing community with concepts such as association, as members of a true community must be committed to both the people and ideals of the organization. In this study, therefore, we formulate SNS community trust as trust in members and trust in the provider, which is the component $\mathbf { \ddot { \rho } } _ { \mathbf { \vec { D } } } \mathbf { \vec { \rho } } _ { \mathbf { \vec { D } } } ^ { * }$ and “c” of Piskopani and Mitrou’s “community trust,” respectively, on the premise that a single cohesive community $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ component “a” of Piskopani and Mitrou’s “community trust”) does not exist on any SNS platform. We term this SNS-context community-oriented trust as pseudo-community trust on SNSs.

## 2.3. Pseudo-community trust on social networking sites

Pseudo-community trust carries important meaning to SNS research because it suggests that a member’s actions, such as self-disclosure, are rarely a response solely to peers in the same association. Instead, such disclosure is occasionally also targeted at and attractive to - purposefully or accidentally – outsiders of their current associations (“future friends”) so that new relationships can grow. Fearing “exclusion” from peer members, many users feel obligated to disclose to the pseudocommunity to maintain a social life [30]. Over time, one may alter their own existing groups in addition to joining new or leaving old ones. Yet, all these fall in place under the roof of the overarching pseudo-community within which all other members and groups reside. Therefore, trust in the pseudo-community, consisting of trust in peer members and trust in the SNS service provider, is relevant to one’s everyday use of the SNS.

We define trust in members as one’s belief that SNS members on the same platform will behave in a manner consistent with one’s confident expectations even when the possibility of opportunism is present. The nature of online social interactions makes one-to-many trust commendable [49]. SNS members may violate one’s positive expecta tions of them. SNS platforms have long attracted scammers, hackers, or predators on SNS platforms [50]. Even benign members may acciden tally disclose other members’ private data. Meanwhile, we define trust in the provider as one’s belief that the service provider will behave in a manner consistent with one’s expectations; that is, the provider will not disclose members’ private data or allow unauthorized secondary use of them. The enormous value associated with the pool of member data may entice SNS providers to capitalize on member privacy, through actions ranging from mining member data, sharing with partners, to selling to data brokers [51].

Unfortunately, SNS studies have not recognized the theoretical importance of pseudo-community trust. Through the novel lens of pseudo-community trust, our study reveals new insights on member self disclosure on SNS.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

In Fig. 1, we present the conceptual model that underscores the importance of pseudo-community trust in promoting member disclo sure. To explore the boundary condition of this effect, we consider members’ value of privacy as an important personal trait that strengthens the effect of pseudo-community trust on self-disclosure. To identify the sources of influences to pseudo-community trust, we follow West and Williams [52]. West and Williams [52] identified four inter related characteristics of a community. Relationship such as trust is associated with Function (goal achievement), Access (presence of others), and Vision (shared purpose). Considering the nature of SNSs (computer-facilitated online interaction), we focus on (1) perceived usefulness, which measures the extent of goal achievement enabled by the SNS platforms; (2) social presence, which measures the digital presence of online peers; and (3) network size, which serves as a reverse indicator of the level of shared purposes among members (members tend to possess a lower level of shared purpose when the group size increases).

## 3.1. Effect of pseudo-community trust and its contingency

We contend that members’ perceived pseudo-community trust will positively relate to their self-disclosure behavior because its two com ponents (provider trust and member trust) have a potential to reduce the perceived risks in sharing personal information. Incidents of SNS pro viders’ malpractice in handling member privacy have been frequently documented [51]. When members consider the service provider trust worthy, they expect a high probability of the company protecting user privacy, leading to a low chance of privacy breach. When SNS members perceive other members to be more trustworthy, they also become less worried about revealing private information due to the expectation that others will safeguard their private information. That is, a high level of pseudo-community trust leads SNS users to anticipate less threat to their disclosed private information. Therefore, they will feel comfortable in sharing more personal information.

H1: Pseudo-community trust positively relates to member self-disclosure.

Furthermore, we posit that the effect of pseudo-community trust on self-disclosure may be contingent upon members’ personal traits. Research on information privacy has found that individuals differ in their value of privacy [53]. Value of privacy refers to the importance that an individual places on keeping personal information private [54]. This value proposition may be cultivated by one’s disposition, culture, and past experiences with privacy preservation. Trust is valuable when risks exist [55]. Individuals with a low value of privacy expect privacy-related loss to be insignificant, thus underestimating perceived risks. As risks are perceived low, pseudo-community trust becomes less important or relevant to one’s decision making; therefore, its effect on self-disclosure becomes weak. On the contrary, individuals who place a high value on privacy tend to anticipate greater privacy risks. When faced with high risks, the trust established within a pseudo-community becomes a crucial factor for SNS users because it can help mitigate these risks and increase the utility of the SNS. That is, member self-disclosure behavior can be significantly accounted for by pseudo-community trust. Thus:

H2: Value of privacy strengthens the relationship between pseudocommunity trust and member self-disclosure.

## 3.2. SNS attributes as antecedents to pseudo-community trust

We posit that members’ pseudo-community trust is affected by SNS service qualities, such as site usefulness. We define members’ perceived SNS usefulness as the degree to which they believe that using a partic ular SNS will enhance their performance in managing social networks [56]. SNS usefulness can affect both components of pseudo-community. SNS provider trust may be improved by SNS usefulness because a function-rich platform indicates the ability of the provider in serving users well. A useful platform also convinces users of a provider’s good intent to help their members – a signal of benevolence. Trust studies consider both ability and benevolence to be chief building mechanisms of trust [57,58]. Therefore, we expect site usefulness to increase the perceived provider trust. In the meanwhile, SNS platforms with useful features (e.g., friends-of-friends, fake account detection, recommenda tions) enable members to find good contacts, reducing the odds of one meeting malicious or dishonest ones. Useful SNS platforms also allow one to locate contacts who share similarities (e.g., background, hobbies). Similarities are key to trust building [55]. As they are able to interact with good or similar people and gain positive experience, members may perceive greater trustworthiness in their peers on the platform. Overall, these arguments support a positive relationship between perceived site usefulness and pseudo-community trust.

H3: Perceived SNS site usefulness positively relates to pseudo-community trust.

We argue that members’ trust in an SNS provider may be affected by website design characteristics such as social presence. Social presence is defined as “the salience of the other in a mediated communication” [59] and it assesses the extent to which a communication medium allows end users to sense others as being psychologically present [60]. When perceived, it conveys the warmth, human contact, sociability, and sensitivity embedded in communication. We expect social presence to affect both components of pseudo-community trust. First, as trust is fashioned within social environment [61], social presence becomes a necessary condition for trust development among SNS members. Lack of social presence, on the other hand, renders it possible for SNS members to withhold information or engage in untrustworthy actions. Where SNS site is concerned, member-site interactions may be viewed in the same way as interpersonal interactions when a site is treated as a social actor. Prior studies [62] found that perceived social presence builds trust be tween members and a site because social presence facilitates user-website interactions. Additionally, a highly socially presented website creates a transparent environment by offering information and social cues that inhibit untrustworthy provider behaviors [63]. Jointly, these arguments support a positive relationship between the perceived social presence by members and pseudo-community trust.

H4: Perceived social presence positively relates to pseudo-community trust.

When network size grows, we anticipate it to hinder the development of both components of pseudo-community trust. First, we elaborate on the likely impact of network size on member trust in SNS provider. While a large network size gives rise to network externalities, the expansion of network size brings threats to privacy. The rising pool of collected member information emerges as a new, lucrative source of revenue that the service providers may tap into. All private member data has a price tag; when accumulated, a large amount of user data entices service providers to capitalize on the data by releasing them to business partners, telemarketing firms, or data brokers. Besides, a growing pri vate member data collection may enable and promote SNS providers to engage in invasive algorithmic inference, resulting in increased odds of misuse such as profiling members racially, judging member attractive ness, or even diagnosing medical conditions (D. [64]). Finally, large SNS firms may be more likely to monetize member data due to the need for more financial resources that support their massive operations. As a result, members of large SNSs may perceive greater uncertainty regarding provider-induced privacy misuse, causing diminished trust in the provider.

Second, we discuss the mechanisms through which network size may sabotage members’ trust in peer members. The sociology literature has long contended that there are potential relationships between social trust (trust toward others) and municipal characteristics. For example, Wirth [65] found that individuals living in large societies feel less close to each other than those who live in smaller societies; large societies are characterized by alienation and anomie. Likewise, Durkheim’s me chanical organic solidarity typology suggested that social trust is affected by a sense of solidarity, which in turn is shaped by the society size [66]. Small cities are associated with a great sense of solidarity due to similarities among residents with respect to ethnicity, religious af filiations, political dispositions, occupations, and economic status. In contrast, large cities are associated with low social solidarity due to a wide range of diversity. Pollock & Inequality [67], therefore, suggested that trust toward others will be greater in small cities than in large cities. It is arguable that a similar pattern applies to the SNS: users of those platforms with large network size may perceived less trust in others.

Since network size hinders the development of both components of pseudo-community trust, we expect a negative relationship between the two.

H5: Perceived network size negatively relates to pseudo-community trust.

## 3.3. Pseudo-community trust as a mediator

Members disclose personal information to help manage relationships with their social contacts. Because SNSs brings uncertainty to shared personal information, pseudo-community trust acts as a relevant factor in explaining members’ disclosure behavior. In contrast, the three website attributes have not shown consistent direct relationships with self-disclosure. For example, although Jia et al. [68] and Shibchurn & Yan [69] reported a direct effect of perceived usefulness on self-disclosure, McKnight et al. [70] and Tanakinjal et al. [71] found a lack of support. Likewise, Vitak [72] reported a direct effect of network size on SNS self-disclosure, whereas Mouakket & Sun [73] reported no direct effect.

It is argued that these website attributes do not have a strong direct relationship with member self-disclosure. They all describe the qualities of a SNS platform, such as the feature set, interface design, and system load, but not the characteristics of platform members. Since member self-disclosure is driven by one’s perception of both the platform pro viders and the site members (e.g., misusing personal data), we contend that these platform attributes alone play a limited role in explaining member self-disclosure. As a result, their potential impacts on member self-disclosure must be facilitated through other factors. Given that these website attributes build pseudo-community trust which then fuels member self-disclosure, we contend a mediating role of pseudocommunity trust: effects of the website attributes on self-disclosure may be effectively manifested through pseudo-community trust. The consideration of pseudo-community trust can better explain the under lying mechanisms, through which website features contribute to mem ber disclosure. We expect:

H6: Pseudo-community trust mediates the effects of perceived SNS site usefulness (a), perceived social presence (b), and perceived network size (c) on member self-disclosure

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

To validate the conceptual model, we used the survey method to collect responses from real SNS users. We administered the survey on Amazon Mechanical Turk, which has become a common source to re cruit subjects for academic research ([74–76]; Stritch et al., 2017). Workers were required to have a Human-Intelligence Task (HIT) approval rate of greater than 95 %. The HIT approval rate represents the proportion of completed tasks that are approved by requesters. In addition to requiring a minimum approval rate for the workers, a quality control question was embedded in the survey to filter unacceptable re sponses. In this question, responders were asked to select a specific choice to make sure they read the questions rather than selecting the options randomly.

Because the current research assesses a user’s experience with one SNS platform, we asked the survey participants to anchor their responses to the SNS platforms they were most familiar with. That is, in case a respondent used multiple SNS platforms, we asked the respondent to select the one platform that the person spent most time with. Subse quently, we asked the respondent to answer the rest of the survey questionnaire based on their own experience with that identified platform.

By the end of the survey distribution period, 255 workers attempted the survey, from which 26 responses were removed due to not completing all questions or failing the quality control question. Thus, we obtained 229 acceptable responses with an average response time of 8 min and 6 s. Table 2 provides the demographic information of the sample.

## 4.2. Construct operationalization

To measure the research constructs, we borrowed existing scales from the literature to achieve a high level of content validity. To mea sure member self-disclosure, we used four items from Krasnova et al. [77] to assess the richness of disclosed personal information on an SNS. For SNS site usefulness, we adapted the four-item scale of Davis [78] by contextualizing it into the SNS context. In accordance with Cyr et al. [79], we used five items to measure the perceived social presence. Following Wang et al. [80], we used six items to measure the perceived size of a SNS platform. With regard to value of privacy, we used three items of Chen [81] to measure the weight that one places on his or her private information.

For the new construct of pseudo-community trust within the SNS context, we created new scales. Because pseudo-community trust in cludes both member trust and service provider trust, we merged the provider trust scale of Malhotra and Agarwal [82] and the member trust scale of Chen and Sharma [83] into one new scale that considers one’s trusting belief in provider and members combined. The new scale con tains four items and it concerns both integrity and benevolence of the pseudo-community on SNS. For the purpose of robustness check, we also created a separate measure of pseudo-community trust by forming a second-order construct, which considered perceived SNS provider trust (measured using [82]) and perceived member trust (measured using [83]) as its underlying first-order construct. Second, a measurement model with the addition of a latent CMV factor must not significantly improve the fit over our measurement model without CMV. With a latent CMV factor, the variance of the re sponses to a specific measure is partitioned into three components: (1) trait, (2) method, and (3) random error ([84], p. 891). ## 5.4. Moderated effect of privacy

We simultaneously tested our theoretical model across two groups (participants with low value of privacy and participants with high value of privacy in an multigroup confirmatory factor analysis [MGCFA]). To allow the test, we applied the same measurement model to check for measurement invariance across two groups of privacy (low vs. high privacy), using an MGCFA, which produced Model 5. Next, we set all the factor loadings to be equal across two groups (low vs. high privacy) (Model 6) in a constrained MGCFA. We obtain measurement invariance across two groups of privacy (ΔCFI/ΔRMSEA < 0.01) (Table 5) [85].

The fit of the model is good (Model: $\chi ^ { 2 } = 6 5 9 . 2 3 ; d f = 3 3 2 , p < . 0 0 1 ;$ $\mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 8 ; \mathrm { G F I } = 0 . 9 0 ; \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 0 ; \mathrm { T L I } = 0 . 9 0 ; \mathrm { S R M R } = 0 . 0 6 ;$ adjusted $\chi ^ { 2 } = 1 . 9 8 )$ . The tests for the moderating effects of value of privacy used multigroup analyses within the SEM framework (Kline, 2005). A stepwise procedure assessed the invariance of the structural paths for different groups. The first step imposed equality constraints on structural paths $( \chi ^ { 2 } \mathrm { H } )$ . The second step involved running a baseline model with no parameter constraint, resulting in a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ with additional degrees of freedom $( \chi ^ { 2 } \mathbf { N } )$ . The test for the moderator effect took the difference in the two values $( \chi ^ { 2 } N - \chi ^ { 2 } H = \chi ^ { 2 } \Delta )$ , which produced a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value with degrees of freedom equal to the difference in degrees of freedom of the $( \chi ^ { 2 } \mathrm { H } )$ and $( \chi ^ { 2 } \mathbf { N } )$ values. To assess the moderating effects of the continuous “value of privacy” variable, we also applied double-mean-centering and estimated latent interactions with SEM. According to Lin et al. [86], a double-mean-centering strategy is superior to single-mean-centering and orthogonalizing approaches. Therefore, we first mean centered all indicators, then product terms for the latent variable indicators. All product terms then were mean-centered and served as indicators of the latent interaction variable. In order to test the interaction effect (value of $\mathrm { p r i v a c y } \ \times$ pseudo-communality trust), we mean-centered all the vari ables, which helped minimize multicollinearity [87]. This reduction in collinearity reduces or eliminates the associated instability of regression estimates and standard errors when collinearity is not removed $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the “bouncing beta weight” problem). ## 6. Discussion, implications, limitations and future research

Our study provides valuable insights into the factors that influence self-disclosure behavior on SNSs). The study used an SEM approach to analyze the relationship between various factors and self-disclosure. The results show that pseudo-community trust was positively related to self disclosure, suggesting that when SNS users trust the pseudo-community, they are more likely to engage in self-disclosure behaviors. In addition, the study found that perceived usefulness and social presence were positively related to pseudo-community trust. This indicates that users who perceive a high level of usefulness and social presence within their SNS are more likely to trust the pseudo-community. On the other hand, perceived network size was negatively related to pseudo-community trust. This implies that users who perceive a large network size are less likely to trust the pseudo-community. Furthermore, the study found that the value of privacy moderates the impact of pseudo-community trust on self-disclosure. Specifically, the positive association between pseudo-community trust and self-disclosure was stronger among users who placed higher weight on their privacy. Finally, this study used a mediation model to analyze the indirect effect of perceived usefulness, social presence, and network size on self-disclosure through the medi ation of pseudo-community trust. The results showed that the indirect effect was significant, suggesting that the relationship between these factors and self-disclosure was mediated by pseudo-community trust.

## 6.1. Research implications

Our research makes several theoretical contributions. First, we theorized the important role of pseudo-community trust in motivating SNS members’ self-disclosure behavior. Because disclosed personal in formation may be misused, community-oriented trust becomes a critical decision factor for SNS members. Given that members hold diverse in terests, values, and standards, SNSs do not represent coherent online communities [91]. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to propose and test a model that uses online trust literature and in corporates relevant antecedent of trust to explore the direct effect of pseudo-community trust on member self-disclosure. Building upon the trust literature, our exploration and development of the lens of pseudo-community trust offers a novel theoretical perspective to the SNS literature. Empirical results further attest to the importance of pseudo-community trust in affecting member self-disclosure. Although prior SNS literature has not distinguished between community trust and pseudo-community trust ([27,28]; P; [29]), the current research un derscores pseudo-community trust as a more appropriate theoretical lens to guide SNS research.

Second, we identified three SNS attributes that cultivate members pseudo-community trust. Specifically, we theorized perceived SNS use fulness, social presence, and network size as important contextual fac tors, and we found evidence that these SNS attributes significantly associate to pseudo-community trust. Furthermore, the current study reported that pseudo-community trust mediates the effects of these SNS attributes on member self-disclosure. Third, we were among the first to contend for the moderating effect of privacy value on the effect of pseudo-community trust. The present study also makes a noticeable methodical contribution. Empirical analysis indicated the two measurements as reliable alterna tives, as they yielded similar results. Both measurements displayed satisfactory qualities (e.g., CFA, global fit). Development of these two alternative scales provides utility to SNS researchers in their future studies that involve pseudo-community trust.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

By revealing the antecedents, mediators, and moderators, our find ings inform practice. Because pseudo-community trust encourages member self-disclosure, it is important that practitioners boost trusting beliefs (i.e., two components of pseudo-community trust) held by members. For example, service providers may improve their perceived trustworthiness through improvements in site usefulness, social pres ence, and network size. Take site usefulness as an example. Facebook has recently improved news feeds via increased preference controls (e. g., prioritizing friends so that important contacts are displayed first), the addition of new features such as “Legacy Contact”, “On This Day”, and controlled scrapbooks of child photos. SNS providers may also increase social presence through design options such as: (1) socially rich texts, images, and videos; (2) awareness enhancing features such as status updates and self-presentation; and (3) social interaction features that connect one member with other individuals or groups [63]. To counter the destructive effect of network size on pseudo-community trust, SNS providers ought to engage in initiatives that demonstrate their willing ness to protect member privacy. Participation in privacy seal programs (e.g., TRUSTArc, WebTrust, PwC Privacy) serves as an example

initiative.

Next, the mediation effect discovered by the present study suggests SNS providers that they can use pseudo-community trust to regulate the effects of website attributes on self-disclosure. For example, although network size indirectly discourages member disclosure, its effect mani fests through pseudo-community trust as it reduces the level of pseudocommunity trust. While a SNS platform has a limited motivation to reduce the size of its members, the platform provider may take actions to increase pseudo-community trust (e.g., using promotional campaigns that enhance members’ perceived trust) to offset the likely damage that network size may bring to members’ pseudo-community trust.

Finally, practitioners should recognize the value of privacy along with its moderating effect on the impact of trust. In this regard, pro viders may expend greater amounts of resource and effort to improve their perceived trustworthiness among members who are profiled, by algorithms or user registration data, as “privacy sensitive.” In doing so, changes in perceived provider trust will produce a greater impact among these members than among those who deem privacy less important.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

Our study is not without limitations. First, the current study employs a cross-sectional design and, therefore, causality cannot be inferred. While a longitudinal analysis is preferred, cross-sectional models must first be established before future research can examine their viability over time [92].

Next, the current study utilizes self-reported survey data to assess member self-disclosure. Prior studies have found a positive correlation between self-reported and actual data of members’ use of Facebook [93]. Nevertheless, future studies should capture actual self-disclosure through the assistance of server-side data.

Future study may also examine how individual SNS users perceive trust in third parties on Facebook. For example, scammers have been found to use Facebook-based quizzes, apps, and games to steal member data [94,95]. A prominent example is the Cambridge Analytica Scandal, where the “This is Your Digital Life” mobile app collected data of about 87 million users without their consent or awareness. Considering the prevalence of such third-party systems on SNS, it is important to un derstand why users may be tricked into believing the trustworthiness of systems that are deceptive.

Moreover, scholars may examine the moderated mediation and mediation comparison, which might generate implications for both theories and practice. For example, one can test whether the path of perceived social presence → trust → disclosure is more dominant than other paths, when the value of privacy is high.

Finally, future studies may investigate the way the pseudocommunity trust affects the content shared by the users (other than personal information). They can address research questions such as: (1) Whether the pseudo-community trust affects the self-disclosed content such as the depth and width of the story or the emotion of the user, so that the content attracts a greater number of readership? And (2) Whether the value of privacy strengthen the member’s confidence, so that they are willing to share more personally stories? We expect pseudo-community trust to cast a positive impact on self-disclosed content because trust is found related to empathy, which encourages the sharing of personal contents [96–98]. Its overall effectiveness in promoting more comprehensive and profound content may also be influenced by other factors such as the supportive and non-judgmental nature of the audience.

## 7. Conclusion

Member self-disclosure carries strategic importance to SNS pro viders. In this research, we explore the role of pseudo-community trust in promoting members to disclose themselves. Pseudo-community trust exists on large-scale SNS platforms as the vast number of members do not necessarily sense the belonging to one single coherent community [47]. We theorize the relationship between pseudo-community trust and member disclosure and also investigate the antecedents to pseudo-community trust. Findings of our paper indicate the validity of our research model. This paper introduces a new theoretical perspective to benefit the literature on member self-disclosure on SNS platforms.
