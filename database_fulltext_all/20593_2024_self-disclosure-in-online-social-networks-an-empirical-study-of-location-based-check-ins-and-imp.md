# Self-disclosure in online social networks: An empirical study of location-based check-ins and impression management

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Health-related lifestyle Impression management Location sharing Observational learning Self-disclosure

## A B S T R A C T

Leveraging online networks like Twitter and Foursquare, our study explores the dynamics of self-disclosure and impression management through location sharing. Grounded in social and hyperpersonal information processing theories, we investigate how peer influence, geographical proximity, and exposure to promotional content impact self-disclosure behaviors. Through an integrated dataset, we determine that friends’ check-ins and their physical proximities significantly sway individual self-disclosure. Particularly, in the presence of healthconscious peers, users tend to avoid check-ins that create a negative health impression. Furthermore, exposure to health-related promotional content significantly influences these behaviors. Our findings provide crucial perspectives on impression management in the digital age.

## 1. Introduction

Self-disclosure, defined as the “process of making the self known to others” ([1], p. 91), is a fundamental element of communication. It allows individuals to express themselves, maintain social relationships, and fulfill emotional needs such as belongingness [2–;4]. Self-disclosure occurs in both offline and online settings, but online social network platforms facilitate this process through features such as information broadcasting, image sharing, and location sharing [5,6]. In these networks, public self-disclosure is prevalent, allowing users to shape their identities for a wider audience.

Technological advances have further amplified the frequency of self disclosures of individuals on online platforms. A recent study has illustrated how smartphones magnify the level of self-disclosure as compared to personal computers [7]. This enhancement is attributed to the ability of smartphones to facilitate the narrowing or focusing of the user’s attention and provide psychological comfort, making the self-disclosure process more intuitive and immediate. Smartphones are effective in this context due to their portability, as well as the ubiquitous access they offer to the internet and online social networks. This constant connectivity enables individuals to easily share diverse life events from various locations, enriching the context and richness of their self-disclosures.

These instant disclosures provide a subtle yet powerful mechanism for impression management. Sharing information about the types of places visited in daily life allows for an implicit presentation of oneself. This kind of disclosure, incorporating location sharing, adds a layer of credibility with environmental cues, making the shared information appear authentic and relatable. Rahman [8] emphasizes that the act of sharing visited locations often serves socially oriented goals rather than merely proving one’s physical presence.<sup>1</sup>

The process of self-disclosure on online platforms involves the traditional communicative elements of a sender, a receiver, and message content, adapted for the digital context. The architecture of social networks uniquely positions individuals as both senders and receivers, providing them (as receivers) with a mix of personal disclosures and promotional content. This exposure influences individual decisionmaking [9], especially in the process of self-presentation when individuals decide what to disclose as senders. Research has identified two primary content types to which individual users are generally exposed: user-generated content (UGC) and firm-generated content (FGC) [10, 11]. Our study explores how these distinct content forms influence public self-disclosure through sharing visited places, with a special focus on health behaviors. Self-disclosures that characterize health-related behaviors, such as checking into a gym or visiting fast-food restaurants, directly reflect personal habits.

Self-disclosure on digital platforms can be explicit or implicit [12]. Explicit disclosure will typically involve the use of a narrative format. Implicit disclosure, on the other hand, occurs when individuals present themselves by sharing activities, interests, and preferences without actually stating them. Research has shown that a majority of individuals prefer to present themselves online through implicit disclosure [12], and sharing one’s location, whether through images or location tagging or narrative text that implies the location, serves as implicit self-disclosure. When someone shares a health-related location, such as a gym or fast-food restaurant, the disclosure can influence communal health practices, underscoring the importance of understanding the roles of self-disclosure and promotional content in public health promotion.

While previous studies have noted that a friend’s individual checkins can inspire others to visit the same place [13], researchers have not delved into the broader impacts of check-ins. The frequency of check-ins in a certain type of location (e.g., a fast-food restaurant) by friends, regardless of the exact geographical location of the place, can subtly shape a person’s future location disclosure behaviors. A key consideration is that friends’ behaviors can influence an individual’s behavior in more complex ways than just direct replication. For example, regular exposure to one type of behavior—such as gym check-ins—may not only motivate a person to mirror that behavior, but also potentially reduce the person’s participation in contrasting behaviors, such as visiting fast-food restaurants. Understanding how these opposing behaviors interact with each other is a significant area that needs more research.

It is also vital to understand the role of perceived distance between friends within this structure. Despite the importance of distance, previous research has provided mixed findings about the role of physical distance in the influence of friends’ posted content within social media platforms [4,13,14]. Indeed, proximity can potentially enhance or dampen the impact of social influence, affecting how strongly an individual follows certain behaviors or avoids contrasting ones.

In addition to the effects of friends’ content, exposure to content from firms and other active social media pages, which promote specific behaviors and often derive financial benefits, can also impact an individual’s self-disclosure behaviors. These sources can shape the narratives around certain behaviors, thereby influencing decisions on which behaviors an individual chooses to self-disclose. However, the influence of a firm may differ from that of friends due to the marketing nature of this content, a dynamic that warrants further investigation to better understand the interplay of influences in location-sharing behaviors.

Our study aims to fill these gaps and empirically answer the following research questions: (1) How do social, spatial, and promotional factors—friends’ behaviors, geographical proximity, and exposure to content from firms and social media pages—impact public location-sharing behaviors, particularly in the context of healthrelated locations? (2) To what extent does impression management serve as an explanatory basis for these factors?

To address these questions, we conceived a research model anchored in the theories of social and hyperpersonal information processing. These theories propose that computer-mediated communication systems, such as online social networks, provide ample opportunities for impression management, even in the absence of nonverbal cues [15]. To enhance the richness of our theoretical model, we incorporated element from self-discrepancy and social comparison theories (Festinger 1957; Higgins 1989) to offer a more comprehensive view of the dynamics at play in online interactions.

To scrutinize these dynamics empirically, we used a custom dataset comprising more than 27,000 active Twitter (now known as X) users who publicly disclosed their location over a span of six months. Our focus was primarily centered on their location-sharing behavior at two specific types of venues: gyms and fast-food restaurants. Our rationale for selecting these venues is twofold. First, these venues represent spaces where individuals partake in activities with either positive or negative health connotations. The frequency of location sharing at these places can thereby serve as an indicator of an individual’s health-related lifestyle habits. Second, the dichotomous impressions created by sharing one’s location at these venues (promoting a healthy lifestyle versus an unhealthy lifestyle) present an opportunity to glean novel insights into individual self-disclosure behaviors within the realm of online social networks.

Our paper presents several pivotal and insightful contributions to the existing literature. First, we illustrate that the influence of friends location sharing extends beyond the simple promotion of a specific venue. Instead, it can have a more profound impact by encouraging individuals to disclose information more frequently about places that echo similar values and characteristics. Second, we elucidate how the geographical proximity of friends can dynamically shape the mechanism of social influence. We demonstrate how such proximity can impact the process of engaging in similar behaviors and discourage contrasting ones. This insight extends our understanding of the social fabric that governs online disclosures and interactions. Third, our research indicates how exposure to firm-related and promotional content on social media platforms, particularly those centered around health-related topics, can craft a normative environment around individuals. This environment in turn can significantly influence their disclosure behaviors. The understanding of this dimension adds a nuanced perspective on the role of targeted content in shaping online interactions. Fourth, we propose a novel methodological approach to weight friends’ social influence in a more dynamic manner. Specifically, it distinguishes among friends’ influences based on specific criteria, such as geographical distance. This methodological innovation promises to enhance the precision of predictive models in social science research and provide a robust tool for future explorations. Finally, we consolidate our results into a theoretical framework that expands our understanding of the process of impression management within the context of online social networks. Our research not only illuminates the intricacies of these dynamics but also offers a robust model for guiding future research in this area. This theoretical expansion could therefore serve as a foundation for further academic exploration into the world of online impression management and peer influence.

The structure of this paper is as follows. We first present a review of the literature on impression management and self-disclosure within the context of online social networks. Subsequently, we delve into the theoretical aspects of the process of impression management and exposure to different types of content in online social networks. Next, we describe our data collection process and introduce the econometric models employed in our study. This is followed by a discussion of our analysis, as well as the robustness and validity checks that we undertook. We then interpret the results of the study and enhance our theoretical framework by proposing a high-level model based on the insights gained. We conclude our paper by outlining the implications of our findings, acknowledging potential limitations, and proposing directions for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Impression management

Impression management is a broad topic defined as “a goal-directed process in which people, either consciously or unconsciously, attempt to influence the perceptions of others about a person, object, or event” ([16], p. 504). When the impression that people want to make becomes self-relevant, the topic of impression management in the literature becomes synonymous with “self-presentation” [17,18].

Self-presentation can be either private or public. Private selfpresentation is a purely cognitive process in which individuals form an image about themselves [17,19]. According to self-discrepancy theory [20], people maintain three representations of self, namely, the actual, ideal, and ought selves. The actual self is who people truly are, whereas the ideal self is who people want to be and is usually self-constructed or formed through comparison with others. Finally, the ought self is who people believe they should be based on social norms and the opinions of others. Research has shown that the degree to which these three selves are aligned affects people’s psychological well-being [21]. In contrast to private self-presentation, public self-presentation always involves an overt behavioral element [17]. People can choose which self they want to present in public and behave in ways that will influence the impressions that others form about them. Research has found that the digital selves produced in a personal web space are relatively consistent with the individuals’ real-life selves [22]. This supports the idea that individuals do not take on entirely new identities but express selected aspects of their real-life identities.

Researchers have investigated the role of wide range of factors in individual public self-presentation. One of the seminal works was done in the 1950s by Goffman. He postulated the world as a stage where people get the chance to present themselves. As a sociologist, he emphasized the role of interpersonal factors in self-presentation and believed that the situational context between individual and audience determines individual behavior [23]. Goffman’s dramaturgical view of individual self-presentation was later supported by more research, which demonstrated that gaining social approval is the primary motivation for such behavior [24–26]. Social researchers offer two prominent modes of public self-presentation, namely, acquisitive and protective self-presentations, which enable individuals to successfully manage the impression that they want to make. In acquisitive self-presentation, individuals put forth what they believe to be a positive presentation of themselves to gain the approval of others. By contrast, in protective self-presentation, people present a neutral or modest version of them selves to avoid being unfavorably viewed by others [24,26]. The in dividual’s choice of the mode of self-presentation is determined by how well they know the expectations of the target audience [21]. Individuals use an acquisitive strategy of self-presentation to form a favorable impression when they know the target group’s expectations, and they use a protective strategy to avoid creating a negative impression when they are uncertain about the target group’s expectations.

Individual self-perception also influences public self-presentation. People may use different modes of self-presentation, not necessarily indicating dishonesty, but demonstrating how self-perception affects the presented self-image [17]. The theory of self-concept further substantiates this perspective, emphasizing the individual’s subjective interpretation of their traits and values in this process [27].

Self-disclosure vs. Self-presentation. The terms self-disclosure and selfpresentation are used interchangeably in the literature. There is, however, a distinction between the two that should be considered. Self disclosure is “regarded as attempts by the actor to acquire, crystallize, or convey accurate information about the self” ([28], p. 22), and self-presentation is “viewed as attempts to package or fabricate information that is in the actor’s best interests” ([28], p. 22). While self-disclosure has been recognized as a vehicle for self-presentation [29], it contains an element of honesty or authenticity [30] that may or may not be present in all forms of self-presentation. That said, self-disclosure does not always provide a complete picture of the individual. People are generally selective in their self-disclosure, revealing only those aspects of themselves that are positive rather than negative [29].

## 2.2. Self-Disclosure in online social networks

Self-disclosure is an important component of online social network [31], and it is crucial to the success of some of these platforms [32]. Individuals are more satisfied with online platforms that promote self-disclosure [33] and tend to use them more frequently [34]. Indeed, online social networks allow for self-disclosure in ways that were previously not possible in offline settings. Some of the characteristics that distinguish online social networks from face-to-face communication include asynchronous communication, various modes of self-disclosure, and, in some cases, anonymity [35]. Individuals can use the asynchronous feature to think through what they want to disclose before sharing it. Online social networks allow individuals to reveal themselves not only in narrative form, but also through shared images, music, and the locations they visit. Finally, anonymity gives individuals an opportunity to self-disclose their problems (e.g., health issues) or aspects of the self that may cause embarrassment or social rejection in offline settings [21].

There are also some limitations on individual self-disclosure within online social networks. In comparison with face-to-face communication, online social networks offer a lower level of social presence<sup>2</sup> and information richness<sup>3</sup> [36]. As a result, self-disclosure on such platforms is characterized by a lack of nonverbal cues and a lower rate of impression formation, similar to other forms of computer-mediated communications ([15], 1996). Indeed, a lack of social presence prevents people from benefiting from nonverbal cues like kinesics, proxemics, posture, paralanguage, and appearance in their disclosures, and a lack of information richness slows down the process of impression management.

Previous research has investigated how self-disclosures of people differ in online and offline settings. Marriott and Buchanan [37] found no evidence of a difference between these two settings in the presentation of the actual self. They contend that online environments are well-integrated into human life and can be viewed as an extension of offline life. Walther [38] argued that when computer-mediated communication involves social relationships, it can become hyper personal, implying that people may find their online relationships more appealing than their face-to-face relationships. One reason for this attraction is the ability to selectively disclose information to others and present a more positive (ideal) self.

Online Location-Sharing. Advances in location-based services have added a new dimension to mobile-based applications, including online social networks. Individuals can bridge their physical and social worlds by sharing their location in online social networks. The location can be shared in these networks through geotagging or place tagging. Indeed, a distinction exists between location as a “space” and location as a “place” [39]. Location as a space describes a geometrical arrangement that can be helpful for activities such as movement; location as a place refers to recognizable and persistent social meaning [39]. For example, individuals who frequently share their location through check-ins at gyms shows their passion for fitness, whereas frequent check-ins at religious places may indicate strong religious beliefs. Research suggests that in the context of online location-sharing, the concept of location “has changed from being something you have (a property or state) to something you do (an action)” ([40], p. 65). Thus, observing an individual’s repeated check-ins at various places on online social network platforms over time contributes to the impression that friends or peer group members form about that individual

Three aspects set location-sharing disclosures apart from many other forms of disclosure. First, spatial cues are present that allow other people to validate the accuracy of the self-disclosure at certain levels. These cues provide a warranting value for the target audience. Warranting value is “a psychological construct that reflects perceptions about the extent to which information is immune to manipulation by the source it describes” ([41], p. 187). The presence of a cue with warranting value allows people to see a resemblance more easily between an individual’s self-disclosed information in online platforms and who the individual actually is in the offline world [42]. Second, location-sharing allows for the observation of diverse and even contrasting behaviors among peers, spanning across a spectrum of geographical distances. This can shape impressions and influence individuals in unexpected ways. Third, location-sharing allows for implicit disclosures. Individuals can use implicit disclosure to share personal and private information that they may or may not be comfortable discussing explicitly because of com munity norms [43]. As a result, a location-sharing disclosure affords an alternative option for people to present themselves.

Note that location sharing is typically self-selected. Research has shown that people have reasons for not sharing their locations at certain places [44]. For example, one of the participants in the Lindqvist et al. [44] study explains why he/she does not check in at fast-food restaurants:

“I never check in to fast food restaurants like McDonalds, Burger King, Taco Bell, etc. I don’t think anyone would be impressed by that sort of check in.” ([44], p. 2413)

Yet another participant framed it thus:

“Checking in at fast food restaurants too often is embarrassing.” ([44], p. 2413)

Such opinions suggest that people are aware of the impressions that their frequent check-ins at various types of places can create. Furthermore, contrary to the opinion of these participants in the Lindqvist et al. [44] study, we observe that individuals exist who frequently check in at fast-food restaurants. This raises an important question: what factors influence people’s location-sharing disclosures? In this study, we intend to answer this question by conceptualizing a model and empirically validating it.

## 3. Theoretical background

Computer-mediated communication (CMC) represents an umbrella term for various types of human-to-human communication over com puter networks. A wide range of theories have been employed to study CMC as it continues to evolve. One of the early theories in this context is social information processing (SIP) theory, developed by Walther [15]. SIP explicates how interpersonal communication occurs through CMC-based technologies. According to the theory, despite the absence of nonverbal cues, CMC-based technologies provide opportunities for impression management [15]. Indeed, SIP suggests that available communication cues on CMC are interchangeable with those in face-to-face communication and that traversing social information using certain cues, such as text-based cues in online communication, may take more time before individuals can form impressions of one another [45]. Walther [38] has also argued that if CMC-based technologies are primarily used for social and recreational purposes, communication over them could become hyperpersonal. He introduced four major components of CMC that contribute to its hyperpersonal characteristics: channel attributes, receivers, senders, and feedback processes.

We assert that while these four components undoubtedly are essential elements of communication in online social networks, we must also consider the interplay among these components, as well as the nature of the information shared on the platform to better understand the dy namics of impression management among users of online social networks. Acknowledging these dynamics allows us to comprehend the complexity of communication processes in online social networks in a more profound manner. Our model explores beyond the established theoretical framework by incorporating additional features. These supplementary facets aim to shed light on the influencing factors and mechanisms that govern individuals’ decisions about location-sharing disclosures and the impressions they seek to portray.

## 3.1. Observational influence of the disclosures of friends

Senders and receivers are two salient components of the hyper personal model that play decisive roles in the process of self-disclosure. Each user operates as both a sender and a receiver at different times, depending on their position within the communication dynamic. The message content that a user receives, as one of the main elements of communication, is typically generated in social media by other platform users or through a firm’s marketing campaign on its official social media pages [10]. This situation with two types of messages represents a more complex channel structure than traditional CMC systems. The content generated by each group not only has different attributes but can also be perceived differently by individual users. Focusing on content generated by other users in this section, we argue that the interaction between receivers and senders can help form interpersonal relationships, which are frequently highlighted as a critical element in the self-disclosure literature [46]. This user-based interaction in online social network often manifests as feedback or observational influences.

While the hyperpersonal model traditionally emphasizes feedback as a behavioral confirmation tool, we propose a shift in focus toward the significant role of observational learning in the context of impression management in online social networks. Observational learning is a concept that enhances SIP theory and is cultivated as users observe and learn from the behaviors of their peers within the network, subsequently influencing the impressions they intend to project. It aligns with findings from related studies that acknowledge the role of social influence, particularly in settings where interactions are not only goal-oriented but also social in nature [46].

Note that within the context of impression management, observational learning not only encourages increased engagement and posts related to behaviors endorsed by others, but also leads to reduced engagement in behaviors that contradict these norms. This dynamic indicates a complex landscape of influence and response.

Several theories have been developed to support the impact of observational learning on human behavior. According to social cogni tive theory, social interactions allow individuals to observe other people’s behaviors and the consequences and use those observations to guide their own behaviors [47]. Social comparison theory argues that observing others allows individuals to compare themselves to others and define themselves [48]. Accordingly, observational learning has been identified as one of the main sources of social influence on human behavior [49]. Social influence occurs when the likelihood of performing a particular action depends on the engagement of the individual’s peers in the related actions [49.50].

Research on individual behavior has argued that social influence i an important factor in the propagation of most human behaviors via social networks [51]. Although online social networks provide a relatively low level of social presence and information richness [36], individuals are repeatedly exposed to their friends’ disclosures. According to SIP theory, it is expected that information is aggregated over time through observational learning, allowing individuals to form impressions about others. In the case of online social networks, the frequency of location sharing in one type of venue by friends should form impressions about the nature of activities and places that their friends tend to participate in and disclose in online social networks. This observation is expected to affect individuals through the mechanism of social influence. Thus, we argue that individuals’ location-sharing disclosures are influenced by those of their friends in the same network.

## 3.2. Observational influence and location proximity of friends’ disclosures

When evaluating location sharing as an observational learning tool in the process of self-disclosure and impression management, it becomes imperative to consider the influence of the geographical proximity of friends’ disclosed locations. This factor not only represents a shared physical space for potential interaction with friends [49], but also cultivates a sense of homophily [14]. Extensive research indicates that the locational proximity of friends can substantively shape an individual’s actions and behaviors [52] and can extend its influence on their online interactions [53]. Prior studies have also emphasized that suggestions received from a closer distance may be perceived as more relevant and trustworthy [54]. A similar pattern has been observed in the purchasing behavior of users, with researchers finding that geographical proximity plays a significant role in the effectiveness of online word of mouth in shaping the purchasing behavior of individuals $[ 1 4 , 5 5 ]$

However, the relationship between proximity and influence can be complex and may vary based on individual factors and contextual nuances, thus necessitating a deeper exploration. Moreover, exploring how the dynamics of influence evolve with increasing distance from the disclosed location is also an area to investigate. It is plausible that individuals perceive location disclosures in their immediate vicinity differently from those farther away. Yet, an unanswered question is whether this holds true when we examine the self-disclosure behavior of individuals on repeated actions over time, actions that may form an impression about themselves, such as their check-ins at gyms and fastfood restaurants, and if the context plays a significant role. We intend to examine this intricate relationship further in our study.

## 3.3. Exposure to firm-generated content on social media

The “channel” serves as a fundamental element of the hyperpersonal model [38]. In contrast to traditional CMC systems in which the channel allows interactions only between individual users, in social media networks the channel also enables the interaction of individual users with a multitude of firm-owned social media pages. By promoting specific content or behaviors, these brand-owned pages can diversify the spectrum of information users receive based on their preferences, subtly shaping the norms and behaviors within these networks.

Prior research on this FGC has assessed its impact on firm perfor mance and consumer behavior [56,57]. These studies demonstrate that engaging FGC not only boosts sales and brand value for firms [56,58], but also influences consumer behavior, enhancing brand loyalty and fostering deeper relationships with brands [56,59]. Kumar et al. [56] highlighted the critical role of consumer receptivity with FGC—such as liking, commenting, and sharing—in amplifying these effects. However, the content posted by firms on social networks can prompt other behavioral manifestations, where individuals leverage self-presentation through activities aligned with promotional content. This self-presentation, echoing the brand’s values or lifestyle, illustrates the profound influence of a brand beyond its direct offerings, shaping how individuals perceive and affiliate themselves with the brand’s broader representation.

In the context of this study, the influence of FGC posted on firms social media pages in promoting behaviors with contrasting health implications becomes particularly noteworthy. Such pages might promote activities associated with a gym (generally perceived as healthy) or they might endorse fast-food consumption (commonly seen as unhealthy). The exposure to these contrasting inputs could lead to distinct patterns of self-disclosure, depending on an individual’s existing attitudes and lifestyle choices. For instance, regular exposure to content promoting physical fitness and healthy lifestyles might motivate users to share their own gym-related activities, shaping an image of themselves as healthconscious individuals. However, users frequently interacting with pages promoting fast-food might be inclined to disclose behaviors aligned with that lifestyle, despite these behaviors being generally seen as less healthy.

Therefore, the exposure to promotional content posted on these brand-owned social media pages represents another source of influence. Firms’ social media pages act as senders, promoting content that is relevant to their themes on a regular basis. As a result, following them on online social network platforms places people in a receiver position and provides another source of influence that can assist them in identifying events or places that provide services related to their topic of interest. Hence, we intend to delve into how this critical aspect of social media shapes the impression management strategies individuals deploy through their location sharing, particularly in the context of health related activities.

## 4. Data collection and measurement

Our data collection took place from January 28 to June 17, 2014, leveraging three main sources to assemble a panel dataset: Twitter, Foursquare, and US census data (American Community Survey 5-year data, 2013). Twitter (now known as X), a highly popular social medium and micro-blogging platform, provides a unique window into people’s daily lives. Individuals use Twitter to share updates about their activities, often incorporating integrations with other social platforms for a more comprehensive portrayal of their lifestyle. This includes Foursquare, a pioneer in location-based social networking, which users can link to their Twitter accounts.

Through this integration, Foursquare permits Twitter users to integrate location check-ins within their tweets. These check-ins allow users to identify and tag the places they visit to share with their social network. This synergistic relationship between Twitter and Foursquare serves as a key resource for our research data. To extract the required data, we used the Twitter API, focusing specifically on tweets that contained Foursquare-tagged check-ins within the United States. The link accompanying each check-in was exploited to gather additional information about the type of venue the user visited. In conjunction with this, the Twitter API facilitated the collection of users’ social network graphs and their patterns of connectivity to social media pages. To maintain a cohesive dataset, we considered only users belonging to the largest interconnected component of the network. Consequently, our final dataset comprised 27,698 users.

The check-in data we collected was supplemented with information about individuals’ gender, popularity, and socioeconomic status. To measure these, we used the Foursquare API to gather data on gender and gauged popularity based on the number of Twitter followers. Socioeconomic data was obtained by identifying each individual’s center-ofactivity and mapping it to the corresponding US census tract data<sup>4</sup> (American Community Survey 5-year data, 2013). We computed each individual’s center-of-activity via a three-step process. Initially, a density-based spatial clustering method (DBSCAN) was used to categorize all check-ins; then, the cluster with the highest frequency of daily check-ins was designated as the individual’s main cluster. Finally, the geographical midpoint of all check-ins in the main cluster was calculated, serving as a proxy for the individual’s primary activity center. Appendix A provides a detailed description of the process along with further validation of the representativeness of our measurement. Fig. 2 shows the distribution of individuals’ centers-of-activity across different US states.

Measurement of Exposure to FGC. To capture the level of exposure of users to firms’ social media pages, we initially employed the homophilybased user embedding (HUE) method [60] to cluster social media pages into different groups. This method uses a heuristic community detection approach called the Louvain method [61] to cluster social pages using a weighted adjacency matrix of these pages. The weights in the adjacency matrix represent the similarity value of social pages:

$$
\operatorname{Sim} \left(S _ {A}, S _ {B}\right) = \frac {\left(\left| V \left(G _ {A} , G _ {B}\right) \right| + \left| E \left(G _ {A} , G _ {B}\right) \right|\right) ^ {2}}{\left(\left| V \left(G _ {A}\right) \right| + \left| E \left(G _ {A}\right) \right|\right) . \left(\left| V \left(G _ {B}\right) \right| + \left| E \left(G _ {B}\right) \right|\right)}
$$

where $s _ { A }$ and $S _ { B }$ are two arbitrary social media pages and $G _ { A }$ and $G _ { B }$ are graphs of their followers in online social networks. $| V ( G )$ | represents the number of followers and $| E ( G ) |$ is the number of relationships between followers in a graph G. ${ \big | } V ( G _ { A } , G _ { B } )$ | and $\vert E ( G _ { A } , G _ { B } )$ | represent the number of common followers and links, respectively, between $G _ { A }$ and G<sub>B</sub>.

Once the clusters were identified, we calculated two separate metrics to capture the level of exposure. ## 5.1. Preliminary model estimations

Our study leverages a panel dataset to investigate two separate binary dependent variables, representing whether individuals checked into gyms and/or fast-food restaurants on each day over a period of approximately 24 weeks. The primary variables of interest in our preliminary analysis included the average number of friends’ check-ins at gyms and fast-food outlets over the seven days leading up to each day in our dataset.

In the directed network structure of Twitter, the following relation ship is represented by A→B, meaning that user A is a follower of user B, and B is considered a friend of A. Consequently, user A can observe and interact with the behaviors and posts shared by B on the platform. We also assess the level of exposure individuals had to the content of two types of firm-owned social media pages (i.e., FGC)—one type promoting healthy behaviors linked with gym activities, and another endorsing unhealthy behaviors associated with fast-food consumption. We use logistic regression for our model estimation and specify the following equation for our model estimation.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {logit} \big (P \big (Y _ {i, t} = 1 \big) \big) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} ^ {*} (\text {Average Friends' Gym Check - ins}) _ {i, [ t - 1, t - 7 ]} \\ \quad + \beta_ {2} ^ {*} (\text {Average Friends' Fast Food Check - ins}) _ {i, [ t - 1, t - 7 ]} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {3} ^ {*} (\text {Average Exposure to Gym FGC}) _ {i, [ t - 1, t - 7 ]} \\ \quad + \beta_ {4} ^ {*} (\text {Average Exposure to Fast Food FGC}) _ {i, [ t - 1, t - 7 ]} \\ \qquad + \sum_ {k} \beta_ {k} ^ {*} (\text {Control Variables}) _ {i k} + \mu_ {i} + \lambda_ {t} + \delta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In our logistic regression model, the probability of individual i checking in at a location (either a gym or a fast-food restaurant) on day t is denoted as $P \big ( Y _ { i , t } = 1 \big )$ . This is expressed as a function of the following variables:

• Average Friends<sup>ʹ</sup> Gym $C h e c k - i n s _ { i , [ t - 1 , t - 7 ] }$ and Average Friends<sup>ʹ</sup> Fast Food $C h e c k - i n s _ { i , [ t - 1 , t - 7 ] }$ represent the daily average number of check-ins by friends of individual i at gym and fast-food venues, respectively, over the seven days prior to day t.

• Average Exposure to Gym $F G C _ { \ i , [ t - 1 , t - 7 ] }$ and Average Exposure to Fast − Food $F G C _ { \ i , [ t - 1 , t - 7 ] }$ indicate the daily average number of exposures of individual i to social media content generated by brandowned social media pages promoting gym-related activities and fast-food consumption, respectively, over the seven days prior to day t.

• Control Variables includes controls for the number of individual i's check-ins at gyms, fast-food restaurants, and other venues in the past seven days; the average number of likes (or feedback) individual i received on their gym and fast-food check-ins in the past seven days; the average number of friends’ check-ins in locations other than gym and fast-food restaurants; the standardized number of followers and friends of individual i on the social media platform; the betweenness centrality of individual i in the social network; the standardized total number of check-ins across all the venues by all individuals in our dataset within a 30-mile radius around individual i’s center of activity; the standardized average distance from individual i’s center of activity to their friends’ centers of activity; and demographic factors like individual gender, as well as other factors like income, median age, poverty level, and education level that were determined based on individual i’s center-of-activity location.

• μ represents random effects for individual i.

• $\lambda _ { t }$ accounts for fixed effects for each day of the week on day t.

• δ accounts for fixed effects for the month of the year associated with day t.

• $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the error term for individual i on day t.

Additionally, our model includes a term to control for time trends in our estimation. The coefficients $\beta _ { 0 }$ to $\beta _ { 4 }$ and $\beta _ { k }$ are the parameters to be estimated. Table 2 shows the results of the regression for the two separate types of location.

The results of our preliminary model demonstrate that an increase in friends’ check-ins at gym and fast-food venues amplifies the likelihood of individuals checking in at the same type of venue, thereby fostering an impression about participating in activities associated with these venues, both for gym and fast-food establishments. This trend is observed in both types of venues, suggesting the potential role of observational learning from similar behavior in shaping self-disclosure behaviors.

Our findings provide support for the opposite effect of friends’ gym check-ins on individual fast-food check-ins, significant at $p < . 0 1$ . We discovered that increased gym check-ins by friends led to a lower likelihood of individuals disclosing check-ins at fast-food places. However, the converse is not true; that is, our study did not find a reverse effect from fast-food disclosures on gym check-ins. It is also important to note that the magnitude of the impact of friends’ fast-food check-ins on individuals’ fast-food check-ins remains three times higher than the negative impact of friends’ gym check-ins. This highlights the strong influence of observational learning in replicating similar behaviors.

Furthermore, our findings underscore the significant influence of exposure to firm-generated content that promotes behaviors associated with gym and fast-food venues. This content positively influences the likelihood of individuals making self-disclosures at these venues, highlighting the powerful role of targeted digital exposure and FGC in molding individual disclosure behaviors. Our results also show that the increased exposure to FGC that promotes gym-related healthy behaviors significantly reduces self-disclosures at fast-food venues, at a significance level of $p < . 0 5$ . These results offer valuable insights into the potential of digital content to guide behaviors toward healthier choices. However, similar to the influence of friends’ behaviors, we did not observe a significant impact of exposure to fast-food-related FGC on individuals’ gym disclosure behaviors.

A notable observation from our model is the discernible difference in the impact sizes between friends’ posted behaviors and firm-generated promotional content. This difference reveals that friends exert a significantly greater influence on individuals than targeted promotional content does.

## 5.2. Identifying the role of geographical proximity

Thus far, our analysis has not differentiated between the impact of self-disclosures from friends who are located proximal to an individual’s center of activity and those situated at a greater distance. To develop a more nuanced understanding of how distance influences the relation ship, we established a more complex model. This model assigns weights to friend check-ins based on the distance of the disclosure location from the individual’s center of activity. This approach allows us to explore the potential gradient effects of distance on the impact of friend selfdisclosures. The following equation shows our new estimation model.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {logit} \big (P \big (Y _ {i, t} = 1 \big) \big) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} * \sum_ {j} \sum_ {s} \exp \big (- \theta_ {1} * D _ {i, j, s} \big) * G _ {i, j, s} \\ + \beta_ {2} * \sum_ {j} \sum_ {s} \exp \big (- \theta_ {2} * D _ {i, j, s} \big) * F _ {i, j, s} \\ + \beta_ {3} * (\text {Average Exposure to Gym FGC}) _ {i, [ t - 1, t - 7 ]} \\ + \beta_ {4} * (\text {Average Exposure to Fast - Food FGC}) _ {i, [ t - 1, t - 7 ]} \\ + \sum_ {k} \beta_ {k} * (\text {Control Variables}) _ {i k} + \mu_ {i} + \lambda_ {t} + \delta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Here, $G _ { i , j , s }$ represents friend $j ^ { \prime } s$ gym check-in on day $s , F _ { i , j , s }$ represents friend $j ^ { \prime } s$ fast-food check-in on day s, and $D _ { i , j , s }$ represents the distance from individual i’s center of activity to friend j’s check-in location on day s. This is summed over all friends over the past 7 days [t − 1, t − 7]. We assume that the weight of friends’ check-ins follows an exponential decay distribution, with closer distances having higher weights.

To estimate our model, we first eliminate the random effect facto from our model and estimate parameters using the Nelder–Mead opti mization method over the logit cost function:

$$
J (\beta , \theta) = - \sum \left[ y _ {i} ^ {*} \log (p _ {i}) + (1 - y _ {i}) ^ {*} \log (1 - p _ {i}) \right]\tag{3}
$$

Once all parameters, including $\theta _ { 1 }$ and $\theta _ { 2 } ,$ , have been estimated, we calculate the weighted average of friends’ check-ins using the estimated θ parameters and re-estimate the β parameters, this time including the individual’s random effect in the model. Table 4 presents the outcomes of our refined estimation models, which include the distance-weighted check-ins of friends in the regression analysis.

Figs. 4 and 5 display the estimated effect size attributed to friends check-ins as a function of distance from the center of activity for core individuals for fast-food and gym disclosures, respectively.

Our refined models, incorporating distance-weighted check-ins from friends, have shown improved model fitness as evidenced by an enhanced Akaike information criterion (AIC) factor for both models. To further test the significance of the θ parameters, we employed the loglikelihood ratio test. For each model, we set $\theta _ { 1 }$ and $\theta _ { 2 }$ alternatively to zero (this creates a reduced model by giving equal weight to all check ins of friends for each type of venue, disregarding their distance from the individual’s center-of-activity). The log-likelihood ratio was then computed against the full model, and a chi-square distribution was used to calculate the corresponding p-value. Detailed results of these models are provided in Appendix B.

Our findings reveal that all θ parameters, except $\theta _ { 2 }$ in Model $^ { 2 , }$ are significant, showing the salience of the effect of location proximity on the exertion of observational influence in the process of impression management. One insightful result relates to the significance of $\theta _ { 1 }$ in Model 1 $( \mathbf { a t } p < . 0 0 1 )$ ). As a consequence of the new weighting strategy, which assigns higher weights to gym check-ins of friends at very close distances to the center of activity of individuals, the negative impact of friends’ gym check-ins on individuals’ fast-food disclosures not only becomes more robust but also shows a higher level of significance at $p <$ .001.

## 5.3. Robustness check models

To further ensure the robustness of our study’s results and the validity of our findings, we conducted a series of robustness checks. One concern with our estimation models is the presence of individuals who have never checked into fast-food restaurants or gyms. These individuals may be concerned about the impression they wish to project and might avoid certain posts regardless of observing friends’ behaviors or exposure to FGC on social media. Their presence in the estimation model could be considered a source of bias. Accordingly, we re-estimated our models after removing users who had no check-ins at all in these venues. This adjustment reduced the number of users in the estimation to 24,831 for fast-food check-ins and 13,509 for gym check-ins. The results of the new analysis are reported as Model 1 in Tables 4 and 5 for fast-food and gym check-ins, respectively. Although our results remain robust, we observe that the θ parameters for weighting the distance of friends behaviors have changed slightly. Particularly, the impact of friends’ gym check-ins on individuals’ fast-food check-ins has broadened, extending the negative effect of friends to a wider area around the center of activity for each individual.

A second concern relates to the endogeneity caused by the presence of other unobserved individual-level characteristics that may influence the results of our estimation models. Our earlier models controlled for the lag of individual behaviors from the past week in the same type of venue to mitigate endogeneity. This time, we switched from using random effects to individual fixed effects and removed the lagged variables related to the dependent variable to further validate our results. Note that in nonlinear models, the use of a large number of fixed effects can lead to the incidental parameter problem [62] and may also cause convergence issues. To counter this, we avoided using them in our main models. The results of the second set of robustness checks are presented as Model 2 in Tables 4 and 5, separately for fast-food and gym check-ins, respectively. The results of the new set of analyses remain largely consistent with our initial findings. although the significance levels of the variables decreased. One notable change in our new estimation is the shift of the effect of gym-related FGC on individual fast-food check-ins to an insignificant coefficient.

A third concern arises from how friendship is defined in our study: a one-way relationship where following another individual is considered friendship and a source for observing behaviors. To counter this potential bias, we considered the behaviors only of friends with whom individuals have a two-way relationship. The results of this new set of analyses are presented as Model 3 in Tables 4 and $^ { 5 , }$ separately for fastfood and gym check-ins. The results remain consistent with our initial models. One noticeable change is a further reduction in the θ parameter for weighting the distance effect of friends’ gym check-ins on individuals’ fast-food check-ins. This adjustment broadens the negative effect over a wider distance for stronger friendships.

The first set of robustness check models provided consistent results regarding friends’ observed behaviors, but there was one inconsistent outcome concerning the influence of gym-related FGC on individuals disclosed fast-food check-ins. To further validate our initial findings, we used a different metric, capturing the level of exposure to FGC related to fast-food and gym behaviors by the extent of following these pages rather than by exposure to their posted content. This new metric lacks a lag structure, is time invariant, and can be endogenous due to reverse causality. This scenario might occur when an individual’s frequent check-ins at specific types of places, such as gyms, influence their interest and behavior in following certain social media pages. For instance, an individual’s increased gym check-ins might lead them to follow more gym and fitness-related social media pages, while showing less interest in pages related to fast-food consumption. To better establish the causal relationship, we incorporated an instrumental variable approach in our analysis, considering the level at which an individual’s friends follow fast-food-related and gym-related social media pages as two instrumental variables. This selection is based on the idea that individuals and their friends often share similar interests, likely resulting in correlated levels of exposure to similar types of social media pages, thereby confirming the relevance of the chosen instrumental variables. However, the following behavior of friends (i.e., which firm-owned social pages they choose to follow) does not directly affect individuals' check-ins. We used a control function approach to address endogeneity in this section.<sup>5</sup> Table 6 presents the results with the new alternative metric for exposure to promotional content.

The outcomes derived from the first stages of the regression underscore the relevance and effectiveness of the chosen instrumental variables. The second-stage regression results provide important insights into the influence of connectivity to social media pages associated with promotional content for gyms and fast-food on individuals’ selfdisclosures at gym and fast-food locations. The new results are consis tent with our initial findings on the effect of exposure to promotional content on individuals’ self-disclosure check-in behaviors. Additionally, the results still indicate that connectivity to gym and fitness-related social media pages and exposure to their promotional content can decrease disclosures at fast-food places.

## 6. Discussion and theoretical expansion

Over the past decade, online social networks have become one of the most popular communication tools for people to stay in touch with family and friends. They allow individuals to connect with others they would otherwise not have connected with via conventional channels. With online social networks, individuals can keep up with the daily activities of friends who live far away or have been associated with them for a long time $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ school friends). In such a social environment, selfdisclosure serves as an impression management tool, allowing people to craft and reveal their “ideal self.” Research has shown that the impression formation process in online communities is slower but more effective in the long term ([38], 2015). The slower process occurs because each piece of self-disclosed content in online social networks contains relatively little information about an individual. However, the accumulation of similar self-disclosures results in the formation of more durable impressions. This is in line with David Hume’s [63] argument about the effect of repetition:

“When many uniform instances appear, and the same object is always followed by the same event; we then begin to entertain the notion of cause and connection. We then feel a new sentiment or impression, to wit, a customary connection in the thought of imagination between one object and its usual attendant.”

This research delves into the interplay of social, spatial, and promotional factors within the landscape of online social networks that influence individuals’ self-disclosure through location sharing. The focal point of the study lies in understanding self-disclosure activities of individuals at two kinds of venues with health-related implications—gyms and fast-food restaurants. Public perception often associates regular gym-goers with a health-conscious, fitness-oriented lifestyle, while those frequently seen at fast-food outlets are considered less conscientious about their health and dietary practices.

Location sharing serves as a unique indicator in this context, revealing subtle cues about an individual’s environmental choices. These choices may allow observers to associate certain values and characteristics typically linked to specific locales with the individual. Even when location sharing does not consistently mirror the actual places an individual visits, it can communicate the type of self-image individuals wish to portray. The study is anchored in four elements of the hyperpersonal model of communication: sender, receiver, channel, and feedback. In online social networks, users—senders and recei vers—constantly interact through disclosure and observation, creating the fabric of interpersonal dynamics integral to the process of impression management. The channel, besides facilitating this sender-receiver interaction and the observation of other users’ generated content, exposes individuals to FGC and promotional content, providing another layer of influence on individuals. Feedback is another important factor in the hyperpersonal model. In this study, we control for feedback in the form of “likes” received on an individual’s disclosures.

The core of this research revolves around the dynamics of peer in fluence in self-disclosure, encapsulating both the alignment with friends’ similar disclosures and avoidance of contrasting impressions. The incorporation of location proximity allows a deeper understanding of peer influence on self-disclosure behaviors within health-related venues. Additionally, the study investigates the role of exposure to promotional content generated by social media pages related to brands and firms linked to fast-food outlets and gyms, probing its impact on individuals’ likelihood of disclosing visits to such places. This approach uncovers another dimension of self-disclosure behaviors, adding to the depth and nuance of our understanding.

Our results demonstrate that the health-related self-disclosures of friends, at both fast-food establishments and gyms, have a positive impact on an individual’s future disclosures at comparable venues. This is consistent with established theories on social influence. Moreover, we found that the geographical proximity of friends’ disclosures imbues an additional layer of influence on subsequent disclosures of the same nature by individuals. However, it is crucial to note that this influence is not exclusively tied to friends in close proximity; instead, it extends to all an individual’s friends, but the weight of influence decreases as a function of distance. Our findings indicate that the decay parameter used in the econometric model is statistically significant. However, in practical terms, observing a meaningful reduction in the weight of the influence necessitates employing a considerably larger distance.

A more nuanced understanding emerges from our study in the case of avoiding contrasting behaviors. We observe that individuals tend to avoid presenting unhealthy impressions (such as self-disclosure at fastfood places) when friends project a healthy image through their gym check-ins. This behavioral pattern is mostly evident with fitnessconscious friends residing in close geographical proximity, implying a stronger connection with people in the nearby vicinity and a heightened awareness of their “ought selves.” As the self-discrepancy theory by Higgins [20] posits, the perceived discrepancy between one’s actual self and one’s “ought self” can engender a sense of guilt. Thus, when individuals discern a prevalent positive norm (like healthy behavior) in their immediate surroundings, they may experience guilt when engaging in, or disclosing about, counterproductive activities. This effect could potentially ripple outward to more distant connections, as decreasing the frequency of an individual’s fast-food disclosures nearby may indirectly influence their far-off friends’ disclosure habits.

These findings further enhance our understanding of the roles of distance and physical proximity in social influence processes. Despite an early prediction that distance would become meaningless with the development of online platforms [64], recent studies emphasize the significant role of proximity in social influence [14,55]. Our study shows that while physical proximity is a significant factor in observational learning, the effects of such mechanisms depend on the context. For instance, our results indicate that increased physical distance reduces the weight of peer influence when the social influence involves mimicking similar behaviors. However. friends at long distances can still exert considerable influence. Conversely, when observing peer behavior (gym and fitness activities) influences individual’s avoidance of contrary behaviors (unhealthy eating), physical distance can become more practically significant. Our results show that such influence is generally limited to peers in close proximity to the individual. Another interesting finding, captured in the section on robustness checks, is the potential for the strength of friendship to mitigate the impact of distance on peer influence in contrasting behaviors. This contributes to the literature on psychological distance, which has previously examined the effects of both spatial and social distance on how individuals mentally represent and process information [65]. Our study demonstrates that these two elements can interact with each other, influencing the impact of these mechanisms on individuals’ information processing.

Last, our research demonstrates that an individual’s exposure to health-related FGC, an interaction that is facilitated by social media channel features, increases their likelihood of disclosing check-ins at venues associated with health-related implications. We observed that heightened exposure to gym-related or fast-food-related social media content increases an individual’s propensity to check-in at gym or fastfood locations. However, our findings show that such an effect is significantly lower than the effects of content generated by friends. Additionally, our results largely suggest that exposure to gym and fitness-related content can significantly reduce an individual’s propensity to disclose visits to fast-food locations. However, because one part of our analysis did not support this effect, further research is needed to investigate this phenomenon more thoroughly.

## 7. Implications

## 7.1. Practical and policy implications

This research raises a variety of significant practical and policy implications. First, our study offers a more nuanced understanding of how individuals manage their impressions online and how the dynamics of social influence interact with the physical proximity of peers within the framework of online social networks. We demonstrate that the social influence exerted by friends’ location sharing extends beyond merely promoting a specific venue and can be broadened to encompass the type of behavior encouraged by a particular location. Online platforms can leverage these insights to devise new features or strategies that foster healthier behaviors via encouraging self-disclosure of those behaviors or effectively target different user groups according to their respective disclosure patterns.

Second, public health practitioners can use these findings to design interventions that promote healthier behaviors, given the clear rela tionship between self-disclosure and health-related choices. If, for example, a strong correlation between gym-going friends and gym check-ins can promote healthier lifestyles, health campaigns could harness this influence to promote healthier behaviors. Additionally, policymakers can use the insights from our study to shape how social media platforms use location sharing and advertising, with an eye on their impact on public health.

Third, the findings of our study are useful to marketers and adver tisers seeking to understand their target audience better. For instance, gym and fast-food brands can strategize their promotional activities by using the insights gleaned on location proximity and the effect of exposure to social media pages related to gyms or fast-food outlets. This understanding can help them formulate more effective campaigns and branding strategies. Fourth, online social network administrators can consider the findings of this study when designing and refining social network platform features, since a deeper understanding of user behavior can lead to a more influential network.

## 7.2. Theoretical implications

Our study presents several theoretical implications. First, we investigate and formulate a theoretical framework that refines the under standing of social influence theory, self-discrepancy theory, and impression management theory within the context of online social networks. This layered perspective enriches the theoretical comprehension of these phenomena and serves as a basis for effectively guiding future research. Specifically, the integration of location proximity into our research model allows the study to contribute to environmental psychology. It elucidates how geographical proximity can mold online selfdisclosure behaviors, an aspect that has received scant attention in prior research.

Second, our theoretical framework juxtaposes user-generated content and firm-generated content, illustrating that new CMC-based channels provide individuals with multiple sources of exposure. These varied sources can influence behaviors at different levels and are moderated by factors attributable to the source of the information. This approach highlights the complex interplay between content types and their impact on user behavior within online environments. Third, we contribute to the field’s methodology by introducing an econometric model and an estimation approach designed to dynamically weight the influence of peers in the context of other factors such as geographical distance. This methodological innovation can have a significant impact on enhancing the precision of predictive models in social science research, permitting more nuanced insights and interpretations. Furthermore, it could provide a robust tool for future research seeking to unravel the complex dynamics of social influence in online and offline contexts.

## 8. Limitations and future research

As with all empirical studies, this work has limitations. First, there are several channels that facilitate the process of self-disclosure, including blogs, online forums, and other social media outlets. This study examines disclosure only through posts on check-ins at Foursquare, posted via Twitter. Clearly, the inclusion of other disclosure channels will provide a richer tapestry for studying impression management. Second, we relied on location-based check-ins as a subliminal indicator of health-related impressions. This surrogate measure does not necessarily indicate true health orientation. For example, checking in at a party held at a gym or visiting a fast-food establishment just to use the Wi-Fi does not necessarily reflect a healthy or unhealthy lifestyle. Nevertheless, we argue that repeated check-ins and tagging of places on social media can represent the impression that individuals wish to project about themselves. Third, we captured a user’s social network at a single point in time early in the process of our data collection, but social networks are dynamic and can evolve over time.

As with any social media activity, check-ins can be disproportionately affected by special events. Large sports events or musical concerts can generate a substantial number of check-ins during a limited window, not just at the event location, but also at related locations, including fast food restaurants. This applies to large participatory events, such as competitive running events, charity drives, political conventions, and similar gatherings. To a lesser extent, conferences, site openings, and cultural events may also generate a spike in check-ins at fast-food

restaurants and gyms. Conversely, situations like pandemics, evacuations, and natural or human-made disasters can cause check-ins to plummet precipitously for extended periods. These events create extremely long and heavy tails in the distribution of check-ins, which need to be treated as outliers. It is important for future studies to consider these potential anomalies when generalizing the findings of our study.

Future extensions of our work could incorporate additional modal ities such as images and textual content along with disclosed locations for making impressions. Another aspect that can be studied is the evo lution of impressions, particularly in the context of changing societal norms. Our work can be extended to other contexts within online social networks, where individual self-disclosures are linked to privacy and security concerns. Another area for future research is examining the effect of friends’ check-ins at different time lags, representing another dimension of psychological distance [65]. This can provide additional insights into varying perceptions of the importance of healthy and hedonistic behaviors.
