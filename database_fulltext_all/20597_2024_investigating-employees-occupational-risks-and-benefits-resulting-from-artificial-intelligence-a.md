## A B S T R A C T

With rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI), more employees are benefiting from or being replaced by AI. Nevertheless, we know little about the extent to which AI affects employees' occupations positively. This study improves the methodologies for quantifying employees' occupational AI benefits and risks. We propose three mechanisms by which AI may benefit employees' careers: productivity-enhanced AI jobs, intelligence-augmented AI jobs, and AI-enabling jobs. We also conduct employee-level analyses regarding how employees' skills, educational backgrounds, and demographics may correlate with occupational risk and AI benefits. Our results suggest that these key factors have distinct effects on different AI benefits.

## 1. Introduction

The last two decades have witnessed rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI). Therefore, there has been a keen interest in understanding the impact of AI on all aspects of our society and lives, especially the labor market $[1-4]$ . The term “AI” in this study refers to computer software that relies on highly sophisticated algorithmic techniques, such as machine learning (ML), to identify patterns in data and make predictions $[5]$ . Under this definition, AI technologies can enable computers or computer-controlled robots and machines to perform tasks by imitating intelligent human behavior. Nowadays, AI appears to shift the frontier between job tasks performed by humans and machines $[6]$ . On one hand, the displacement effect of AI on low-skilled jobs may decrease labor demand and wages $[7-9]$ . Specifically, occupations mainly consisting of codifiable, routine tasks have been disproportionately replaced by computerization and advanced algorithms $[10-12]$ . On the other hand, AI has become a countervailing force that increases labor demand by creating new job roles and enhancing the productivity of existing positions $[13,14]$ . For example, AI-based tools can perform repetitive administrative tasks, allowing chief executives or managers to focus on value-added tasks, thereby enhancing their productivity $[15]$ .

Despite great interest in the literature about AI's impact on occupations, we are still far from a comprehensive understanding of the effect of AI on occupations. The existing literature has predominantly focused on quantifying occupational automation scores or AI exposure scores, with little theoretical discussion or empirical evidence on the potential benefits AI may bring to employees $[6,10,16]$ . This may be due to the lack of well-accepted methods and the limited data available to distinguish employees' occupational AI benefits from AI displacement effects $[6]$ . Overlooking the benefits of AI in jobs may exaggerate AI displacement risks. To fill these gaps, this study proposes a new methodology for quantifying occupational AI benefits and AI displacement risks. Specifically, we seek to address the following research questions: Which occupations are more likely to benefit from or be displaced by AI? Which employee characteristics (e.g., skills, educational majors, and degrees) correlate with employees' occupational AI benefits and AI automation risks?

To answer these questions, we build on Frey and Osborne $[10]$ to develop a more nuanced understanding of how AI impacts a comprehensive list of occupations positively and negatively. Motivated by gaps in the extant literature, which solely examines the displacement or exposure effects of AI, we not only theorize three distinct mechanisms under which AI may benefit a job but also construct new measures to quantify these benefits for each occupation. The first mechanism, productivity-enhanced AI jobs, enhances employees' productivity through the automation of supplemental work activities (WAs) by AI. AI may automate some but not all WAs in almost all occupations $[17]$ .

Accordingly, we assume that if the core value-added WAs of an occupation cannot be automated by AI, whereas the supplemental WAs can be automated by AI, employees in these occupations will become more productive. For example, AI applications in the healthcare industry can efficiently retrieve and summarize clinical information from documentation $[18]$ , thus relieving doctors and nurses of tedious paperwork and increasing patient care productivity $[19]$ . The second mechanism, intelligence-augmented AI jobs, refers to jobs that benefit from enhanced decision-making through the integration of AI into the workplace. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to propose a method for quantifying the intelligence augmentation (IA) score for each job. The emerging adoption of AI can refine human WAs by assisting humans in decision-making, information discovery, and complex problem-solving activities when humans collaborate with AI to execute tasks $[14,15,20]$ . This concept has been documented as “Intelligence Augmentation”. For example, AI tools could support medical experts in developing new drug candidates by facilitating the exploration and identification of hidden patterns in vast datasets and automating predictions of drug compounds $[21,22]$ . The last mechanism, AI-enabling jobs, refers to jobs involved in the R&D, installation, operation, and sales of AI products or services. $^{1}$ Typical occupations in this category include software development, system integration engineering, and marketing specializing in AI products.

Using Occupational Information Network (O\*NET) data, we propose a novel and granular quantitative method for evaluating AI benefits and AI automation risks. O\*NET provides detailed information on the work activities of each occupation as well as the requisite abilities, skills, and knowledge—collectively termed “skills” in this study. Our estimate of the AI automation score derives from the annotation of these fine-grained skills rather than occupations or detailed work activities as documented in the literature. This decision stems from the premise that both AI and human beings need to meet the minimum skill requirements to perform each WA. Coding at the skill level facilitates a more nuanced understanding of whether AI can automate a WA. Given this skill coding, we introduce a novel framework that first constructs distinct AI automation scores for core and supplemental WAs and then integrates these scores to quantify the productivity-enhanced AI score and the intelligence-augmented AI score for each occupation. This hierarchical solution is grounded in our conceptual framework and in previous findings $[9,15,19,24,25]$ , representing an important novel aspect of this study. Finally, we merge massive online public employee resumes with the O\*NET dataset to further examine the critical employee characteristics that render them susceptible to AI displacement or AI benefit. Drawing upon human capital theory, we investigate several employee characteristics, including educational background and skill sets (e.g., social skills or IT skills) accumulated from prior work experience.

This study contributes to information systems (IS) research by conducting a thorough analysis of both the positive and negative effects of AI on the labor market, using massive online resume data. IS scholars have strongly advocated improving understanding of the human-algorithm connection and the management of AI in recent years $[26–28]$ . Unlike prior studies focusing on measuring occupational AI exposure scores $[10,16,29]$ , our study examines the positive effect of AI on the labor market and provides employee-level empirical evidence on the relationship between employees' human capital characteristics and different mechanisms of occupational AI benefit. In addition, our study proposes an alternative and unique method that estimates AI automation scores at the core and supplemental WA levels, thus adding to the theory of AI job replacement and the existing research on constructing occupational AI exposure measures $[6,10,16,17]$ . Our resume data also offers new insights into understanding and forecasting the future of work in the AI era. Accordingly, this study enriches the broader economics literature on the impact of skill-biased technological change on the labor market $[7,11,12,30–32]$ . It also has important practical implications for policymakers and employees in the labor market.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Occupational impacts of AI

Researchers have dedicated substantial attention to the impact of AI on human decision-making, labor markets, firm-level productivity, organizational creativity and firm performance, as well as overall economic growth $[3,16,21,26,33–36]$ . For example, Fügener, Grahl, Gupta, and Ketter $[37]$ indicate that human–AI collaboration can improve performance in classification tasks, but only when AI delegates work to humans rather than vice versa.

Our research is more closely aligned with the extensive literature on the occupational impact of technological change and automation. Early studies in this domain predominantly focused on technologies such as computers, automation equipment, and information technologies. The work of Autor, Levy, and Murnane $[11]$ is one of the pioneering studies that formalize a task-based theory on how the adoption of computer technology changes the tasks performed by employees and shapes the demand for human skills. They reveal that computer technology replaces laborers engaged in routine tasks amenable to programmed rules, while complementing laborers in performing nonroutine tasks that require a higher level of flexibility, creativity, problem-solving capabilities, and complex communications $[11]$ . In recent years, rapid advances in AI have impacted numerous occupations and industries, prompting a surge in investigating the potential influence of AI on the labor market both theoretically and empirically. For example, Acemoglu and Restrepo conducted a series of studies that develop an economic model based on a productivity analysis approach to examine how machines replace human labor and the consequent effects on employment and wage stagnation $[7,13,38]$ . Apart from the displacement effect, they underscore four countervailing forces of AI benefits: productivity effect, capital accumulation, improvements in existing automation technologies, and the creation of new tasks $[7,13,38]$ . Huang and Rust $[17]$ develop a conceptual framework to analyze the job replacement effects of AI, particularly in the service sector. They categorize service job requirements into four types of intelligence: mechanical, analytical, intuitive, and empathetic. They propose that AI will gradually replace these four types of intelligence in the stated order, with mechanical intelligence tasks being replaced first and tasks requiring empathetic intelligence being replaced last. This sequential order implies the growing importance of soft skills, such as intuitive and empathetic intelligence, for the future workforce. Additionally, theoretical research has aimed to identify which types of tasks are more likely to be displaced by AI. Brynjolfsson and Mitchell $[2]$ establish eight criteria for classifying tasks into higher or lower AI displacement risks. Tasks with higher displacement risks typically do not require specialized dexterity, physical skills, mobility, or long chains of reasoning or explanations. Conversely, tasks that necessitate flexibility, judgment, creativity, social interaction, and a tacit understanding of common sense are the most difficult to automate $[32,39]$ . With the emergence of powerful generative AI, Noy and Zhang $[40]$ examine the effects of ChatGPT on the productivity of college-educated professionals engaged in mid-level professional writing tasks. Their experimental results indicate that ChatGPT significantly enhances the quality of outputs produced by low-ability workers and reduces the time required for all workers.

## 2.2. Occupational AI exposure measures

To empirically quantify the effect of AI on occupations, researchers have focused on constructing computerization or AI exposure scores for occupations based on O\*NET data. O\*NET provides a hierarchical structure that decomposes each occupation into three levels: generalized work activities (WAs), detailed work activities (DWAs), and tasks required for all major occupations. Generalized WAs are shared by all occupations, while DWAs and tasks are more specific to each occupation. A more detailed discussion of the data structure is provided in Section 3.

Frey and Osborne [10] manually code 70 O\*NET occupations using a binary variable to indicate whether a job is automatable or not. Using these annotated data, they apply a supervised learning method (i.e., a classification algorithm) to estimate the automation probability for 702 O\*NET occupations using nine O\*NET skill variables as predictors, including finger dexterity, originality, fine arts, negotiation, and persuasion. They conclude that 47 % of U.S. jobs are at a high risk of automation, including cashiers, clerks, and taxi and bus drivers [10]. A limitation of their study is that their occupation-level analysis does not consider the fact that computerization may replace some but not all tasks of each occupation. Along this line of research, Brynjolfsson, Mitchell, and Rock [16] refine the measure of suitability for ML for each occupation and conclude that few jobs can be fully automated with ML. First, the AI exposure scores for 2069 DWAs of 964 O\*NET occupations are rated for their suitability for ML by human coders from a crowd-sourcing platform. The DWA-level AI exposure scores are then aggregated back to the occupational level. Unlike Frey and Osborne [10], Brynjolfsson, Mitchell, and Rock [16] construct the exposure score at the DWA level rather than the occupational level, shifting the focus from the full automation of occupations to job redesign and business process reengineering. In addition, Felten, Manav, and Robert [6] create an AI occupational exposure score by coding 52 O\*NET occupational abilities and then aggregating ability-level scores to the occupational level. However, this AI exposure score fails to differentiate AI displacement effects from AI benefits [6]. $^{2}$ In addition to O\*NET data, Webb [41] constructs an occupational AI exposure score by computing the overlap between the text of job task descriptions derived from O\*NET and the text of patent descriptions obtained from Google Patents data. The results indicate that high-skill occupations are the most exposed to AI, with notable examples including clinical laboratory technicians and chemical engineers, both of whom are involved in tasks related to prediction, optimization, and pattern detection [41]. However, that study does not account for the potential labor-augmenting impacts of AI beyond task-level substitution. Another closely related study by Ace-moglu, Autor, Hazell, and Restrepo [42] examines the impact of AI on labor markets using establishment-level data on online job vacancies in the U.S. and incorporating three different AI exposure scores developed in prior studies [6,16,41]. Their findings indicate an increase in AI-related vacancies in establishments where workers engage in tasks compatible with AI's current capabilities (i.e., higher AI exposure scores). The findings also show that AI-exposed establishments reduce their non-AI and overall hiring practices [42]. However, they do not find a significant impact of AI exposure on overall employment or wages at the occupation or industry level.

## 2.3. Human capital and employee skills

Human capital refers to the knowledge, skills, and abilities embodied in individuals $[43]$ . The resource-based theory of competitive advantage highlights the pivotal role of human capital, particularly in terms of employees' education and training, in enhancing firm performance $[44–46]$ . In addition, human capital theory is fundamental to understanding job mobility and employee wages $[47,48]$ . Autor and Handel $[49]$ develop a conceptual framework demonstrating that within-occupation variation in job tasks is systematically associated with employees' human capital and demographic characteristics. Moreover, employee-level variation in job tasks is a significant predictor of wage differences $[49]$ .

Another body of work studies the impact of technological change on employment and job tasks $[12,30]$ . Technological change alters the demand for specific skills, resulting in the transformation of occupational skill requirements. This phenomenon, known as skill-biased technological change (SBTC), indicates that technological change tends to replace labor in routine tasks while increasing demand for more educated employees $[12]$ . As a result, SBTC reduces the demand for middle-skilled occupations compared with high-skilled and low-skilled occupations $[11,12,30]$ . In the context of AI, employees with higher human capital are less likely to be automated since their skills add economic value to firms through enhanced productivity $[50]$ . Moreover, human capital facilitates adaptation to new technological landscapes and augments decision-making capabilities, as task-specific human capital serves as a proxy for skill transferability $[51]$ , aiding in the execution of new job tasks created by technological shifts. Next, we delve into several key human capital characteristics that determine the extent to which AI impacts employees' job roles.

This study focuses on four common types of skills—social skills, analytical skills, artistic skills, and IT skills—where current AI still exhibits a relatively low skill level and fails to surpass humans in WAs that demand those skills. First, there is growing evidence that the labor market increasingly rewards social skills $[32]$ . Although commercial AI applications can automate routine WAs requiring basic communication skills, AI is still far from performing WAs that require higher social skills, including building relationships, showing empathy, detecting implicit emotion from communications, or exhibiting leadership $[17]$ . Social skills are also vital in most organizational teamwork settings to reduce coordination costs and increase team productivity $[32]$ . Occupations in marketing or service sectors, which typically involve routine paperwork, often demand high social skills. As a result, occupations requiring high social skills are less likely to be displaced; instead, they can benefit from the automation of supplemental routine WAs $[32,52,53]$ . Second, analytical skills are essential for performing complex data-intensive and information-intensive WAs $[17]$ . These “analytics-hungry” WAs cannot be automated fully by the current wave of AI innovations $[54]$ . Big data systems still rely on human input for data preprocessing, model building, and result interpretation, all of which require deep domain knowledge $[17]$ . At the same time, employees with strong analytical skills are more likely to work in R&D, data analytics, and decision-making. For these employees, AI can improve the output quality of their jobs via intelligence augmentation $[55]$ . Third, artistic skills play an important role in many jobs requiring creativity and complex cognitive thinking processes. While advanced AI can create basic artwork such as logos and original art pieces by mimicking masterpieces $[56]$ or composing simple music $[57]$ , it has yet to reach the level of sophistication required to fully automate the complex thinking processes of almost all artists or designers. In other words, the artistic value of AI-created art is still far from that of human-created artwork $[58]$ . Nevertheless, current AI can augment the productivity of artists. For example, Elgammal and Mazzone $[59]$ demonstrate that artists using AI-based systems can generate new artwork inspired by AI-driven aesthetics more efficiently and refine their artistic styles through AI interactions. Fourth, with rapid technological change, IT skills are crucial in the labor market due to their potential to enhance productivity across various industries $[53]$ . IT skills are fundamental to software development, IT-related services, and almost all R&D jobs $[54]$ . While state-of-the-art AI cannot replace these roles, it can assist knowledge workers by reducing the time spent on routine tasks such as information searching, documentation, and reporting. More importantly, the development of data mining and business analytics affects many occupations that involve judgment and decision-making $[15,19]$ . IT skills are an important part of business analytics skills and are expected to complement complex human judgment in fields ranging from project management to medical diagnosis $[53]$ . Meanwhile, employees with IT skills are more likely to work in job roles directly related to new AI products, including developing, marketing, or servicing AI products, as IT skills are essential for these job roles.

## 2.4. Conceptual framework

While the existing literature offers valuable insights into how new technologies such as computerization, ML, or AI affect various occupations, several research gaps still exist. First, most existing studies develop measures of occupational exposure to new technologies by focusing on specific applications of computerization or AI rather than considering AI broadly $[6,10,16]$ . While a few studies, such as that of Felten, Manav, and Robert $[6]$ , have taken a broad view of AI technologies to develop AI-related measures, they are still agnostic about whether AI will augment or replace employees. In contrast, this study develops measures for both occupational AI displacement risk scores and three types of AI benefit scores, thus distinguishing between AI displacement risks and AI benefits. Second, prior studies have constructed automation or AI scores at relatively high levels, such as occupational or DWA levels. We argue that AI cannot fully automate most jobs and can only partially automate certain jobs or WAs, sometimes at a lower quality. Another issue that has received little attention in the literature is the quality of the outcomes of DWAs. Therefore, we propose a framework that annotates the fine-grained skills required by each WA. These granular skills can help to elucidate the micro-scale impact of AI on jobs $[1]$ . Third, prior empirical research has been conducted at the occupational level, with limited empirical evidence linking occupational displacement risk or AI benefit scores with employee-level human capital characteristics. Drawing upon human capital theory, this study aims to address this gap by exploring the relationship between these risk and AI benefit scores and various employee-level factors, such as employee skills and educational background. Table 1 summarizes prior empirical work on quantifying AI scores for occupations, comparing previous research with our study.

Based on the existing literature, we theorize the AI automation risk and AI benefits of each occupation in the following conceptual framework. First, each occupation consists of a set of WAs defined in O\*NET. Some WAs are more important than others due to their varying level of value creation. An occupation will be fully replaced by AI in practice only when AI can replace sufficient core WAs. At the same time, we define productivity-enhanced AI jobs as jobs with enhanced productivity through the automation of supplemental WAs by AI. Next, we assume that both human beings and AI require a set of skills, knowledge, and capabilities at a minimum quality level to perform each WA. This study relies on O\*NET's categorization and definitions for the full list of skills, knowledge, and capabilities, collectively referred to as "skills" in this study. This comprehensive list of skills is associated with each WA, AI, and human employee. The interpretation of the WA-skill pair indicates that different WAs require different levels of sophistication in skills, knowledge, or capabilities. The AI-skill pairing (or human-skill pairing) suggests that each AI (or human labor) has skills with certain sophistication levels. Only those AI technologies or human beings that achieve the minimum required sophistication level across all requisite skills can effectively perform a given WA. When AI can perform a WA as well as a human, the WA is likely to be automated due to AI's ability to perform the WA faster, without rest, and on a larger scale than humans. Consequently, even if AI performs slightly worse than humans, companies may still deploy AI to replace human workers to save costs. As AI technologies evolve, AI algorithms can perform increasingly more WAs with outcomes comparable to or even surpassing those of humans. Current AI or machines already outperform human beings in memorizing and searching for information and making simple calculations $[14,19]$ . Therefore, AI algorithms have the potential to augment complex data-intensive and information-intensive WAs that require analytical skills $[17]$ . For example, AI can help platform managers screen out bad ideas $[60]$ , thereby augmenting decision-making processes when employees utilize AI at work $[14,15,60]$ . Consequently, we define intelligence-augmented AI jobs as those that benefit from enhanced decision-making through the integration of AI into the workplace. Additionally, with the proliferation of AI, an increasing number of firms are using AI or considering its adoption in their products and services $[23]$ . Employees may benefit from AI by collaborating directly with these AI suppliers or engaging with these AI products. Therefore, we identify AI-enabling jobs, which are defined as jobs related to the R&D installation, operation, and sale of AI products or services.

## 3. Data

In this paper, we employ two data sources: O\*NET and a large online professional social networking website. First, we gathered data from O\*NET version 23.1, released in 2018, which is a publicly available dataset collected from a survey administered by the U.S. Department of Labor. As the successor to the Dictionary of Occupational Titles, O\*NET is a more recent database and is updated regularly. O\*NET collects detailed occupation-level characteristics by surveying employees in the United States, including abilities, skills, and knowledge (collectively considered as “skills” in this study), for a comprehensive list of occupations. Therefore, an emerging stream of literature has employed O\*NET data to measure occupational work or task content $[6,10,14,16]$ . In our study, we use “occupation” and “job” interchangeably. O\*NET also provides a comprehensive hierarchical structure to decompose each occupation into different levels, including general WAs, DWAs, and specific tasks in occupations. In O\*NET, there are 1110 occupations that share 41 WAs, 2070 DWAs, and 19,387 specific tasks. Among these occupations, WAs are shared by occupations, whereas DWAs and tasks are more specific to each occupation.

We rely on the categorization and definition of O\*NET for the full list of skills, knowledge, and abilities. Precisely defining or distinguishing between these three terms is not the focus of this study. This paper loosely uses the term “skill” to refer to these constructs. This study constructs AI automation scores for WAs based on skill information for two main reasons. First, in contrast to more granular decompositions of occupations (e.g., DWAs and tasks), all 41 WAs are well defined and shared by 1110 occupations; therefore, due to the smaller number of WAs, we can manually evaluate the quality of coding and compare different occupations based on their WA ratings. Second, given that different occupations have varying skill requirements, skills are a valuable source for analyzing the mechanisms by which different occupations may benefit from AI. Specifically, O\*NET discloses two ratings of skills: the importance score (IM) and the level score (LV). The importance score ranges from 1 to 5, and the level score ranges from 0 to 7. The importance score indicates the degree of importance a particular characteristic has for an occupation, while the level score represents the sophistication of a skill required for an occupation. For example, consider the role of a bank teller, which involves a high degree of human communication, but for which the level of communication proficiency required is lower than that required for a CEO; in such a role, communication thus has a high importance score but a low level score. Prior studies have relied on either the level score or the importance score when constructing AI scores $[10,11,29]$ , whereas our study employs both importance and level scores to construct AI automation scores.

Using O\*NET data allows us to construct measures for AI automation scores and AI benefits scores for productivity-enhanced AI jobs and intelligence-augmented AI jobs. However, O\*NET data are an occupation-level dataset that cannot be utilized to investigate individual-level research questions. To explore how employee characteristics (e.g., skills, educational majors, and degrees) correlate with employees' occupational AI benefits and AI automation risks, we collected millions of resumes of employees working in the United States from a large online social networking website. $^{3}$ Many studies have utilized data from such online professional social networking websites $[61–64]$ . After processing the raw data following Ge, Huang, and Png $[62]$ , we obtained valuable information from the public resume of each employee. The list of employee-level variables includes gender and race information, employment history (such as employers, job titles, starting and ending years, industry, and total years of work experience), and educational background (such as degree, major, university, and start/end year of each degree), as well as the number of connections. Next, we matched the job titles from resumes with O\*NET job titles based on the job titles' cosine similarity metric calculated by Google Word2Vec [65]. $^{4}$ We only retained profiles where all job titles could be mapped to O\*NET, allowing us to construct variables based on the O\*NET properties of job titles from the entire work history. $^{5}$ The final cross-sectional dataset comprises 450,075 users.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Measures

## 4.1.1. Dependent variables (DVs)

This study differs from existing research by introducing three distinct sets of dependent variables for identifying jobs that may benefit from AI: (1) two cases of the AI automation scores of the current job title of each individual, (2) the extent to which AI can augment an occupation, and (3) whether an individual has an AI-enabling job title or not.

First, when the AI automation risk score is constructed, one novelty of this study is the differentiation of AI automation scores for occupations based on their core and supplemental WAs. The categorization of WAs is based on the categorization provided by O\*NET. We posit that AI may automate some but not all WAs of an occupation. If the core value-added WAs of an occupation can be automated by AI, we assume that this occupation is at high risk of AI automation. Consequently, we construct the AI automation scores at the WA level rather than the occupation level. Specifically, we use the core-WA-level AI automation score to measure the AI displacement risk of an occupation. Among the occupations with low AI displacement risk (low core-WA-level score), we define productivity-enhanced AI jobs as occupations with only high AI automation scores of supplemental WAs.

The procedure for coding AI automation scores is as follows. We first code AI automation scores for O\*NET detailed skill elements s (AIScore $_{s}$ ), including abilities, skills, interests, work styles, and work context of the occupation, on a 5-point scale from “unlikely to be replaced by AI” to “very likely to be replaced by AI given the current technologies.” $^{6}$ Our coding process adheres closely to guidelines in the literature [2,10,11,14,31]. For example, we code “originality,” “social perceptiveness,” “persuasion,” “negotiation,” and “management of personnel resources” as skills and abilities that are unlikely to be replaced by AI. These items are highly relevant to creative intelligence and social skills, which remain difficult to automate with current AI technologies [10,32]. In contrast, we code “static strength,” “operation monitoring,” and “operation and control” as skills and abilities that are very likely to be replaced by AI, since these skills are essential to routine WAs that can be performed effectively by machines [10,11]. In addition, “artistic” and “social” within the interest category are coded as very unlikely to be automated by AI, while “conventional” interest is deemed highly susceptible to AI replacement. This is because artistic and social occupations often entail tasks involving designs, patterns, and interpersonal communication, while conventional occupations typically require following established procedures and routines for job task execution. Similarly, work style elements such as “leadership,” “cooperation,” “concern for other,” “social orientation,” and “innovation” are categorized as very unlikely to be replaced by AI $[2,10,17]$ . In terms of work context elements, “coordinate or lead others” is considered very unlikely to undergo AI replacement, whereas elements such as “spend time making repetitive motions,” “degree of automation,” and “importance of treating same tasks” are regarded as very likely to be replaced by AI $[6,10,32]$ .

Given the coding scores at the skill level $(AIScore_{s})$ , the next step is to aggregate the AI automation scores to the WA level based on the skill-level ratings for WAs. However, O\*NET does not provide skill-level weights for WAs; instead, weights are given only at the occupation level. Considering that all occupations share a common set of WAs and skill elements in O\*NET, we employ a neural network to learn the weight matrix $(w_{s,k}^{IM})$ between each detailed skill element s and WA k. Specifically, our neural network is designed without hidden layers and imposes the constraint of non-negative weights to learn the weight $w_{s,k}^{IM}$ . This structural design relies on the implicit assumption that each WA can be approximated by a linear combination of skill elements required to perform this WA. Therefore, the inputs of this neural network are the importance scores of all skills at the occupation level, and the outputs are the importance scores of the 41 WAs for each occupation. The output weight matrix is then normalized so that the total weight of skills for each WA is 1. Given the estimated skill weights at the WA level $(w_{s,k}^{IM})$ , our calculation of the WA-level AI automation score $(AIScore_{k})$ is given by

$$
A I S c o r e _ {k} = \sum_ {s} A I S c o r e _ {s} \times w _ {s, k} ^ {I M},\tag{1}
$$

where $AIScore_{s}$ is the manually labeled AI automation scores for detailed skillset s and $w_{s,k}^{IM}$ is the estimated importance weight matrix between skillset s and the specific WA k. After $AIScore_{k}$ is derived, the next step is to aggregate AI automation scores of core WAs ( $K^{core}$ ) to the occupational level. Specifically, O\*NET classifies tasks as core and supplemental tasks within an occupation and links them to WAs. Consequently, we can classify WAs as core or supplemental WAs based on the categorization of tasks.

As mentioned, O\*NET discloses two different ratings: the importance score and the level score of each job characteristic for each occupation $j$ . The importance score indicates the degree of importance a particular characteristic has to occupation $j$ , whereas the level score represents the sophistication of the knowledge level required to perform the occupation [10]. Since these two ratings capture different dimensions of WAs required for occupations, we aggregate these two ratings to construct the key dependent variable, AI automation scores at the core WA level ( $AIScore_j^{core}$ ), by

$$
A I S c o r e _ {j} ^ {\text {core}} = \frac {\sum_ {k \in K ^ {\text {core}}} \left(A I S c o r e _ {k} - w _ {k , j} ^ {L V}\right) \times w _ {k , j} ^ {I M}}{\sum_ {k \in K ^ {\text {core}}} w _ {k , j} ^ {I M}},\tag{2}
$$

where $w_{k,j}^{LV}$ and $w_{k,j}^{IM}$ are the level and importance weight matrices between core WA k and occupation j, respectively. $AIScore_{k}$ refers to AI automation scores for WA k. $^{7}$ The higher the $AIScore_{j}^{core}$ , the higher the probability of being replaced by AI for a specific occupation. In the robustness checks section, we also construct $AIScore_{j}^{core}$ using an alternative approach, following Felten, Manav, and Robert $[6]$ to aggregate the importance rating and level rating. This alternative construction yields robust results.

To measure productivity-enhanced AI jobs, we compute AI automation scores of occupations by aggregating the AI automation scores of supplemental WAs ( $AIScore_{j}^{noncore}$ ) using a similar equation (3),

$$
A I S c o r e _ {j} ^ {\text { noncore }} = \frac {\sum_ {k \in K ^ {\text { noncore }}} \left(A I S c o r e _ {k} - w _ {k , j} ^ {L V}\right) \times w _ {k , j} ^ {I M}}{\sum_ {k \in K ^ {\text { noncore }}} w _ {k , j} ^ {I M}},\tag{3}
$$

where $w_{k,j}^{LV}$ is the level weight matrix between supplemental WA k and occupation j. $w_{k,j}^{IM}$ represents the importance weight matrix between supplemental WA k and occupation j. $AIScore_{k}$ is the AI automation score for WA k. Using $AIScore_{j}^{core}$ and $AIScore_{j}^{noncore}$ , we can identify occupations that benefit from AI by automating only the supplemental WAs while not the core WAs. Specifically, among the occupations with low AI displacement risk (low $AIScore_{j}^{core}$ ), the higher the AI automation scores of supplemental WAs ( $AIScore_{j}^{noncore}$ ), the higher the probability that the occupation will benefit from AI through enhanced productivity. $^{8}$ Table 2 shows examples of occupations with AI automation risk and these occupations' required top three O\*NET WAs. Some typical productivity-enhanced AI jobs are listed in panel (a) of Table 3. Table 2 shows that jobs with high AI automation risk include farmworkers, mail machine operators, and sewing machine operators. In contrast, jobs that may benefit from AI in different ways include chief executives, surgeons, judges, teachers, and software developers (see Table 3). This is because the core WAs required by jobs with high AI automation risk include handling and moving objects or inspecting equipment, structures, or materials. These WAs will likely be replaced by machines or advanced AI algorithms [10,65]. By contrast, core WAs such as thinking creatively, making decisions, and solving problems are more likely to benefit from AI [14,19].

The second dependent variable of this study is the degree to which an occupation is intellectually augmented by AI. We construct an index of occupation-level IA intensity (IAIntensity $_{j}$ ) using the level score of specific skills disclosed by O\*NET. Following the guidance in extant studies $[9,15,19,24,25,66]$ , we define the IA intensity of an occupation as the average of nine O\*NET variables: judgment and decision-making, complex problem solving, critical thinking, analytical thinking, deductive reasoning, inductive reasoning, category flexibility, learning strategies, and near vision. $^{9}$ We select these skill variables for the following reasons. First, the AI automation of prediction tasks leads to better decision-making by reducing errors and improving efficiency $[14]$ . For example, using ML-based prediction models in the healthcare industry can reduce insufficient or excessive diagnostic tests for patients, making doctors' treatment decisions more accurate $[67,68]$ . Hence, we include the item “judgment and decision making.” Second, AI algorithms have enhanced the performance of searching for information, analyzing data, making decisions, and solving problems $[9,14,15,19,69]$ . Therefore, we include the item “complex problem solving.” Third, both critical thinking and analytical thinking require using logic to identify solutions to problems through information analysis. Likewise, deductive reasoning, inductive reasoning, and category flexibility require applying different rules for problem solving by analyzing information. Hence, we include these five O\*NET variables in the IA intensity measure since these items are closely related to information analysis and decision-making, which can be augmented by AI to various extents $[17,55]$ . Fourth, regarding the term “learning strategies,” AI algorithms have been deployed in massive online course platforms to provide personalized assignments and assessments based on students’ interaction on forums and their performance on prior assignments $[70]$ . In this way, teachers can tailor their teaching more effectively. Fifth, AI-based pattern recognition and computer vision in healthcare can see more detail in images than human eyes, which can augment the diagnosis of diseases for medical professionals $[19,24]$ . Consequently, we also include “near vision.” The examples of jobs with high IA intensity and their required WAs are listed in Table 3, panel (b).

For the third dependent variable, we construct AIJob to measure whether an employee has an AI-enabling job. ## 4.1.2. Independent variables

Our independent variables include four individual-level skills, educational major, and educational degree. First, based on the existing literature $[72–74]$ , we construct four variables of different skills s accumulated from prior work experience. Specifically, we focus on social skills (Social), analytical skills (Analytical), artistic skills (Artistic), and IT skills (IT). $^{11}$ Following Autor, Levy, and Murnane $[11]$ and Deming $[32]$ , we compute the skill intensity of each occupation ( $SkillIntensity_{s,j}$ ) utilizing the level score disclosed in O\*NET. First, the social skill intensity of an occupation is defined as the average of four items in O\*NET: coordination, negotiation, persuasion, and social perceptiveness [32]. Second, nonroutine analytical skill intensity is calculated as the average of three O\*NET variables that capture an occupation's mathematical requirements: mathematical reasoning ability, mathematics skill, and mathematics knowledge [11]. Third, artistic skill intensity is defined as knowledge of fine arts. Fourth, IT skill intensity is measured by programming skills. Subsequently, we normalize the values of occupation-level skill intensity with a range from 0 to 1 by min-max normalization.

After obtaining the skill intensity of each occupation, we construct the variables of skills accumulated through the work histories of each employee. Specifically, given $SkillIntensity_{s,j}$ , we calculate the individual i's skills ( $Skill_{s,i}$ ) accumulated from prior work experience by considering the weighted average of the skill intensity of each prior occupation j in the professional career path. The weight is the number of years working in that occupation j ( $Tenure_{i,j}$ ). The formula for measuring specific skill s (Social, Analytical, Artistic, and IT) of individual i is defined as follows:

$$
\text { Skill } _ {s, i} = \sum_ {j} \text { Tenure } _ {i, j} \times \text { SkillIntensity } _ {s, j}.\tag{4}
$$

In addition to these four skills, we construct dummy variables to measure employees' educational major (Major) and the highest educational degree of employees (Associate, Undergraduate, and Graduate). We consider the following nine major types: majors in schools of science (Science), engineering (Engineer), computing (Computing), business (Business), arts and design (Arts), social science (Social science), law (Law), education (Education), and medicine (Medicine). Majors in schools of computing include computer science and information systems. The other eight major types are defined by the departments of each school in a typical large university. Social science is used as the benchmark for different major dummy variables in regression analyses. Regarding the highest educational degree, we construct three dummy variables (Associate, Undergraduate, and Graduate). If the highest degree is the associate degree, then Associate equals 1; otherwise, it is 0. Similarly, if a professional's highest degree is a bachelor's degree, then Undergraduate is equal to 1. If a professional's highest degree is a master's or doctorate, we code Graduate as 1; otherwise, it is coded as 0.

## 4.1.3. Control variables

We control for three types of employee characteristics: demographics, educational quality, and prior work experience. All these variables are constructed based on public professional social networking website data. First, we include gender dummy (Female) and race dummy variables (Caucasian, African American, Asian, and Others). For race, the benchmark race is “others.” Second, we control for the university ranking of each employee by using QS\_100, which captures whether the ranking of a university is within the top 100 in the 2012 QS World University Rankings. Third, we include the logarithm of the number of connections of employees (Inconnections), the total years of senior leadership work experience (Leader experience), the total years of prior work experience (Work experience), the number of job changes per year (Turnover), and the “consistency” of job-hopping experience between different job categories (Erraticism). In calculating Leader experience, senior leadership is defined as job titles that contain keywords such as “founder,” “director,” “CEO,” or “CIO,” following Armstrong and Sambamurthy [75].

The variable Erraticism is adapted from Leung [76], who measures the consistency of job functions in an employee's work history. Leung [76] operationalized Erraticism as the average dissimilarity (distance) between two consecutive and different job titles: Erraticism = $\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N-1} Distance}{N-1}$ . Distance is calculated as one minus job similarity, and N represents the total number of chronologically ordered jobs. Utilizing data from Elance.com, the job similarity between job i and job j in Leung [76] is operationalized as the number of times these two job categories occur in all employees' past histories, summed and then divided by the total number of occurrences of job i on the website. Unlike Leung [76], we employ Word2Vec [65] to calculate the cosine similarity between two job titles. The lower the value of Erraticism, the more similar the job titles of two consecutive jobs along one career path are [76]. In addition, we control for firm size, which is measured by the total number of employees (Firm size). We also include the industry dummies (Industry) obtained from the large professional social networking website. Table 4 presents the summary statistics of the key variables.

## 4.2. Empirical model

To examine the effects of individual factors on AI automation risk and benefits, we estimate the model

$$
\begin{array}{r l} Y _ {i} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S o c i a l _ {i} + \beta_ {2} A n a l y t i c a l _ {i} + \beta_ {3} A r t i s t i c _ {i} + \beta_ {4} I T _ {i} + \beta_ {5} M a j o r _ {i} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {6} D e g r e e _ {i} + \beta_ {7} C o n t r o l s _ {i} + e _ {i}, \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where $Y_{i}$ represents AI automation scores ( $AIScore_{i}^{core}$ and $AIScore_{i}^{noncore}$ ), IA intensity ( $IAIntensity_{i}$ ), or the dummy variable that represents the AI-enabling job title of individual i ( $AIJob_{i}$ ). The higher the AI automation scores for core WAs, the higher the probability of being replaced by AI. The higher the index of occupation-level IA intensity ( $IAIntensity_{j}$ ), the higher the degree to which AI augments an occupation. $Social_{i}$ , $Analytical_{i}$ , $Artistic_{i}$ , and $IT_{i}$ capture employee i's accumulated skills from prior work experience. As for $Major_{i}$ , we use eight dummy variables to represent the nine major types described in Section 4.1.2. In terms of the highest degree ( $Degree_{i}$ ), we include Associate, Undergraduate, and Graduate. $\beta$ is the coefficient of interest, and $e_{i}$ is the error term. We use Stata 15 to estimate the pooled ordinary least squares (OLS) regression results. We explain our empirical results as correlations rather than causal relationships. To alleviate the potential endogeneity issue arising from omitted variable bias, we include a comprehensive list of individual-level control variables in our regression models.

## 5. Estimation results

## 5.1. Results on productivity-enhanced AI jobs

Model 1 in Table 5 reports the OLS results on productivity-enhanced AI jobs. We only include professionals whose AI automation score for core WAs falls within the bottom 25 %. $^{12}$ In other words, our sample in Model 1 is restricted to occupations that are less likely to be replaced by AI. Results suggest that employees with higher social, artistic, and IT skills accumulated from prior work experience are more likely to perform productivity-enhanced AI jobs because AI can help them complete less critical routine tasks. For example, by handling mundane and repetitive administrative tasks across the organization, AI can enhance the productivity of chief executives or IT project managers with high social and IT skills, allowing them to focus on impactful tasks $[15]$ . As another example, teachers with high social skills could be relieved of routine tasks, since AI algorithms could assist in answering students' routine questions, enabling teachers to focus on value-adding tasks such as conducting research and mentoring students $[19]$ . In total, these skill variables account for 0.173 out of 0.379 of the $R^{2}$ value, implying that the statistical significance is also economically significant. The results are consistent with prior studies indicating that soft skills and digital skills play important roles in the AI era $[53–55]$ .

As for educational majors, Model 1 suggests that employees who majored in computing, law, and medicine are more likely to perform productivity-enhanced AI jobs than those who majored in social science. In particular, employees who majored in law are significantly associated with the AI automation score for supplemental WAs, showing a $5.93\%$ increase in this association compared to major in social science. This could be explained by the successful application of AI in routine tasks such as preprocessing legal documents in the law industry [10,69]. Meanwhile, employees such as nurses with medical degrees may benefit from higher work productivity since AI algorithms can support their routine and administrative tasks, such as prescribing medications, scheduling bed assignments, and conducting clinical documentation [77]. The major types also account for roughly one-third of $R^2$ (incremental $R^2 = 0.128$ ). We also find that only professionals whose highest degrees are postgraduate degrees are more likely to work in productivity-enhanced AI jobs. This finding is consistent with Frank et al. [1], who argue that AI is much more likely to influence highly educated employees than previous waves of technological innovations. In addition, we find that Caucasians and Asians are more likely to hold productivity-enhanced AI jobs than employees of other races. Meanwhile, when employees move erratically between very different job titles, they are less likely to perform productivity-enhanced AI jobs.

## 5.2. Results on intelligence-augmented AI jobs

Model 2 in Table 5 reports the OLS results for intelligence-augmented AI jobs. The results suggest that social skills, analytical skills, and IT skills are associated with intelligence-augmented AI jobs at a significance level of 0.01. This is consistent with findings in the current literature that AI-based systems can augment the decision-making tasks of chief executives, analysts, and surgeons through information search, discovery activities, and data-driven simulations $[14,15,55]$ . For example, actuaries with a high level of nonroutine analytical skills can use AI algorithms to make faster and more reliable decisions. $^{13}$ As for IT skills, we conjecture that professionals with higher IT skills have a higher probability of working well with AI, since they are more likely to have domain knowledge in IT and AI. Therefore, employees with higher IT skills accumulated from prior work experience are more likely to have intelligence-augmented AI jobs.

As for majors, results in Model 2 show that majors in computing, science, engineering, law, and medicine are positively correlated with performing intelligence-augmented AI jobs at a significance level of 0.01. Employees' major types account for a small but significant proportion of $R^2$ in augmented-intelligence AI jobs (incremental $R^2 = 0.026$ ). In particular, employees with a degree in law or medicine have a $10\%$ increase in their occupational IA intensity score ranking. One possible explanation is that these majors provide more training in professional decision-making and complex problem solving. Also, graduates from these two majors may be less likely to pursue unrelated careers. Meanwhile, AI advances are expected to improve data analytics and decision-making tasks that humans perform in these industries [4,8,10, 14,15,24,41]. For example, a judge could issue the final verdict for defendants with less human bias based on sentencing recommendations from AI-based criminal justice algorithms [69].

In addition, a highest degree at the undergraduate or postgraduate level is positively correlated with performing an intelligence-augmented AI job. Another interesting finding is that employees' educational degree plays a more critical role in intelligence-augmented AI jobs than their major, indicating that AI benefit resulting from IA is more influential for well-educated employees across majors. Moreover, we can observe a positive and significant correlation between employees' senior leadership experience and the IA intensity of their occupations. In contrast, the quality of the university and the race of employees play less important roles in intelligence-augmented AI jobs. Consistent with Model 1, individuals who move erratically between very different job titles have a lower probability of performing an intelligence-augmented AI job.

## 5.3. Results on AI-enabling jobs

In Table 5, Model 3 reports the OLS results of AI-enabling jobs. Specifically, the results demonstrate that IT skills, with the highest coefficient among the four skills, are positively associated with the probability of having an AI-enabling job. This is intuitive, because employees with IT skills are more likely to acquire new and advanced AI knowledge and have AI-enabling jobs. Regarding educational background, we find that employees graduating from computing and engineering schools are more likely to have AI-enabling jobs. These results are consistent with the typical AI-enabling occupations reported in Table 3, panel (c), including software developers, robotics engineers, computer and information research scientists, and information technology project managers. In addition, our results suggest that having an undergraduate or postgraduate degree as one's highest degree is positively correlated with the probability of working for an AI-enabling job, consistent with our findings for intelligence-augmented jobs. This observation provides additional evidence that AI is likely to benefit employees with higher educational degrees.

The results of Model 3 also indicate that female workers are less likely to hold AI-enabling jobs. This is consistent with the fact that women only held 22 % of jobs in AI areas, according to the World Economic Forum. $^{14}$ A possible explanation is that females usually self-report their technical skills on their resumes at a lower rate than males, and technology companies express less interest in a female candidate than in a male candidate with comparable qualifications $[78]$ . In terms of race, we can see from Model 3 that Asians are more likely to hold an AI-enabling job than employees of other races. Moreover, the results indicate that employees who graduated from a higher-ranking university are more likely to perform an AI-enabling job. These findings provide additional evidence that AI is expected to benefit employees with better educational backgrounds. Furthermore, employees who move erratically between very different job titles are less likely to have AI-enabling jobs. Taken together with Models 1 and 2, our findings suggest that employees with highly erratic job histories are less likely to benefit from AI in all three mechanisms. A possible explanation could be that employees with highly erratic job histories may be less likely to accumulate essential skills required by jobs that benefit from AI. Moreover, employers prefer hiring employees who move between similar jobs over those who move erratically $[76]$ .

## 5.4. Results on the AI automation risk of core WAs

This section discusses the OLS results on the individual-level risk of AI automation. As shown in Model 4 in Table 5, individuals with high social, analytical, artistic, and IT skills accumulated from prior work experience are less likely to be replaced by AI. These important skills account for significant amounts of incremental variance in jobs at risk of AI automation (incremental $R^{2} = 0.053$ ). The findings corroborate the literature arguing that soft skills, digital skills, and complex cognitive skills are increasingly important in the labor market due to rapid technological change [32,54,55]. In terms of educational background, we can observe that majors in computing, science, law, and medicine are correlated with a lower AI automation risk than those who majored in social sciences. For example, employees who majored in medicine show a 0.6 % decrease in the AI automation scores of occupations compared with employees who majored in social science. In contrast, holding a graduate degree is likely to reduce the roughly 10 % AI automation risk by 3.03 %. The magnitudes of these coefficients indicate that the effects of educational degrees are economically more significant than the effects of majors. This is interesting because educational majors are not as important to AI automation risk as expected. In contrast, educational degrees may play a more important role in reducing AI automation risk than educational majors. A possible explanation is that a higher educational degree is a key signaling factor for human capital in the labor market [79], leading to more job opportunities requiring more advanced skills that cannot be replaced by AI in the short run.

As for control variables, the results of Model 4 suggest that females have a higher AI automation risk than males. This is consistent with the literature arguing that females are more likely to face higher automation risk $[73,80]$ . At the same time, we find that Caucasians and Asians have lower AI automation risk than employees of other races, which is consistent with the findings of Muro, Maxim, and Whiton $[81]$ . Moreover, professionals graduating from a university that ranks within the top 100 in QS Rankings face a lower AI automation risk. This variable could be a proxy variable for employees' IQ, diligence, or family background. From Model 4, we also observe that social connections and senior leadership experiences negatively correlate with AI automation risk. Employees with longer work experience and higher turnover rates are positively associated with AI automation risk. When individuals move erratically between very different job positions, they have a higher probability of being replaced by AI. One possible explanation is that employers are less likely to hire workers with highly erratic job histories $[76]$ .

## 5.5. Summary of empirical results

To understand the most important factors that correlate with the three mechanisms of AI benefits and AI automation risk, we calculate the standardized regression coefficients by multiplying the original regression coefficient by the standard deviations of the explanatory variables and then dividing by the standard deviation of the dependent variable. In this way, we can compare the magnitudes of the coefficients of our key variables and identify the most important factors that correlate with AI benefits and automation risk. Table 6 reports the most important factors that correlate with different AI benefits and AI automation risks based on the standardized coefficients. For example, results suggest that the most important individual characteristics in alleviating AI automation risk are skills accumulated from prior work experience, educational degree (undergraduate or graduate), senior leadership experience, and total years of work experience. Therefore, individuals with a high educational degree and accumulated skills along their career path may have fewer concerns about being replaced by AI.

## 6. Robustness checks

## 6.1. Alternative measures of DVs: AI automation scores and IA scores

In the first robustness check, we construct AI automation scores using a different procedure. In our main analysis, the AI automation scores of each occupation are calculated based on WAs that are classified as “core WA” or “supplemental WA,” which are inferred from task categorization in O\*NET. In the robustness check, we define the core WAs as the three WAs with the highest importance scores and the supplemental WAs as the three WAs with the lowest importance scores. WA importance scores for occupations can be obtained from O\*NET. Since the importance score from O\*NET indicates the degree of importance a particular characteristic has for a specific occupation $[10]$ , the most important WAs identified can proxy for the core WAs of an occupation. With this new approach of identifying core and supplemental WAs, we construct $AIScore^{core}$ and $AIScore^{noncore}$ following Eqs. (2) and (3). The results of the new AI automation scores for supplemental and core WAs are shown in Model 1 and Model 3 of Table 7, respectively. Likewise, Model 1 reports the results conditional on low AI automation scores of the core WAs. We find that most results in Table 7 are qualitatively similar to the main results in Table 5. For example, the results in Model 3 suggest that individuals with high social, analytical, artistic, and IT skills accumulated from prior work experience are less likely to be replaced by AI.

We conduct another robustness check by reconstructing our $AIS-core^{core}$ based on the method presented in Felten, Manav, and Robert [6] using the following equation:

$$
A I S c o r e _ {j} ^ {\text { core }} = \frac {\sum_ {k \in K ^ {\text { core }}} A I S c o r e _ {k} \times w _ {k , j} ^ {I M} \times w _ {k , j} ^ {L V}}{\sum_ {k \in K ^ {\text { core }}} w _ {k , j} ^ {I M} \times w _ {k , j} ^ {L V}}.\tag{6}
$$

In contrast to our original calculation of $AIScore^{core}$ using Eq. (2), we multiply both $w_{k,j}^{IM}$ and $w_{k,j}^{LV}$ in Eq. (6) rather than subtracting $w_{k,j}^{LV}$ from $AIScore_{k}$ . The approach of Felten, Manav, and Robert [6] provides a complementary method for aggregating the importance score and level score of each WA required for occupations when constructing our key dependent variable. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the original $AIScore^{core}$ in Eq. (2) and the new $AIScore^{core}$ obtained from Eq. (6) is 0.7, suggesting that our method of calculating this AI score is robust even when applying minor changes in the formula.

In addition, we validate our measure of $AIScore^{core}$ by comparing our empirical results with the results using the occupation-level suitability for machine learning (SML) score, as developed by Brynjolfsson, Mitchell, and Rock [16]. In particular, a high SML score value suggests that ML might have the greater potential to transform a job. Details on SML measures can be found in Brynjolfsson, Mitchell, and Rock [16]. The results employing the SML score as a DV are presented in Model 4 of Table 7. Results in Model 4 indicate that employees with higher social, analytical, artistic, and IT skills accumulated from prior work experience are less likely to be affected by ML. We find that most results in Model 4 are consistent with our main results in Table 5, implying the robustness of our key measures.

## 6.2. Alternative measures of independent variables: skills of employees

In the main analysis, conducted using Eq. (4), we multiply the skill intensity of each job by $Tenure_{i,j}$ . As a result, if an employee has a long work history, all skill variables may be greater than those with less work experience. Although experienced employees could arguably accumulate more skills from long years of work experience, we examine an alternative measure that is less varied in career length. For this purpose, we propose Eq. (7) to measure an individual's specific skill $s$ (Social, Analytical, Artistic, and IT),

$$
\text { Skill } _ {s, i} = \frac {\sum_ {j} \text { Tenure } _ {i , j} \times \text { SkillIntensity } _ {s , j}}{\text { Work   experience } _ {i}},\tag{7}
$$

where Work experience measures the total years of work experience of individual i. Table 8 reports the estimation results, which are qualitatively the same as those in Table 5. For example, results in Model 2 indicate that employees with a higher level of social, nonroutine analytical, and IT skills accumulated from prior work experience are more likely to work in intelligence-augmented AI jobs.

## 7. Discussion

Given the tremendous effect of AI on the labor market, it is imperative to investigate this important phenomenon systematically $[6]$ . This paper proposes a novel and sophisticated methodology to quantify the benefits of AI for employees' occupations under three different mechanisms. Utilizing data from O\*NET and a large professional social networking website, we identify employee-level factors that may correlate with occupational AI automation risks and benefits. Our empirical results show that jobs with the highest AI displacement risks include farmworkers, mail machine operators, and sewing machine operators. Conversely, jobs such as chief executives, surgeons, judges, teachers, and software developers are likely to benefit from AI. Furthermore, we find that four important skills accumulated from prior work experience, certain educational majors, and higher educational degrees can help employees benefit from AI advances. Interestingly, among these skills, analytical skills play a more important role in performing intelligence-augmented AI jobs, whereas professionals with IT skills are likely to benefit from AI through all three mechanisms. In addition, employees with educational backgrounds in certain majors (computing, science, engineering, law, and medicine) and high educational degrees have greater potential to benefit from AI through the different mechanisms.

## 7.1. Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the literature in several respects. First, with the emerging adoption of AI, we respond to the call of IS researchers to improve our understanding of the human–algorithm connection and the management of AI $[26–28]$ . Although there is a burgeoning body of IS literature focused on human decision-making with AI and human–robot interactions, including customer service chatbots or robot advisors in financial services $[4,26,82–86]$ , there has been a scarcity of research investigating the effects of AI on the future of work $[36]$ . Unlike prior studies that mainly focus on constructing occupational AI exposure scores $[10,16,29]$ , our study focuses on the positive effect of AI on the labor market. Specifically, we propose a conceptual framework and identify three mechanisms by which AI may bring benefits to occupations, thereby differentiating AI displacement risk scores from AI benefit scores. This distinction also closes the research gap noted by Felten, Manav, and Robert $[6]$ , who remained agnostic regarding whether the effect of AI on occupations manifests as displacement or complementarity.

Second, our study not only corroborates the theory of AI job replacement $[17]$ but also enriches the existing literature on constructing AI exposure measures for occupations $[6,10,16]$ . Unlike prior research that codes AI automation scores at the level of occupation or DWAs $[6,10,16]$ , we propose an alternative and unique method for estimating AI automation scores of core and supplemental WAs by coding the detailed skills required for each occupation.

Third, in response to the call for more measures and empirical insights to understand AI's impact on the labor market [1,6], our study is the first to provide systematic employee-level empirical evidence on the relationship between employees' human capital characteristics and occupational AI benefits by utilizing massive unstructured data from online resumes. In this way, our resume data can enable new and promising techniques for understanding and forecasting the future of work due to AI advances [1].

Fourth, as Acemoglu and Restrepo $[66]$ emphasize, this research stream is essential, since we have little information about the types of skills that new technologies may complement. Accordingly, this study proposes a novel perspective for understanding and forecasting the human capital required for employees in the AI era by examining the relationship between employees' human capital characteristics (e.g., different skills and educational backgrounds) and occupational AI benefits and AI automation scores, contributing to the broader economics literature on the impacts of skill-biased technological change on the labor market $[7,11,12,30–32]$ . This study can also enrich the literature on human capital by highlighting the role of skills (e.g., social skills, IT skills) and educational background in occupational AI benefits and automation risks.

## 7.2. Practical implications

This study has several important implications for policymakers. First, it is critical to understand the dual impact of AI on occupations to develop well-informed policies. Policymakers need better access to employee-level data and quantitative methods or indicators to estimate future workforce displacement by AI $[1]$ . Our study provides a new and comprehensive technique for constructing occupation-level AI scores (AI automation risk score and three AI benefit scores), which can be further used to measure AI automation risk and AI benefits at the firm, industry, or regional level. These measures can help policymakers identify and forecast the employees, occupations, companies, industries, and geographic areas most likely to be replaced by or benefit from AI advances. Furthermore, recent advances in ChatGPT and the periodic updates of O\*NET data enable future research to quantify the impact of ChatGPT on the labor market and improve our occupational AI benefit and risk measures. In this way, governments can better prepare to prescribe effective solutions for different companies, industries, and geographic areas if their societies face more considerable AI exposure.

Second, our results provide supporting evidence of the growing importance of soft skills and IT skills in the digital world $[52,54]$ and thus help policymakers understand the shifts in human capital investment. However, the rapid advances of technical change in various industries are shortening the half-lives of employees' skills acquired through traditional university education $[87]$ . Therefore, governments and universities could design (re)training programs to strengthen employees' existing skills or develop new skills to alleviate concerns over AI displacement risks and to benefit from AI advances. For example, with the emergence of AI-enabling jobs, governments can support long-term training programs to equip employees with digital or AI skills.

Third, prior studies have demonstrated that the mismatch between the supply and demand for skills due to rapid technological change is a major cause of long-term unemployment $[88]$ . By identifying skills and educational majors that may suffer or benefit from AI and, the present study helps policymakers reassess educational systems (such as curriculum design) and launch lifelong learning programs to mitigate shortages or surpluses in the labor market. Special attention can be given to employees with less human capital, since they are more likely to be displaced by AI.

Fourth, our results imply that employees with higher educational degrees are less likely to be displaced by AI automation and more likely to perform intelligence-augmented jobs. Therefore, it is reasonable to conjecture that even if the core WAs of highly educated employees can be automated, these individuals may be able to find new jobs in the labor market. To alleviate employees' concerns about being replaced by AI, policymakers should invest persistently in higher education to improve the average educational level of employees in the labor market.

Fifth, our empirical results suggest that female workers face a higher risk of AI automation and are less likely to perform intelligence-augmented AI jobs than male workers. Policymakers can be judicious in establishing policies to increase the representation of women within the workforce, especially in the IT, law, engineering, and healthcare industries. Policymakers could promote women's participation in obtaining science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) degrees through current initiatives to attract women to the STEM fields and reskill women in existing jobs to work well with AI tools. For example, governments can provide job-related training for women to help them pursue higher-paying STEM jobs [89].

Our study also has implications for employees and job candidates in the labor market. First, our empirical results indicate strong correlations between employees' skills and educational characteristics (majors, educational degrees, and the university's QS ranking) and occupational AI benefits or displacement risks. These findings can inform individuals making decisions about pursuing future education and improving their human capital in response to rapid advances in AI. By elevating their educational qualifications and cultivating sophisticated skills, individuals can prevent being displaced by AI advances. Consequently, younger generations can better equip themselves to navigate a competitive job market that will see potentially disruptive impacts from AI automation. In addition, since individuals with specific educational majors are more likely to have their jobs augmented by AI, they need to not only accumulate knowledge in their domain but also adapt and develop digital and AI skills that help them make the most of AI in decision-making. Specifically, employees are encouraged to acquire at least basic digital and IT skills to automate routine tasks or use IT or AI tools to enhance the quality of their (decision-making) WAs. Employees can actively participate in lifelong training programs to update their skills in the AI era, especially those with long work histories. Moreover, employees can build and maintain social connections and accumulate senior leadership experience to reduce AI automation risks and increase the probability of benefiting from AI.

## 7.3. Limitations and future research

Our study is subject to several limitations, which open avenues for further research. First, we are restricted to analyzing cross-sectional data. Our choice of cross-sectional data rather than panel data is mainly driven by the nature of our research questions, which do not focus on how AI may affect employees' career paths. In addition, O\*NET does not update annually, restricting our ability to conduct panel data analysis $[11]$ . The second limitation stems from our reliance on self-reported online resume data, which may inevitably introduce distortions $[62]$ . The public resume data from the professional social networking website predominantly represent well-educated professionals. However, this issue is not unique to our dataset, as similar challenges occur in datasets sourced from other professional social networks, job recruiting sites, and job-related surveys. Using a large sample collected from professional employment-oriented online platforms is among the few better options, as supported by an increasing number of studies conducted with similar data $[62,90,91]$ . Future studies can explore alternative data sources to validate our results.

Another concern is the generalizability of our findings. Our empirical results rely on U.S. O\*NET data and the online resume data of U.S. employees from a large online social networking website. Our employee-level sample includes more well-educated and relatively younger professionals $[62,92]$ , on whom the impact of AI may not be representative in relation to less-educated labor cohorts. Variations in labor characteristics across different countries may also affect our results. Therefore, we call for future research to examine the impact of AI on the labor market in other countries by utilizing different data samples. Fourth, our results can only be explained as correlations rather than causal relationships. Although we cannot identify causal effects, we include a comprehensive list of control variables to alleviate potential endogeneity issues. The causal impact of AI automation and AI benefits on employment or wages deserves further exploration.

We view this study as a first step in empirical investigations into the relationship between skills and AI benefits in the labor market. Much work needs to be done if we are to better understand the role of AI in the workforce and the demand for different skillsets. For example, recent advances in ChatGPT, a language model released by Open AI, have garnered significant attention from both practitioners and researchers investigating its impact on occupations. While our empirical findings offer insights, they may not fully represent the broader impact of ChatGPT on different occupations. We speculate that individuals with social skills, IT skills, and higher educational degrees are more likely to be affected by ChatGPT $[93,94]$ . A notable illustration is found among postsecondary teachers, who have greater exposure to advances in ChatGPT $[93]$ . However, we remain agnostic regarding whether ChatGPT functions as a complement or substitute in terms of occupations. Thus, future research can examine how ChatGPT positively and negatively affects our occupations, along with the requisite skills and educational background for AI-augmented jobs. Additionally, future research can explore how many workers can be redeployed (without re-training) after AI takes over their jobs. If a large proportion of workers displaced by AI have the four critical skills identified in our study, their transitions to new jobs could be relatively smooth, thus mitigating the extent of AI-induced job displacement reported in existing literature. Finally, future research can delve into AI automation and AI benefits at the firm level. Given the AI automation risk and the IA intensity of each occupation, we can derive the percentage of jobs that will be eliminated by AI and those that will be enhanced by AI. This could provide valuable insights into whether firms stand to gain or lose productivity as a result of AI advances.
