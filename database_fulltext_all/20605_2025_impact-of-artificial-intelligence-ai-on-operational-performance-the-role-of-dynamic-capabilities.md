# Impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on operational performance: The role of dynamic capabilities

David T.W. Wong <sup>a,\*</sup> , Eric W.T. Ngai <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Lee Shau Kee School of Business and Administration, Hong Kong Metropolitan University, Kowloon, Hong Kong, China

<sup>b</sup> Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hung Hom, Kowloon, Hong Kong, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI) AI adoption Dynamic capabilities (DCs) theory Survey research Technology–organization–environment (TOE) framework Operational performance Telecommunications industry

## A B S T R A C T

This study examines the role of dynamic capabilities (DCs) in the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) systems in China’s telecommunications industry and their impact on firms’ operational performance. Drawing on DCs theory and the technology–organization–environment (TOE) framework, this study investigates how TOE factors influence DCs for AI system adoption, which subsequently impacts operational performance. It also examines the mediating role of DCs in the relationship between TOE factors and operational performance. Despite numerous studies on AI system adoption. the underlving mechanism of DCs requires further investigation. Survey data were collected from 205 senior executives of telecommunications firms in China. Analysis of the data reveals that TOE factors positively influence DCs for AI system adoption, which in turn have a positive effect on operational performance. Additionally, competitive pressure positively moderates the relationship between technological factors (i.e., technology compatibility and expected benefits) and operational performance. These findings provide managerial and theoretical insights into AI system adoption.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, capital goods manufacturers have shifted from being product sellers to solution providers [1]. Moving away from the tradi tional concept of selling, manufacturing, or designing products, they offer valuable and customized solutions to the specific problems faced by their customers [2]. A solution encapsulates the service and product components that are necessary to provide a unified response to a cus tomer’s business and operational needs [3]. The shift from product provision to solution provision has become increasingly important for manufacturers to maintain their competitiveness [4]. At the same time, the landscape of business operations has been profoundly transformed by the continuous advancement of digital technologies, with artificial intelligence (AI) emerging as a foundational element [5]. AI refers to the capability of machines to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, and decision-making. This transformative technology is reshaping industries by enhancing opera tional efficiency, improving customer service, and driving innovation. The concept of AI was introduced in 1955 and refers to the ability of a machine to think and perform tasks in ways that simulate human behavior patterns [6]. Today, AI plays a critical role in enabling manufacturers to perform tasks ranging from simple to complex, leading to improvements in customer service, product quality, and business operations [7,8]. In general, there are three servitization requirements to achieve scalable customized solution delivery: stimulating cognitive engagement, analyzing data to provide cognitive insights, and auto mating business processes using AI collaboration and recommendation systems to enhance personalization [9]. AI algorithms provide external (to the customer) and internal (to the firm) benefits along a servitization path [10].

Because of its ability to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, and self-correction [11], AI differs from traditional analytics, which focuses primarily on analyzing and interpreting data, without the adaptive and autonomous capabilities inherent in AI systems. Therefore, in this study, AI systems are explicitly defined and differentiated from other technologies. This includes providing examples and characteristics of AI systems to ensure that re spondents have a clear understanding of what they constitute. AI sys tems provide proactive, data-driven services; generate insights; analyze, organize, store, and collect field data (on the Cloud); and connect equipment [8]. Therefore, to some extent, AI systems can enhance the ability of manufacturing firms to provide digital servitization solutions.

For example, Barbieri et al. [10] found that manufacturers using AI systems were better able to focus on delivering high-value solutions to their clients through advanced services beyond the sale of standardized products, such as customer support, preventive maintenance, remote monitoring, and condition monitoring. AI systems can potentially facilitate the digital servitization of manufacturing firms, as they can be used to automate routine tasks [12] such as validation, training, veri fication, selection of the most appropriate algorithms, ingestion, normalization, cleansing, and data collection [10,13]. In general, AI applications can contribute to the advancement of sophisticated services and data-driven models in the field of digital servitization, enabling manufacturers to achieve greater competitiveness and better economic results and increase their capabilities to deliver new solutions. For example, telecommunications firms can use the aggregated information provided by AI to inform their decisions and prioritize areas highlighted by knowledge visualization. These capabilities can help firms improve their agility and reduce their operating costs [14].

The telecommunications industry, characterized by rapid techno logical advancements and dynamic customer demands, provides a unique context in which to explore the role of AI in enhancing dynamic capabilities (DCs). These capabilities, such as sensing capability, seizing capability, analytics capability, and process-oriented dynamic capabil ities (PODC), are essential to harness the potential of AI in a competitive environment. Indeed, AI’s impact is particularly pronounced in the telecommunications industry, where it plays a crucial role in advancing network design, analysis, and deployment. AI systems, encompassing technologies such as deep learning, machine learning, and natural lan guage processing (NLP), enable telecom operators to uncover valuable insights, enhance customer satisfaction, streamline operations, and boost revenue. This strategic integration of AI marks a fundamental shift in the telecommunications landscape, fostering accelerated innovation and heightened efficiency. In the telecommunications industry, AI sys tems can improve the accuracy of knowledge sharing, the speed of in formation use and access, and maximize the co-exploration of outbound activities [15,16].

Furthermore, knowledge about market experts and consumers can be integrated into AI systems to enable suggestions for areas of improve ment, identify knowledge gaps, automatically categorize content, improve customer behavior analysis, provide quicker answers to frequently asked questions, and accelerate the identification of new in formation [17]. For instance, telecommunications firms can use customer mobility data combined with other sources, such as informa tion about transport disruptions or strikes, upcoming events with ex pected attendance gleaned from social media, and weather reports, to provide services to third parties backed by AI applications [14]. Furthermore, AI systems can help manufacturers overcome production scalability limitations and shift to a solution provider business model through the integration of complementary modular services that rely on intangible and mutable resources [18]. As such, servitization offers opportunities for manufacturer customization to facilitate the transition from a product provider business model to a solution provider business model [19]. In addition to the increased production capabilities and efficiencies of scale enabled by digitalization, the need for dynamic integration of offerings and the degree of customer embeddedness to produce customized service innovations augmented by solution-based advanced servitization demonstrates the value for manufacturers of more autonomous, advanced, and analytical deep learning-based capa bilities [2,20,21], especially in the telecommunications industry. For example, AI can help telecommunications firms monitor and control their product and service quality through customer sentiment aggrega tion and defect isolation, thereby helping them reduce their operating costs and liability and improve customer satisfaction [14].

This study is motivated by two primary factors. First, despite the transformative potential of AI for business operations, the application of AI to real-world use cases remains limited, particularly in the telecom munications industry. Many firms in this industry are in the early stages of considering and planning for AI adoption, highlighting the impor tance of studying the mechanisms driving AI system adoption and its impact on operational performance. This focus aligns with the increasing demand for insights into the AI adoption process and its outcomes in organizational contexts where technological investments are substantial. Second, although prior studies have demonstrated a positive impact of AI adoption on organizational performance [22–24], there is limited knowledge about how firms can realize these benefits in practice. Few studies have explored the role of the business environment in this process, particularly in the telecommunications industry. This gap reflects an ongoing debate in the information systems literature regarding the role of DCs in successful technology adoption. This study therefore investigates the mediating role of DCs in the relationship be tween technology–organization–environment (TOE) factors and opera tional performance. Additionally, it examines whether a competitive business environment moderates the influence of technological factors (i.e., technology compatibility and expected benefits) on DCs for AI adoption. For instance, firms operating in highly competitive environ ments may receive more government support (such as training oppor tunities and information programs) that can facilitate AI adoption. However, there has been little research on how interactions between external environment factors and technological considerations affect firms’ operational performance. Thus, this study aims to clarify these dynamics by assessing whether and how external environmental factors moderate the influence of technological factors on DCs in the context of AI system adoption.

Based on these gaps, this study addresses the following research questions:

(1) What TOE factors influence firms’ DCs (sensing capability, seizing capability, PODC, and analytics capability) for AI system adoption?

(2) What is the mediating effect of DCs for AI system adoption on the relationship between TOE factors and operational performance?

(3) What is the moderating effect of competitive pressure on the re lationships between technological factors (i.e., expected benefits and technology compatibility) and DCs (sensing capability, seizing capability, PODC, and analytics capability) for AI system adoption?

This study develops a research model using the TOE framework [25] to address the above three research questions (see Fig. 1). The TOE framework provides valuable insights into the factors that challenge and motivate firms’ technology adoption [26] and has been mainly used in the field of information systems to study the effects of a firm’s envi ronmental, organizational, and technological contexts on its perfor mance [27–29]. Furthermore, based on extensive empirical evidence and a strong theoretical foundation, several studies on technology adoption have used the TOE framework [30,31]. Accordingly, this framework is used in this study to explore the effects of AI system adoption and the macro-environment on firms’ operational performance.

Although previous studies have extensively documented the positive impact of AI adoption on organizational performance, they have often lacked a detailed exploration of the DCs that enable successful AI adoption and value creation, particularly in competitive industries such as telecommunications. Similar to prior studies, this study confirms the positive effects of AI adoption on performance, but it differs from the literature by addressing the gap in understanding of the role of DCs. Specifically, it contributes to the literature by focusing on the telecom munications sector, a high-stakes environment characterized by unique challenges and competitive pressures. However, it uniquely integrates DCs theory with the TOE framework, offering a comprehensive perspective on how specific DCs, namely sensing capability, seizing capability, PODC, and analytics capability, mediate the relationship between TOE factors and operational performance. Furthermore, this study expands the theoretical discourse by investigating the moderating effect of competitive pressure, an often overlooked factor, on the rela tionship between technological factors and DCs. The findings reveal how external forces shape AI adoption decisions and outcomes, a dynamic neglected in previous work. By examining these interactions, this research provides new insights into the interplay between external environmental factors and technological considerations in the context of AI system adoption. Thus, in addition to confirming the positive impact of AI, the findings highlight the critical role of DCs and extend prior studies by exploring the moderating influence of competitive pressure, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of AI adoption in competitive industries.

## 2. Related literature

## 2.1. DCs and DC theory

In this study, an AI artifact is conceptualized as a system that com bines advanced analytics, machine learning models, and data processing capabilities to support decision-making, automation, and operational efficiencies within firms. This model positions an AI artifact as a tool that enhances a firm’s DCs, namely its sensing, seizing, analyzing, and transforming capabilities, which are essential in rapidly changing en vironments. “DCs” refers to a firm’s potential to reconfigure, integrate, and coordinate internal and external competencies to cope with rapid changes in commercial environments [32–37]. In this study, four key

DCs are classified: sensing capability, indicating that AI artifacts enhance a firm’s ability to detect and interpret market opportunities and threats by processing large amounts of data and generating actionable insights [37,38]; seizing capability, suggesting that AI systems enable firms to effectively mobilize their resources and capture the value of identified opportunities by providing advanced data analytics and decision-making tools [35,37,38]; analytics capability, indicating that AI artifacts strengthen firms’ ability to analyze complex datasets, un cover patterns, and derive insights that inform strategic decisions [39, 40]; and PODC, suggesting that AI systems support the reconfiguration and optimization of business processes, enhancing operational effi ciency and adaptability in response to changing market demands [32,35, 37,38,41].

An AI artifact does not function independently; it requires integra tion into existing business processes to contribute effectively to DCs. Indeed, DC theory posits that the renewal of existing organizational capabilities is essential for the survival of competitive firms [36]. Therefore, unlike operational capabilities, which allow firms to survive in the current environment, DCs enable firms to adapt and respond to external changes by creating, modifying, and extending their opera tional capabilities. DC theory further emphasizes that the forces that drive organizational capabilities are context-specific and path-dependent, emerging from the unique history of individual firms [33,34]. AI systems act as enablers of DCs, integrating into organiza tional processes to enhance firms’ ability to adapt to environmental and technological changes. Specifically, AI enhances sensing, seizing, and analytics capabilities while supporting PODC, which collectively enable firms to respond more effectively to competitive pressures and improve their operational performance, as well as improve customer service and accelerate response times.

Organizational capabilities, including DCs, are inherently processoriented. In line with the resource-based view (RBV), DCs are posi tioned as the organizational processes through which firms achieve greater adaptability and competitive advantage. Recognizing this, this study empirically examines how DCs mediate the relationship between

AI adoption and operational performance, addressing the “black box” of how AI adoption translates into performance improvements. In sum mary, DCs theory provides a robust theoretical perspective to explain why and how firms develop and renew their operational capabilities in response to dynamic changes. The integration of AI artifacts into DCs serves as a critical mechanism for firms to adapt, innovate, and compete effectively in rapidly evolving environments.

## 2.2. The TOE framework

The TOE framework has been widely used in the field of information systems to study the effects of technological, organizational, and envi ronmental contexts on firms’ decision-making [42,43]. The technolog ical context concerns technology-related factors that affect the business value of information technology (IT) and firms’ decision-making [44]. The organizational context concerns the effects of a firm’s characteris tics [45]. The environmental context involves external factors beyond a firm’s control that influence its decisions.

Although other theoretical frameworks, such as diffusion of in novations (DOI) theory, the $\mathrm { R B V , }$ and institutional theory, offer valuable insights, they have limitations when applied to AI adoption. Specifically, DOI theory provides valuable insights into the AI adoption process, but it does not fully capture the organizational and environmental complex ities involved in AI adoption. The RBV $[ 4 6 , 4 7 ]$ emphasizes the strategic resources and capabilities that firms leverage to gain competitive ad vantages. Although it highlights the importance of organizational ca pabilities, it does not adequately address the technological and environmental factors that influence AI adoption. Finally, institutional theory [48] examines how institutional pressures, such as norms, reg ulations, and cultural expectations, shape organizational behavior. Although relevant, this theory does not provide a comprehensive view of the technological and organizational factors at play. In contrast, the TOE framework integrates technological, organizational, and environmental factors, offering a more robust foundation for studying AI adoption, particularly in competitive industries. This framework enables a balanced analysis of the internal and external factors that drive AI adoption, making it particularly useful for understanding the dynamics of AI implementation in organizations.

The TOE framework identifies three key aspects of a firm’s context that influence its AI adoption: the technological context. which examines the characteristics of AI technologies, such as their perceived ben efits, compatibility, and complexity, essential for assessing how AI systems can be integrated into existing organizational processes; the organizational context, which focuses on a firm’s internal characteris tics, such as its resources, capabilities, and readiness for change, essential for evaluating the extent to which the firm is prepared to adopt and leverage AI technologies; and the environmental context, which considers the external environment, such as competitive pressure, that plays a pivotal role in shaping AI adoption.

Due to its comprehensive approach, the TOE framework is particu larly suited to understanding the dynamics of AI adoption in the tele communications industry. Using the TOE framework, this study can effectively capture the interplay between technology, organization, and environment, providing valuable insights into the mechanisms that drive AI adoption and its impact on operational performance. A defini tion of each construct based on the literature is provided in Table 1.

Some studies have examined the TOE framework and DCs. For example, Abdurrahman et al. [56] explored innovation and digital transformation by applying DCs within the TOE framework. Chittipaka et al. [57] used the TOE framework to investigate the technological, organizational, and environmental dimensions of blockchain technology adoption in supply chains. Anthony Jnr [58] identified social, techno logical, and organizational factors that affect telehealth adoption and evaluated opportunities for telehealth adoption in public health emer gencies using the TOE framework. Raj and Jeyaraj [59] identified different factors that affect the adoption of Industry 4.0 by using the TOE framework, such as perceived usefulness (i.e., technological factors), infrastructure support (i.e., organizational factors), and government support (i.e., environmental factors). Although previous studies have extensively documented the positive impact of AI adoption on organi zational performance, they have often lacked a detailed exploration of the DCs that enable successful AI adoption and value creation in firms, especially in a highly competitive industry such as telecommunications. This study bridges these gaps by offering theoretical insights into AI adoption and practical guidance for firms managing the challenges of AI implementation. A summary of AI adoption studies from 2020 to 2024 is included in Appendix A.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

This section draws on the TOE framework to develop the research model and hypotheses in the context of the telecommunications in dustry. Information and communications technology has enhanced the growth of various developing and developed regions [60], and the telecommunications industry has actively incorporated AI-based sys tems to improve various aspects of its service provision. In this study, a research model (see Fig. 1) that draws on DCs theory and the TOE framework is developed to understand AI adoption in firms. The choice of these theories aligns with the goal of examining how AI adoption capabilities impact operational performance in a telecommunications context. Specifically, DCs theory guides the conceptualization of capa bilities essential for AI adoption, namely sensing capability, seizing capability, PODC, and analytics capability. PODC is used as a substitute for reconfiguration capability, consistent with definitions in previous studies [32,41], while analytics capability is regarded as a core DC necessary to assess the effectiveness of AI implementation [50,61–63]. Thus, from a DCs perspective, AI implementation capabilities are pro posed as higher-order constructs with multiple sub-capabilities. In par allel, the TOE framework allows for the categorization and analysis of the external and internal conditions (i.e., technological, organizational, and environmental factors) that support these DCs, in line with Grant’s [64] approach to examining the emergence of organizational capabil ities. The TOE framework has long been validated in the IT literature for its robustness in capturing contextual factors [25], making it a suitable complement to DCs theory in the context of AI adoption. The combi nation of DCs theory and the TOE framework provides a holistic model of AI implementation capabilities, clarifying how TOE factors interact to foster DCs and drive AI-enabled operational performance.

## 3.1. Mediating role of DCs

Wilden and Gudergan [50] pointed out that DCs promote the alignment of marketing and technological capabilities with market conditions [65,66], which can lead to performance differentials. Helfat and Peteraf [67] argued that DCs include sensing, seizing, and reconfi guring, while Adner and Helfat [68] suggested that managers may also have DCs to build, integrate, reconfigure, and competitively reposition their firm’s organizational capabilities and resources. Roberts et al. [69] also stated that DCs include the ability to sense and seize opportunities and reconfigure a firm’s assets. Furthermore, Mikalef et al. [70] defined DCs as a firm’s ability to (a) sense and shape opportunities and threats, (b) seize opportunities, and (c) maintain competitiveness by identifying, enhancing, combining, and protecting its intangible and tangible assets when necessary [35].

## 3.1.1. PODC

“PODC” refers to a firm’s ability to change (e.g., renew, refresh, adjust, adapt, improve) its business processes to improve its competi tiveness [32]. Studies have identified several mediating functions of PODC on firm performance. For example, Kim et al. [32] surveyed 243 senior executives in Korean firms and showed that PODC mediated the relationship between IT infrastructure flexibility and firm performance. Chen et al. [71] found that business process dynamic capability medi ated the relationship between IT capability and firm performance using matched survey data from 214 business and IT executives of Chinese manufacturing firms. Fosso-Wamba et al. [41] showed that PODC mediated the relationship between big data analytics capability and firm performance using a cross-sectional sample of 297 business analysts and IT managers with business analytics and big data experience in China.

It is widely recognized that IT, including AI, cannot generate value on its own, but with performance being influenced by the integration and interaction of IT with other organizational and information systems factors, particularly PODC [71]. Unfortunately, few empirical studies have investigated the relationships between AI system adoption, PODC, and firm performance. Linking AI to PODC is essential to provide practitioners with actionable guidelines for making decisions about AI implementation, acquisition, and development, and to develop a deeper understanding of the role of AI adoption in firm performance. Therefore, a thorough examination of the mediating role of PODC in the relation ship between AI adoption and firm performance can generate important insights.

PODC enhances the effectiveness of a firm’s operational processes by enabling the deployment/redeployment of resources, the configuration/ reconfiguration of its resource base, and the assimilation and acquisition of external and internal knowledge, which should match the firm’s vision [72]. PODC can also help firms make accurate and timely de cisions [73]. According to Butler and Murphy [74], firms with strong PODC find it easier than their competitors to make inefficient opera tional processes cheaper, faster, and better and to transform them into processes capable of responding to dynamic environments. These firms outperform their competitors by responding more efficiently in dynamic environments through enhancements in information sharing, coordi nation, and communication [75], particularly in the telecommunica tions industry. For example, telecommunications firms are using AI to detect profitable and new market segments via trend forecasting or spotting, identify themes in unlabeled data, modify their manufacturing process to seize opportunities for higher profit margins, increase their market share, and gain first-mover advantages [14]. In the telecom munications industry, for instance, firms with strong PODC can sense their core customers’ needs more quickly or in real time, better identify thematic areas, or improve their understanding of their customer base through the use of diverse sources of core information and by changing their business processes to satisfy and retain customers and, ultimately, increase their profitability [14]. Excellent PODC can give firms a competitive advantage and lead to superior firm performance [76]. Based on the above discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed:

## H1a. PODC is positively associated with operational performance.

## 3.1.2. Sensing capability

According to Day [53], sensing capability is defined as a firm’s ability to take action based on knowledge acquired from its environ ment. Sensing capability involves processes of data collection and interpretation, allocation of resources and tasks, and decision-making and communication of information [77]. Sensing capability enables firms to evaluate opportunities and threats by scanning, interpreting, learning, calibrating, and filtering data and information. These searches take place at the core or periphery of a firm’s business systems, including its existing and potential collaborators (e.g., suppliers) [37]. Sensing capability has a positive impact on firm performance [78], by improving the firm’s ability to respond to market opportunities, such as American Airlines’ decision support system related to reservation systems. As a result, the firm can develop specialized knowledge and expertise with its partners and increase its market intelligence to improve its performance [79]. Based on the above discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1b. Sensing capability is positively associated with operational performance.

## 3.1.3. Seizing capability

Seizing capability is conceptualized as a firm’s ability to respond to threats and opportunities [54]. Seizing capability contributes to the decision-making process throughout the allocation of investments and the eventual specification of the business model [35]. Seizing opportu nities involves evaluating emerging and existing capabilities, and marketplace acceptance is more likely to be achieved through possible investments in relevant technologies and designs [35,80]. Seizing an opportunity may require designing a business model for a new venture [67]. According to the literature [35,81], a firm’s seizing capability enables it to become competitive; specifically, its sensing capability identifies new opportunities, while its seizing capability captures these opportunities. In the context of AI system adoption, firms capture and respond to opportunities identified through their sensing capability and identify new solutions to customer problems that lead to better opera tional performance. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1c. Seizing capability is positively associated with operational performance.

## 3.1.4. Analytics capability

“Analytics capability” refers to a firm’s ability to process, analyze, and transform data into actionable insights to support decision-making, with pattern detection further enhanced by AI and advances in digita lization [39,40]. This capability relies heavily on the data science and analytics expertise of employees, serving as a critical enabler of value creation and innovation [39]. Firms with strong analytics capabilities can combine existing technologies or develop new ones to improve processes and create value, particularly in environments requiring continuous adaptation [82]. Investments in analytics capability have been shown to drive operational excellence and competitive advantage. For example, firms like Amazon and Walmart leverage big data analytics to optimize supply chain management, enhancing efficiency and gaining a competitive edge in their respective markets [83]. Such investments enable firms to respond dynamically to changing market conditions, reduce inefficiencies, and improve decision-making processes.

Studies have also identified analytics capability as a key organiza tional resource. In the context of DCs, analytics capability meets the criteria of value, rarity, and inimitability. It supports firms in adapting to volatile and uncertain market environments by enhancing decisionmaking and operational agility [50,61–63]. Unlike static resources, analytics capability allows firms to continuously sense, seize, and reconfigure resources to address new opportunities and threats, partic ularly in supply chain management. By facilitating the rapid processing of large datasets, analytics capability enables firms to uncover patterns, optimize operations, and achieve superior performance. Based on this discussion, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1d. Analytics capability is positively associated with operational performance.

## 3.2. TOE factors as antecedents of AI system adoption

To develop a rigorous research model, an extensive review of the literature was conducted and four TOE factors were identified as drivers of AI system implementation. These highly relevant and explanatory factors are competitive pressure, organizational readiness, expected benefits, and technology compatibility. Competitive pressure is consid ered an environmental factor because it describes the current state of technology use in the competitive environment [84–86]. Organizational readiness is considered an organizational factor because it captures the availability of a firm’s resources to adopt technology [58,84]. Tech nology compatibility and expected benefits are considered technological factors because they represent the congruence with and instrumental benefits of technology use given the practices of existing and potential users [49.84–87].

## 3.2.1. Technological drivers of DCs

In this study, variables such as technology compatibility are selected, as these are widely recognized in the literature as important de terminants of technology adoption [50]. These factors assess how well AI systems align with existing processes and the perceived benefits they offer [88].

Expected benefits: “Expected benefits” refers to the strategic and operational advantages that firms hope to achieve by adopting a given technology. According to the literature, these benefits can be either direct or indirect. Direct benefits include operational cost savings and internal efficiencies, such as reduced error rates, data re-entry, and paperwork. Indirect benefits include opportunities that arise from technology usage, such as potential process reengineering and customer service improvements. Intangible benefits such as improved coordina tion and knowledge sharing may also have value for decision-makers. Adopting the right tools based on advanced analytics, machine learning, and AI can change the way employees and firms coordinate and communicate their work and work together, resulting in less tunnel vision, more synchronization, and greater efficiency [88].

Firms, particularly those in the telecommunications industry, expect great potential strategic and operational benefits from AI systems, even though they are still at an early stage of implementation. These expected benefits include tailored customer service, development of new revenue streams, new product offerings, workflow improvements, and cost re ductions. DCs for AI system adoption work by providing the required flexibility to derive benefits from technological advancements, such as autonomous data testing and continuous deployment. For example,

Vodafone’s customer service has been enhanced by applying autono mous, conversational interfaces using AI to operate across various channels, such as chatbots, apps, home assistants, and the web [89,90]. Rogers [91] also pointed out that the expected benefits of an innovative technology influence firms’ intention to adopt the technology. When the level of awareness of positive changes is high, people may actively participate in and accept the implementation of AI. In summary, given the increasing embeddedness of AI in business processes, the extent to which firms have the ability to quickly modify their business processes depends on their ability to adopt and leverage AI systems, which is re flected in the benefits they expect from such systems. Corporate decision-makers need to weigh the expected benefits against the costs of adopting AI systems in their firms, particularly in telecommunications operations that involve complex decision-making processes and are highly data-sensitive. However, if a firm’s managers believe that adopting AI systems is beneficial, they need to go further and evaluate how their firm can leverage these benefits to meet its business needs by making extensive use of these AI systems. Hence, the following hy pothesis is proposed:

H2. The effect of the expected benefits of AI systems is mediated by firms (a) PODC, (b) sensing capability, (c) seizing capability, and (d) analytics capability, and is positively associated with operational performance.

Technology compatibility: Compatibility is defined as “the degree to which the innovation is perceived as consistent with the existing values, past experiences, and needs of the potential adopter” [92 p 223], and has been frequently cited in studies on innovation adoption. When using this definition in the organizational context, technology compatibility can be viewed as the congruence between a given technology and a firm’s business practices and value system, such as organizational culture. A high level of technology compatibility leads to faster adoption [93]. When firms transform their network architecture into software-defined networks with automation enabled by virtualization technologies. the role of AI is to leverage the automation capabilities of networks such that they can self-orchestrate, self-diagnose, and self-repair. The ex pected time and costs of AI technology implementation are lower in IT environments that are compatible with AI technology than in other environments. For example, telecommunications firms can use AI to allocate their resources by forecasting areas of greatest importance and detecting failures; they can then change their business process to pro actively detect failures and reorganize their resources to concentrate on areas predicted to be important, thus limiting slack resources and reducing operating expenses and failures [14]. As such, the compati bility of Al systems with a firm's existing business practices and culture is evaluated in this study. Technology compatibility is assumed to be a relevant attribute of AI systems when examining the technological fac tors that influence the adoption of AI systems. Specifically, AI system adoption is conceptualized as a DC, with “path dependency” being one of the important characteristics of organizational DCs (i.e., organiza tional capability is shaped by a firm’s past experience and history) [33, 34]. Firm managers and decision-makers are expected to be more likely to apply and use AI systems if they believe that adopting such systems is compatible with their firm’s existing organizational value and work practices. It is thus hypothesized that firms’ DCs grow alongside their technology compatibility for the reconstruction of business processes to improve competitiveness.

H3. The effect of technology compatibility is mediated by firms’ (a) PODC, (b) sensing capability, (c) seizing capability, and (d) analytics capability, and is positively associated with operational performance.

## 3.2.2. Organizational driver of DCs

Organizational readiness: Organizational readiness is considered a key organizational factor because it encompasses a firm’s readiness to adopt and integrate new technologies, which is essential for successful AI implementation. Organizational readiness includes aspects such as resource availability, management support, and employee skills [51, 94]. The literature review conducted for this study highlighted that organizational readiness was a consistent predictor of technology adoption success. Organizational readiness represents the necessary organizational resources available to use AI systems [44]. The required resources include IT sophistication and financial capital [95], the latter including IT human resources (i.e., technological expertise and knowl edge) and IT infrastructure components [50]. This study argues that having IT professionals with the ability and skills to use AI is a key in dicator of organizational readiness. Moreover, firms with excellent IT expertise can develop cost-efficient and reliable systems, anticipate their IT service needs better than their competitors, and meet the strong de mand for IT strategies aligned with business strategies [31]. Firms that do not possess the necessary level of IT expertise cannot quickly change their business processes in the event of changing market circumstances [96], particularly in the telecommunications industry. The role of DCs is to give firms the flexibility they need, starting with organizational ini tiatives such as end-to-end collaboration across different business units. For example, telecommunications firms can use financial resources and IT expertise to develop services that can monitor their customers’ needs and use machine learning to uncover emerging threats and opportu nities. This is a proactive approach because it not only monitors customers’ wants and needs in real time but also predicts their future needs [14]. This study suggests that the availability of competent or capable professionals for AI implementation is an important factor for organi zational readiness in the context of AI system adoption, as the IT man agement literature has indicated that organizational conditions are critical factors for AI adoption [31]. Hence, organizational readiness is expected to play a vital role in shaping the attitude of decision-makers and managers toward AI system adoption. Specifically, decision-makers and managers will support the adoption of AI systems if they believe that their firm has sufficient capabilities to promote this adoption. Thus, organizational readiness is expected to grow alongside firms’ DCs to enable them to modify their business processes to improve their competitiveness.

H4. The effect of organizational readiness is mediated by firms’ (a) PODC, (b) sensing capability, (c) seizing capability, and (d) analytics capability, and is positively associated with operational performance.

## 3.2.3. Environmental driver of DCs

Competitive pressure: Competitive pressure is chosen as an environ mental factor because of its relevance in incentivizing firms to adopt innovative technologies to maintain their competitiveness and comply with industry standards [44]. Competitive pressure is an aspect of the external environment that can increase firms’ use of AI systems. Thi study investigates the pressures that firms face from their stakeholders (e.g., competitors, suppliers, and customers), based on studies on the influence of competitive pressures on firms’ IT adoption. Firms are under pressure when their competitors adopt new technologies and therefore prefer to immediately adopt the same technologies to maintain their competitiveness [28]. In dynamic environments, it is difficult to maintain a competitive advantage because the rapid pace of change can render obsolete or neutralize the benefits of current competitive ad vantages. To operate more effectively and efficiently in dynamic and volatile environments, firms must adopt AI systems. For instance, as customer retention management is essential in today’s competitive market, especially in the telecommunications industry, firms are using machine learning to identify customer groups likely to leave (i.e., churn prediction) and modify their business processes to provide more competitive services or make attractive offers to these groups [97]. The mediating role of DCs clearly shows how AL systems can be a source of competitive advantage in uncertain environments, as firms can use AI systems to change their business processes with the aim of improving their competitiveness. Although the effect of competitive pressure on a firm's IT adoption has been discussed in the literature [86.52], studies have generally ignored the theoretical “black box” related to DCs. Hence, this study posits that DCs play an important role in translating the characteristics of the external environment into operational perfor mance. DC mediation clearly highlights how AI systems can be a source of competitive advantage in dynamic environments, as firms use AI systems to modify their current business process with the aim of enhancing their competitiveness. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5. The effect of competitive pressure is mediated by firms’ (a) PODC, (b) sensing capability, (c) seizing capability, and (d) analytics capability, and is positively associated with operational performance.

## 3.2.4. Moderating role of competitive pressure

In addition to the direct effect of the macro-environment on DCs, studies have shown that the influence of firm attributes on DCs is moderated by the competitiveness of the domestic environment. Accordingly, competitive pressure is expected to moderate the re lationships between expected benefits and DCs and between technology compatibility and DCs in the technological context. Support for inno vation in the business environment determines policies, procedures, practices, and how things are done. In fact, an environment that sup ports innovation is a prerequisite for firms’ DCs, particularly in the telecommunications industry. Lei et al. [42] suggested that there is an interaction effect between the macro-environment and firms’ IT usage. The high levels of pressure on firms from competitors, customers, and suppliers strongly support the relationship between the technological drivers of DCs and firms’ actual DCs. Therefore, the following hypoth eses are proposed:

H6a. Competitive pressure moderates the effect of expected benefits on firms’ (a) PODC, (b) sensing capability, (c) seizing capability, and (d) an alytics capability.

H6b. Competitive pressure moderates the effect of technology compatibility on firms’ (a) PODC, (b) sensing capability, (c) seizing capability, and (d) analytics capability.

## 4. Research methodology

Positivist research approaches assert that objective reality in the realm of phenomena can be measured in data and expressed in causal relationships [98]. Therefore, this study used survey measures to cap ture social reality with the objective of determining DCs for AI system adoption and addressing the research questions. With this general approach, this study initially explored the literature to identify the factors that influence DCs for AI system adoption, the effects of DCs on operational performance, and the mediating role of DCs in the re lationships between competitive pressure, organizational readiness, technology compatibility, expected benefits, and operational perfor mance. The research model was conceptualized using the DCs approach. The survey was developed and the hypotheses were validated using partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling (SEM). A field study approach was used to collect data from executives of telecom munications firms through an online questionnaire to test the research hypotheses. The following sections present the research design, research method, measurement issues, instrument and measurement items, and control variables used in this study.

## 4.1. Research design

Telecommunications is an example of an industry that has been at the forefront of AI adoption and can reap many benefits from its use. The telecommunications industry consists of wireless and wired telecom munications activities (ISIC Rev. 4 code 61) and provides the modern telecommunications networks that are essential to global value chains [99]. Telecommunications firms have undergone many transformations to deal with fierce competition and adapt to dramatic changes. The popularity of AI technology in the industry has been boosted by the advent of fifth-generation mobile network (5G) technologies [100]. The adoption of AI technology by major telecommunications operator around the world has generated many positive effects. For example, SK Telecom uses machine learning to evaluate network traffic to strengthen its network operations and detect anomalies [101]: AT&T uses AI al. gorithms to check and repair its base stations using drones [102]. AI powers applications used for customer support, network optimization, predictive infrastructure maintenance, and personalized marketing. To enhance customer support, telecommunications firms use AI-powered chatbots to handle customer queries, troubleshoot common issues, and provide 24/7 support; they also deploy virtual assistants, including voice-activated devices, to help customers manage their accounts, make payments, and access services (e.g., China Mobile, China Telcom, and China Unicom). AI is also used by telecommunications firms to optimize network performance, route traffic, and allocate resources effectively fo better connectivity and quality of service. AI-based systems can also be used for predictive maintenance, as AI algorithms can help to analyze network data to predict and prevent potential network outages or equipment failures, ensuring uninterrupted service. Furthermore, AI-based systems can be used for fraud detection, as AI algorithms can detect unusual patterns in call records and transactions to identify po tential fraud or security breaches. Personalized marketing is another area in which telecommunications firms use AI-based systems: the AI application analyzes customer data to create targeted marketing cam paigns and offers based on individual preferences and behaviors (such as promoting 5G service to 4G users). For network security, AI-based sys tems can monitor network traffic for anomalies (such as excessive traffic flow), thereby identifying potential cyber threats and enhancing cybersecurity. AI has also been used strategically by telecommunica tions firms as a type of IT to improve their competitiveness, with ap plications including intelligent customer relationship management systems, maintenance and monitoring of network operations, and vir tual assistants to support marketing and customer service [103]. How ever, there are challenges associated with AI adoption. Many AI applications do not vet generate business value and some remain at the conceptual stage. Therefore, by providing a broader and deeper under standing of the AI adoption process in the telecommunications industry, this study has implications for AI vendors, governments, potential AI adopters, and academics in related fields.

This study focused on the telecommunications sector in China to examine AI adoption and its impact on operational performance because of the rapid technological advancements and competitive dynamics that define the Chinese telecommunications industry, making it a suitable setting in which to explore the role of DCs in AI system adoption. China’s pivotal role in the global information and communications technology industry further justifies this choice, as China represents one of the fastest-growing markets and a major supplier worldwide. The telecom munications industry in particular has actively adopted AI-based sys tems to enhance various aspects of service provision, from network optimization to customer experience management. Therefore, by analyzing AI adoption in this context, the study aimed to provide valuable insights to advance both theoretical understanding and prac tical application. Specifically, the study findings highlight the strategic importance of AI in strengthening DCs and driving operational perfor mance, with implications for firms navigating similar technological and competitive landscapes.

This study used a cross-sectional survey to collect data from tele communications firms in China. Data collection was conducted in three stages. First, the survey questionnaire was developed. Second, 15 managers from the Chinese telecommunications industry were inter viewed to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the measurement items, with some items being reworded and clarified based on the managers’ feedback. Third, the revised questionnaire was used to collect survey data from the CEOs/COOs, directors, IT managers, sales/ marketing managers, product development managers, and manufacturing/production managers of China’s telecommunications firms [104].

In the context of this survey, an AI system is a set of advanced technologies designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence. These systems are able to learn from data, make decisions, and improve over time without explicit programming. In the telecom munications industry, AI systems include machine learning algorithms, NLP, and autonomous decision-making.

Machine Learning Algorithms: These algorithms, such as neural net works and decision trees, are used to analyze large volumes of network data to optimize performance, predict maintenance needs, and enhance service delivery. For instance, AI systems can use real-time data analysis to optimize network performance, predict equipment failures, and automate maintenance processes, ensuring more reliable and efficient network operations. Additionally, algorithms such as clustering and regression can forecast customer behavior and market trends, allowing firms to adapt their offerings and marketing strategies to meet evolving customer needs [31].

NLP: This technology enables AI systems to understand and respond to human language, facilitating interactions with customers through chatbots and virtual assistants. For example, AI-driven chatbots use NLP algorithms such as sentiment analysis and entity recognition to provide personalized customer support, improving response times and customer satisfaction. By learning from interactions, these systems can continu ously enhance their quality of service, adapt to new queries, and improve their conversational capabilities [31].

Autonomous Decision-Making: AI systems can make real-time de cisions to manage network traffic, detect fraud, and personalize customer experiences. For example, AI algorithms such as anomaly detection and reinforcement learning can identify unusual patterns and anomalies in data, enabling telecommunications firms to detect and prevent fraudulent activity more effectively. These systems can also dynamically adjust network resources to optimize traffic flow and enhance user experiences [31].

These AI systems are distinct from traditional data analytics tools because they have the ability to adapt and evolve based on new infor mation, providing more dynamic and efficient solutions to the complex challenges of the telecommunications industry. By leveraging these AI technologies, telecommunications firms can significantly improve their operational efficiency, customer satisfaction, and competitive advantage.

## 4.2. Research method

The sample source for this study was the China Internet Network Information Center (CINIC) [104]. Data were collected from senior ex ecutives of network infrastructure providers, service providers, equip ment manufacturers, software and solution providers, and network management and optimization service providers in Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Zhuhai [60]. Specifically, 483 potential respondents were randomly selected from the CINIC list of corporations. Primary names and email addresses were double-checked, even though a random sampling method was used, to avoid overlap, as a business may be registered under multiple names. The diversity of the sample made it possible to explore the patterns of behavior in the telecommunications industry, making the results more generalizable than they would have been if a narrower sample had been used. An invitation letter and the questionnaire were emailed to the selected potential respondents. The invitation letter explained that the study focused only on the telecom munications industry and included a confidentiality agreement and a description of the research objective. To ensure data integrity and maintain the appropriate unit of analysis, the following measures were implemented: (a) each survey response was assigned a unique serial number to track and identify submissions; (b) a thorough manual review of the responses was conducted to identify and remove duplicates, which involved cross-referencing the serial numbers to ensure that no two re sponses came from the same firm; and (c) all duplicates were removed from the dataset to prevent double counting and ensure that each response corresponded to a distinct firm. These steps were taken to ensure that the data accurately reflected the organizational level of analysis, thereby preserving the validity and reliability of the findings.

After two rounds of email reminders, 257 completed questionnaires were received (response rate = 53.2 %). After checking the returned questionnaires, 52 were deemed invalid due to unrealistic responses, leaving a final sample of 205 respondents (response rate = 42.44 %). According to Malhotra and Grove [105], this is acceptable for an empirical study. All of the respondents were senior executives who had worked at their firms for more than five years. Furthermore, non-response bias was assessed by a t-test of differences between earlier and later respondents [106]. The questionnaires received were grouped into two “waves” based on their return date: the first wave comprised respondents who replied within the first 30 days and the second wave comprised those who replied within the last 15 days of the survey period [106]. The second wave of respondents was considered representative of non-respondents. Other empirical studies in operations management have adopted a similar methodology [107,108]. Using this approach, it was possible to determine whether there were significant differences between earlier and later respondents in terms of industry type, firm size, or job title. Among the 205 valid questionnaires, 168 (81.95 %) were received within the first 30 days and 37 (18.05 %) within the last 15 days of the survey period. There were no significant differences be tween the two waves in terms of industry type (t-value = 0.531, p > 0.05), firm size (t-value = 0.483, p > 0.05), or job title (t-value = 0.674, $p > 0 . 0 5 )$ , indicating the absence of non-response bias. Appendix B shows the respondents’ profiles.

## 4.3. Measurement issues

A series of measures were adopted to test and reduce common method variance (CMV). First, to reduce the short-term memory effect of previous scales, multiple items were used to measure each construct [109]. Second, this study focused on distinct constructs to ensure face validity [109]. Third, Harman's single-factor test was used to evaluate potential CMV [110]. The highest covariance was 22.5 % for a single factor, indicating no interference from CMV in the empirical results [111]. Fourth, PLS with a common method factor involving all of the principal constructs’ indicators was used [112]. The variance of each indicator was then calculated as substantively explained by the principal construct and method factor. The results showed that the average method variance was 0.010 and the average substantively explained variance of the indicators was 0.628 (see Appendix C), giving a ratio of substantive variance to method variance of 63:1. Furthermore, all loadings of the method factor were non-significant. Considering the small magnitude and non-significance of method variance, it was concluded that there was no interference from CMV in the study.

## 4.4. Instrument and measurement items

In creating the instrument for this study, the suggestion of Fawcett et al. [113] to adopt—and potentially adapt—pre-existing scales fo established constructs that have been shown to be reliable and valid was followed. Competitive pressure was operationalized using three items adapted from Chen et al. [50]. The respondents were asked to rate the extent to which their competitors, suppliers, and customers were implementing AI systems, on a scale from 1 (none have adopted AI systems) to 7 (almost all have adopted AI systems). Analytics capability was operationalized using five items adapted from Srinivasan and Swink [114]. Sensing capability was operationalized using four items adapted from Pavlou and El Sawy [115]. Seizing capability was operationalized using five items adapted from Wilden et al. [116]. Expected benefits were operationalized by six items adapted from Chen et al. [50].

Specifically, the respondents were asked to rate their level of agreement with the expected benefits of AI systems, on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Technology compatibility was oper ationalized by three items adapted from Chen et al. [50]. Specifically, the respondents were asked to rate their agreement that AI systems were easy to incorporate into their firm’s practices, fit their firm’s culture, and were consistent with their business practices, on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Organizational readiness was oper ationalized by four items adapted from Chen et al. [50]. Specifically, the respondents were asked to rate the extent to which each of the four factors prevented their business unit from fully exploiting AI systems, on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (to a great extent). PODC was measured using the five-item PODC scale of Fosso-Wamba et al. [41], which was designed to evaluate a firm’s business process relative to its competitors, including connection (e.g., communication, information sharing) be tween parties, reduction of costs and human labor, introduction of complex analytical methods, and introduction of detailed information. Operational performance was operationalized with seven items adapted from Liu et al. [117]. Specifically, the respondents were asked to rate their firm’s performance over the past three years relative to their main competitors, on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). All of the scale items are provided in Appendix D.

## 4.5. Control variables

This study controlled for firm size, as this variable has been shown to be correlated with operational performance [118]. Specifically, larger firms tend to be better managed and have better organizational struc tures and technical support (e.g., AI systems) in place than smaller firms. It also controlled for firm age, as years of business experience can affect operational performance [119,120]. Industry type was also controlled for, as firms in different industries face unique internal contingencies and external environmental conditions [121]. Finally, this study controlled for location because location plays an important role in giving a firm the ability to obtain important resources and capabilities and therefore should be included as a control variable in performance research [122].

## 5. Data analysis and results

To determine the nomological validity of the research model, PLS with a two-stage analytical approach was used to analyze the survey data. This study first evaluated the measurement model by assessing its validity and reliability and then evaluated the structural model by assessing the strength of the hypothesized relationships between the study variables. The psychometric properties of the scales in the context of the structural model were assessed by conducting reliability and discriminant validity assessments.

Second, an exploratory factor analysis was conducted to determine the item–total correlation of all constructs. As shown in Table 3, all items had higher factor loadings on their own construct than on other constructs, providing evidence of convergent and discriminant validity. Fourth, discriminant validity was confirmed, as the square root of the average variance extracted for each construct was greater than its correlation with other constructs [125]. ## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

This study used PLS-SEM to test the structural model with the SmartPLS 3.0 software package. Adopting a similar approach to that recommended by Chin [125], the discriminant validity, convergent validity, and construct reliability of the research model were tested. ## 5.5. Endogeneity tests

A main concern in empirical studies is endogeneity. As Peel [133] pointed out, there could be omitted variables that are correlated with the dependent and independent variables. If endogeneity produces biased estimates of the influence of the key variables on the outcome, the PLS algorithm will be flawed and the study results will be invalid [123]. Potential endogeneity in this study was examined by testing the endo geneity of the exogenous variables (i.e., organizational readiness, competitive pressure, technology compatibility, expected benefits, and DCs) in the research model. Free covariances between the residuals of organizational readiness, competitive pressure, technology compati bility, expected benefits, DCs, and operational performance were enabled to avoid potential bias from one or more omitted variables in our model. A series of Durbin–Wu–Hausman tests [130] were per formed, and the results showed that the relationships between competitive pressure and DCs (PODC, p = 0.756; sensing capability, p = 0.531; seizing capability, p = 0.575; analytics capability, p = 0.612), expected benefits and DCs (PODC, p = 0.483; sensing capability, p = 0.427; seizing capability, p = 0.504; analytics capability, p = 0.563), technology compatibility and DCs (PODC, p = 0.251; sensing capability, p = 0.449; seizing capability, p = 0.386; analytics capability, p = 0.394), organizational readiness and DCs (PODC, p = 0.347; sensing capability, p = 0.493; seizing capability, p = 0.451; analytics capability, p = 0.372), and DCs and operational performance (PODC, p = 0.585; sensing capability, p = 0.422; seizing capability, p = 0.465; analytics capability, p = 0.483) were not influenced by endogeneity. These results indicate that omitted variable bias and endogeneity were not concerns in this study.

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Discussion

This study of the consequences and antecedents of DCs at the firm level provides a comprehensive evaluation of how operational perfor mance can be affected by DCs. Moreover. it provides theoretical explanations to better understand this phenomenon. This section discusses the main findings and their implications for the research questions. Specif ically, the findings demonstrate the explanatory power of the TOE framework and enrich it in the context of AI system adoption. The data analysis results show that environmental (competitive pressure), orga nizational (organizational readiness), and technological (technology compatibility and expected benefits) components directly influenced DCs in the model, supporting the hypotheses and aligning with the TOE framework. Furthermore, DCs, rather than the technological, organiza tional, and environmental factors, directly influenced operational per formance. The results thus revealed that DCs mediated the relationships between AI adoption factors and organizational outcomes. These find ings confirm that the TOE factors examined are antecedents of DCs for AI system adoption and are key components of strong operational perfor mance. Moreover, the results showed that competitive pressure (i.e., an environmental factor) positively moderated the relationship between technology compatibility and expected benefits, (i.e., two technological factors) and DCs. Specifically, high competitive pressure was associated with a stronger relationship between these two technological factors and DCs than low competitive pressure. This suggests that managers perceive AI systems as being able to help their firms achieve greater DCs in a more dynamic environment for better performance. ## 6.2. Contributions

## 6.2.1. Empirical contributions

This study makes important empirical contributions to the literature by integrating DCs into the TOE framework, providing a novel perspective on how AI adoption interacts with organizational capabil ities to influence operational performance. Specifically, it demonstrates how AI can be strategically leveraged to enhance performance in the telecommunications industry.

First, this study is one of the first empirical investigations to explore the mediating role of DCs in the AI adoption process. The results show that different DCs (i.e., sensing capability, seizing capability, PODC, and analytics capability) are key mediators of the relationships between TOE factors and firms’ operational performance. This empirical validation of DCs as a mechanism for AI adoption provides new insights into how firms’ capabilities influence their successful implementation of AI systems.

Second, this study reveals that competitive pressure is a key moderator influencing the relationships between technological factors (i.e., technology compatibility and expected benefits) and DCs. This finding is in line with a previous study [44] suggesting that external pressure significantly influences a firm’s ability to adopt and implement technological innovations. However, the present study extends this idea to the context of the telecommunications industry, where competition plays a particularly important role.

Third, this study places particular emphasis on analytics capability as a vital DC for successful AI implementation. Although previous research, such as Wu et al. [39], has highlighted the importance of data analytics in AI applications, the present study is one of the first to empirically demonstrate that analytics capability directly influences AI system adoption and therefore operational performance. This finding un derscores the need for firms to build and refine their analytics capabil ities as they pursue their AI-driven transformation.

Finally, integrating DCs theory into the TOE framework not only extends the TOE framework but also provides valuable insights for practitioners in the telecommunications industry seeking to harness the transformative potential of AI.

## 6.2.2. Conceptual contributions

In addition to its empirical contributions, this study makes an important conceptual contribution by adapting the TOE framework to incorporate DCs as mediators. emphasizing the distinct role of AI in enhancing these capabilities. Specifically, it underscores how AI systems enhance DCs in the telecommunications industry, enabling firms to compete effectively in a dynamic environment. Regarding the distinct contributions of each DC, this study shows that AI systems enhance sensing capability by enabling firms to detect and interpret changes in the market environment, customer preferences, and technological ad vancements. In the telecommunications sector, this allows firms to anticipate opportunities and threats, ensuring timely and informed re sponses [37,38]. In addition, the results reveal that AI systems help firms seize opportunities through advanced data analytics and decision sup port tools. This capability is particularly important in the fast-paced telecommunications industry, where firms must quickly capitalize on new opportunities to maintain their competitive advantage [35,37,38]. Furthermore, this study illustrates that by processing large amounts of data, AI systems significantly strengthen firms’ analytics capability, enabling them to derive actionable insights. This empowers telecom munications firms to optimize decision-making, allocate resources effectively, and personalize customer experiences, thereby improving operational performance [39,40]. Finally, the results show that AI sys tems support the reconfiguration and optimization of business pro cesses, thus enhancing firms’ adaptability and operational efficiency. Given the rapid technological advancements and evolving customer demands in the telecommunications industry, PODC enables firms to streamline operations and improve service delivery [32,35,37,38,41]. These contributions bridge theoretical gaps and provide a foundation for further exploration of AI adoption and its implications for dynamic organizational capabilities.

Furthermore, the findings of this study contribute to the literature. Specifically, this study supports DCs theory by showing how sensing and seizing capabilities, along with the addition of PODC and analytics capability, form the foundation of a firm’s ability to adopt and imple ment AI systems. This finding aligns with the study of Abdurrahman et al. [56], who also emphasized the need for firms to develop a broad range of capabilities to effectively leverage new technologies. However, the present study goes further by conceptualizing these capabilities in the context of AI adoption, thus contributing to a new understanding of the mechanisms that link organizational capabilities to technological transformation. Additionally, the TOE framework proved useful in identifying the external and internal factors that drive AI adoption. The findings of this study extend the work of Chen et al. [103] by incorpo rating a more nuanced view of how external factors (such as competitive pressure) moderate the influence of technology factors on the adoption of AI systems. The relationships between TOE factors and DCs provide a deeper understanding of how the external environment interacts with a firm’s internal capabilities to shape its AI adoption.

## 6.3.2. Implications for policymakers

Policymakers can use the findings of this study to shape the broader environment for AI adoption in industries such as telecommunications. The findings demonstrate that competitive pressure moderates the re lationships between technological factors (i.e., technology compatibility and expected benefits) and the DCs that firms need for AI adoption. This suggests that a highly competitive environment, combined with gov ernment support, may encourage firms to invest in AI adoption, which can lead to better operational performance. Therefore, governments should not only foster competition but also provide targeted support mechanisms, such as subsidies, training programs, and innovation hubs, that help firms build the capabilities needed for AI integration. This study also highlights that fostering an ecosystem of collaboration be tween businesses and public institutions can significantly enhance the speed and efficiency of AI adoption. Policymakers should therefore facilitate partnerships between universities, research institutions, and businesses to create knowledge-sharing platforms, which will enable faster diffusion of AI technologies and better alignment of AI solutions with organizational needs.

## 6.3.3. Implications for society

This study has important implications for society, as AI technologies are increasingly integrated into industries such as telecommunications. By examining the interplay between technological, organizational, and environmental factors on operational performance, the study provides insights that extend beyond organizational boundaries and impact so ciety as a whole.

First, as AI adoption reshapes industries, there is a societal need to prepare the workforce for new roles that emphasize collaboration with AI systems. This study highlights the importance of developing DCs, such as sensing and analytics capabilities, which are crucial for effective AI integration. Societal efforts should focus on enhancing educational programs and training initiatives that equip individuals with these skills, ensuring that they can thrive in an AI-enhanced work environment.

Second, although this study focused primarily on operational per formance, its results suggest that AI adoption strategies should also consider long-term societal impacts. This involves prioritizing fair ness, transparency, and ethical considerations in AI deployment. In doing so, society can deploy AI systems that enhance efficiency, promote social justice, and minimize potential negative conse quences, such as technostress and the erosion of social relationships. Third, this study’s findings regarding the role of competitive pressure in AI adoption highlight the need for equitable access to AI tech nologies across different sectors and communities. Policymakers and industry leaders must work together to bridge the digital divide to ensure that all segments of society benefit from AI advances, which means investing in infrastructure and providing resources to un derserved areas, thereby promoting inclusivity in the digital transformation.

Finally, although not directly addressed in this study, the increasing autonomy of AI systems highlights the importance of designing technologies with features that enhance user control and custom ization. This approach can help alleviate the fear that AI will replace human roles and foster greater acceptance and trust in AI technol ogies. Future research could therefore further explore these aspects to provide deeper insights.

Overall, this study provides a roadmap for societal adaptation to AI adoption, emphasizing the need for collaboration among stakeholders to ensure that AI technologies contribute positively to societal progress. By focusing on skills development, fairness, ethical deployment, and trust building, society can harness the transformative potential of AI while preserving social well-being.

## 7. Limitations and future research

Despite its valuable insights into the adoption of AI technologies, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it focused exclusively on firms in the telecommunications industry in China. Although this industry is at the forefront of AI adoption, the findings may not fully apply to other industries or regions, particularly in countries where AI adoption is at an early stage or where industry specific factors play an important role. Future research could extend this study by comparing AI adoption across different industries and countries, providing a more comprehensive perspective on the role of

DCs in AI implementation. Second, this study used a cross-sectional research design, collecting data at a single point in time. As a result, the findings do not account for the longitudinal effects of AI adoption on DCs or operational performance over time. Future studies could use longitudinal or panel data to assess how DCs evolve and influence AI performance over the long term. Third, the use of survey data may have introduced bias based on the respondents’ perceptions of organizational capabilities and AI adoption. Although measures were taken to minimize such bias by focusing on experienced professionals, future research could use alternative data collection methods, such as case studies or interviews, to better understand the challenges and processes involved in building DCs for AI system adoption. Finally, this study focused on TOE factors and four DCs (i.e., sensing capability, seizing capability, PODC, and analytics capability). Other factors, such as organizational culture, leadership, and external partnerships, could also play critical roles in AI implementation. Future research could explore additional factors or examine how the interaction between internal and external capabilities impacts the AI adoption process.

## 7.1. Boundary conditions

This study was conducted in the context of the Chinese telecom munications industry, which served as a critical boundary condition for understanding AI adoption and its impact on operational performance. This context is characterized by several unique factors that influence the adoption and effectiveness of AI systems. First, the Chinese telecom munications industry operates under a distinct regulatory framework that shapes the adoption of new technologies, including AI. Government policies and regulations can either facilitate or hinder AI integration, affecting how firms implement AI systems and leverage them to improve their operations. Second, the telecommunications market in China is highly competitive and rapidly evolving, with firms constantly seeking innovative solutions to maintain their competitive edge. This dynamic environment creates both opportunities and challenges for AI adoption, as firms must navigate market pressures while integrating advanced technologies. Finally, China is at the forefront of technological innova tion, especially AI development. The telecommunications industry benefits from this technological leadership, providing fertile ground to explore how AI can enhance DCs and operational performance.

By explicitly discussing these boundary conditions, this study high lights the specificities of the Chinese telecommunications industry that influence AI adoption. This focus not only justifies the choice of context but also provides insights into how regulatory policies and market dy namics shape the effectiveness of AI systems in enhancing operational performance.

## 7.2. Future research directions

Based on the limitations discussed previously, the following di rections for future research are proposed.

First, future studies could examine AI adoption across different in dustries (e.g., manufacturing, healthcare, retail) or regions (e.g., Europe, North America) to explore how DCs and external environ mental factors influence AI implementation in various contexts. Comparative studies could help identify universal trends or industry specific variations in the adoption process.

Second, to understand the long-term impact of AI adoption on organizational capabilities and performance, future research should use longitudinal research designs. This approach would allow scholars to track the evolution of DCs and how AI systems influence operational performance over time, providing richer insights into the sustainability of AI adoption.

Third, as this study focused on TOE factors and four DCs, future studies could explore other factors, such as organizational learning, knowledge management, and innovation capability. These addi tional capabilities could provide a more comprehensive under standing of how firms can adapt and thrive in an AI-driven business environment.

Fourth, future research could investigate the role of leadership and organizational culture in developing DCs for AI adoption.

Understanding how top executives drive the development of sensing and seizing capabilities or how organizational culture supports or hinders AI adoption could provide valuable insights for firms seeking to develop a sustainable AI strategy.

Finally, future research could explore the impact of external col laborations, such as partnerships with technology vendors, academic institutions, and government bodies, on the DCs required for AI adoption. This would shed light on how firms can leverage external resources and knowledge to enhance their internal capabilities and accelerate AI implementation.

## 8. Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the adoption of AI systems in the telecommunications industry in China, focusing on the mediating role of DCs and the moderating influence of competitive pressure. It advances understanding of AI adoption by integrating the TOE frame work with DCs theory, providing a more comprehensive conceptual model that links external, organizational, and technological factors to operational performance outcomes. By identifying the key capabilities needed for AI implementation, this study offers both practical and theoretical contributions to the literature on AI adoption and DCs.

This study proposes a nomological network to examine the effect of DCs for AI system adoption on operational performance and the factors that influence such DCs. The findings provide a theoretical under standing of AI platforms for digital servitization and solution delivery, and guide managers on what to expect from the adoption of AI systems. Overall, the findings show that all of the tested environmental (competitive pressure), organizational (organizational readiness), and technological (expected benefits and technology compatibility) factors positively influence firms’ DCs for AI system adoption; competitive pressure positively moderates the relationship between technological factors and operational performance; and DCs positively influence operational performance.
