# Einstein, Heisenberg, Kant: methodological distinction and conditions of possibilities

Emmanuel Monod <sup></sup>

Georgia State University, USA

## Abstract

The concepts of objectivity and causality, as they are used in general in IS research, and in particular in critical realism, are no longer valid in Physics. Therefore, IS research should consider the ‘‘copernician revolution’’ that happened in Physics and in other sciences. Bhor, Heisenberg and Einstein themselves refer to Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason. The two fundamental concepts are therefore no more objectivity and causality, but the ‘‘methodological distinction’’ and the ‘‘conditions of possibilities’’. This kantian framework, commonly used by other human and social sciences, like sociology or history, indicates a way for an epistemological pluralism in IS research. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Published by Elsevier Ltd.

## 1. Introduction

Realists have been fighting against nominalists since at least the Middle-Ages. This dispute was known as the ‘‘Quarrel of the Universals’’ and happened especially between England and France. Indeed, if one of the most important spokesman for ‘‘realism’’ was Duns Scotus in England, some of the most known nominalists are Roscelin de Compie\`gne and, to some extent, Pierre Abe´lard, one of the founders of the Sorbonne University.<sup>1</sup> While nominalists thought that we should avoid creating concepts without necessity because they were just words without any necessary connection with existing entities, realists claimed that these entities really existed.

They called them ‘‘Universals’’ (Libe´ra, 1996). Even in the Middle-Ages, nothing was really new about this dispute. The origin of this belief in the existence of the Universals might be found in Plato who claimed that we are influenced by ‘‘ideas’’ existing independently from us. The platonician ‘‘conceptualists’’ (also called ‘‘idealists’’) were criticized by Epicurus who claimed that these so-called ‘‘ideas’’ had no other existence than being names describing the physical objects. Just as realists were challenged by nominalists, conceptualists were challenged by materialists.

In the 20-century, realism happened to have a new revival through the leading British figure of Roy Bhaskar. This neo-realism was variously termed ‘‘transcendental’’ or ‘‘critical realism’’. This paper is a research commentary about the stimulating paper of John Mingers about Critical Realism (CR) in the present issue of Information and Organization. In this paper, John Mingers claims that Critical Realism successfully addresses ‘‘unresolved problems’’ within the philosophy of science ‘‘whether it be natural or social’’ (Mingers, 2004). These ‘‘problems’’ are (1) the observer-dependence, (2) the ‘‘impoverished explanation in empiricism’’ (id.) and (3) the ‘‘dislocation between natural and social science’’ (id.). In order to simplify the vocabulary, we propose to name these three problems (1) objectivity (2) causality and (3) human sciences.

The first part will be devoted to the question of objectivity. It starts with the claim of Mingers that ‘‘natural phenomena are independent from our conceptions of them’’ (id.). The point is that this idea of objectivity belongs to the old out-of-date Physics of Newton and Galileo. It collapsed because of the Uncertainty Relations (Heisenberg, 1962) and the Principle of Complementarity (Bohr, 1963). Contemporary physics do not rely any more on objectivity (Einstein, 1990). The point is that Bohr (1963) referred to Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason. For Einstein: ‘‘This is a great thing accomplished by Kant to recognize that there would be no sense to posit an external world without its understandability’’ (Einstein, 1990: p. 22). This Critique of Pure Reason is known in epistemology for having established the ‘‘Copernician revolution’’ in all sciences, and not only Physics. Kant’s ‘‘Copernician Revolution’’ is a critique of objectivity in sciences.

Causality will be discussed in the second part. As this principle was also the second belief of the old out-of-date Newtonian Physics that collapsed in contemporary Physics, it will be also discussed in the first part. However, Critical Realism claims to solve the ‘‘weaknesses of explanation’’ in empiricism. Confronted with this ‘‘weakness’’, Critical Realism argues for a strong explanation that goes back to the causality of rationalism. Beyond the collapse of the causality in Physics, we will discuss how this problem of explanation was solved in the Critique of Pure Reason by Kant’s ‘‘conditions of possibility’’ that opened a way both for Physics and for Human and Social Sciences.

The third part will address the ‘‘dislocation between natural and social science’’ (Mingers, 2004) and the way Critical Realism addresses this question through a ‘‘critical naturalism in social science’’ (id.) i.e. a methodological universalism. We are here confronted with the unlimited claim of rationality which refuses to include senses and intuition. Against such an unwarranted claim of reason, Kant undertook a ‘‘critique of our power of reason’’ (Kant, 1781: p. 8). The Critique of Pure

Reason is a warning against the temptation of reason to venture beyond the boundaries of experience. Such misuse of reason is called antinomy: when reason ventures beyond the boundaries of experience. Faced with this issue, Kant proposed a methodological distinction.

## 2. From objectivity to the ‘‘Copernician revolution’’

The question of objectivity in natural sciences appears to be pretty much contradictory in Critical Realism argumentation. On the one hand, CR claims that ‘‘natural phenomena are independent from our conceptions of them’’ (objectivity) (Mingers, 2004), but on the other, it is said that this ‘‘subject dependence is also accepted within natural science such as physics with the Heisenberg uncertainty principle’’ (subjectivity) (id.). So is natural science observer-dependent or not? Is there only objectivity or also subjectivity? The least we can say is that contemporary physics cannot avoid the observer-dependence, i.e. include subjectivity. Therefore, if John Mingers were more consistent, he would not declare anymore that ‘‘natural phenomena are independent from our conception of them’’ (objectivity) (id.). Indeed, when Critical Realism recognizes that ‘‘subject dependence is also accepted in natural science (. . .) with the Heisenberg uncertainty principle’’ why not recognizing that the consequences are that, in Physics, there was a collapse of both objectivity and causality?

Let us remind the recent evolution of Physics. The old out-of-date Physics of Newton and Galileo relied especially on two principles: causality and objectivity. Causality is the belief on determination: in order to describe a phenomenon we must rely on the analysis of causes. This principle of causality leads to the explanation of a phenomenon, i.e. its prediction with certainty. Objectivity is the belief of the existence of an external reality that we could describe directly, without any reference to the observer. This means that there is one and only one single description of a phenomenon and that this description corresponds to the phenomenon. This correspondence is the criteria of truth. There is a sharp separation between the world and the self, between the object and the subject, between objectivity and subjectivity.

This old out-of-date Physics has been replaced between the two World Wars by the contemporary Physics, also called ‘‘Quantum Physics’’. The two main authors of this new Physics are Heisenberg (1962) and Niels Bohr (1963). In the contemporary Physics, objectivity and causality have been replaced by Uncertainty Relations and the Principle of Complementarity. As it was written by Einstein: ‘‘It is not likely that a future knowledge might constrain physics to leave its present day theoretical foundations, of statistical nature, in favour of deterministic foundations, that would deal directly with physical reality’’ (Einstein, 1990: p. 95). The ‘‘statistical nature’’ of the ‘‘present day theoretical foundations’’ of Physics is the end of determinism, i.e. causality. The fact that we cannot ‘‘deal directly with physical reality’’ is the end of objectivity.

## 2.1. Causality and objectivity in Physics

In order to present more clearly the Uncertainty Relations and the Principle of Complementarity in Physics, the sections of this part will describe the collapse of the two basic principles of the old Newtonian Physics: causality and objectivity.

## 2.1.1. Causality

Causality collapsed in Physics because of ‘‘Uncertainty Relations’’ of Heisenberg. Einstein declares ‘‘As was successfully proved by Heisenberg, any decision relative to a deterministic structure of nature is definitively excluded’’ (Einstein, 1990: p. 95). In the old out-of-date Newtonian Physics, when we knew the position and the speed of a particle, we could predict (explain) its trajectory, its ‘‘behavior’’ through causal (deterministic) laws. This principle of causality relies on the assumption that we can measure in the same time the position and the speed of a particle. This assumption is wrong. It is impossible to have in the same time an exact measurement of the position and of the speed of a particle. Or, to be more precise, the more accurate is our knowledge of its position, the more uncertain is our knowledge of its speed (or reciprocally). This is called the Uncertainty Relations proved by Heisenberg: ‘‘The knowledge of the position of a particle is complementary to the knowledge of its speed. If we know the one with high accuracy we cannot know the other with high accuracy; yet, we must know both in order to determine the behavior of the system’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 37). The consequence is that ‘‘No causal law can be proved any more, but only statistical laws’’ (Einstein, 1990: p. 59). Or, quoting the Principle of Complementarity of Niels Bohr: ‘‘Any move back to a mode of description compatible with the principle of causality is excluded’’ (Bohr, 1963: p. 173). If we cannot explain, predict, or ‘‘determine the behavior of the system’’, there is no room left for an out-of-date principle in Physics: causality.

## 2.1.2. Objectivity

We said previously that objectivity in the old out-of-date Newtonian Physics relied on the belief that there is one and only one single description of a phenomenon and that this description is true because it corresponds to the phenomenon. This unique correspondence between the phenomenon and its description collapsed with the Principle of Complementarity of Nobel Price Neils Bohr (1963). The biggest problem of Newtonian Physics was the nature of a strange phenomenon: light. Everything in the world was supposed to be made of only two types of entities: matter or energy. In order to be consistent with objectivity, light was considered as energy. Neils Bohr destroyed this principle of objectivity when he showed that light was in the same time energy and matter. And the worst was when this duality between energy and matter (between a wave<sup>2</sup> and a corpuscle<sup>3</sup>) was depending on the means of observation. And each of these descriptions, or picture of light, was only partially true. According to Heisenberg, ‘‘Bohr considered the two pictures— particle picture and wave picture—as two complementary descriptions (. . .). Any of these descriptions can be only partially true’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 31). ‘‘The study of complementary phenomena implies experimental devices that exclude one another reciprocally’’ (Bohr, 1963: p. 209). However, Critical Realism tries to make us believe that statistical probabilities are laws when we read: ‘‘the laws might only be expressed in terms of statistical probabilities’’ (Mingers, 2004). But what is a law? A law also relies on the assumption of causality and objectivity. We already discussed the collapse of causality in contemporary Physics. How about objectivity? We just said that there is not one, but two or maybe sometimes more description of a phenomenon. But there is more: probability is not a law. It is simply a formalization of our subjectivity. A probability is simply a way to say ‘‘we don’t know’’. As Heisenberg says: ‘‘The probability function (. . .) contains statements about possibilities (. . .) and statements about our knowledge of the system, which of course are subjective in so far as they may be diferent for diferent observers’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 41). On the one hand, we have our subjectivity. On the other, only ‘‘possibilities’’, i.e.: not facts. Therefore, where is the world of events of Critical Realism? Where is the objective world of generative mechanisms of Critical Realism? Heisenberg’s answer is clear: ‘‘We cannot completely objectify the result of an observation (. . .). It looks as if we had introduced an element of subjectivism into the theory, as if we meant to say: what happens depends on the way of observing it or on the fact we observe it’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 38). Like a ghost in the machine, the element of ‘‘subjectivity into the theory’’ is so high that we cannot exclude the possibility that we invent what we measure.

## 2.2. The ‘‘Copernician revolution’’ of the Critique of Pure Reason

The ‘‘Copernician revolution’’ of Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason has been recognized as one of the most important contributions to the philosophy of science by major scientists (Bohr, 1963; Einstein, 1990; Heisenberg, 1962; Husserl, 1936; Piaget, 1967; Popper, 1972). According to Kant, a revolution is ‘‘a transformation of our way of thinking’’ (Kant, 1781: p. 21). His Critique of Pure Reason might be compared to the Copernician revolution in Astronomy because: ‘‘The situation here is the same as was that of Copernicus when he first thought of explaining the motions of celestial bodies’’ (Kant, p. 21). In order to reconcile the never-ending debates between empiricists and rationalists, on the one hand, and materialists and conceptualists, on the other hand, Kant criticizes the ‘‘power of reason’’. Before Kant, most of these conflicting trends in philosophy of science originate in the belief that our intuition (or perception) must conform to the object that we observe. According to Descartes, for instance, the objects are real objects which exist in the world independently from the observer, who observes them with his intuition (perception) that is subjective. The object of research is an ‘‘external object’’ without the need to make reference to ourselves. This is also the assumption of the out-of-date classical physics of Galileo and Newton, before the revolution of the contemporary quantum physics: ‘‘In classical physics science started from the belief—or should one say from the illusion?—that we could describe the world without any reference to ourselves’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 43).

But what is an object? Especially what is an object of research<sup>4</sup> like an organization, an information system or a corporate culture? Or in physics the Big Bang, gravitation or evolution of the Universe? ‘‘The Critique is not in error when it teaches us to take the object in two diferent senses: as appearances and as thing in itself’’ (Kant, p. 29) ‘‘we distinguish this object as appearance from the same object as object in itself’’ (p. 101). Kant is going to define two aspects of the object: on the one hand ‘‘things in themselves’’ (noumena), independent from the observer, and on the other hand, phenomena. The word phenomenon is derived from the Greek ‘‘phainomenon’’ signifying appearance. Phenomena are appearances. Here is the ‘‘Copernician Revolution’’ of Kant. The phenomenon is located within our intuition and leaves the ‘‘real’’ object unknown. Let us consider the definition of phenomena: ‘‘Appearances are called by us ‘‘beings of senses’’ (phenomena)<sup>5</sup> because we distinguish the way in which we intuit them from the character that they have in themselves’’ (p. 316). Phenomena are ‘‘being of senses’’, appearances, laying in the intuition of the observer. ‘‘Things in themselves’’, opposed to phenomena, are unknowable: ‘‘Our rational cognition applies only to appearances and leaves the thing in itself uncognized to us’’ (Kant, p. 24). Quoting Nobel price Niels Bohr: ‘‘It is impossible to make a clear distinction between the behavior of the objects and the interaction with measurement instruments that are used to define the conditions under which the phenomenon appears’’ (Bohr, 1963: p. 207).

If we call objects ‘‘phenomenons’’, we therefore accept that we are only talking about appearances, and that these appearances are the domain of our intuition: ‘‘objects are appearances that conform to our way of presenting’’ (Kant, p. 24) ‘‘the object (as object of the senses) conforms to the character of our power of intuition’ (p. 21) ‘‘being appearances (phenomenon) cannot exist in themselves, but can exist only in us. What may be the case regarding objects in themselves and apart from all this receptivity of our sensibility remains to us entirely unknown. All we know is the way in which we perceive them’’ (p. 94). May be one of the most well-known sentence of Kant’s ‘‘Copernician revolution’’ is ‘‘we all cognize a priori about things what we ourselves put into them’’ (p. 23).

More radically, if there is no observer, the phenomenon cannot be, and the object disappears, precisely because it appears only within the intuition of the observer: ‘‘And if we annul ourselves as subject, or even annul only the subjective character of the senses generally, then this entire character of objects and all their relations (. . .)—would vanish’’ (Kant, p. 94). Contemporary Physics came to the same conclusion: ‘‘Nature is earlier than man, but man is earlier than natural science’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 44). Any phenomenon in physics, such as big bang, gravitation, evolution, are not Nature but discourses about Nature.

This is exactly the opposite in Critical Realism with its ‘‘stratified ontology’’ that claims that: ‘‘science is about objects, entities and structures that exist (even perhaps unobservable) and generate the events that we observe’’. Moreover, when one reads the Critical Realism definition of phenomena (‘‘events that are experienced and agreed on by both sides in the argument’’), the least we can say is that little attention is paid to either contemporary physics or the Copernician revolution in epistemology. It seems that we are here in the situation of the out-of-date classical physics that still ‘‘starts from the belief—or should one say from the illusion?—that we could describe the world without any reference to ourselves’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 43) as Galileo did.

## 3. From causality to the conditions of possibility

The second problem according to Critical Realism is the impoverishment of explanation through induction and falsification. John Mingers also declares that positivism is ‘‘more generally’’ empiricism.

## 3.1. Explanation in Critical Realism

According to Critical Realism, the impoverishment of explanation is due to the double reduction that empiricism operates: from laws to events and from events to experience. Explanation is poor because it consists ‘‘of the logical deduction of particular events given some antecedent conditions and a set of law’’. . . .‘‘It was accepted that the laws might only be expressed in terms of statistical probabilities and that they could not be proved to be true inductively’’ (Mingers, 2004). Such situation can be explained by the influence of Hume’s scepticism concerning anything not directly observable leading to debates about the existence of ‘‘theoretical entities’’, i.e. the structures claimed by critical realism that cannot be observed directly. Ontologically, the criterion for accepting the existence of something is its perceptibility.

By contrast, CR claims to address successfully this impoverishment problem through the existence of ‘‘intransitive domains’’ of events ‘‘that are independent from our perceptions of them’’ (Mingers, 2004). The ‘‘causal criterion of existence’’ is opposed by the author to the ‘‘perceptual criterion of existence’’. It claims that, despite the fact that we often cannot observe some entities, they however ‘‘have a causal efect on the world’’ and this is suficient to imply that they exist. ‘‘Intransitive’’ domains are the realm of ‘‘enduring entities’’ that ‘‘generate the events that we observe (even though perhaps unobservable)’’ (id.). They may be ‘‘objects, forces, social structures, or ideas’’ (id.). However, as John Mingers says, ‘‘we can gain reliable knowledge of them’’ (id.).

## 3.2. Critical Realism is a rationalism

In general epistemology, positivism is not included in a more general category that would be empiricism. Rather, under the general category of positivism, there are at least three epistemological trends: empiricism, functionalism and rationalism. Indeed, the two conflicting origins of knowledge throughout the modern history of science have been reason and experience. The ‘‘empiricists’’ with John Locke ‘‘derive all concepts from experience’’ (Kant, 1781: p. 773). In this perspective, knowledge is limited to what may be tested by empirical observation. What may be known a priori, independently from experience, is restricted to analytical statements (often tautologies).

Rationalists, with Leibniz, believed that the concepts only came from reason ‘‘independently from experience’’ (Kant, 1781: p. 772). In this second perspective, knowledge comes from reason alone, as opposed to sense-experience. Everything can be known. All knowledge can be expressed in the form of a system. Critical realism is indeed opposed to sense-experience when it argues the existence of ‘‘intransitive domain’’ of events that ‘‘are independent from our perceptions of them’ (Mingers, 2004). Especially, the claim that ‘‘the world would exist whether or not human did’’ (id.) is clearly a rational one. The CR ‘‘causal criterion of existence’’ is opposed to the ‘‘perceptual criterion of existence’’, which is another way to show the opposition to empiricism. This causal criterion claiming that some entities ‘‘have a causal efect on the world’’ (id.) and that this is suficient to imply that they exist is a pure rationalist argument. It refers to Leibniz’s ‘‘principle of reason’’: every event in the world has a cause.

Functionalism is a development of rationalism that had a strong development in sociology (Talcott Parsons, 1966). This perspective relies on the biological analogy. Societies (and organizations) are seen as an organic whole. Each constituent part works to maintain the other in an organic solidarity. Even if critical realism is much more mechanistic than organic, a functionalist aspect may be found in the ‘‘second form of stratification’’ (Mingers, 2004) where one causal power at one level (e.g. chemical reactions) can be seen as generated by those of a lower level (atomic valency). This ideal world without conflict is just working (functioning) like a human body.

## 3.3. The conditions of possibility

We already described how the basic idea of rationalism, i.e. causality, collapsed in contemporary physics because of the Uncertainty Principle of Heisenberg and the Complementarity Principle of Niels Bohr. The point is that, in order to solve the enigma of the nature of light and to establish his Principle of Complementarity, Bohr referred to Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason (Folse, 1978). Critical Realism also refers to Kant through the ‘‘transcendental argument’’. Indeed, CR relies on a ‘‘transcendental argument’’ that ‘‘begins with some accepted phenomenon and asks what must the world be like for this to occur’’ (Mingers, 2004). Although recognizing that the term ‘‘transcendental’’ is based on Kant, CR calls Kant transcendentalism a ‘‘transcendental idealism’’. Kant is supposed to search for the ‘‘universal conditions of the possibility of knowledge at all, namely that our minds structure our experience of reality in terms of space, time and causality’’ (id.). Therefore, such ‘‘knowledge’’ is ‘‘universal and a-historical’’ (id.). In contrast, critical realism asserts that conditions of knowledge do not arise in our mind but in the structure of reality. This is a misunderstanding of transcendentalism.

First, transcendentalism is not about a ‘‘knowledge universal and ahistorical’’ (id.). Let us see how Kant defines it: ‘‘I call transcendental all cognition that deals not so much with objects as rather with our way of cognizing objects in general’’ (Kant, 1781: p. 64) ‘‘What here constitutes the object (of our inquiry) is not the nature of things, which is inexhaustible, but the understanding that makes judgments about the things’’ (Kant, p. 65). Therefore, Kant’s transcendentalism is not about a ‘‘knowledge universal and a-historical’’, but conversely about an inquiry asking what are the possibilities and the conditions of knowledge, i.e. its limitations. This inquiry about limitations might very well end with the conclusion of a contingency knowledge that is limited to a historical context.

As a matter of fact, the conditions of possibility of knowledge are not ‘‘space, time and causality’’ (Mingers, 2004), but the fact that we must question our apprehension of space and time if we want to be aware of what happens during intuition of phenomena. The second point is that the third condition of possibility is not causality. Just as any intuition of phenomenon needs to question the apprehension of time and space, the explanation of these phenomena must question the analytical categories on which such an explanation relies. The important point is that these categories are not limited to causality. ‘‘If we abstract all content of a judgment as such and pay attention only to the mere form’’ (Kant, p. 130), therefore there are four headings: quantity, quality, relation and modality. Regarding to their quantity, judgments can be universal, particular or singular. As to their quality, they might be afirmative, negative or infinite. Coming to their relation, they may be based on causality like the hypothetical judgment, but also on inherence or on community (‘‘interaction between agent and patient’’, p. 132). Finally, modality might be based on necessity (apodictic) but also on existence (assertotic) or on possibility (problematic). Therefore, the conditions of possibility are much more than causality only (Table 2).

Coming now to the ‘‘transcendental argument’’ of Critical Realism, one could argue that there is nothing transcendental in an approach that ‘‘begins with some accepted phenomenon and asks what must the world be like for this to occur’’. Especially when CR begins with ‘‘some accepted phenomenon’’ this implies that the conditions of possibility of intuition are not challenged. And when the analytical categories are simplified into the single causality, how can one call this a discussion? This is not a transcendental argument. Indeed, it does not relies on intuition or on categories. Moreover, because we know that for CR, the ‘‘world’’ is essentially causal laws, asking ‘‘what must the world be like’’ is simply asking ‘‘what is the hidden causal law behind this phenomenon’’. Therefore, Critical Realism fails to grasp the native meaning of transcendentalism and clings to its rationalist tenets. Transcendentalism brought by Kant is about ‘‘The eternal mystery of the world: its understandability’’ (Einstein, 1990: p. 22).

This lack of reference is disappointing because the author claims to be in search of some pluralism. The point is that Kant referred to Transcendentalism precisely to overcome the opposition between empiricism and rationalism that will allow, as we will see later, a pluralism in epistemology: ‘‘There is no doubt that all our cognitions start with experience, which does not mean that all of it arises from experience<sup>6</sup>’’ (Kant, p. 44). In other words, there must be categories that are a priori, i.e. independent of experience and which create the ‘‘conditions of possibilities for the experience’’ (Kant, p. 44). These ‘‘conditions of possibilities’’ are what Kant calls ‘‘transcendental’’. Among the diferent trends in epistemology, the most advanced inquiry about these conditions of possibilities of concepts and intuitions in the Kantian tradition is Husserl’s phenomenology (Husserl, 1936) (Table 3).

## 4.1. Two worlds, one method?

Critical Realism is based on ‘‘critical naturalism in social science’’ (Mingers, 2004) because ‘‘social science is essentially similar to natural science in its realist character’’ (id.). There should be one single universal method: a methodological universalism. But on the other hand, the social world has a ‘‘particular nature’’. Even if there are ‘‘intransitive social structures’’ (id.) that ‘‘govern activities’’ (id.), these ‘‘Social structures do not exist independently of the agents’ conceptions of what they are doing’’ (id.). Critical Realism also recognizes Giddens’ ‘‘double hermeneutic’’ principle, arguing ‘‘objects of social science are already socially structured whereas those of natural science are not’’ (id.). Therefore, are there one or two worlds? If the social world has a ‘‘particular nature’’, if social structures are agent dependent and if they are socially structured, can we not conclude that there are two worlds: one natural and one social? This would entail one single method for two worlds: a methodological universalism as opposed to an ontological dualism.

Critical Realism is here reproducing the same extreme positioning as positivism in sociology. The acknowledged founder of positivism, Auguste Comte (1995), was also the creator of the word ‘‘sociology’’ based on the combination of the words ‘‘social physics’’. His intention was a modeling of the methods of social science on those of natural science. He was systematically criticized by Wilhem Dilthey (1883), the founder of human sciences, who advocated for an alternative method. The same opposition can be found between the follower of Auguste Comte, Emile Dukheim who, against Max Weber (1965), argued for a ‘‘methodological individualism’’ that included motives and intentions as causal agents. Indeed, Durkheim (1968) in his Rules of the sociological method argued to ‘‘consider social facts as things’’. Therefore, according to Durkheim, motives and intentions of causal agents had to be rejected in favor of social structures for the establishment of laws of the causal relations of social phenomena, just like Critical Realism refuses to include in explanations individuals’ beliefs and actions.

## 4.2. An antinomy

Kant (1781) built his Critique of Pure Reason as a critique of the misuse of reason. This is a critique of the claim of rationality to cover every kind of topics without asking what are the boundaries of this knowledge. Without such questioning, reason is subject to antinomy, i.e. ‘‘when it ventures beyond the boundaries of experience’’ (Kant, p. 6). Like the historical positivism, Critical Realism rejects consciousness, cultural norms, symbolic meaning and intentionality. These are however distinctive human attributes that strongly suggest a diference between natural science and the study of human social life. This rejection led to an instrumental view of science that has been extensively criticized (Habermas, 1984). Nowadays, if we consider now the status of the sociological or historical research, the situation is no longer that of a single positivist mainstream arguing that structures are the sole worthwhile object of research just like in the 19th century. There are now various trends ranging from the sociology of action (Touraine, 1965) to interpretive sociology, comprehensive sociology (Weber, 1965), symbolic interactionnism (Mead, 1962), phenomenological sociology, ethnomethodology, political interactionnism (Crozier and Friedberg, 1977) or hermeneutics (Gadamer, 1975). In all these trends, meaning and action, unlike structures, are regarded as the prime object of research of sociology.

Moreover, by postulating some unobservable structures or mechanisms that generate social phenomena, Critical Realism is not laying its theory open to experience. In this respect, it is comparable to Marxism and Freudism. These two perspectives both claim to explain and predict, which is the basis of their claims to scientificity. However, neither the ‘‘capitalist mode of production’’ in Marxism (Marx, 1973) or the ‘‘unconscious’’ in Freudism (Freud, 1992) can be proved. For each phenomenon ‘‘explained’’ by Marxism or Freudism, there are many other approaches. For instance, against Marx, alternative approaches were proposed, such as Ricardo’s ‘‘value-utility’’ or Schumpeter’s ‘‘creative destruction’’. Instead of Freud’s theory of unconscious, Sartre (1963) proposed to use the existentialist concept of mauvaise foi (‘‘bad faith’’) and Heidegger (1962) the phenomenological concept of sorge (‘‘caring for the others’’).

Indeed, Critical Realism depicts the mysterious world of ‘‘generative mechanisms’’ as ‘‘a complex interaction between dynamic, open, stratified systems, both material and non-material, where particular structures give rise to certain causal powers, tendencies, or ways of acting. The interaction of these generative mechanisms (. . .) causes the presence or absence of actual events’’ (Mingers, 2004). As is the case for the ‘‘capitalist mode of production’’ or the ‘‘unconscious’’, there is no way to validate or invalidate the existence of this world of generative mechanisms. Critical Realism, like Marxism and Freudism, appears in a state of antinomy.

## 4.3. Concepts and intuitions

This debate refers to another century-old debate in epistemology that is maybe more ancient than the rationalist/empiricist opposition about the origin of knowledge. This second debate forms the object of knowledge. For centuries, ‘‘Materialism’’ has been opposed to ‘‘conceptualism’’, also called ‘‘intellectualism’’ or ‘‘idealism’’. Conceptualists, led by Plato, claimed that ‘‘in the senses, there is nothing but illusion, (therefore) only understanding cognizes the true’’ (Kant, p. 772). Materialists, led by Epicurus, ‘‘granted intellectual concepts, but assumed only sensible objects’’ (Kant, p. 772).

What is the situation in human and social sciences? The most ancient human science, history, was originally developed on historiography, i.e. the story of individual ‘‘great men’’ that usually were supposed to have a rational intention (Droysen, 1858). However, in his Critique of Historical Reason, Dilthey (1883), proposed to define the human sciences as the sciences of understanding, in opposition of the sciences of explanation that were the natural sciences. The advantage of this approach is to include intuitions, consciousness, cultural norms, symbolic meaning and intentionality while proposing a non-positive approach to experience. The weakness is that Dilthey contrasted the natural sciences and social sciences in terms of their subject-matter. This criterion followed logically from the alternative premise that reality can be divided into autonomous sectors—a fundamental distinction being yet that between the realm of nature and the realm of spirit, each sector is the prerogative of a separate category of science. Gadamer (1975, 1976) and Heidegger (1962) followed that claim that the human world is totally diferent from the natural world. There we stand in a new configuration: the two worlds, two methods configuration (Table 4).

## 4.4. The methodological distinction

We have seen in the previous part that Kant proposed a way to overcome the opposition between experience and reason through the ‘‘conditions of possibility’’. In the same book, the Critique of Pure Reason, he also indicated a way to overcome the confrontation between materialism and conceptualism. This he achieved through the ‘‘methodological distinction of representations’’ (Kant, p. 29). All objects must be considered simultaneously in ‘‘two points of view’’ (p. 23). This, Kant explains, originates in the fact that ‘‘Human cognition has two stems, intuition and concepts. Through intuitions objects are given to us. Through concepts they are thought’’ (p. 67). ‘‘Intuition is our ability to receive representations (our receptivity for impressions). Concepts are our ability to cognize an object through these representations (the spontaneity of concepts)’’ (p. 106). The point is that these two cognitions are independent (Table 5).

The approach which developed this methodological distinction is general hermeneutics (Schleiermacher, 1810): there is one world, and two methods.

## 4.5. One world, two methods

Indeed, the basic principle of General Hermeneutics (Schleiermacher, 1810), also called ‘‘theory of interpretation’’ is that ‘‘understanding (a text) always involves two (interpretations): to understand what is said in the context of the language with its possibilities, and to understand it as a fact in the thinking of the speaker’’ (x II.5). These two interpretations are called ‘‘grammatical’’ and ‘‘psychological’’. The grammatical interpretation ‘‘considers the (text) as a product of language’’ (IX. 1). The psychological interpretation considers ‘‘how (the text), as a fact in the person’s mind, has emerged’’ (IX.2). This principle is consistent with the Critique of Pure Reason because it considers the same object, i.e. a text, with two points of view: as a phenomenon determined by the context, and as an object of thought resulting from the freedom of the speaker.

This methodological pluralism was followed by Windelband (1915), who, arguing from the premises that reality is indivisible, proposed an a priori logical distinction between natural and social sciences on the basis of their methods. Natural sciences, according to Windelband, use a ‘‘nomothetic’’ or generalizing method, since they seek to discover law-like and general relationships and properties, whereas social sciences employ an ideographic or individualizing procedure, since they are interested in the non-recurring events in reality and the particular or unique aspect of an event. Windelband explicitely refers to Kant’s methodological distinction. The point is that this epistemological pluralism is used in sociology (Crozier and Friedberg, 1977; Touraine, 1965), in history (Bourde´ and Martin, 1997) and in historical approaches in IS (Mason, Mc Kenney, and Copeland, 1997).

## 5. Conclusion: are problems invented in order to impose solutions?

A classical organizational behavior is to invent problems in order to impose one’s own solutions (Allison, 1971; Cohen, March, and Olson, 1972). It seems that this applies for each so-called ‘‘problems’’ that Critical realism claims to ‘‘address successfully’’: (1) observer-dependence, (2) impoverished explanation and (3) the ‘‘dislocation between natural and social science’’.

Observer-dependence is not a problem but a characteristic of contemporary physics. ‘‘It is impossible to come back to the idea of an objective real world whose smallest parts exist objectively in the same sense as stones or trees exist, independently of whether or not we observe them’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 117). Contemporary physics is against realism.

Therefore, one can regret that explanation is impoverished and might feel tempted to reach for a pure causality. But, in contemporary physics,‘‘The interaction between the object and the measurement instruments implies the necessity to renounce definitively the ideal of causality’’ (Bohr, 1935: p. 701). The new approach to physics is statistical, and this approach is against the ‘‘ideal’’ of causality. Therefore, physics is against all concepts of realism: from ‘‘enduring entities’’ (Mingers, 2004)

to ‘‘generative mechanisms’’ (id.). Once again, the ‘‘impoverishment of explanation’ (id.) is not a problem but a characteristic of contemporary physics.

Maybe human and social sciences are the last refuge where rationalists like critical realists can venture that ignorance of the developments of Physics will enable them to promote an out-of-date Galileism. The third problem, the ‘‘dislocation between social and natural sciences’’ (id.) is once again invented. Instead of the ‘‘ideal’’ of trying to impose an out-of-date Galileist rationality in the social world, most of the contemporary trends of sociology (Crozier and Friedberg, 1977; Mead, 1962; Touraine, 1965; Weber, 1965) include meaning and action, and not only structures, as the prime object of research of sociology. In that respect, they follow explicitly (Touraine, 1965; Weber, 1965) the nomothetic/ideographic distinction (Windelband, 1915) and the methodological distinction of Kant (1781). This is also the case in history (Bourde´ and Martin, 1997) and in historical approaches in IS (Mason, Mc Kenney, and Copeland, 1997).

Therefore, why try to hold so strongly to rationalism when the reference discipline, Physics, has abandoned it and when human and social sciences did everything they could to emancipate from it? Just like old classical physics refused the evidences brought up by contemporary physics: ‘‘There is nothing surprising in the fact that Newton refused to hear anything about a wave theory of light, because such a theory did not match with his theory (. . .) We understand that he clang to a corpuscular theory of light’’ (Einstein, 1990: p. 81). If CR ‘‘refuses to hear anything’’ we might have to leave the word to Heisenberg: The position to which the Cartesian partition has led (The world, i.e., the extended thing, ‘‘exist’’) was what one may call metaphysical realism’’ (Heisenberg, 1962: p. 69). Indeed, following Kant’s definition of metaphysics, this situation happens when we resort to principles that go beyond all possible use in experience’’ (Kant, 1781: p. 6). In order to avoid it, we saw how phenomenology and hermeneutics proposed non-empiricists approaches to experience, especially through Kant’s ‘‘conditions of possibility’’ and ‘‘methodological distinction’’.

However, we hope that Critical Realism will remember its other ideal: methodological pluralism, and therefore will recognize that contemporary physics and many trends of human and social sciences followed the way opened by the Critique of Pure Reason: epistemological pluralism.
