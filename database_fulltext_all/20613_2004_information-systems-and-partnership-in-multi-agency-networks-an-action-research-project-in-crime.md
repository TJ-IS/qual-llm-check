# Information systems and partnership in multi-agency networks: an action research project in crime reduction

David Wastell <sup>a,</sup>, Peter Kawalek <sup>b</sup>, Peter Langmead-Jones <sup>c</sup>, Rob Ormerod <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Computation, University of Manchester Institute of Science and Technology, Sackville Street, Manchester M60 1QD, UK

<sup>b</sup> Manchester Business School, Oxford Road, Manchester M15 6PB, UK <sup>c</sup> Corporate Development, Lancashire Constabulary, P.O. Box 77, Preston PR4 5SB, UK <sup>d</sup> Lancashire County Council, P.O. Box 78, County Hall, Preston PR1 8XJ, UK

## Abstract

Partnership working is becoming an increasingly common methodology in the public sector for addressing complex social issues such as poverty, economic development and crime. Information systems have a vital role to play in enabling such inter-organisational networks and in facilitating the multi-disciplinary collaboration that is essential to joint working. This paper reports a UK-based action research project in the field of community safety. The practical aim was to develop a multi-agency data exchange (MADE) to support 14 crime reduction partnerships in the county of Lancashire in the preparation of their crime reduction strategies, as required by recent government legislation. From a research perspective, the project aforded an opportunity to explore the role of information systems in supporting public sector partnerships, and hence to open up a relatively unresearched element of the broader eGovernment agenda. From a practitioner perspective, MADE has been highly successful; it has provided substantial help to partnerships in the production of their strategies and there is strong demand for the project’s continuation. On the research side, the work has provided valuable insights into the design and development of information systems to support multi-agency collaboration and on the barriers to information sharing. The concept of a ‘‘partnership decision support system’’ emerges from the work, and a number of profitable directions for future research are adumbrated. Methodologically, it is concluded that action research is a valuable research tool for developing relevant and holistic theory in IS. <sup>#</sup> 2004 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Information systems; Action research; Partnership; Crime reduction; Networked organisations; eGovernment; Process consultation; Organisational learning

## 1. Introduction: public sector partnership

The potential of IT to enable new organisational forms is evidenced in a burgeoning research discourse on diverse themes such as e-commerce (Laudon & Laudon, 2000), virtual organisations (Dhillon & Hackney, 2003; Venkatraman & Henderson, 1998) and inter-organisational information systems (Leverick & Cooper, 1998; Mutch, 1996). The transcendence of geographical space aforded by IT facilitates the rapid sharing of information upon which such novel, distributed arrangements critically depend. This paper is concerned with the potential of IT to support inter-organisational collaboration. Interest in partnership infuses commercial domains as wide-ranging as manufacturing (D’Amours, Montreuil, LeFrancois, & Soumis, 1999; Leverick & Cooper, 1998), private health care (Payton, 2002; Pouloudi, 1999) and tourism (Mutch, 1996). It is also increasingly manifest in the non-commercial world, with widespread acceptance taking hold that efective public service delivery enjoins collaboration between agencies rather than bureaucratic monoliths operating in ‘‘splendid isolation’’ (Balloch & Taylor, 2001). The need for partnership working is rapidly emerging in all areas of public sector endeavour (e.g. urban regeneration, health and social care) in the UK and elsewhere, and is one of the main themes in the ubiquitous rhetoric to modernise government and to reform public services (Stoker, 1999; Taylor, 2001).

Information systems have a key role to play in enabling such innovative arrangements. This is explicitly articulated in the imperatives of the eGovernment discourse to use IT to break down functional silos and integrate (‘‘join-up’’) service delivery within and across agencies (Wastell, Kawalek, & Newman, 2003). Pratchett (1999) emphasises the pivotal role of information systems in enabling coordinated policy development and service delivery in an increasingly chaotic milieu made up of multiple private, public and voluntary sector agencies. By providing timely and accurate information, such systems enable local needs and social contexts to be understood, and provide the technological infrastructure without which the efective governance of multi-agency networks would not be feasible. By helping build a ‘‘concrete pattern of bridges, links and flows of communication’’, information systems can help bring meaning and coherence to the fragmentation and disorder that could otherwise follow the dismantling of traditional institutions (Trevillon, 2001). Signoretta and Craglia (2002) have also emphasised the ‘‘crucial role’’ accorded to IS in the partnership paradigm, and in the general putsch towards ‘‘joined up government’’ in the UK. In the Canadian context, Allen, Juillet, Paquet, and Roy (2001) discuss the issues raised in terms of public accountability, governance and internal resistance to change provoked by the shift from traditional bureaucratic structures to the ‘‘horizontal’’ delivery arrangements of the partnership model.

The UK policy background regarding partnership can be traced back to the incoming Labour government of 1997 which instigated a range of national initiatives (Balloch & Taylor, 2001) all premised on joint working between disparate government departments and other public/voluntary sector agencies. The ideological roots of this paradigm change are controversial (Taylor, 2001). Crow and Longford (2000) deliver a trenchant critique of the pro-capital, neo-liberal agenda underlying much of the impetus to modernise the public sector, with Goris and Walters (1999) elaborating this polemic in the specific context of community partnership for crime prevention. In redefining the boundaries of the state through the involvement of a broader range of agencies (including the private sector), there are obvious similarities with the reforms of the preceding Thatcher era in the UK (Bevir & Rhodes, 2001), with its championing of the private sector, the need for cost-eficiency and the idolisation of the market. There are clear ideological diferences, though. Whereas the ‘‘New Right’’ was firmly committed to the superior eficiency of markets and the private sector, the reforms of ‘‘New Labour’’ arguably derive from a diferent tradition drawing on socialist precepts. Of course, value for money is important, but the ideology underlying current reforms is ostensibly aimed at improving services by developing collaborative solutions to complex problems, involving networks of specialist organisations (Bevir & Rhodes, 2001). The objectives of contemporary partnership practice appear to be ethically laudable, aiming at improving social and welfare outcomes as well as increasing eficiency and public involvement (Trevillon, 2001).

The partnership approach has been extensively deployed in the area of crime reduction (Goris & Walters, 1999; Ward, 1997), the application domain featured in this paper. Kemshall and Ross (2000) portray the inception of partnership as a shift away from the traditional view of crime as primarily a policing issue, to a broader perspective emphasising community-based, strategic interventions addressing the underlying causes of crime and its management. In the UK, the new Labour administration introduced legislation early in its first term to give efect to the partnership approach, with the Crime and Disorder Act (1988) placing a statutory duty on local authorities, the police and other agencies to work together to tackle crime and disorder within each local government district. Crime and disorder reduction partnerships (CDRPs) were set up. Their first task was to carry out formal ‘‘audits’’ of the distribution of crime and anti-social behaviour within their areas, with this audit to be used as the basis of local crime reduction strategies involving multi-agency collaboration and intervention. The first round of audits was performed in 1998, with the process of audit and strategic planning to be repeated every 3 years, meaning that preparations for the second audit were beginning to take shape in 2000, with audits due in 2001 and strategies by April 2002.

In the autumn of 2000, the two academic co-authors were invited to take part in a project to set up a multi-agency information system to inform the upcoming crime audits in the county of Lancashire. We were approached because of previous collaborative links and also our growing reputation locally for applied eGovernment research on the re-engineering of public services. We agreed to undertake the work as it aforded an inviting opportunity to investigate at close hand the design and implementation of multi-agency information systems, to address the success factors bearing on the development of such systems, to examine the role of IS in supporting efective inter-agency partnership and to explore the problems to be encountered around key areas such as information sharing.

The project was, however, a daunting one. The county is a large and relatively populous one (1.42 million in 1998) comprising 14 local authority districts, ranging across the socio-demographic spectrum from densely populated urban areas (often blighted by high levels of deprivation and attendant social problems) to more prosperous, geographically difuse rural districts. Not only was it required to support all 14 CDRPs in the production of their audits but data from at least six county level data providers (as well as other published datasets) had to be collected, processed and disseminated. The resources available to carry out the work were limited. The project team comprised two academic researchers and representatives from the two lead agencies (Lancashire Constabulary and Lancashire County Council). The academics conducted the work in their research time; the practitioners had to carry out their roles as an adjunct to already congested work schedules. Despite these challenges, the project has been a success, and the purpose of this paper is to recount the history of the work and to report on the theoretical insights it has generated.

## 2. Methodology

The project is representative of a mode of enquiry known as action research (Reason & Bradbury, 2001). Given its highly applied nature, the discipline of IS (Avison, Lau, Neilsen, & Myers, 1999; Lau, 1999) has long been seen as a natural application domain for action research (AR). Greenwood and Levin (1998) define AR as social research carried out by a team of professional researchers in collaboration with members of an organisation or community, the aim being to support action leading to a more just or satisfying situation for the stakeholders. AR is characterised by three ‘‘commitments’’ shared by its votaries: to research (that AR is a potent source of knowledge), to democratic participation (practitioners and researchers work on equal terms) and to action (the aim is to apply the knowledge gained to bring about beneficial change).

Given the wide variety of its use in IS research, Baskerville and Wood-Harper (1998) propose a useful typology of AR ‘‘phenotypes’’. The present work represents an example of ‘‘canonical AR’’ in their schema, corresponding closely to the widely cited definition propounded by Hult and Lennung (1978):

Action research simultaneously assists in practical problem-solving and expands scientific knowledge. . . being performed collaboratively in an immediate situation using data feed back in a cyclical process aiming at an increased understanding of a social situation.

Two imperatives are thus dialectically entwined in AR, a problem-solving rationale and a research-oriented one (Mckay & Marshall, 2001). The former is preoccupied with the resolution of some organisational problem, via a research based intervention; the latter involves using the intervention as a vehicle for the production of new knowledge of interest to the scientific community. Regarding the latter imperative, no sharply delineated research question motivated the present work; the study was a heuristic one, in which empirical research (initial fact finding, evaluation) would be combined with relevant elements of established theory to generate insights and understanding in a relatively new domain.

Despite much diversity in its practice, there is a generic triadic structure to canonical AR, comprising a diagnostic stage (fact finding, reconnaissance) which then informs an intervention followed by a phase of reflection in which the efectiveness of the intervention is evaluated and the knowledge gained is refined and codified. This basic learning cycle iterates until the presenting issue is satisfactorily resolved. Although learning took place continuously throughout the present project, at a macro level there were three primary episodes, reflecting the progressive shift in emphasis from diagnosis into intervention, thence into the reflective activities of evaluation and dissemination. In the sense that there was a single high-level pass through the process, the present work represents an example of linear AR (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). However, learning cycles iterated throughout at the micro level with the research agenda evolving in response to an increasingly better understanding of the organisational problem and the solution required.

There were four members of the AR team, two academics and two practitioners. The team worked highly collaboratively, meeting regularly every 2–3 weeks, and were in frequent communication via email and telephone. Whilst there was no sharp demarcation of roles, the primary responsibilities of the academics were to carry out detailed fact finding, to advise on IS methodology, to provide theoretical input on eGovernment and to develop IT products, assisting in their implementation and evaluation. The main responsibilities of the practitioners were to furnish local knowledge and practical experience, to facilitate the project by providing advice and guidance, to represent the project as a managerial entity, and to take the lead in senior negotiations with the various partner agencies. All members were fully involved in reflection, learning and knowledge production. The presentations in the dissemination phase, for instance, were collaboratively prepared and given by diferent team members on diferent occasions. This paper is jointly authored by the whole team.

The present narrative has been constructed from contemporaneous notes, emails, diary entries, working documents (e.g. workshop minutes, evaluation reports) and the retrospections of AR team members. Data for theory building and validation derived from these same sources, and reflected a mixture of opportunistically gathered material (e.g. oral discussions with practitioners) and planned data gathering exercises (workshops and evaluation reports). For example, detailed minutes of the user workshops were taken (either by team members or appointed rapporteurs); these notes were then thoroughly discussed and implications elaborated by the team as a whole.

The account and findings reported here were developed collaboratively and represent the consensual position of the team. The primary theory-building tool was inductive reflection, founded on our collective experiences and formal data sources, supplemented by prior practical knowledge and informed by concepts from the research literature. Explicit theorisation typically took place in preparing joint presentations, especially in the dissemination phase, where there was a clear injunction to identify elements of transferable good practice and lessons learned. There was an excellent collaborative spirit in the team throughout the project; diferences of view were infrequent and minor, and were resolved at a practical level through rational discussion as part of an ongoing, evolving consensus. Nuances of opinion are inevitable in any group, but as one of the practitioners put it when commenting on a draft of the paper: ‘‘there was so much common ground, we were collectively, and more importantly individually, able to develop the thinking in consistent and complementary ways’’.

## 3. Project history

Table 1 provides a time-line showing the main events that occurred in the lifetime of the project. A more detailed description of each phase now follows.

## 3.1. Diagnosis: user requirements and product design

The main aims of the first phase were to define user requirements (i.e. what information did partners require, when did they need it) and to explore product design issues. Work began in January 2001 with the acronym multi-agency data exchange (MADE) being adopted for the project. Early discussions were primarily methodological. Two success factors are well known as critical to the outcome of IS projects, namely user involvement and senior level commitment. Regarding the latter, a Steering Group was set up involving senior representatives from all key partners, many of whom would supply data: the County Council, Lancashire Constabulary, Fire and Ambulance Services, Lancashire Probation Service and the Youth Ofending Team (YOT). The Steering Group met roughly every month, to review progress reports and to help deal with any problematic issues. Achievements and progress were further reported (via the practitioner co-authors) to the Chief Oficers’ Group for the county. This standing body was made up of the chief executives of the various agencies across Lancashire involved in community safety and had sanctioned the funding for the project.

Acknowledging the importance of user involvement, the AR team made a crucial decision to progress the project through a series of user workshops supplemented by one-on-one interviews. These methods would provide the main vehicle for eliciting requirements, establishing legitimacy and communicating progress. The scale of the problem that we were facing considerably exercised the Team. Given the limited resources available, it was clearly not feasible to produce detailed crime audits for all 14 districts. Instead, it was resolved to adopt a capacity-building approach, inspired by the process consultation philosophy of Schein, 1995, 1999). Ellis, Kiely, and Pettigrew (2001) define process consulting as ‘‘a developmental activity. . . to improve the client’s ability to anticipate and solve novel problems in the future’’, i.e. helping clients to help themselves, rather than delivering solutions. Accordingly, the Team made the key design decision to develop products that would foster and support the ability of districts to carry out their audits for themselves. The aim of building sustainable capacity in the user community resonated strongly with the emancipatory ethic of the overarching AR process.

In these early days, the Team also reflected on likely requirements and design concepts. These initial discussions culminated in the first user workshop held at the end of February. It was convened to launch the project within the user community, to present the Team’s initial ideas and to provide a forum for discussion regarding data requirements and timescales. The workshop was very well attended with around 60 delegates representing the CDRPs, the data providers and other interested parties. Members of the AR team introduced the central concept of the project, namely the provision of a mechanism for gathering, processing and making available key datasets to the CDRPs to inform the production of their audits. The primary benefit for both users and providers would be to replace many potential data requests by a much smaller number of transactions with a central ‘‘information brokerage’’ (see Fig. 1). The providers would need to provide data once only to the exchange (i.e. for all districts) and equally the users would have a single point of contact for receiving data from all providers. Another benefit was the provision of sophisticated processing beyond the capabilities of typical users and providers. MADE would also remove any personal details from the raw data to comply with data protection requirements. What would be supplied to users would be an integrated product containing datasets from all the provider agencies, suitably cleaned, standardised, depersonalised and geographically coded. Politically, MADE also benefited users by providing collective bargaining power; rather than negotiating individually with providers, they would be part of a more powerful consortium.

Beyond these general features, the nature of the MADE products was not delineated and the attendees were divided into a number of ‘‘syndicate groups’’ to consider questions regarding the datasets that were desired, when data would be needed and how it would be used. A consensus emerged around the following key information needs: socio-economic data (population, deprivation levels), the incidence of crimes and disorder, profiles of victims and ofenders, details of fire, ambulance and road trafic incidents, health information, education data (e.g. truancy) and data from social services regarding vulnerable people. General reactions to the project were favourable though concerns were raised regarding the levels of computer literacy in the partnerships, the availability of data from providers, confidentiality/disclosure issues. Regarding timescales, the need for early delivery of high priority products (e.g. crime data) was stressed.

A further workshop was held in early April to discuss data requirements in greater detail; again this was well attended. By this time, crime data had been obtained from the police, and had been mapped at local government ward level. The use of a GIS package for analysing crime distribution was demonstrated by one of the researchers. Although a positive reaction had been expected, there was a general rumble of discontent that this was ‘‘much too technical’’ and that partnerships could not be expected to use such a sophisticated tool. Group discussion ensued. More suggestions were made as to datasets and a number of other general issues were aired, e.g. the need to portray temporal trends and for interpretive commentary to accompany the data.

Following the workshop, the team met to reflect on the overall position. What had starkly emerged as the main issue was the generally low level of ‘‘analytical capability’’ that existed across the partnerships, i.e. the ability to pose relevant questions and to use formal methods to analyse data in order to address these questions. Our ‘‘organisational diagnosis’’ had moved on; no longer was the primary problem seen as one of IT provision and data supply, the ‘‘real’’ problem was the intellectual inability of many partnership members to recognise the utility of data to support collaborative decision-making, compounded by the low level of human resource available for analytic work (the typical district had only a single dedicated staf member, sometimes part-time, for community safety). This epiphanic moment put an even stronger emphasis on the need to help partnerships help themselves, by building their analytical capacity through the provision of an easy-to-use set of tools.

At around this time, the Team also decided to create a project web-site for communicating with partnerships and for distributing products. A short interlude of one-on-one field interviews was also carried out with a sample of five community safety oficers (CSOs), to talk further about data needs and to investigate resources available. A third and final workshop was held in early May to finalise data requirements as well as the general form of the product to be delivered. The first week of July was announced as the date for the first full release of the product. A suggested audit methodology was also outlined by the Team, with the identification of multi-agency hotspots at the core of the suggested approach (i.e. looking for geographical areas where several agencies had a common problem). Several key technical decisions were made as a result of discussions in this workshop. The need for readily usable tools inspired the decision to publish ward-level statistical data, rather than individual records, as the principal MADE product. Crucially, this data would be embedded in an Excel spreadsheet containing tables of data for the whole county together with tools for carrying out simple analyses. Excel was chosen because all users would be familiar with this software at least at a rudimentary level, and we wanted to deliver a package that was flexible and readily extensible by the users themselves.

A meeting was arranged involving the Team and a prominent CSO (who had had extensive experience in the previous audit and was widely acknowledged for his expertise) to define a set of ward-level statistical indicators, covering all the datasets that had already been delivered or were expected imminently. The set of indicators was known as the ward-profile. It comprised a selection of social and demographic indices drawn from nationally published sources (e.g. the 1991

National Census) together with metrics covering a broad range of criminal activities and categories of anti-social behaviour (e.g. domestic burglaries, juvenile nuisance). Indicators reflecting fire and ambulance call-outs were also included as were statistics regarding road trafic accidents and casualties. The draft ward profile was sent out by email to data providers and users for consultation. Following some feedback, the definitive profile was finalised at the end of May. It comprised 128 indicators.

## 3.2. Intervention: product development and implementation

Recognising the pressing timescale, the team had commenced development work as soon as clear requirements were settled in any area, especially regarding data supply. There were notable successes, e.g. agreements with the Police, Fire and Ambulance Services were quickly struck and data was duly delivered. The Probation Service and YOT were also positive. Meetings with health service representatives, however, made no progress and attempts to obtain data regarding drug abuse also stalled. Problems also began to emerge with other agencies who we had expected to be more forthcoming, i.e. County Council departments such as Social Services and Education. Data protection was often cited as an impediment and the need for detailed protocols stipulated for the release of data. To a degree, these concerns were spurious. The project team had reflected long and hard on our position regarding data protection. In terms of UK legislation, we constituted ‘‘data processors’’ not ‘‘data controllers’’; our role was merely to process information on the behalf of the owners, publishing material only in a form that precluded the identification of individuals. Nonetheless, considerable time was spent with one supplier (Education) drafting a data sharing protocol. In the end, this was never signed. Trust was clearly a factor in this recalcitrance, expressed in persistent demands for reassurance over the uses to which the data would be put. There was anxiety that the information would be publicly disclosed in a way that could reflect poorly on the performance of individual schools.

The first release of MADE products occurred on schedule and contained the most important sources of data necessary to complete the crime audits. The technical work behind this was formidable. In summary, datasets were delivered to the researchers in the form of comma delimited files (or equivalent). The datasets were typically large (e.g. over 100 k records for crime). Where locational coding was in the form of an address, proprietary software was used to map this into a grid reference. The data was then imported into an Access database, and plotted against digital maps using a commercial GIS package (MAPINFO) in order to attach ward names to individual records. A highly complex SQL query involving multiple views and joins was then used to produce the set of 128 ward-level indicators from the underlying base tables.

A CD-ROM containing the main release of products was distributed to all partnerships at the beginning of July. Crime and disorder audits were due by the end of September, giving partnerships 3 months to complete their audits using the products. The CD-ROM contained the following:

. The Ward Profiler, colloquially known as ‘‘The Interface’’. It comprised the ward-level indicators for all 300 wards across the 14 districts. Tools were provided to allow easy navigation around this complex data structure, and to carry out simple district level analyses, e.g. ward histograms. A simple tool helped identify ‘‘multi-agency hotspots’’ by highlighting wards where the combination of crime, fire and ambulance incidents was unusually high.

. The Full Database, i.e. individual data relating to crimes, incidents, ofenders, victims, fire and ambulance call-outs, road trafic collisions. No personal data was published; grid references were also removed to protect confidentiality.

. Full documentation, comprising a User Guide and a Technical Manual, the latter containing meta-data regarding operational definitions and sources, and comments on data interpretation.

Four half-day ‘‘launch events’’ were held shortly after the delivery of the CD in venues across the county. Their purpose was to introduce the products to users and to provide some guidance as to how they should be used. Around 90 delegates attended in total, representing all providers and users. Recognising the need to give partnerships on-site assistance, an independent consultancy (ECC) was engaged. At the end of July, there was a supplementary product release comprising a new version of the Interface with enhanced functionality and the previous year’s crime data, enabling simple time trends to be identified. Following this, work continued on the procurement of the remaining datasets. In contrast to the early summer period, progress was desultory. Probation data was received in early August, but YOT data was not fully delivered until the end of that month. No real progress was made on obtaining educational data, with the department eventually conceding that they could not provide individual data at post-code level. Email discussions regarding the indicators to be used for the new datasets drew to a close in mid-October, and the second edition of the Interface was finally published at the end of the month. Subsequently, data was received from the Social Services department and was published as a separate set of indicators in mid-November.

## 3.3. Evaluation and dissemination

To assess the outcome of the project from the practitioner interest and to address aspects of the research agenda, two evaluations have been carried out. An initial assessment was made by ECC as part of their support role in the late summer, and a more formal questionnaire survey performed after the end of the project. ECC’s final report was generally very positive, though there were elements of helpful criticism. It commented that the MADE products:

provided an excellent basis for the provision of information for the audits. They have not only enabled CDRPs to produce crime and disorder profiles for their districts but also to consider relationships with contextual information in terms of demographics and measures of deprivation. . . The system may be considered self contained requiring relatively little training or accompanying documentation. . . [It] enables users to extract key information through the use of pull down menus, minimising the level of expertise and time to generate meaningful outputs.

The report notes clear support from the districts for the project and demand for its continuation. Whilst commenting that partnerships may have been over-dependent on the ‘‘simple point-and-click functionality of the Interface and its standard outputs’’, nonetheless MADE had stimulated the development of a more analytical approach. The report compliments the MADE team on their ‘‘capacity and willingness to respond to criticism and calls for improvements’’. It concludes with a number of recommendations regarding key areas for future work, including the provision of a simple ‘‘toolkit’’ comprising ‘‘worked examples, step-by-step analytical procedures accompanied by explanatory text, examples of best practice’’, a notion strongly reminiscent of the ‘‘transition package’’ as elaborated by Fowler and Patrick (1997).

The second formal evaluation was completed in the summer of 2002. It took the form of a telephone survey, conducted in confidence by an independent third party, with those incumbent CSOs who had been originally involved in producing the audits. The interviews were based on a structured questionnaire, containing a broad range of questions (with standard adjectival answers, such as ‘‘very useful’’, and a space for open comment) clustered in two parts. Part I comprised a direct assessment of the usefulness of the MADE products, whereas Part II focused on the extent to which MADE had contributed to the efectiveness of their partnership. Completed responses were obtained from 11 of the 14 Districts.

Eight interviewees said that MADE had either been ‘‘very useful’’ or ‘‘extremely useful’’ in carrying out their audit, satisfying most or all of their data needs; three had found MADE to be at least ‘‘of some value’’. All said they wished to continue using the products. Regarding datasets, those delivered in the first release had been the most heavily used (mainly crime and road trafic data). As noted in ECC’s report, the full database of individual records had not been used to any significant degree, partly because the skills necessary to manipulate data in an Access database were lacking but also because the value and relevance of such in-depth analysis was not perceived. All but one regarded the decision to use standard software tools (i.e. Excel) as either ‘‘very important’’ or ‘‘critical’’ to the success of the project. The reliance of users on standard outputs was also confirmed by the survey; only two had used any analytical methods beyond those aforded by the simple, predefined methods built into the Interface.

Regarding the contribution of MADE to efective partnership working, all regarded ‘‘their strategic priorities as founded on a strong base of empirical evidence’’, either to a large or considerable degree. In all but two partnerships, MADE was seen as having contributed to this, again to a large or a considerable degree. When asked whether their partnership had ‘‘truly shared objectives’’, there was a roughly even split with six averring this to be true to large or considerable degree. Half of this group felt that MADE had contributed significantly to this sense of mutuality. The overwhelming majority (nine) of partnerships felt that they followed a ‘‘intelligence-led approach’’ to problem-solving, with MADE contributing to this to a large or considerable degree. Five partnerships stated that MADE had helped considerably to develop their analytical capability, and two indicated that it had helped to some degree.

The aim of an AR project is to generate knowledge through collaborative endeavour involving practitioners and researchers, enabling efective intervention in a situation of concern. Much valuable knowledge was produced over the 9 months of the project regarding the development and implementation of information systems to support multi-agency collaboration. The initial ‘‘learnings’’ were summarised in a joint presentation given by the Team in late 2001 at a workshop convened to share good practice across the North West region. An overview of MADE was given, together with examples of how the products could be used to generate statistical information and models supporting crime analysis and decisionmaking (see Table 2). The Team also highlighted learning points regarding the development of MADE. These findings have been presented at two further workshops, and have been elaborated in subsequent discussions amongst the Team, and with members of the wider community of practitioners (locally and nationally) involved in crime reduction partnership. In June 2002, a workshop was held on ‘‘public sector data sharing’’, attended by 40 delegates from a wide range of public sector bodies, including local authorities and the National Health Service. Group discussions focused inter alia on the issues raised by the MADE team, validating and extending their generality.

In summary, MADE has clearly been a success from a practitioner perspective. The team members collaborated highly efectively, their discussions and debates culminating in an agreed set of project objectives, a development methodology and a set of critical product design precepts. These ideas were subsequently deployed leading to the implementation of a multi-agency database and supporting analytical toolkit. The strong demand for the project’s continuation provides further evidence of success, resulting in the founding (in early 2003) of a MADE facility in County Hall with an initial year’s funding, partly from the districts themselves. The learnings of the original project have significantly informed the role and services provided, e.g. the emphasis on developing local capacity, the need to be responsive to users, the importance of building trust. The decision to base the facility in the heart of the practitioner community was a considered one, making a strong statement that MADE was ready to ‘‘stand on its own feet’’, firmly located in the world of practice. The academics still retain an involvement, but this is limited to the transfer of residual technical expertise and the undertaking of bespoke research as part of the portfolio of services provided. That our role is now marginal is eloquent testimony that the desired AR outcome of building self-sustaining capacity (in the design and deployment of multi-agency information systems) has been achieved. The success of the MADE ‘‘philosophy’’ has been further corroborated by the recent decision to extend funding for at least a further year.

## 4. Discussion

Although MADE has been a practical success, what of the second imperative of AR, namely the production of new knowledge of wider interest to the IS research community? What has been discussed so far is the production of local concrete knowledge relevant to solving the immediate ‘‘problem’’. Whilst of undoubted value, what of the project’s wider ‘‘research contribution’’ in terms of the design and development of information systems to underpin the nascent multi-agency approach that figures so prominently in the public sector modernisation agenda. At this point we will coin a new concept, that of the partnership decision support system (PDSS) to refer to a software environment incorporating a multi-agency database integrated with a set of analytical tools to support decision-making. In essence, MADE has furnished such a PDSS, which not only facilitated the auditing process but also helped identify areas of shared strategic concern and provided a mechanism for evaluating interventions. In many ways, the emergence of this concept can been seen as the main contribution of the research, arising from the epiphanic moment when we re-framed our foundational aim as building analytical capacity. What can the work tell us about the design and development of such systems which prima facie would seem to be integral to the success of the partnership paradigm? This is where the research contribution lies.

The findings are structured in four main areas which will be discussed in turn. First, regarding PDSS design, there is strong confirmation for the value of the information brokerage concept (PD1). This component of a PDSS provides considerable benefit to partners by simplifying the transaction network that would otherwise be entailed in the supply and receipt of data, as well as assisting in other costly areas such as the provision of sophisticated data processing. Efective partnerships are communities, and shared information systems have a key role to play in building the network of communal ties that underpin crossboundary collaboration. This brings us to the second key ingredient of an efective PDSS, namely the need for simple, easy-to-use tools, i.e. cognitively tractable artefacts (PD2, PD3). Partnership working is potentially very threatening for individuals and organisations. It entails addressing a complex, fragmented reality no longer simplified by bureaucratic specialisation (Trevillon, 2001). Moreover, collaboration brings with it the risks and dependencies of joint working with relatively unknown and potentially unreliable third parties (Gould, Ebers, & Clinchy, 1999).

Drawing on psychodynamic theory, Wastell (1999) has argued that information systems can function as ‘‘transitional objects’’ (TO), i.e. as entities that reduce the complexity of the world to tractable proportions, instilling in ‘‘learners’’ the confidence to leave go of former dependencies and to explore new ideas and realities. The MADE products, and the Interface in particular, were intentionally designed to provide such psychological and cognitive support. By providing a simple set of statistical indicators embedded in a familiar ofice tool, the Interface provided a highly efective TO. The simplicity of the DSS was also vital to its functioning as another form of ‘‘object’’, albeit deriving from a diferent intellectual tradition. In her sociological analyses of collaborative work, Star (1993) elaborates the concept of the ‘‘boundary object’’ (BO), a device that mediates diferent actor-worlds ‘‘facilitating cooperation while allowing diversity in interpretation’’ (Kartsen, Lyytinen, Hurskainen, & Kostelainen, 2001). An efective BO (such as a map) has a structure that can convey diferent meanings, but being common to more than one world has an ‘‘immutable’’ quality enabling translation between diferent semantic communities. Shared information systems can clearly play this role (Star, 1993; Briers and Chua, 2001) by providing a common representation enabling heterogeneous collectives to cooperate efectively even in the face of diverging goals and interests.

Bringing these two ideas together, an efective PDSS reflects a natural fusion of both types of entity; it should function as both boundary object (by afording a common window into multiple realities, enabling multi-agency cooperation) and transitional object (by supporting agencies through their faltering first steps in collaborative work). Both functions are important and we will coin the term ‘‘transitional boundary object’’ to refer to entities that enable progressively deeper multidisciplinary collaboration. Efective PDSS design should therefore aim at nurturing partnership by providing a collaborative informated workspace representing multiple realities in a transparent, readily understood format. The development of communal ties is further facilitated by providing easy-to-use ‘‘convivial tools’’ (Illich, 1973) focusing on meaningful areas of inter-agency decision-making and coordination (PD4).

Regarding the development methodology for PDSS, the learnings in Table 3 provide less that is new apropos of extant IS theory. The study reconfirms the importance of two generic factors long recognised as vital to project success, namely user involvement and senior level commitment (DM1), as well as re-emphasising the importance of user training and support as success factors (Laudon & Laudon, 2000; Senn, 1990). The concept of IS development as capacity building (DM2) has a novel aspect which could be explored further, as does the idea of ‘‘external entities’’ having greater vigour and creativity (DM3). This resonates with the work of Christensen (1997) in the context of free-market innovation. Christensen argues that if an innovation requires radically new processes to be established, founded on new value sets, then these should be developed by a ‘‘heavyweight team’’ operating autonomously outwith existing organisational structures. MADE functioned as such an autonomous heavyweight; it prospered because its very raison d’etre was dependent on its success in creating a multi-agency IS. The use of web-sites to support distributed IS development is also of note (DM4) though of incidental interest here.

Regarding partnership working, the problems associated with this new approach have been well documented, with dysfunctions attributed to factors as diverse as: status and resource imbalances (Craig & Manthorpe, 1999 Glendinning, Abbott, & Coleman, 2001); incongruent professional cultures (MacKian, 2002); organisational insularity and the marginal nature of partnership activity (Balloch & Taylor, 2001; Hiscock & Pearson, 1999). Partnership is a vital element of the drive towards holistic government, yet the transition to horizontally organised structures with fluid governance arrangements and new accountability relationships is profoundly threatening to the established order of monolithic vertically governed bureaucracies, and considerable inertia is to be expected (Allen et al., 2001; Wastell et al., 2003). The limited success found here in establishing genuine partnership working provides corroboratory evidence of resistance to the new paradigm.

Our results (EP1, EP2) confirm, however, that a well-designed IS can help overcome this inertia by promoting common understanding, facilitating coordinated action, building trust and fostering a strong sense of mutuality (Brinkerhof, 2002; Taylor, 2001). Opaque organisational boundaries are replaced by a permeable membrane, enabling multiple ties and common understandings to be forged, building the social capital (Coleman, 1988) of the partnership across a broad front. Collaboration is assisted by making key information widely and equally available to all partners and by providing tools to identify shared problems objectively.

Through these means the infrastructure for democracy, accountability and ‘‘principled conduct’’ can be fashioned, providing a firm foundation for a healthy and vigorous community (Medd, 2001; Trevillon, 2001). The PDSS further assists in the design of joint interventions and provides a source of feedback, essential for performance improvement and organisational learning (Senge, 1990). By facilitating mutual understanding and providing a forum for rational debate, trust is nurtured, without which cooperative endeavour is only ineficiently achieved (e.g. through explicit contracting) if at all. Whilst provision of such technology is important, it is not a suficient condition for efective collaboration (EP3). Commitment at a senior level in partners and the permeation of this throughout the organisation is vital to create an endogenous culture disposed to external collaboration (Allen et al., 2001; Taylor, 2001). Further partnership building measures beyond IS provision are required, as noted by others in the context of multi-agency crime prevention (Pino, 2001; Virta, 2002). The latter author recommends, for example, a ‘‘learning by doing’’ approach to developing efective partnership.

The willingness and ability of partnering agencies to share data is a sine qua non for successful PDSS development. Although MADE was generally successful in this regard, the development of shared information systems is highly problematic (DS1). Distrust of commercial rivals seriously inhibits data sharing in the private sector (Leverick & Cooper, 1998) and is equally problematic in the public arena, especially where there is strong divergence in organisational values, such as the present situation where law enforcement and welfare agencies are involved (6, Leat, Seltzer, & Stoker, 2002; Squires & Measor, 2001). Signoretta and Craglia (2002) describe a failed project similar to the present in the area of children’s welfare services. They identified four causal factors in the project’s demise: lack of communication between managerial representatives and technical subordinates involved in the delivery of data; low organisational priority given to tasks (such as data extraction) associated with partnership work; the use of data protection legislation as a tool for resisting rather than enabling data sharing; technical dificulties, such as the lack of relevant historical data.

The importance of these potential barriers was confirmed here, with one or more of the above factors implicated in those instances where datasets were not supplied or were tardily delivered (DS1, DS2,DS3). What may be called ‘‘organisational shame’’ was a common malaise, with defensiveness over data supply covering up an embarrassing lack of technical or organisational capability. The findings suggest a number of measures to promote a greater willingness to share data, including the constructive use of data protection legislation as an enabling instrument (DS2), the need for active senior level commitment (DS3), the use of non-aligned trusted intermediaries (DS4), and dedicated information gate keepers (DS5). The importance of trust runs as a leitmotif through these nostrums (DS4 in particular). It is worth emphasising that the dynamic relations between trust, information sharing and collaboration are reciprocal and mutually reinforcing. Trust encourages data sharing which promotes collaboration, which in turn strengthens trust in a virtuous circle of positive feedback (Senge, 1990).

## 5. Methodological epilogue

Finally, we will conclude with some brief methodological reflections on the crucible present study, beginning with validity issues. AR is commonly criticised as lacking in internal validity due to the inherent lack of control over ‘‘variables’’ in operational situations. Advocates of AR argue the opposite epistemological case. Greenwood and Levin (1998) contend that ‘‘action is the only way sensible way to generate and test new knowledge’’. In a similar vein, Wastell (1999) and Schein (1987) have argued that crucible of a real world application in many ways provides a sterner test than the confirmatory methods of more conventional enquiry. Winter (2002) considers the validity of AR narratives in some depth, arguing that group accounts such as the present are valid in the sense that they embody the ‘‘genuine voice’’ of those engaged in the work. They are valid and indeed authoritative in the sense that they are ‘‘authentic’’. Thus we would claim internal validity for our findings in the sense that the accrued knowledge reflects the collective view of the AR team engaged in practical action and that the model has provided the underpinnings for successful social change. External validity is more problematic as it is for any substantive theory derived from a single field site. Whilst awaiting further detailed research in cognate settings, our rhetoric must rely on face validity, clear resonances with the extant literature, and confirmatory feedback from dissemination activities.

The heuristic value of AR for mapping out new domains is argued by Wastell (2001) and Baskerville and Pries-Heje (1999). The present work represents a substantial incursion into such terra incognita, through which we have opened up a relatively under-explored region of the eGovernment project. Many areas are clearly identifiable as topics for future research and some are being actively addressed in our current work, including further exploration of the cultural barriers to information sharing and how these may be overcome. An ethnographic study of information sharing and decision-making in partnerships is also in train. Drawing on the ‘‘transitional boundary object’’ concept, this will feed into the design of a significantly enhanced PDSS, incorporating a broader set of operational and decision support tools.

Although some would say that the goals of action and research are so incompatible as to question the fundamental viability of AR (Marris & Rein, 1967; Schein, 1995) we believe that AR is of demonstrable value as a scientific methodology. It assists in building bridges between theory and practice, answering the call for greater relevance so often voiced in the IS field (Zmud, 1998) and social science more generally. Collaborative projects such as the present are beneficial if only by bringing the research and practitioner communities closer together. Both sides benefit: practitioners gain from the application of academic theory and a researchbased discipline to the solution of their practical problems; for researchers the encounter with the real world inspires an agenda that is relevant to the pressing problems of the day and afords a test-bed for the validity of their theories. Although such encounters lack the scientific control of more positivistic enquiry, this complexity is a source of strength. The methodological knowledge garnered and theorised here provides a valuable contribution to both practice and theory regarding the development and design of information systems to support an important new paradigm in public sector policy-making, governance and service delivery. The PDSS concept, at first faintly descried but taking sharper form through the unfolding research process, makes an important contribution in its own right by providing a novel design perspective on the technology required to underpin the emerging partnership approach.
