# Seeking the new and the critical in critical realism: de´ja\` vu?

Heinz K. Klein

MIS Department, Temple University, Speakman Hall 209, Philadelphia, USA

## Abstract

Critical realism (CR) is a research philosophy that has beeen proposed with the claim that it could be a way of ‘‘- - - providing a consistent and coherent underpinning philosophy for information systems’’. The purpose of this paper is to examine this claim critically in light of the advances made in hermeneutics and critical social theory as alternative research philosphies. CR is found lagging behind these philosophies in epistemology, ontology and actionoriented value formation. It is concluded that critical realism could nevertheless play a useful role as a catalyst in the contemporary methodological debate in the applied social sciences by broadening the frame of reference of positivists beyond the issues already recognized in the post-positivist literature.

## 1. Introduction

Critical realism (CR) is a research philosophy that Mingers recently proposed (based on Bhaskar, 1989) with the astonishing claim that it could be a way of ‘‘. . . providing a consistent and coherent underpinning philosophy for information systems’’ (Mingers, 2002, 2004a, but also Mingers, 2004b—simply ‘‘Mingers-02+’’ from now on). It thus promises a major contribution, because one of the major issues that IS as a field currently faces is its internal fragmentation. Under the banner of methodological pluralism, the tolerance of diverse and to some extent incompatible research approaches has grown, but also undermined academic debate across the whole spectrum of the discipline. We are split not only by specializing in diferent parts of the domain of inquiry of interest to IS as a discipline, but also by radically diferent methodological and philosophical orientations. This also has been noted by Mingers (1980, 1984b, 2001a, b and c). The manifold views of the nature of IS and the most adequate research methods have led to a communication breakdown, which manifests itself most obviously in the diferent languages that we speak. Common terms that usually unite a set of scholars into a community, such as rigor, meaning, practical relevance, information, knowledge and so forth, no longer have shared meanings across the methodological divides. A positive sign is that this issue has become widely recognized and is now being addressed with the recent establishment of new special interest groups in AIS on philosophy and epistemology of IS (cf. http://www.cis.gsu.edu/emonod/philosophy). These institutional measures need to be supported with theoretical and intellectual eforts to revitalize the discourse in the house of inquiry such as has been fostered by the editors of MISQ (Applegate, 1999), JAIS (Straub, 2003) and now information and organization. Such eforts can revolve around either synthesis (creating new conceptual links) or clarification of genuine diferences that cannot and should not be blurred with superficial metaphors and redefinitions.

In this situation, it is very appropriate to advocate an integrative research philosophy that distinguishes itself by the claim that it could be a way of ‘‘resolving or dissolving’’ most of the issues arising from the diversity of philosophical positions, ‘‘. . . particularly positivism and interpretivism’’ (cf. Mingers, 2003). This claim promises not only to relegate Burrell and Morgan’s classical analysis of incommensurable paradigms to the scrap heap of old ideas, but also to help to establish a new research community structure for IS as a discipline. In due time this new and more integrated research community would challenge Banville and Landry’s (1989) characterization of the IS discipline as a ‘‘fragmented adhocracy’’. But before accepting any claims to overcoming paradigmatic diferences, it is every important to understand these diferences. In this paper we need to focus on the diferences between CR and the three major research philosophies that have become widely accepted, as a road map to classify the underlying philosophical assumptions (or paradigms) of current IS research practices. They are the positivist, interpretive and critical research philosophies (cf. Fig. 1 in http://www.qual.auckland.ac.nz/; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). From this perspective, it is unfortunate that space limitations prevented Mingers (2003) from dealing explicitly with interpretivist and critical research philosophies in order to highlight CR’s diferences more clearly. For example, the engagement with some key principles of ‘‘The Logic of the Social Sciences’’ (Habermas, 1967, 1988) might have encouraged the reconsideration of the adequacy of CR as a general philosophical underpinning of IS research. Therefore, in the following, it will be necessary to reference interpretivist and critical research philosophies. For that purpose, I will presume familiarity with some of the applied research literature that is easily accessible on interpretivist and critical research, such as Myers’ website, which is linked to ISWORLD (http://www.qual. auckland.ac.nz).<sup>2</sup>

In light of these important implications, the purpose of this paper is to examine the philosophical basis of CR’s integrative claim and to evaluate its intellectual credibility. It is important to debate this for two reasons, one having to do with the possibility of bridging the methodological divides in the field and the other with the importance of scientific methodology. Regarding the first reason, CR could make an essential contribution to better communication within the discipline. This could help not only to overcome the negative consequences of fragmentation, but also to address the relevancy problem as has been argued elsewhere: ‘‘In order for our field to become more relevant to its external stakeholders, IS research must become more relevant for ourselves and we must strengthen the communicative function of IS research’’ (Hirschheim and Klein, 2003: p. 31). Second, methodology, the body of thought about the nature of methods, is of intrinsic value to any discipline, including but not limited to IS. Science difers from common sense or conventionally accepted professional practice primarily by extensive and critical reflection of its methods. The critical reflection of its methods and their limits in response to the results that their application produces is the primary means by which a research community continually improves the quality of its knowledge. Therefore, any claim to provide an ‘‘underpinning philosophy’’ for information systems research deserves immediate and serious consideration, because the evaluation of such claims promises to increase the sophistication when reflecting on issues related to the scope, legitimacy and limits of research methods.

This essay is organized as follows. Section 2 begins with a brief outline of the larger philosophical background. It provides some historical perspective for interpreting the meaning and implications of CR. In approaching this task, it is also important to introduce my own background perspective briefly in order to reveal the subjectivity behind this essay. Next, in Sections 3, 4, and 5, I will analyze the assumptions that CR makes about three important domains of research in the cultural and social sciences. The first two domains concern the ontology and epistemology of IS research. They are addressed in Sections 3 and 4. Section 5 addresses the third domain, i.e. the ontological status of social norms and values in CR. Norms and values are an important concern for any applied discipline, because we as human beings could not function without them. They are at the core of human behavior not just in profit and non-profit organizations, but also in the daily lifeworld of each individual. Therefore, norms and values are an important domain for social research in general and IS research in particular (as is further explained in Klein and Hirschheim (2001)). The conclusions in Section 6 summarize my overall assessment of the potential contribution of CR to IS research in the form as Mingers has presented it.

## 2. On the evolution of contemporary epistemology and research philosophies

When in the following I speak of science and research or scientists and researchers, I have the continental European usage in mind. In this very broad sense, research or science refers to the organized and institutionalized communities of practice, known as the research establishment, devoted to establishing claims to knowledge with critical regard to proper methods of investigation. University research from mathematics and physics to philosophy and law is an important component of the societal research establishment. IS, as a field, obviously occupies a middle position in the disciplinary spectrum between natural science and philosophy. Along with other applied sciences, it straddles the fuzzy boundary between the physical-natural and human-social domains along with medicine, industrial engineering, software engineering, management science, architecture, city planning and so forth. This broad usage of the meaning of science as encompassing all disciplines contributing to knowledge with an explicit and critically reflected methodology does not imply that all of these disciplines subscribe to a common ideal of knowledge and inquiry methods, let alone agree on a pool of accepted methods and quality standards. Rather we can and must draw distinctions between diferent branches of science with appropriate adjectives such as physical sciences, social and cultural sciences and so forth. Epistemology is the complex of philosophic schools dealing with the two questions: given the limits of our minds and senses, what is knowledge and how can we obtain it? Philosophy of science is one specific type of epistemology, namely that of the natural sciences. One of the critical issues of post-enlightenment was that certain types of philosophies tried to reduce all epistemology to philosophy of science. This lead to the positivist ideal of the methodical unity of all sciences, including the social and cultural ones. CR is suspect of being the most recent incarnation of this positivist ‘‘ideal of unified science’’.

In this essay, I am commenting from the perspective of a former Popperian realist who, piece by piece, had to give up that position. I would welcome the opportunity to return to some form of realism in the social, human and cultural sciences if arguments could be found that deal with the issues raised by at least four important intellectual developments (or surprising philosophical turns or even ‘‘revolutions’’). <sup>3</sup> They are summarized in Table 1.

Interestingly, three of these four philosophical–intellectual turns or mind shifts (the exception is the first) occurred at about the same era, namely in the years before and after World War II.

These four philosophical turns are reinforcing each other to a large degree. Together, they have afected the debate of research methodology in the natural and social sciences so fundamentally and informed public opinion of the nature of science and human knowledge so deeply as no other event in the history of civilization. They are introduced here because any research philosophy that makes the claim to providing a generally acceptable underpinning for a unified research philosophy in the applied cultural and social sciences would somehow have to reflect the key insights arising from them. As Table 1 illustrates this appears not to be the case for CR, at least not in the form as Mingers-02+ represents it. CR is therefore suspect of overextending its philosophical foundation.

The first revolution is, of course, Kant’s revolutionary insight that anything we can come to know at all is determined by the faculties of our mind. There may very well be a reality independent of your experiences and investigations, but it remains unknowable without our a priori or innate reasoning capacities. This is often called the ‘‘Copernican revolution’’ in epistemology (Niiniluoto, 2002), because it puts the human mind in the middle of our universe of knowledge as the active engine of idea generation and reasoning. This contrasts the assumption that the sense impressions of reality shape the contents of our mind, as Locke and Bacon had postulated. The mind is in charge of inquiry because ‘‘sensations cannot think’’, but for input the mind depends on our senses, because ‘‘reason cannot see’’ (Kant, 1966). <sup>4</sup> The second revolution, occurring much later in the middle of the 20th century, is the so-called linguistic turn (‘‘language may be the only reality that we have’’). It was initiated with the translation and post-war publication of Wittgenstein’s Philosophical Investigations, which Wittgenstein had begun drafting during the 1930s. The third revolution is named after Kuhn (1970), because he established the notion of paradigm dependence of science as a social process. Together with the classical critique of empiricism by Hume, it dealt the final death blow to the notion of immutable truths. The fourth major mind shift, came about by the gradual assimilation of Husserl’s and Heidegger’s philosophies of consciousness (i.e. ‘‘Bewusstseinsphilosophie’’). The philosophy of consciousness established an intersubjective basis for analyzing social meanings by linking them to the ‘‘lifeworld’’ of everyday experiences (cf. Berger and Luckmann, 1967 for a popular account of this). This in turn led to recognizing the importance of the subjectivity of a reflective subject as an important contribution to scientific investigations. Subjectivity is of particular importance in the social and cultural sciences (and IS research as demonstrated by Schultze 2000, 2002)), but ultimately afects all scientific inquiry including the natural sciences. The subjectivity can be interpreted as consisting of ‘‘the system of mental patterns of perception, appreciation, and action, which has been acquired by an individual via past socializations and is brought to bear on a particular situation of action’’ (Tsoukas, 1996: p. 17). One way to analyze the meanings of subjectivity in more detail is to trace their relationship to personal experiences in a social lifeworld. Meanings are acquired by being born into and embedded in the structures of a lifeworld, which include the day to day interactions with the people around us: family and friends, colleagues, neighbors, sports club acquaintances and so forth. Schutz and Luckmann (1974) have analyzed these structures and processes guided by Husserl’s ideas on phenomenology (cf. Husserl, 1927). This line of analysis also revealed the importance of the so-called double hermeneutic for research in the social and cultural sciences (cf. Gadamer 1960, 1975; Bleicher, 1980; and Bernstein, 1983) whereas the natural sciences are characterized by a single hermeneutic (discussed further at the end of section 4 in this article). The rise of hermeneutic philosophy is sometimes dubbed the ‘‘hermeneutic turn’’ analogous to the linguistic turn, with which it ultimately united.

While the first three revolutionary developments in varying degrees apply to all the sciences, this is not true for the double hermeneutic. An important point is that the questions of bias and reflexivity, which the double hermeneutic raises for the social–cultural sciences, do not exist in the physical sciences at all. Therefore, this is at least one of the ‘‘real’’ undeniable diferences between the natural and physical sciences. Therefore any research claiming to provide a general foundation for social research needs to account in some way for the double hermeneutic. This concept also lends support to a second diference, related to the linguistic turn and concerns the status of ‘‘natural laws’’. Whereas regularities in the physical sciences can be thought of being the consequences of natural laws, the overwhelming amount of regularities or ‘‘patterns’’ in the social world are the results of the interpretation of meanings, values and norms—be they shared or contested. Does anyone doubt that the ontological and epistemological status of norms and values is very diferent from that of natural laws? Does anyone doubt that meanings, values and norms are linguistically mediated in the study domain of the social and cultural sciences in ways, which in a realist sense, simply do not exist in the natural sciences? My skepticism of CR arises from the observation that CR, at least in the current version as presented by Mingers-02+, mostly based on Bhaskar (1989), appears to be framed in a way that is sensitive only to two of the above four philosophical turns or mind shifts. This of course, does not mean that CR could not be revised in some way to improve upon this.

Dealing with all four of the above philosophical turns in detail is too cumbersome for this essay. In the remainder, I will therefore limit the scope to three issues that cut across the linguistic turn and the philosophy of consciousness: they concern ontology, epistemology and meaning (hermeneutics), and social values. If my analysis of CR against this philosophical background leads to much skepticism of the potential contribution of CR in the remaining part of this essay, this should not be confused with a rejection. I do believe that CR appears to advance the current state of the debate even though it may fail to resolve certain fundamental ontological, epistemological and emancipatory issues. My skeptical stance should be taken as a call for clarification. This disclaimer is articulated more clearly, if put in the words of one of CR’s most articulate advocates, Bhaskar (1989: p. viii). This essay ‘‘. . . should be seen as an attempt to start, or rather continue, an argument, not to conclude one. It leaves loose ends and threads. Some, I hope, the reader will pick up and pursue for her- or himself. Others I intend to pursue . . .’’ Other venues, in which I pursued related topics, are Hirschheim and Klein (2003), Klein and Lyytinen, (1985, 2004), Klein and Hirschheim (1998, 2001), Klein and Huynh (2004), Lyytinen and Klein (1985).

## 3. On the ontology of CR

What is CR? First, it is a type of philosophy of science, which can be most easily grasped as a version of post-positivism (cf. Vasque, 1995). This means CR proposes a positive empiricist ontology and epistemology that takes an explicit account of the critiques of naive empiricism and its reformulations before and after World War II that were advanced by Lakatos, Feyerabend and Kuhn (cf. Kuhn, 1970; Lakatos, 1970; Lakatos & Musgrave, 1970) against Popper’s fallibility version of positivism as documented in Popper (1979). This characterization of CR leaves open a wide spectrum of possible positions and argumentation strategies. It is therefore almost impossible to pin down a concrete concept of CR without writing a book (which may not be worthwhile because of the rate of change with which preferred positions in CR are modified). For an introductory working definition of CR, I refer to Mingers’ (2004a) section ‘‘An introduction to critical realism’’.

Mingers-02+ correctly notes that CR is foremost a type of ontology with secondary epistemological consequences: ‘‘Realism refocuses our attention on ontology’’ (Ackroyd and Fleetwood (2000b: p. 11). Therefore, it is appropriate to begin with the ontological assumptions of CR. Unfortunately and as was just indicated, in spite of its focus on ontology the position of CR remains ambiguous. Mingers (2004a), introduces the realism stance first in the section on ‘‘Arguments establishing a stratified ontological domain’’ (p. 5–7) and then revokes it (on p. 8) by conceding that ‘‘there are many attributes, essentially human ones, that are unavoidably social, . . .’’ (this is inconsistency is also evident in Bhaskar, 1989). If the latter is the case, then CR allows for multiple ontologies, i.e. a natural and social world. It is odd that neither Mingers nor Bhaskar comment on the epistemological implication of this ontological dualism. I would have little quarrel with the dualism (or some pluralism) in the ontology if it was clearly maintained. However, positions advocating multiple ontologies are by no means new.

In 1979 Popper already had proposed a three-world ontology, which Willmott (2000: p. 77) revitalized to support the objective existence of culture. Unfortunately, he is unaware that Habermas (1981) critically analyzed Popper’s threeworld ontology and adopted two of Popper’s three worlds with only very slight modifications, but substantially changed the third world. <sup>5</sup> By CR admitting to unavoidably social attributes, it is no longer clear to what extent CR’s ontology actually difers from two of Habermas’ three worlds, namely the objective, external world and the shared social world. My question to the advocates of CR is how they proposes to account for subjectivity in studying social phenomena without recognizing a subjective world of meanings feelings and states of mind, which is not directly accessible to others, but certainly subject to evidence about sincerity or false portrayal. Is our own subjective world not the most real to all of us? And do we not want to grant such a world to all of our fellow human beings?

The realist position of CR is also expressed by introducing the notion of an ‘‘intransitive domain’’, which is then deemed to apply to the social world in a ‘‘retroductive methodology that explains events by hypothesizing causal mechanisms’’. It remains unclear if this is any diferent from Popper’s ‘‘conjectures and refutations’’ approach. Presumably, ‘‘causal mechanisms’’ in this quote refers to their meaning in the natural sciences and not to the Aristotelian ‘‘teleological causality’’. The later is very useful to explain goal-oriented behavior in the social sciences, but not fruitful in the natural sciences because they lack the notion of will and purpose in their domains of investigation (except for some parts of biology).

In order to understand better the sense in which CR ‘‘reclaims’’ realism (as if it had been lost), the following compares its definitions with interpretivism to show that it does not introduce anything new. First, we learn ‘‘that there must be a domain of events that are independent of our perceptions of them . . . and ‘‘that these events would exist whether or not they were observed or there were observers’’. From this starting point, CR then proposes a threefold stratification: The REAL, the ACTUAL and the EMPIRICAL. The REAL are the causal mechanisms and structures with enduring properties that produce actual events a subset of which then is empirically observed.

Apart from renaming matters a little and with the exception of postulating (natural science type) causal mechanisms, I see no diference from this to what could be accomplished with an interpretivist ontology (exempting extreme positions). So why duplicate? A group of people goes about their lives and the events making up their lives exist independently of an ethnographer observing them. This is the ACTUAL and in interpretivist research might be called the research site or setting (cf. principle 2 in Klein & Myers, 1999). For the interpretivist, the social construction comes now into play when selecting some attributes of a subset of events as ‘‘data’’, which CR calls the EMPIRICAL (cf. principle 3 in Klein & Myers, 1999). Both positions agree that this is always a subset of the total research situation. CR is deficient in reflecting on how the subsetting (selecting the EMP-RICAL FROM THE ACTUAL) is accomplished by relying on some imperfect presuppositions (the contextual and theoretical pre-understandings, cf. the interpretivist principles 2 and 4) and by the interaction with the field study participants (cf. principle 3). Therefore, interpretivism admits more openly to the role of subjectivity in theory construction, but shares with CR the need for controls in form of dialogical interaction with research participants in the field and the research community in general, especially reviewers.

Next in CR is the principle of abstraction. It attempts to construct ‘‘theoretical, general concepts that describe the nature of human understanding and social action’’ (Mingers, 2004a) that occurred in the research setting and can explain what was observed, i.e. the EMPIRICAL. The same applies to interpretivism. From what is observed, interpretivism seeks to infer underlying theoretical structures by conjecture and social construction (cf. the analysis of three sample cases in Klein and Myers, 1999 with principle 4). In the research practice, this implies interpreting the meanings of data, for example, as resistance, a form of social control, as giving or taking orders, etc. (cf. more elaborate examples in Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997). The theoretical structures could also include causal mechanisms, but they are not limited to them. Hence, interpretivism afords a broader notion of the REAL than CR even though it prefers to avoid the term ‘‘real’’. However, this is only a terminological diference, as CR, too, looks upon the underlying causal mechanism as fallible conjectures.

Therefore, we reach the conclusion that interpretivism has a richer ontology and is more explicitly reflective in its data collection, interpretation and theory construction. These are, of course, important epistemic diferences, but ontologically, there is little diference between CR and middle of the road interpretivist approaches if one admits that the social world requires diferent forms of inquiry than the natural, physical world. This takes us to epistemology and the issue of meaning.

## 4. Epistemological implications of CR and the issue of meaning

So far little has been said about CR’s concepts of the nature of knowledge and truth including CR’s approach to establishing the truth or falsehood of our knowledge claims. Here lie major epistemological diferences to interpretivism and to the critical social theory of Habermas (1984, 1987).

How does CR define truth and knowledge? Mingers-02+ remains silent about this and he is in good company, because neither Bhaskar (1989) nor Ackwood and Fleetwood (2000) provide much help. In the case of Bhaskar (1989), this is somewhat surprising, because of his scholarly engagement with Popper and the evolution of post-positivist epistemology, in particular the contributions of the most prominent insider critics of positivism, Feyerabend, Lakatos and Kuhn (cf. Bhaskar, 1989, Chapter 3: pp. 36). One would expect that this chapter would end with a clear statement of how CR epistemology either adopts post-positivism or reaches beyond it. Unfortunately this is not the case. The closest Bhaskar (1989) comes to a definition of knowledge and truth is the following: ‘‘The upshot of the critical naturalist argument is an elucidation of how the ontological specificities of the subject-matter of social science permit a non-arbitrary procedure for arriving at (fallible and iteratively corrigible) real definitions of forms of social life, already identified and understood in a pre- or earlier-scientific way’’. It is not clear if the phrase ‘‘forms of social life’’ is influenced by Wittgenstein, because the linguistic turn is not reflected in Bhaskar (1989) who makes only two, very casual, references to Wittgenstein’s Philosophical Investigations (on p. 61 and 147). A full realization of the linguistic turn, of course, would destroy CR’s claim of a unified, naturalist ontology because of the issue of socially created meanings.

We must infer from Mingers’-02+, Bhaskar’s (1989) and Ackroyd and Fleetwood’s (2000b) treatment that CR imports its concept of knowledge from postpositivism. Knowledge is a representation of an aspect of reality as facts or nomological statements that cannot be falsified and is ‘‘capable of generating explanatory hypotheses subject to non-predictive but empirical test’’ (Bhaskar, 1989: p. 189). It is well-known that this kind of definition can only deal with factual propositions and conjectures. (For a fuller discussion of the limits of this kind of truth concept, I refer to Habermas’ 1973a). Even hypothetical statements are dificult to capture. For example, most of us would accept the following statement as a (trivial) truth: if France still had a king, he would most likely, speak French as his native tongue and govern from Paris. This statement cannot be tested; neither does it correspond to any reality. Yet, good arguments could be produced for its correctness.

One of the most important limitations of the positivist ideal of knowledge is that it cannot deal with the rationality of arguments that involve values and norms (as in law, ethics and matters of good taste, aesthetics—cf. the next section).

Three more important aspects of CR epistemology need to be stressed in comparison to Interpretivism. These concern its transcendentalist meta-framework, causality in social research and the interpretation of social data as ‘‘meaningful’’. The first aspect is only a diference in emphasis. It directs our attention to CR’s explicit stipulation of transcendentalism in its arguments establishing a stratified ontological domain. ‘‘The form of argument is a transcendental one’’, (p. 5, Mingers’, 2004a emphasis), almost in the Kantian sense: transcendental means that CR is not based on realism, i.e. experience or data of a real world, but on theoretical constructions that precede experience in that they cannot be directly derived from sense data. Transcendental conditions describe conditions of any experience or other forms of inquiry. By explicitly focusing on its transcendentalist assumptions, CR is actually more reflective than the current practice of interpretivist research. This reflects a limitation of research practice and not of interpretivism’s philosophical base (as is referenced in Klein and Myers, 1999, especially Gadamer, 1960, 1975 and Bleicher, 1980). Habermas critical social theory also explicitly considers its transcendentalism.

The second diference between CR and interpretivism relates to what counts as knowledge of the underlying social structures to explain the observations of the research setting. Here, CR appears to be limited to causal mechanisms. In interpretivism, inferences could relate not only to causality, but also to human intentions or a priori theory constructs such as structuration, instrumental reason, communicative distortion, forms of social control, etc. Interpretivists tend to subscribe to the attitude that causal mechanisms alone are insuficient to explain all that should be explained. CR would have to carry the burden of proof to show that natural causal mechanisms are as efective and satisfactory in the social world for explaining what is happening as they are in the natural world. Prima facie evidence appears to favor interpretivism which would hold that relating to intentions, social norms, feelings and perception that in turn may have emerged or in some cases been ‘‘caused’’ by other events leads to a more adequate account of social phenomena. As a simple example consider if causality alone, can be satisfactory to appreciate why and how a terrorist act occurred or to understand why an IS succeeded or failed in a specific case. The author thinks this is unlikely as any such account will have to relate to human intentions, willful choices that could have been made diferently, interpretations, socially constructed beliefs, etc. Defining all these phenomena as possible causes leads to contradictory definitions of causality.

The third and perhaps most important diference is that interpretivism expressively admits that the researcher can only interpret what is happening in the research setting, the ACTUAL, by relating to his own lifeworld, i.e. the meanings that the researcher carries with her from her own prior experiences and uses as resource for interpreting what she experiences in the field. The larger the distance between the lifeworld of the setting and the researcher’s own, the more dificult and error prone is this process. Earlier this was called the double hermeneutic. Social research investigates phenomena in a domain (ontologically prior to the investigation) that are themselves meaningfully structured. Therefore, the EMPIRICAL can only be understood by grasping the meanings attributed to the EMPIRICAL by the subjects. And the researchers can only gain access to these meanings through their own pre-understandings that are necessary for initiating the hermeneutic cycles that are the only means of possibly grasping the meanings in the EMPIRICAL adequately after several trial iterations (cf. principle 1 in Klein and Myers, 1999, based on Gadamer, 1975). The natural scientist interpreting vapor traces in a cloud chamber or electromagnetic signals from outer space, also faces a hermeneutic interpretation problem, but only with a single hermeneutic defined by the current state of knowledge in her research community. Unless one assumes that the signals come from an extra-terrestrial, human-like intelligence that has structured them meaningfully (as the ‘‘texts’’ sent with one of the early space crafts set adrift from earth), these data are themselves not socially meaningfully structured. Instead, their structure is supposed to be the result of unchanging natural laws, some underlying mechanism, not imbued with consciousness, purpose and linguistic communication competence. These laws are the result of the social construction carried out by the community of which the natural scientist is a member. The elements in the cloud chamber can not structure themselves meaningfully as a group of people would, neither can they function as a communication partner in the way in which a social researcher interacts with her research domain.

Unfortunately, CR remains silent about how this issue of the double hermeneutic is to be handled even though Bhaskar does recognize it as part of the limits of naturalism: ‘‘The chief relational diference (between the process of scientific discovery in the natural and the social sciences—hkk) is that the social sciences are part of their own field of inquiry, in principle susceptible to explanation in terms of the concepts and laws of explanatory theories they employ; so that they are internal with respect to their subject matter in a way in which natural science is not. This qualifies the sense in which the objects of social scientific investigation can be said to be intransitive, or exist and act independently of it. For it is . . . indeed likely, . . ., that its objects do not exist independently of, and maybe causally afected by social science; just as . . . social science is afected or conditioned by developments in, . . ., the rest of society’’ (Bhaskar, 1989: p. 84). Subsequently in the same chapter, Bhaskar (1989: p. 86) notes: ‘‘It is here that the hermeneutical tradition, in highlighting what may be called the conceptual moment in social scientific work, has made a real contribution’’. Unfortunately, no solution is ofered how to address the issue. Rather Bhaskar rescues CR by falsely attributing two mistakes to the hermeneutic tradition that were discussed earlier, its alleged denial that social phenomena exist independently of the observer and that it would be unable to discover to what extent social phenomena are illusionary, due to systemic (ideological or linguistic) distortions as I would express it. This is a patent straw man. Interpretivists can rely on a number of principles for this purpose, which Klein and Myers (1999) summarized under numbers 3, 5, 6 and 7. Especially 7, the principle of suspicion, provides a bridge to critical forms of social analysis surpassing the critical potential of CR.

## 5. CR’s ambivalence with regard to social norms and values

The explicit consideration of norms and values appears to be conspicuous by their absence in the theoretical foundations from CR at least as presented in Mingers-02+ and Ackroyd and Fleetwood (2000b). Surprisingly, the values of freedom and emancipation are at the core of Chapters 6 and 8 in Bhaskar (1989).<sup>6</sup> Of course, as any field research necessarily discovers values in people’s lives, value issues invariably crop up at least implicitly in any substantive (empirical) contributions regardless whether they claim to follow CR’s methodological tenets or not and such is the case in the empirical studies of Ackroyd and Fleetwood (2000a). However, here is exactly the rub: The analysis and investigation of values are in no way connected to or guided by a theoretical base, i.e. the value discourse is not logically connected in any way to CR’s ontology or epistemology—sketchy and vague as the latter may be. This break is obvious from the reference to Marxism in the footnote above and from seeking recourse to Rorty’s (1980) analysis of freedom when raising the question, how freedom is possible in Chapter 8. This deficiency is not addressed in Chapter 6 where Bhaskar (1989) attacks the value neutrality of social science and ends with an appeal to Freud and Marx for ‘‘depthinvestigation’’ to achieve emancipation: ‘‘The object of the depth-investigation is emancipation’’ (Bhaskar, 1989: p. 113).

A second major weakness in Bhaskar’s (1989) treatment of values is the failure to distinguish between cultural values and political value perspectives that tend to become an ideology for the ruling elite. Clearly Bhaskar’s appeal to Marxism is at the ideological level while his reference to Freud and emancipation appears to be more at the social–cultural level with implications for personal values. However, there is no systematic treatment where values in general come from, how we should address value conflicts and how cultural values might change.

If Bhaskar had taken his cues for developing the contemporary version of his CR more explicitly from Hartmann (1926, 1949a, b), then he could have easily found a place for a systematic treatment of values by admitting the importance of teleological action. Hartmann took up ideas from Max Scheler to deal with the value issue. Scheler was concerned with the permanent values in human personality and human action. The notion of teleological action would also provide CR with more explicit links to other theories of social action, e.g. Max Weber, Parsons and Mead. This is obvious from Habermas (1984). Nevertheless, with his concept of ‘‘depth-investigation’’ Bhaskar does come close to recognizing some of the issues that Habermas’ theory of moral-practical discourse treats in much more systematic and penetrating ways. Bhaskar is less systematic because he fails to analyze the different ontological status of social norms and cultural values. Bhaskar is less penetrating because unlike discourse theory, CR does not establish a link between the notion of truth (‘‘normative rightness’’) and resolving practical questions of the application of norms and values through informative argumentation. This Bhaskar’s (1989) version of CR appears to have succumbed to the positivist attitude that the rightness of norms and actions is more or less an arbitrary personal decision that cannot be grounded in good reasons. This has the dysfunctional consequence that value conflicts at the collective (institutional or societal) level can only be resolved by voting or political strife, because values are ‘‘beyond reason’’. We shall return to this important point in the conclusions of this essay.

The likely reason for the failure of CR to incorporate norms and values in its ontology and epistemology systematically is the descendence of its language and thought from positivism. Positivists have always had a problem recognizing ethical reasoning as a form of knowledge, because nothing like this occurs in nature (with the possible exception of higher level animals living in herds some of which have a complex family and social life). Yet, the experience of values and norms is no less immediate than the daily rise of the sun. It is therefore not farfetched to postulate a separate ontological domain for them as Habermas has done with his ‘‘social world of shared norms and values’’.

Except for extraneous commitment to certain types of values as Bhaskar (1989) has espoused, CR appears to avoid if not even deny any specific ‘‘humanness’’ of social research. This is apparent where Mingers (2004a) writes: ‘‘We now move to the second major argument of critical realism, that social science is essentially similar to natural science in its realist character albeit with modifications to reflect the particular nature of the social world. ‘‘What are discussed under ‘‘modifications’’ are social constructions and the shared nature of language. What are omitted are issues of interest, power and responsibility. Hence, CR appears to be susceptible to two extremes. One is to subscribe to the old claim that research is socially ‘‘neutral’’, that research has met its ethical imperatives if it strives for objectivity and impartiality. Bhaskar (1989) explicitly rejects this and Mingers-02+ remains silent about it. The other extreme is to submit to the danger of value dogmatism by importing certain values from the outside (as Bhaskar appears to suggest with his reference to Marxism and Freud, p. 110). In any case, in the field of IS, social value neutrality is impossible for IS research, because all IS by definition are designed to serve some human interests more than others. Therefore, the choices made during IS design and implementation always involve moral value judgments. Any philosophy that does not have a clear approach to normative issues must remain incomplete and therefore cannot provide a general philosophical underpinning of IS research (and other applied disciplines).

From this state of afairs two further questions arise: which values should be considered (ontology of values) and how should value choices be made ‘‘reasonably’’, i.e. based on justifiable grounds as opposed to being made arbitrarily or selfishly as in a Hobbesian world. In other work Klein and Hirschheim (1998), suggested that an ontology of values for ISD (information systems development) could take Immanuel Kant’s classification of ‘practical rules’ as a possible starting point to add details to Habermas’ social world of norms and values from the perspective of the domain of ISD (and possibly other domains). This classification is useful as a starting point only, because it does not address fundamental value distinctions at the level of the individual, culture and society (including the diference between cultural values and ideologies). Practical rules are simply rules for doing things. From a practical point of view, norms and rules are the most actionoriented manifestation of values. Rules are important in the practice of any profession: e.g. management, engineering, medicine, law, but they play a particularly prominent role in ISD. Kant specified three types of rules: rules of skill, rules of prudence, and categorical (moral) rules (Kant, 1964).<sup>7</sup> Categorical rules are particularly important when our discipline has to deal with social value conflicts in practice; they can be regarded as ultimate values or ethical standards. Klein and Hirschheim (1998, 2001) provide a fuller treatment of these ideas. They also noted that the past emphasis of IS research focused on rules of skill and to some extent on rules of prudence (as in the literature on IS planning, user participation and organizational implementation), but failed to address the issue of ultimate value standards. However, all forms of normative knowledge are required to formulate and apply rules in IS practice. Therefore, research concerning the design of information systems must not be limited to rules of skill and prudence in technical design, but should also address what is good or bad, or right or wrong in any particular application. As systems development also has many efects on the economy and political system, we as a discipline also need to distinguish between various types of rules and values. They exist at the levels of individuals, culture and society; they include ideological perspectives legitimating (or undermining) elitist power positions. CR appears to have no way to guide research into these issues, at least not as presented in Bhaskar (1989), Mingers-02+ or Ackroyd and Fleetwood (2000a).

Once the question of a possible ontology of relevant values for ISD has been addressed, there remains the epistemological issue of dealing with value conflicts. Habermas discourse theory provides a starting point to approach this issue. (For introductory treatment of the pertinent literature, cf. Klein, 2004, especially Habermas, 1983, 1990). The key point to note here is that CR does at best partially see these issues (cf. Bhaskar, 1989, Chapters 6–9), but that it does not seem to suggest any avenues for approaching them in future IS research. This is even more disconcerting, when maybe ‘‘the biggest challenge for the future of IS research is to come to grips with the need for normative knowledge in our field that goes beyond rules of skill’’. (Klein, 1999: p. 23) Therefore, the omission of any ontology and epistemology of values as objects of research is perhaps the most glaring limitation of CR.

## 6. Conclusions: in what sense is CR ‘‘critical’’?

The previous analysis leads to two conclusions. First in the form as presented by Bhaskar (1989) and Mingers (2004a), CR does not ofer any new conceptual developments in the philosophy of science beyond those of Popper, Lakatos and Kuhn and other post-positivists who reformulated the realist and epistemological assumptions of positivism. Second, it lags conceptual advances that have taken place in the transformation of philosophy outside positivism. This leads to three implications for the meaning in which CR is ‘‘critical’’ and these in turn restrict the usefulness of CR as an integrative philosophical basis for applied research in the social sciences including IS research. The following three subsections concentrate on the following three limitations. First CR can claim to be ‘‘critical’’ in the sense as post-positivists were critical of earlier versions of empiricism, in particular of Carnap’s Vienna School of logical positivism and its central tenet that scientific statements should be verifiable. The phrase ‘‘de´ja\` vu’’ in the title refers to this aspect of CR. It signals that CR does have a limited, critical epistemology, but it is not new from a philosophical perspective. Second, CR goes somewhat beyond this, by recognizing the existence of a double hermeneutic in the social sciences. Subsection 6.2 briefly expands on this implication. Finally, Subsection 6.3 addresses the most serious limitation of CR: it is completely uncritical about its own philosophical foundations and possible groundings for its proposed central value perspective of emancipation.

## 6.1. CR’s limited, critical epistemology

CR avoids the fallacy of logical positivism, which tried to find a rational basis for justifying truth claims on the basis of a limited set of statements of facts (verification). CR also is critical of Popper’s claim that while we may not be able to ‘‘verify’’ statements, we are able to falsify them over time. Mingers (2003) clearly states and Bhaskar concurs with this:

‘‘. . . That we can never know for certain, since science is always fallible. More practically, however, the intransitivity of real structures means that they will always have the potential for efects that go beyond us,’’

But the so-called post-positivists, specifically Feyerabend, Kuhn and Lakatos, already knew all of this (cf. Kuhn, 1970; Lakatos, 1970; Lakatos & Musgrave, 1970). Even Popper spoke of the impossibility of anticipating all consequences when he advocated piecemeal social engineering over grandiose planning schemes in the ‘‘The Poverty of Historicism’’. The principle also underlies Popper’s doctrine of fallibilism for which he is rightly famous, because he was one of the first to introduce it into the epistemological debate of the 20th century (previously, Hume introduced psychological skepticism). This is why at the beginning this paper characterized CR as being simply a specific version of post-positivism (cf. Vasquez, 1995). Insofar as CR is simply a restatement of post-positivism, it shares its fundamental limitations as a research philosophy for the social and cultural sciences. It does not go much further than simply meeting the criticism of earlier versions of positivism.

However, just because CR provides a convenient synthesis of the results from the post-positivist discussion, it is a better platform than Popper or Kuhn for empirical social research. Mingers makes an important contribution to the philosophical discourse of IS research by drawing wide-spread attention to CR synthesis. If he succeeds with this, it will move the positivist stream of IS research beyond the current stage which is mostly still stuck in the Popperian frame of reference before the full impacts of Kuhn, Lakatos and Feyerabend.

## 6.2. A limited critical vision of falsificationism and the ideal of a unified science

In addition, CR does broaden the positivist frame of reference by at least admitting—even though not systematically incorporating—two aspects from the hermeneutic turn: one of these is the recognition that values are important, the other that the social and cultural sciences ‘‘are part of their own field of inquiry . . . in a way which the natural science is not.’’ (Bhaskar, 1989: p. 84). This amounts to recognizing the double hermeneutic of social research and with this a social domain that is ontologically separate and epistemologically diferent from the natural sciences. Unfortunately, CR fails to realize the full consequences of this point. It therefore lags behind hermeneutic philosophy and Habermas Critical Social Theory, which discovered and dealt with this point much earlier and in a much more penetrating way (cf. Gadamer, 1960, 1975, Foster, 1991 and Habermas, 1967, 1988).

In various places, the previous analysis of CR found that it lacks realism, because its concept of the real leaves out important dimensions of social reality and provides no analytical base for systematically including these missing ontological elements. In this regard, it also falls behind interpretivism (cf. Klein and Myers MISQ, 1999) and Habermas’ (1981, 1984) Theory of Communicative Action (cf. Klein, Huynh for a general introduction from the perspective of IS research).

## 6.3. The unrealized emancipatory potential of CR

CR’s current most significant shortcoming is that it has advanced its program in the past without appropriately reflecting the limits and the relativity of its foundations, in particular with regard to its philosophical position and the politicalpractical value of emancipation. Granted, Bhaskar (1989) engaged some of the great emancipatory spirits of the past, in particular Freud’s psychoanalysis and Marx’ dialectical materialism, but from the perspectives of current philosophical debate this needs much updating. As indicated in several places of this paper, Bhaskar provides conceptual gateways that open CR to become informed by the hermeneutic tradition and the contributions of the first and second generation of the Frankfurt School of Critical Social Theory. Bhaskar’s (1989) references on the issue human need satisfaction already overlap with the references in Ju¨rgen Habermas’ critical social theory to Marx and Freud, albeit not in a critical ways as was elaborated 20 years earlier in Habermas (1968a, b, 1974, 1981, and 1984). However, most recently, Bhaskar (2002) has included debates with discourse theory and ethnomethodolgy.

If CR were to absorb the results of Habermas’ famous controversies with Gadamer and Foucault, this could help to elevate CR’s epistemological and emancipatory agenda with the principal contemporary critical contributors of the French school. Of particular promise are the works of Michael Foucault and Pierre Bourdieu (cf. Elliott, 1999: p. 1–17 and 97–118 for introductions). As it stands now, CR’s socio-theoretic theory base is simply out of date, but—and this is the open research question—what would a CR look like if a fundamental overhaul of its theoretical foundations was initiated? Such an overhaul could take its cue from the four revolutionary mind shifts as sketched in the introduction (cf. Table 1). From this conceptual base CR could trace the consequences for ontology, epistemology, and the establishment of values, norms and ideologies, i.e. the kind of normative rationality issues that received systematic treatment in Taylor (1992), Habermas (1990) <sup>8</sup> and Foster (1991). So far CR has not yet achieved the level of reflection demonstrated in CST and philosophical hermeneutics almost two decades earlier. In fact in its level of reflection it falls back behind Kant’s Critique of Reason. Bhaskar (1989: p. 190) mistakenly believes that he can justify the attribute ‘‘critical’’ in CR with reference to Kant, who called his philosophy of transcendental idealism a ‘‘critical philosophy’’. So far, CR has not managed to absorb the Kantian heritage and to build on it. Therefore, CR does not qualify as a critical philosophy in the sense of Kant. For future research, it will be important to distinguish the immediate implications of CR from its future reformulations that could very well strengthen the ontological, epistemological and critical potential of CR. In this paper, I strictly limited myself to the form of CR as presented by Mingers (2004), which is mostly based on Bhaskar (1989).
